I am an operations researcher by trade: some twenty years spent on mixed-integer programming, multiobjective optimisation and global search over nonconvex feasible regions, with the working vocabulary that implies — feasible sets, objective functions, duality, optimality conditions, Pareto fronts, attainment and uniqueness of solutions. I also have a layman's interest in Leibniz, and I have long suspected that the doctrine that this is the best of all possible worlds is, at bottom, a claim in my own field: that the actual world is the global optimum of a constrained optimisation problem, and that God is a solver who reached the global optimum rather than settling for a local one. I want that suspicion examined by someone who can do both sides of the translation, and I want it done in the direction I trust least: my vocabulary applied to his, not the other way round.

Please restate the Leibnizian doctrine in the vocabulary of mathematical optimisation, as faithfully as you can. I take the obvious mappings to be something like: possible worlds as points in a feasible region; the composibility of substances as the constraints that carve out that region; degrees of perfection as the objective function; God's choice among worlds as the act of solving; and the principle of sufficient reason as the demand that the chosen solution satisfy stated optimality conditions rather than being picked arbitrarily. But I want you to press on the parts I have probably tidied up too quickly, and where my phrasing above is loose, tighten it or say it cannot be tightened. In particular:

1. Is 'best' a single scalarised objective or a vector-valued one requiring Pareto dominance? Leibniz himself gestures at a trade-off between the simplicity of laws and the richness of phenomena; I cannot tell whether that is a second objective, a constraint, or something my vocabulary has no place for. And if it is vector-valued, what does the doctrine say when the Pareto front contains more than one point?
2. Does the doctrine commit Leibniz to attainment of the optimum (a best world must exist, not merely be approached), and to its uniqueness? Uniqueness seems required for the identity claim that this world is the best — if two worlds tie, sufficient reason apparently cannot choose between them, and the actual one looks arbitrary. But I notice that requiring uniqueness is a substantive mathematical demand that Leibniz never states, so far as I know. Say whether the doctrine needs it, and whether it can get it.
3. Theodicy as duality. The claim that God permits evil because preventing it would cost more good elsewhere strikes me as a shadow-price claim: evils as the dual variables attached to the constraints, each evil indexed to a binding constraint and priced by the marginal good it buys. Restate the free-will defence and the 'best whole' defence in those terms, and say whether the translation is illuminating or merely clever.
4. What epistemic status does the identity claim have in my vocabulary? 'The actual world is the global optimum' is a verification claim made, it seems, without an oracle: we are inside the solution and cannot evaluate the objective elsewhere, or even enumerate the feasible set. Is there anything in optimisation theory that corresponds to asserting global optimality from the interior of a single solution, or is the claim in principle unchecked from where we stand — and did Leibniz think it was being checked, or trusted?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I most want. Sort the results of the translation into three piles: what carried over cleanly; what had to be distorted to fit, and how; and what could not be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Candidates for the third pile, as I see them: the moral necessity of God's choice (my solvers are not tempted by feasible-but-suboptimal points, and I doubt that is what Leibniz meant by God's not being free otherwise); the notion that the constraints themselves — the composibility of essences — are prior to any optimisation and not chosen; and whatever is meant by the perfection of a world being more than the sum of values assignable to its parts. But test my candidates rather than accepting them. Finally, take a stand on the usual residue question: where something failed to cross, is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because Leibniz's formulation was less clear than it looked? If you think the doctrine, once stated in my terms, either collapses into triviality (the objective is defined so that whatever is actual is optimal) or into an uncheckable assertion, say so plainly — I would rather know that than be flattered about my analogy.

Please write the answer in British English throughout.
