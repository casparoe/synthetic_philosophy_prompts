I am a retired physical chemist — forty years of reaction kinetics, where "expected value" always just meant an integral I could evaluate — and I now co-lead a monthly science-and-philosophy seminar at a lifelong-learning institute. The members are retired engineers, a former actuary, a statistician, and two physicians: all numerate, mostly skeptical of anything that smells like philosophy trading on technicality. Next month I am to run a seventy-five-minute session on the Pasadena paradox and the general idea that an expected value can be undefined rather than merely very large or hard to compute. The group knows the St. Petersburg game only vaguely, if at all.

Act as an expert teacher of formal decision theory who has spent a career explaining technical material to mathematically competent non-philosophers. Think carefully about dependency structure before writing anything, and begin your response with a brief brainstorm about what this specific audience will find hard, rather than what a generic audience would. Then do not explain the paradox to me; plan how I should explain it to them. Specifically, I need:

1. The order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what. Does the St. Petersburg game have to come first, or does it breed the very "infinities are silly" reflex I then have to undo? How much of the machinery of conditional versus absolute convergence do I need, and where should the rearrangement idea — which the actuary and at least one engineer will recognize — enter?

2. The one carrying example. I assume it is a coin-flip game whose payoffs alternate in sign and shrink like the harmonic series, so that the expectation is conditionally convergent and its "value" depends on the order of summation — but tell me what the example must contain and what it must be stripped of so the room cannot dismiss it as a trick of bookkeeping.

3. The misconception map: what this particular audience will predictably think, and where in the sequence each error should be headed off. My guesses: that the game is a mathematical curiosity nature never offers, so the undefinedness is inert; that truncating at any finite bankroll "solves" it; that undefined means zero or means "we can't calculate it"; and that this is all measure-theoretic pedantry about ordering conventions.

4. Whether to bring in a real-world analogue — long-tailed catastrophic risks, ruin problems, the actuary's own territory — to make the undefinedness bite, or whether that contaminates the session with empirical disputes I can't referee.

5. One question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they have understood that an expectation can fail to exist while every finite truncation exists, as opposed to merely registering that infinities are weird.

Also tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level — the decision-theoretic repair attempts, the various proposed valuation conventions — and how to say so in a way the group will accept as principled rather than evasive. Plan only; do not deliver the session's content.
