I'm editing a small anthology of satirical writing that uses the conventions of business documents to send up ideas from the philosophy of biology, and the next piece I need is on the units-of-selection debate. Concretely, I want a fictional annual letter to stakeholders—around 700 words, formatted like a fund-manager's shareholder letter, with section headers such as a salutation, a review of portfolio performance, a risk-factors section, and a forward-looking disclaimer—from a firm called something like The Heritable Lineage Trust, whose clients are genes and whose assets are the organisms those genes "own." The chairman, writing in the smug, self-satisfied voice of a gene-level selectionist, should treat the gene's-eye view not as a bookkeeping choice but as a literal discovery about where all the causal action in evolution resides, with organisms and groups cast as mere conveyances, employees, or corporate vehicles.

The humor has to come from real content in the debate, so that an evolutionary biologist would recognize the author knows the literature. To that end, please work in the following, each rendered in corporate idiom: cancer as a rebellion at the cellular level, where lower-level units defect and go freelance within the firm, illustrating multilevel selection's point that selection operates at more than one rung; meiotic drive genes as rogue traders who enrich their own account at the expense of the broader portfolio (segregation distortion); inclusive fitness as a subsidiaries-and-affiliates program that justifies brazen nepotism as diversification; mitochondria as an ancient acquisition that kept its own board and never fully integrated; and a dismissive paragraph about a rival firm, the Collective Group Partnership or similar, which claims whole populations and groups are the real clients—the chairman should ultimately have to admit, sotto voce, that the rival's ledger is just his ledger with the column headings rearranged, which is of course the equivalence point: that the different framings are accounting transforms of one another rather than competing empirical claims. The target of the satire is thus gene-centrism's rhetorical overreach—the Dawkinsian habit of talking as though the Williams-era bookkeeping lesson had demonstrated that vehicles and groups are causally idle—rather than the gene's-eye view itself, which should remain perfectly legible as one respectable way of keeping the books.

Please keep the irony deadpan throughout: the chairman never winks at the reader, never doubts his own thesis, and every complaint he files about his "assets" (the organisms keep doing annoying causal work; the cells keep behaving as though the firm were a collective; the vehicles keep taking credit at the annual meeting) should quietly concede the real point. End with a forward-looking-statements disclaimer that, read straight, admits the firm's entire success depends on the vehicles it claims are mere conveyances. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the Dawkins, Williams, and Sober-and-Wilson literature and still readable to someone who doesn't, and it should stand alone as a corporate document—no outside narrator, no explanatory footnotes.
