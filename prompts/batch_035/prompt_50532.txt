I'm a third year PhD student in economics — welfare/micro, so I live in social choice land: SWFs, ordinal vs cardinal utility, interpersonal comparability assumptions, Pareto and anonymity axioms, Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, ex ante vs ex post evaluation under uncertainty, that whole toolbox. For distribution I mostly care about the normative side, and I keep bumping into this complaint from the political philosophers, the "separateness of persons" objection to utilitarianism. I want to actually understand it, not the garbled verion my secondhand sources keep giving me. As best I can tell the idea is: utilitarianism adds utility across persons the same way you'd add it across states of the world for a single person, and something about that is supposed to be a moral mistake — it treats lives as containers of utility whose contents can be shifted around between people, whereas each person is supposed to be having their own life, with claims that can't just be traded off against someone else's gains. I've gathered that philosophers take this very seriously, and that utilitarians have replies — I've seen the names Parfit and Nagel thrown around here — but I've never seen the whole thing layed out in terms I can actually evaluate.

So here's what I'm asking for. Restate this debate — the objection, and the main utilitarian responses to it — in the vocabulary I actually use. Additive separability across persons, the interpersonal comparability assumptions, anonymity vs dictatorship conditions, the difference between aggregating over states of nature for one agent and aggregating over agents, welfarism as a domain restriction, whatever else genuinely belongs. Do it as a real translation, and keep it honest: don't sneak in the economic framework's own commitments and present them as if they were what the philosophers meant.

Then give me the residue report, which is honestly the part I care about most:

Is that right, and is that all the objection amounts to?
