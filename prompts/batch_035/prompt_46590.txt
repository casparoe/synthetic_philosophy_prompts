My AP Biology class has been arguing about what "life" actually is, and my lab partner and I have gotten stuck on a case our teacher invented, so I want an outside opinion in the form of a careful probability estimate. The case: a lab constructs a lipid vesicle containing a single RNA strand. The vesicle membrane lets small molecules diffuse in; the RNA acts as a template that nucleotides assemble onto, so it gets copied, and it also catalyzes the production of new membrane lipid from precursor molecules that diffuse in. When the vesicle grows large enough, it becomes unstable and divides into two, with the RNA copies and lipids split roughly evenly between the daughters. Crucially, the copying step was designed to be error-free: the system reproduces, but it cannot evolve, because no variant is ever generated. It does carry out chemistry of its own (template copying, lipid synthesis), but nothing we would normally call a metabolism, and it has no genes besides the one template. My credence that this system should be counted as alive, once all the arguing is done, is about 0.6; my lab partner's is about 0.15, and neither of us can say exactly where our disagreement lives.

Two other cases seem to pull in different directions and might help decompose the question. First, my partner brought up the idea of certain self-reproducing crystals: a crystal grows on a surface, inherits its growth faults when it splits, and the faults affect how fast the pieces grow, so it arguably has heredity and even selection, but all of its "activity" is crystallization — no autonomous chemistry. If that counts as life, then evolution might be doing all the defining work and metabolism none. Second, there are the reports from a few years ago of ancient campion seeds germinating after something like thirty thousand years frozen in permafrost. Nobody doubts those seeds were alive, but the intuition there seems to run entirely through ancestry — those seeds descend from living things — which suggests a genealogical definition would classify anything by descent alone, and I don't know whether that is a strength or a fatal triviality. So the real question underneath my 0.6 is a stack of sub-questions, roughly: whether life has a definition by necessary and sufficient conditions at all, as opposed to being a cluster concept we can only circumscribe; whether, if definable, the right definition is functional rather than genealogical; whether, if functional, it requires the capacity for Darwinian evolution (which my teacher's vesicle lacks) or self-maintaining chemistry suffices; and whether the vesicle satisfies whatever further conditions remain, such as self-maintenance in the right sense.

Please give me your own final credence that the vesicle should be counted as alive, with the derivation laid out. I'd like the whole answer as a single JSON object with this schema: a field "final_credence" holding a number between 0 and 1; a field "subquestions", an array of objects each with "name" (a short label for one sub-question of the stack above or one you think I missed), "probability" (a number between 0 and 1), and "justification" (one sentence); a field "combination" explaining in a few sentences how the sub-question probabilities produce the final number; a field "most_sensitive" naming the single sub-question the final number turns on most and why; a field "disagreement_locus" saying which sub-question most likely explains the gap between my 0.6 and my partner's 0.15; a field "shifting_evidence" describing the argument or discovery that would move your number the most; and an optional field "notes" for anything that does not fit the other fields. Please use decimal probabilities, not percentages, and keep each justification to a single sentence as specified.
