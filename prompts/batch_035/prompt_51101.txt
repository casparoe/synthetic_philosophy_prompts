I teach an upper-level political theory seminar that closes with a unit on Hegelian philosophy of history and its contemporary aftermath. For the take-home final, I want one essay question on Fukuyama's end of history thesis — the 1989 essay and the 1992 book — that cannot be answered by reciting the standard beat sheet (thesis announced, seemed triumphant, then 9/11, then China, then populism). Students who have only memorized the criticism of the thesis should do no better on this question than students who have never heard of it.

The question must force students to notice that the thesis is really two logically separable claims fused together: an empirical-mechanistic claim about the direction of history (driven by modern natural science and the economics it forces, plus the logic of recognition) and a normative-anthropological claim about what liberal democracy uniquely provides (universal and equal recognition, cashed out through the concept of thymos and its two species, megalothymia and isothymia). The criticism in the ordinary newspapers touches only the first claim; the philosophically interesting action is in whether the two claims can come apart.

To get at that, the question should be built around a hypothetical regime I want you to construct in the question itself. Call it the Directorate: a prosperous, technologically advanced, non-electoral state that has been stable for eighty years and shows no sign of liberalizing. Its distinctive feature is that it appears to have solved the recognition problem without democracy: status is distributed through highly competitive, meritocratically ranked professional orders; national pride is channeled into scientific and technological achievement, in which citizens genuinely share; and citizens report high life satisfaction and, notably, low thymotic frustration — very little of the wounded pride and demand to be seen that Fukuyama treats as the engine of political change. There is no meaningful electoral contestation, and dissenters are not crushed so much as absorbed into ever more elaborate hierarchies of distinction. The essay question should ask: if the Directorate is genuinely stable, does it refute the end of history thesis? Students must argue that the answer hinges on whether the recognition the normative claim invokes is specifically political recognition among citizens or a more generic satisfaction of the desire to be esteemed — and that which answer one gives determines what would even count as evidence against the thesis.

Please produce three things. First, the essay question itself, addressed to the student, including a compact statement of the Directorate scenario and any specific instructions about what the answer must do. Second, a model answer of about 700 words that shows what a genuinely excellent response looks like — one that separates the two claims, identifies the hinge about the political versus generic reading of recognition, considers what each reading predicts the Directorate's long-run trajectory would be (e.g., whether low measured thymotic frustration is decisive evidence or a measurement artifact under a political reading), and says something about whether the thesis is falsifiable at all if read the second way. The model answer may draw on Hegel and Kojève's idea of the universal homogeneous state where it genuinely earns its place, but should not be a history of ideas tour. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically fumble each one. The scheme should also state explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer: name the specific moves an A answer contains that a B answer lacks, and what the characteristic B answer — fluent recitation of the standard criticisms plus hand-waving about China — gets and does not get.
