A disagreement at a family dinner has me thinking about what probability statements actually mean, and I'd like your honest credence on a precise question rather than a tour of the standard positions.

The occasion: my brother, who plays poker semi-professionally, was describing a hand in which he put his opponent on a 30% chance of bluffing. My cousin, who does Bayesian statistics for a living, pushed back — not on the number, but on what could ground it. He said that before the cards are shown, there is no fact of the matter about whether the opponent was bluffing; the 30% is just my brother's degree of belief, and a different player with different experience could reasonably hold 20%, with neither of them being wrong. My brother replied that this can't be the whole story: his estimate wasn't pulled from nowhere — it tracked the opponent's timing, bet sizing, and past behavior — and a player whose numbers systematically fail to track these things loses money, which makes no sense if the numbers are mere feelings. My cousin granted that some credences are better than others as predictors, but insisted that "better calibrated against frequencies across many hands" is all the objectivity there is; the single hand itself, before the reveal, carries no probability as a feature of the world.

So here is what I want from you: your credence that my brother is right, where "right" means that for at least some one-off, unrepeatable events there is a determinate single-case probability that is a property of the situation itself, not merely of observers' evidence. Decompose it into terms: how much rides on whether objective chances exist in physics at all (quantum events being the usual candidate), how much on whether such chances could plausibly extend beyond quantum contexts into messy human affairs like poker tells, how much on whether being "better calibrated" really is all that distinguishes a good probability assignment from a bad one, and any further terms you think belong. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, combine them into your overall figure, and then tell me which term you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would shift it most. Finally, two smaller things: say whether you think my brother and my cousin are primarily disagreeing about metaphysics (what kinds of facts exist) or about semantics (what the word "probability" means), and tell me whether your number would come out differently if the single event in question were a particular radium atom decaying over the next 1,600 years rather than a poker bluff — and if so, which term in your decomposition moves.
