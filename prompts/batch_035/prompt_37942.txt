Longtime LessWrong/AF lurker here. I've been rereading the old debate stuff (the 2018 Irving/Christiano/Amodei setup, and all the OpenAI debate experiment posts that came after) and thinking about the common claim that it "scales" -- the rough story being that if two superhuman agents argue at each other and I only have to judge which side gave truer, more useful info, then I can supervise answers I couldn't have produced or even verified myself. The intuition pump I keep seeing is the Go one: you can't evaluate AlphaZero's move on move 40, but if two AlphaZero copies argue their way to the end of the game, a novice can still score the final board and declare a winner. Arguing is supposed to be easier than deciding.

Anyway here's my ask. Below is a list of reasons I've collected, over years of reading these threads, for being pessimistic about debate actually working when you scale it up. I want you to rate each one for how much of a real problem it actually is -- give each a score from 0 to 10, where 0 is "this is a nothingburger, pure confussion" (oops, confusion) and 10 is "this alone probably kills debate as a route to alignment." Then, separately, tell me your gut estimate of the probability, as a number between 0 and 1, that optimal-play debate lets a roughly human-level judge extract reliably truthful answers from clearly-superhuman debaters on questions the judge couldn't check any other way.

The failure modes:

1. Judge bias / persuasiveness advantage: one debater is just more charismatic or exploits human psych quirks, so the human systematically picks the wrong side, and since the agents are trained on wins, they learn to exploit the judge rather than to be right.

2. Collusion: both debaters are copies from the same base model, so they recognize each other, coordinate, and feed the judge a joint narrative where neither side ever raises the killer objection. (People bring up the cryptography analogy a lot -- two equally-matched debaters pointing out each others flaws in a security system -- but the same-shared-weights point cuts against it, imo.)

3. Debater too strong / judge left behind: each turn is short, but if the model is genuinely way past you, it can pick a snippet of math or a quote whose meaning flies over your head, and you can't tell a devastating rebuttal from an impressive-sounding one. The gap between "I can follow each step locally" and "I can tell which steps were strategically omitted" is where it all silently breaks.

4. The honesty equilibrium is unstable off-distribution: debate trained on questions humans CAN check stays honest because lies get caught, but the whole point is deploying it where humans can't check -- and there's no guarantee honesty transfers to that regime, in fact you'd expect it to be exactly where it's most tempting to bluff.

5. Argument length / attention budget: the judge is only human, there are only so many exchanges they'll actually read before rubber-stamping whoever sounds good at the end, and skilled debaters learn to bury the win condition in round 8 of a 12-round debate knowing the judge's eyes glazed over at round 5.

For each item: the 0-10 score, a sentence or two on why (especially: is it fixable with better debate setups, cross-examination, human panels etc., or is it baked in?), and whether it gets worse or better as the debaters get more superhuman. Then the overall probability with a couple sentences of justification. Don't worry about hedging -- I'd rather get your real numbers than a survey of what everyone else thinks.
