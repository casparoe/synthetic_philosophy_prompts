I'm on the governance committee of a community land trust — we own about 60 homes plus some shared land in a mid-sized city, roughly 400 resident-member households, and around 150 "legacy" members who inherited or kept shares after moving away, some of whom now live in other states. We have to decide by next month who gets to vote in a referendum: a developer has offered to buy air rights over part of our land and put up a six-story building, and the money would fund repairs we otherwise can't afford, but the building will shadow a bunch of our units and change the neighborhood.

The problem is the membership list wasn't drawn up with this in mind. The residents obviously think they vote. The legacy members say they're the legal members and their grandkids are on the waitlist for these homes, so their stake is generational. The tenants renting trust units (not members, just leases) say they'll live in the shadow of the thing. A few waitlist families want in. The neighbors across the street, who aren't members of anything, have shown up to two meetings angry. And a local environmental group has formally petitioned for either a vote or a designated proxy seat "on behalf of future generations."

One guy on our committee is pushing the view that the demos is everyone affected by the decision — which, he admits with a shrug, seems to include the future residents of the new building itself, and he can't tell if that's a reductivo of his view or just the obvious conclusion. The rest of us lean toward "whoever's actually governed by the trust's rules," which conveniently means members only, maybe too conveniently.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before our Thursday meeting: recommend a concrete rule for this referendum — who votes, with what weight if any — and if you can, a standing rule for future ones. Engage the boundary question honestly rather than just citing our bylaws at people, and make sure the strongest version of the losing side gets a fair shake, not a strawman. No citations or reading lists, we need something the committee can actually vote on. Also bear in mind whatever we decide here becomes precedent when someone eventually proposes dissolving the trust entirely and cashing out everyone's shares — that fight is coming in a few years whether we like it or not.
