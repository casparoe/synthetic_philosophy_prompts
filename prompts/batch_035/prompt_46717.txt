For the past two years I have served on the review board that signs off on algorithmic systems for my city's courts, hospital, schools, and credit union, and I have kept a private ledger of my verdicts. Rereading it, I find that I cannot state the principle behind my own calls — worse, I suspect some of them cannot all be true at once. I would like you to reconstruct, or construct, the principle, if one exists.

The cases and my calls:

1. A pretrial risk score is well calibrated within each racial group: when it tells a judge a defendant has a 30 percent chance of reoffending, defendants with that score reoffend about 30 percent of the time, within each group. But because base rates differ between the groups, the tool yields false positives about twice as often for Black defendants as for white ones, and a high-risk flag leads to pretrial detention. My call: unfair.

2. The very same score, with identical calibration and identical unequal error rates, is used by the probation office to decide whom to invite into a voluntary job-placement program — an invitation one may decline, carrying no sanction, and Black defendants are erroneously invited somewhat more often. My call: fair.

3. A hospital's sepsis-alert model is likewise well calibrated within each group, and likewise produces false alarms at different rates across groups because prevalence differs. Each false alarm costs a blood draw and an hour of a nurse's attention. My call: fair — indeed the fair option, since equalizing error rates would mean missing more true cases overall.

4. A lender computes a single risk score for all applicants but sets different cutoffs by group: an applicant from one group is approved at a score for which an applicant from another, otherwise identical in every particular the lender can see, is denied. My call: unfair, and the wrongness seems to be located at the level of the two individuals standing side by side.

5. A school district flags children for reading intervention using a lower threshold for children from neighborhoods whose schools have been underfunded for decades, so that two children with identical scores and identical backgrounds receive different treatment. My call: fair, and I would defend it as something the district owes those children.

6. A hiring tool deletes the protected-attribute field but trains on postal code and extracurriculars, which between them carry much the same information. My call: unfair, and not made fair by the deletion.

My tentative thought is that what matters is who bears the cost of an error and how heavy that cost is — detention on one end, a declined invitation or a blood draw on the other — together with whether group-conditional treatment corrects an accumulated group-level wrong or merely exploits a statistical regularity. But I cannot get this thought to cover everything, and our statistician tells me something I have never fully absorbed: that a tool cannot be calibrated within each group and have equal error rates across groups unless the base rates are the same, so that any principle I hold has to choose. I would like you to state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that it yields a determinate verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on, which the board will hear next month: a regulator requires an insurer to charge equal average premiums to two groups with genuinely different accident rates, so that an excellent driver in the higher-rate group pays substantially more than a reckless one in the other. Please be candid about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate — I am more attached to some than others, with case 1 and case 4 feeling non-negotiable and case 3 the one I would surrender first — and where you overturn a verdict, tell me why the principle's cost is worth paying.
