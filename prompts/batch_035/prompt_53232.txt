I got back two weeks ago from a ten-day silent meditation retreat, and a sentence my teacher kept repeating has been following me around since: pleasure is just the absence of suffering. She used it to argue that we should stop chasing positive experiences — excitement, ambition, indulgence — because these merely paper over an itch, and that the good life consists entirely in removing itches: tranquility rather than thrills. On retreat this felt profound; now that I'm home and deciding whether to accept a job offer that genuinely excites me, I want to know what claim I'd actually be accepting. It seems to me the one-liner could be doing several distinct jobs: a phenomenological claim about what pleasant experiences are made of (that the pleasure of eating when hungry is nothing but hunger fading), a semantic claim about how the word "pleasure" works, an empirical claim about hedonic asymmetry (that relief runs deeper and lasts longer than craving-driven enjoyment), and a normative claim that only the removal of suffering counts, positive states being worthless or merely instrumental. A friend who follows effective-altruism debates added a fifth possibility: that only the absence of suffering has moral weight, so creating happy beings matters not at all — though I can't tell whether that follows from any of the other readings or just borrows the sentence's rhetoric. Please lay these and any other readings out as a menu, and for each say whether it is plausible, whether it is substantive or a platitude, and — most importantly — whether it would actually support my teacher's advice to stop pursuing exciting experiences. I want to know whether I was given one insight or four claims sharing a sentence.
