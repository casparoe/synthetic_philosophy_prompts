I've gotten pulled into a long-running argument with a friend who restores wooden boats, and it has become clear to me that we don't even agree on what would count as settling it. The trigger was a situation at the maritime museum where he volunteers: a century-old sloop whose hull has been re-planked so many times that no original timber survives above the waterline, while the discarded planks sit numbered in crates in the basement. A private collector has now offered to buy the crates and reassemble them into a second hull, and he and the collector's lawyer each insist, with total confidence, that only one of the resulting vessels is "the" sloop. Before I go back to my friend and argue about temporal parts or whatever else, I want the specification first: what would a theory of identity-over-time for artifacts actually have to deliver before it counts as adequate?

Write me the desiderata. The cases it must get right should include at least the obvious ones — gradual replacement where each step seems to preserve the object, reassembly of discarded parts into a duplicate, and situations where the two happen side by side — but I'm also interested in the messier institutional cases: curators replacing the majority of an object and calling it conservation, restorations governed by professional charters, insurance and provenance practices that treat a largely rebuilt object as continuous for legal purposes. Include the distinctions the account has to respect, such as that between the identity of the object and the identity of its matter, and between metaphysical identity and socially conferred continuity. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say why it's a requirement rather than a preference.

Then tell me which of the desiderata are non-negotiable and which are really just prejudices of one camp — I've noticed that people who favor continuity of form and people who favor origin or matter seem to be demanding different things from the same word. Flag any pair or subset of the requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, especially anything involving matching our ordinary judgments about the classic cases, since I have a suspicion that ordinary practice is itself inconsistent and that the full list is not satisfiable by any single criterion. Finally, say which single requirement does the most work to exclude otherwise attractive views. Skip a survey of the literature — what I want is the checklist, because my friend and I can't currently agree on what would even falsify our positions.
