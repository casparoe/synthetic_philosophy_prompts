I teach a graduate seminar on ethical theory, and a student has produced an argument that, if sound, would show that Scanlon's contractualism cannot both retain its signature resistance to aggregation and permit the taxation that any modern state requires. I have been unable to locate the flaw to my own satisfaction, and I would like a careful, step-by-step diagnosis. Please write your response in American English.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) On Scanlon's contractualist test, an act or policy is impermissible if some principle permitting it could be reasonably rejected by at least one person, where a person has grounds for rejection when the permitted principle would leave that person worse off than the relevant alternative would.

(2) Either the strength of a person's complaint is fixed independently of how the alternative principle treats other people, or it is not. If it is not—if my complaint can be weakened by the benefits a rival principle confers on third parties—then complaints are being weighed against one another across persons, and the contractualist has abandoned the individualist restriction that is supposed to distinguish the view from aggregative consequentialism. So if contractualism is to remain a distinct alternative to utilitarianism, the strength of each person's complaint must be fixed independently of the gains that the rejected principle's alternative distributes to others.

(3) Consider a tax code that funds disability benefits, and consider Alice, a high earner with no disabled relatives who will never draw on those programs. The code is worse for Alice, all things considered, than its abolition would be: she pays more and receives nothing she would not otherwise have. By (2), the strength of her complaint is whatever it is regardless of how the revenue relieves others.

(4) Since Alice's complaint is, by (2), insensitive to the benefits the code provides to others, a principle permitting the tax code could be reasonably rejected by her simply in virtue of the fact that the code is worse for her.

(5) Every redistributive tax code is worse than its abolition for at least one taxpayer in Alice's position. So no redistributive tax code is permissible: the state may never tax some for the sake of others.

(6) My student takes this to be a reductio of contractualism: either the view forbids the welfare state, or, to avoid that result, it must let the many beneficiaries' gains weaken Alice's complaint, at which point it has capitulated to aggregation and can no longer explain what it was supposed to explain.

I am confident something slips between (3) and (4)—perhaps in the passage from "worse off for her" to "reasonably rejectable by her"—but I cannot state the diagnosis cleanly. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and give up something in (2) through (4), but I want to know precisely what I am paying. If I allow others' benefits to weaken Alice's complaint, have I not simply conceded the aggregation the view exists to resist? And if being made worse off never suffices for reasonable rejection, what becomes of the contractualist explanation of why a surgeon may not harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five others—does that explanation not rely on exactly the independence of the victim's complaint that step (2) demands? Please identify the weakest step, explain why it initially appeared sound, and assess what each available rejection commits the contractualist to.
