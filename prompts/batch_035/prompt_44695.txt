I'm a retired pharmacologist who fell into the philosophy rabbit hole a couple years ago, and there's a pattern in medical ethics I keep bumping into that I want someone to take apart for me. Catholic hospitals in the US (and honestly a lot of secular medical ethics too) draw lines that make no sense to me chemically. In an ectopic pregnancy, a surgeon can remove the whole fallopian tube with the embryo still inside it, but procedures that remove the embryo while saving the tube get agonized over or refused. A pregnant woman with uterine cancer can have the entire uterus taken out, fetus and all, but the same ethics committee will not allow directly collapsing the fetal skull to get the child out during an obstructed labor, even to save the mother. And in palliative care, we'll run a morphine drip high enough that respiratory depression will almost certainly kill the patient within days, so long as the dose is chosen for pain, but the same dose chosen to end things is euthanasia. To a guy who spent forty years on dose-response curves, this is bizzare: same molecule, same receptor, same patient, same dead patient, and the moral difference supposedly lives in something invisible in the doctor's head.

The committees say it's the doctrine of double effect -- harm you foresee but don't intend is one thing, harm you intend as a means or an end is another. The trolley literature apparently runs on the same muscle: flip the switch and the one death is a side effect, push the man off the footbridge and it's a means, and everybody flips but almost nobody pushes. Okay. But it seems to me that a practice this concrete must be presupposing an entire theory of intention and moral responsibility underneath itself, and I want to know what that theory is and whether it can actually hold the weight.

Specifically: What does the hospital practice strictly presuppose about intention -- that intentions are sharp, stable, inspectable things, and that they matter morally more than outcomes? And what does it merely fit well with, so that some other view (bright-line rules, screening out bad actors, self-defense logic) would license the same lines without the metaphysics of the inner life? I gather the experimental philosophers showed that whether ordinary people call a side effect intentional depends on whether it's bad -- the chairman who helps or harms the environment case -- and if everyday intention judgments are that contaminated, I want to know whether a practice that turns on intended vs. foreseen can still claim to be principled or whether it's just drawing the line where our mood already was. Also, is the whole thing internally consistent? These same institutions treat physicians as culpable for foreseeable harms they never intended at all -- that's basicaly what a negligence finding is -- so how does excusing the foreseen death in the operating room sit next to punishing the foreseen harm in the ICU? And last, if we swapped the presupposed view for a plain outcomes-and-probabilities one, which of the hospital's actual lines would move, and which ones would it turn out the doctrine was never holding up anyway?

Don't bother with citations or telling me who wrote what -- I just want the reasoning worked through. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
