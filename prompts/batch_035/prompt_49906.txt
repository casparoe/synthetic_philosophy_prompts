I referee submissions for a mid-tier general philosophy journal, and over the past few years a growing share of the papers I see contain, or provoke, an accusation of merely verbal dispute: the author claims that some familiar controversy — composition, persistence, free will, natural kinds — is verbal all the way down, or that a rival has dressed up a substantive disagreement as merely verbal. Meanwhile I suspect some referees use "merely verbal" as a lazy way to recommend rejection. I see perhaps a dozen such papers a year, I have a few weeks per report, I cannot do a deep literature dive for each one, and any verdict I write can be appealed to the associate editor, so my reasoning has to be articulable to someone who has not read the surrounding literature.

Please turn the tools the verbal-disputes and metaontology literature actually offers into a decision procedure I can run on a manuscript in an afternoon. I have in mind the diagnostics associated with Chalmers's paper on verbal disputes (the ban on settling disputes by stipulation, the possibility-of-disagreement considerations, the distinction between disputes about the extension of a term and disputes about the underlying facts), Sider's idea that the question is which questions are substantive rather than which words mean what, and Hirsch-style appeals to ordinary quantifier usage — but translate these into something usable, not a reading list. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask of the paper, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; at which points the procedure terminates in a "substantive," "verely verbal," or "cannot tell" verdict; and where it stops and hands the call to me as a judgment call, with an indication of what kind of judgment. It should handle the hard cases explicitly: parties who use the same words with different meanings yet still disagree substantively in a way that matters (metalinguistic negotiation in the style of Plunkett and Sundell, if you find that distinction survives simplification); disputes where one side explicitly offers a stipulation and the other treats it as a concession; and cases where the disagreement is about which concept we ought to use rather than about what the world contains.

Then run the procedure on three manuscripts:

1. A paper arguing that the debate over mereological composition — whether there is an object composed of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower — is merely verbal, because the parties agree on all the facts about the distribution of matter and disagree only about what to quantify over.
2. A paper arguing that compatibilism and incompatibilism about free will are not merely verbal, on the ground that even if the parties fix the meaning of "free," they disagree about whether determinism undermines desert-entailing control.
3. A paper claiming that the dispute over whether species are individuals or classes is verbal in the philosophy of biology, because biologists successfully do their work either way.

One of these three should come out against my own view, which you can stipulate as: I am sympathetic to the idea that the composition debate really is verbal, and skeptical of that claim about free will, and I will not appreciate whatever verdict the procedure returns in at least one of those cases. Run it honestly and tell me which verdict I will not like and why the procedure nonetheless returns it.

Finally, state the procedure's known failure modes: where it will misfire, what it cannot in principle settle (for instance, whether a journal should care that a dispute is verbal if both sides write well enough to keep it interesting), and where the underlying metaontology — the assumption that meanings are there to be discovered, as opposed to fixed partly by our practical interests — undercuts the whole exercise rather than merely complicating it. Be candid about which steps of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as tests.
