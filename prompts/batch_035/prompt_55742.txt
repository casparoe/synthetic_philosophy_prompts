I'm a PhD student acting as teaching assistant for a large intermediate microeconomics course, and I'm writing the take-home essay question for the final week, on revealed preference and the meaning of utility. My problem: every draft I write can be answered by reciting the textbook — "Samuelson showed utility can be recovered from choices, utility is ordinal not cardinal, there's a representation theorem, done." I want a question that forces students to actually grapple with the circularity sitting at the bottom of the program: choices are explained by preferences, but preferences are *defined* by choices, so what is the theory actually asserting? To make that concrete, I want to build the question around a scenario I came up with in section and which the students found genuinely puzzling: a consumer who, facing literally the same budget set on different days — same prices, same income, same options — buys bundle A about 70% of the time and bundle B about 30% of the time. On the strict textbook story, revealed preference presupposes single-valued, stable choice, so she looks like a counterexample; but a defender could reply that the frequencies themselves are now the data, and that the program can absorb her by redefining the objects of preference (over lotteries, or over choice situations) rather than abandoning anything.

So please produce three things. First, the question as I'd hand it out, roughly a paragraph, asking students to address: (i) what status the revealed preference claim has when preference is defined in terms of choice — is it a stipulative definition, an empirical hypothesis, or a measurement convention? (ii) whether the split-choice consumer breaks the program or can be absorbed by it, and what each answer costs; and (iii) what follows for the meaning of "utility" — whether on this view utility levels are just a summary of choice dispositions, and whether talk of utility gains, or anything smacking of interpersonal comparison, is outright meaningless or merely undefined-but-extensible. Second, a model answer of around 600 words that takes a definite position on (i) rather than surveying all three options neutrally. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 with named must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a line drawing the A/B boundary — for the A/B distinction I care most about whether the student sees that "define preference by choice" and "test theories of choice" pull in opposite directions once the definition is doing the explanatory work, so an answer that treats those as two independent facts should cap out at a B.
