Take the conversion of geology to plate tectonics between roughly 1960 and 1970 and analyze it as a Kuhnian paradigm shift — then tell me where the analysis breaks. I know the standard vocabulary (normal science, crisis, anomaly, incommensurability), so I don't need a recitation of definitions; I want a disciplined mapping and then an honest accounting of what the Kuhnian apparatus explains poorly in this case. So we're working from the same facts, here is the episode as I understand it, which I believe is uncontroversial: Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift around 1912, and it was rejected for half a century, most emphatically by American geologists, largely because he had no plausible mechanism; after the Second World War, paleomagnetic work began accumulating results awkward for the view that continents are fixed, and in the early 1960s Harry Hess's seafloor-spreading hypothesis and the Vine–Matthews account of magnetic stripes on the ocean floor supplied a mechanism; by the late 1960s a large majority of the profession, including many senior figures who had built careers under the previous consensus, had accepted some version of mobilism — a conversion that took years rather than decades, let alone the generational turnover Kuhn seemed to expect.

Please do four things. First, construct the mapping explicitly: what was the pre-revolution paradigm supposed to be (fixism? permanentism? the contractionist program?), where exactly the crisis lay, which anomalies counted as anomalies and for whom, and what the revolutionary reorganization consisted in — in particular, what changed in the profession's standards of evidence rather than merely in its conclusions. Second, assess the fit honestly. This episode looks like a hard case for several Kuhnian claims: the conversion was rapid and instrument-driven rather than gestalt-like; scientists on both sides appear to have understood each other perfectly well and to have appealed to shared standards (a good mechanism, quantitative data), which sits awkwardly with incommensurability as usually glossed; and opponents seem to have been converted by evidence rather than dying off, which strains the claim that paradigm change is a conversion that persuasion cannot produce. Historians such as Naomi Oreskes have gone further and argued that the earlier rejection of Wegener was methodologically defensible rather than a case of paradigm-blindness — the theory genuinely lacked a mechanism, and holding that against it was good scientific practice, not dogma. Third, consider whether Kuhn's own later softening — his retreat from wholesale to local incommensurability, and his claim that taxonomic shifts affect only a handful of terms central to the change — rescues the framework for this case, or whether the softening has cost the thesis its content. Fourth, and this is what I most want, tell me what the episode shows about the theory in the other direction: does plate tectonics confirm, refute, or simply fall outside the scope of a theory whose evidence base was drawn mostly from physics and chemistry, where the losers of old debates rarely got to publish again? Where the Kuhnian apparatus is silent here, say so plainly rather than stretching it to fit; and where you think a different model of theory change — Bayesian, Lakatosian, or something else — handles this episode better, say which parts of the story it captures that Kuhn misses, and what it in turn fails to explain.
