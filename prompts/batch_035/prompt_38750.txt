I'm in a reading group on embodied and extended cognition that's been going about a year and a half, and the extended mind debate has stalled in a way that's really bugging me. One side follows Clark and Chalmers's Otto thought experiment: an Alzheimer's patient who keeps key info in a notebook he always carries, consults the way you'd consult memory, and trusts as much as you trust your own recall. Their line is that if a process plays the same functional role as an inner one, it shouldn't matter that it's on paper instead of in the head. The other side are the internalists, and their workhorse is the coupling-constitution distinction: the notebook is reliably coupled to Otto's remembering, fine, but being causally coupled to a cognitive process doesn't make you part of it, the same way a hammer coupled to my hammering doesn't become part of my hand. When the extenders push back, the internalists demand a mark of the cognitive — some principled criterion for what counts as a cognitive process at all — and the extenders' best attempts run into the bloat problem: if trust plus reliable access plus past use is enough, then my phone, my calendar, arguably my spouse all count as parts of my mind, and even the extenders flinch at that. But when the internalists try to rule out the phone, they end up gesturing at the skull-and-spinal-cord boundary, which is exactly the boundary the whole debate was supposed to question, so it looks circular from the other side.

Here's my working theory, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: this is a verbal disagreement, or maybe two different projects wearing one word. The extenders are carving things up by functional role — asking what jobs need doing and letting whatever does the job count — while the internalists are doing item-level taxonomy, sorting processes into ones that are cognitive and ones that merely support cognition, and the word 'cognitive' is doing double duty across the two. That would explain why every exchange goes the same way: extenders say 'plays the role,' internalists say 'wrong kind of thing,' and nobody budges. But I'm honestly not sure, because the bloat asymmetry cuts against my theory — the fact that everyone, extenders included, wants to say the spouse is NOT part of my mind suggests there's a fact of the matter that both sides are tracking, and a purely verbal reading should predict the extenders would happily bite that bullet.

So: diagnose this thing for me. Is it verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or something else — maybe a hidden normative question about what we want the concept of an individual mind FOR (responsibility, praise, privacy of thought) that neither side ever puts on the table? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says in the exchange as I've laid it out, not with a survey of the literature. And then be concrete about what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a non-circular mark of the cognitive, a constitutive/causal distinction that doesn't just restate the internalist verdict, dropping the word 'cognitive' for talk of cognitive systems, or admitting that nothing will move it. If you go with the verbal reading, you also owe me an answer to the bloat point, since that's the strongest evidence my own theory can't handle. And if you think it's genuinely substantive after all, tell me what experiment or thought experiment could conceivably settle it, because extrememly smart people have been at this since the late nineties and the positions look basically frozen.
