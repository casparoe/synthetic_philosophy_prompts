I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of art seminar this term, where we've been reading on the aesthetic testimony problem — whether one can legitimately form beliefs about the aesthetic value of a work on the basis of other people's say-so, or whether genuine aesthetic judgment requires first-hand acquaintance with the work. I also have a practical stake in this: I serve on the jury for our university's annual student art prize, and the pragmatics of the job keep forcing the question on me.

Here is the setup. We receive roughly 180 submissions across painting, sculpture, photography, video, sound, installation, and performance. Each of the five jurors is expected to review everything, but the review window is two and a half weeks, most of us are in classes all day, and the final rankings must be signed off by the jury chair, a studio-art lecturer who is explicitly worried that we defer too much to one another and to faculty endorsements. Some works can only be judged on site — a sound installation you have to sit inside, a scent piece, a durational performance scheduled at specific hours — and for some of those, video documentation is all that will ever be available to most jurors. We also have, for each submission, a faculty sponsor's letter, and a small number of pieces come with reputations attached (last year's runner-up's new series, a piece already picked up by a student-run gallery).

What I would like from you is a decision procedure the jury can actually run, not an essay on the philosophy. It should tell us, in order: when testimony about a work is admissible as a filter for our attention, when it is admissible as evidence toward our verdict, and when it must be quarantined entirely — along with what counts as a sufficient basis for each ruling. It should incorporate what the philosophical literature actually establishes (the acquaintance principle, Hume's appeal to qualified judges, the distinction between deferring about a verdict and deferring about where to look, and the point that testimony may justify a belief without transmitting knowledge), but translated into language a juror can apply in a few minutes per submission. It should be honest about which steps are genuinely settled by the theory and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it should mark the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to a juror as a matter of values.

Then run it on three cases. First, a painting that arrives with a letter from a well-regarded professor calling it the strongest student work she has seen in a decade. Second, a six-hour durational performance for which only a nine-minute edited video and a written description by the performer's classmates exist, and which cannot be restaged before our deadline. Third, a photograph by a student whose work took second prize last year, submitted this year with no supporting materials and no reviews yet. I want to see at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I or the chair will dislike, and I want the procedure's limits stated plainly — what it cannot do for us, and where following it scrupulously would still leave us open to the charge that our prizes were awarded on the strength of reputation rather than anyone's genuine acquaintance with the work.
