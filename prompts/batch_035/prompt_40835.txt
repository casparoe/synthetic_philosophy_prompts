I'm a graduate instructor running the discussion section for a large lecture course in applied ethics, and next week's 75-minute section covers the ethics of consent — medical procedures, research participation, that cluster of cases. There are about 22 undergraduates. Rather than walking them through the standard checklist (was the patient informed? was there pressure? were they competent?), I want them to hit the problem from the inside: I'd like a classroom activity where they actually make agreements with each other, under conditions I quietly manipulate, and then discover for themselves that "they agreed to it" settles less than it seems to. My vague idea is some kind of small internal market or trading game with tokens — where the rules let the facilitator secretly assign different students different conditions before deals are struck: some get false information about what a token will be worth at the end, some are told there's a penalty for ending the session with an odd number of trades (so refusing a deal becomes costly), some are forced to decide within a few seconds while counting backward or otherwise distracted. Then a phase where deals can be challenged and the group has to rule on which ones count.

I'd like you to design this in full: materials, rules, a token or point scheme that works without real money, the hidden role cards, a timeline that fits 75 minutes including the debrief, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the grounds people usually give for voiding consent — without me having to name those grounds first. Ideally the debrief should push past a checklist: I want the students to notice that the three conditions they'll discover (bad information, costly refusal, impaired decision-making) feel like different kinds of problem, and to argue about whether they share some common structure or are just an unprincipled list. A few students will inevitably try to game the margins — a deal that was only slightly rushed, pressure that came from a peer rather than from me — so please include how to use those attempts rather than shut them down. And be frank with me about what the activity cannot reproduce (real stakes, power asymmetry that persists after the session, the fact that real consent often has to be ongoing rather than one-shot), so I know what I must supply in the closing minutes.

Please write everything in American English, at a level suitable for bright undergraduates, and keep it practical enough that a first-time facilitator could run it from your description alone. If parts of the design have known failure modes when run with large groups — students disengaging, the market collapsing, someone taking the manipulation personally — flag those honestly along with fixes.
