In a philosophy of language tutorial on vagueness, the question came up whether there is a fact of the matter about exactly how many grains of sand it takes to make a heap, or about whether a patient in intensive care was already dead at a particular instant on a night when she was dying gradually. Our tutor waved both questions aside with the remark that vagueness is a feature of language, not of the world: heaps and deaths and the rest of reality are what they are, she said, and any indeterminacy enters only when we describe them with predicates like "is a heap" or "is dead." Everyone nodded, but I noticed the line was doing two different jobs in the room. For some it seemed to mean something close to a grammatical platitude — that vagueness is a property of predicates and sentences, not of piles of sand, much as it would be odd to call a physical object "ambiguous." For others it seemed to entail that there simply is no fact of the matter about the heap question and the death question — no answer for facts to settle either way. And I dimly gather that this second reading is precisely what the major theories of vagueness divide over: I've read that epistemicists hold there is a sharp boundary that we cannot know, while supervaluationists say a borderline statement lacks a truth value because it comes out differently on different admissible precisifications. If that's right, then the platitude reading and the no-fact reading come apart badly, and it is not obvious which one, if either, my tutor was entitled to.

Could you separate out the distinct precise claims that this slogan about language and the world could be expressing? I'd like them laid out as a list I can bring back to tutorial, ordered from weakest to strongest, with a verdict on each: which readings are near-tautologies, which are substantive but defensible, which are substantive and probably false, and which are actually incoherent. Some candidate distinctions I've half-formed, and would like you to correct or extend: the claim that only linguistic items are vague while concrete objects cannot be; the claim that if a statement is borderline then there is no fact of the matter that makes it true or false, as opposed to its being true or false in virtue of facts we cannot know; the claim that nothing in the world is indeterminate, understood not just about objects but about facts themselves; and the claim that language could in principle be made fully precise, so that whatever indeterminacy exists is in principle eliminable rather than built in. For each reading, please also say whether it would actually support dismissing the questions my tutor dismissed — the question about how many grains make a heap and the question about the moment of death — or whether, like the grammatical platitude, it is too weak to touch them. Finally, I want to know where the slogan leaves the choice between epistemicism and supervaluationism: does any reading of it amount to taking sides in that dispute, and if my tutor meant that reading, what would she owe us by way of argument?
