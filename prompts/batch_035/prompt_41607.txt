In 1936–1938, the New Deal's Federal Writers' Project sent interviewers — in the Southern states overwhelmingly white, and often locally connected to the communities they visited — to record the testimonies of roughly two thousand African Americans who had survived slavery, most of them by then in their eighties and nineties. The resulting transcripts, later deposited at the Library of Congress, form one of the largest bodies of first-person testimony about slavery in existence. They are also, by wide agreement, deeply compromised documents: rendered in "dialect" as white interviewers heard it, sometimes conducted within earshot of white neighbors or even the families of former owners, gathered under the conditions of Jim Crow, and at times nudged toward sentimental, loyal-old-servant framings by the interviewers themselves.

Analyze this episode through the lens of archives and power: the view that an archive is not a passive storehouse but an instrument that produces, at every stage, both what is later remembered and what is later unavailable — silences are manufactured, not merely suffered. Map the episode onto that framework with care: identify each point in the chain where power operated and specify what silence each point produced. The chain runs from the selection of interviewees (who was still alive, who was reachable, who was deemed a suitable informant), through the scene of the interview itself (who was present, what could safely be said to a white stranger in 1930s Alabama, what a narrator might strategically withhold or perform), through the transcription and its notorious orthography, through the depositing and cataloguing of the collection, and finally to the later scholarly use, where historians have oscillated between treating the narratives as precious voice and as unusable distortion.

Then run the analysis in both directions. First: what does the framework genuinely illuminate here — does it explain features of the documents that a naive "source criticism" approach would miss? Second: where is the framework silent or strained? In particular, does it risk its own form of silencing, by casting the elderly narrators as pure objects of archival power when at least some of them plainly managed what they said, chose their audiences, and embedded recoverable content in testimony that was otherwise shaped for white ears? Note honestly whether the framework has anything to say about how a historian should proceed once the compromises are catalogued — does it yield a method for reading these documents, or only a vocabulary for regretting them?

Conclude by saying what the episode shows about the framework itself: whether it merely redescribes what historians already knew, or whether it changes what a responsible historian should actually do with these narratives — and, if the latter, in one or two sentences, what that changed practice looks like.
