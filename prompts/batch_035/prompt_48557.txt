The exam question read:

"Jana runs a five-person indie game studio. She and her lead programmer have shipped two previous games of comparable scope, and in both cases the actual release date landed within about 15% of the timeline they predicted at kickoff. For their new game, Jana constructs a careful task-by-task breakdown (art assets, level design, engine work, QA passes) and concludes it will take 18 months from kickoff to polished release. Meanwhile, a reference class of comparable indie games — similar team size, budget, and scope — shows a median of about 4 years from kickoff to polished release, and the majority of those projects overran their own initial schedules. (a) Explain the inside view / outside view distinction, and classify Jana's various inputs under it. (b) Does epistemic modesty require Jana to abandon her 18-month estimate in favor of one anchored on the reference class? Defend your answer."

And here is the student's answer, which I'd like you to grade:

"The inside view treats a case by attending to its specific details — the particular steps, obstacles, and causal story of how it will unfold. The outside view instead treats the case as one member of a reference class and asks how cases of that kind typically turn out, largely setting the case-specific details aside. Kahneman and Tversky introduced this distinction in their work on the planning fallacy: people making predictions about their own projects lean on the inside view, which is why they systematically underestimate how long things will take. In Jana's case, the task-by-task breakdown is clearly the inside view and the statistics on comparable games are clearly the outside view. Her own track record is interesting — it's specific to her, so it looks like inside view, but you could equally call it a personal reference class, so it straddles the two.

On whether modesty requires abandoning the 18-month estimate: 'requires' is too strong, but in practice I think she should move nearly all the way to the reference class anyway. Epistemic modesty is, at bottom, deference to the wisdom of the crowd, and Tetlock's fox-and-hedgehog research is the best evidence we have: hedgehogs, who push one big inside-view story, are the worst forecasters, while foxes, who aggregate many perspectives, are the best. So the modest procedure for Jana is to ask other experienced indie developers how long a project like hers takes and weight that judgment heavily — the outside view, in other words, is what the relevant crowd of judges believes, and that crowd would say well over 18 months.

Moreover, in the later forecasting tournaments, superforecasters were found to set a base rate early and then basically hold it, adjusting only slightly, and that pattern is precisely what made them accurate — which supports treating the reference class as the anchor. And although Jana was right about her last two games, the planning fallacy research shows that essentially everyone predicts they will be the exception, so her sense that she's different is exactly the sense everyone in the reference class had. Two successful predictions are also far too few to establish that she is genuinely calibrated rather than lucky. So my conclusion: modesty doesn't strictly require it, but given the reference class median of about 4 years, the rational estimate for her project is about 4 years, and her detailed breakdown, though it feels more rigorous, is characteristic of the planning fallacy — a symptom of it, not a cure."
