I'm in a reading group on the jurisprudence of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, and we've taken to ruling on hypothetical variants of the cases those tribunals faced. The recurring issues are the ones the real tribunals stumbled over: retroactivity, since the defendants were charged under counts written after the conduct, in tension with the principle that no one may be punished except under a law that existed at the time; and victors' justice, that is, whether it matters that the prosecuting powers were themselves guilty of comparable conduct. Our collective rulings so far:

- A camp guard who beat and starved prisoners of war in his charge: convicted.
- An interior-ministry official who, two years before the war began, organized the forced deportation of his own state's ethnic minority — lawful under domestic law at the time, with no treaty then in force covering a state's peacetime treatment of its own population — charged under a crimes-against-humanity count the tribunal's charter created after the fact: convicted.
- A submarine commander who sank merchant vessels without warning, where the record showed that the victorious navies had done the same throughout the war: acquitted on that count.
- A radio broadcaster whose addresses called for the destruction of that minority: convicted.
- The defeated foreign minister, who planned the war of aggression and argued that no positive law at the time made aggressive war an offense for which an individual could be punished: convicted.
- A general who passed the deportation orders down the chain of command, adding no cruelty of his own, and pleaded superior orders: convicted.

In none of the cases we convicted was there evidence that the victorious powers had engaged in anything comparable.

Our next case: an officer of the defeated power's security service who, during the war, tortured captured resistance suspects — protracted beatings, starvation rations, repeated near-drowning — to extract intelligence about sabotage networks. The conduct plainly violated the laws of war as they then stood and would strike anyone as gravely wrong. But archives opened last year show that the victorious powers' own interrogation units used substantially the same methods, with official sanction, on prisoners of the other side throughout the same war.

Predict our ruling. More than that: identify the rules that could each fit our six earlier rulings (for instance, something like convict whenever the conduct was gravely wrong by any decent standard, whatever the law books said, as against something like acquit wherever the prosecuting powers did the same themselves), and say what each of those rules predicts here. Tell me which of our earlier rulings is the closest analog, and be explicit about the point at which the rules genuinely part ways — I suspect it matters whether our acquittal of the submarine commander rested on what the victors had done or on that practice's being, at the time, a contested mode of war between combatants rather than a wrong against the helpless. Commit to a best guess and defend it rather than ending on a hedge. Finally, tell me what further case, put to our group, would best separate whatever rules still look tied after this one.
