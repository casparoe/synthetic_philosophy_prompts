I serve on the citizens' advisory board for my state's wildlife agency, and we've been handed a question I don't feel equipped to answer, so I'm asking you to walk me through it.

Our state protects a population of wild canids that was listed decades ago as a distinct endangered species. New genetic analyses show the surviving animals carry substantial ancestry from a common related species, and there is now a genuine scientific dispute about origins. One camp holds that our population is an ancient, distinct lineage that has recently been swamped by interbreeding with the common species; the other holds that it originated as a hybrid mixture of two other species only a few centuries ago and was never a separate branch at all. A landowners' coalition has petitioned to strip the protection, arguing that our statute safeguards endangered species and that, on the hybrid-origin account, these animals simply aren't one. Meanwhile, the biologists on the recovery team point out that the interbreeding is itself partly a consequence of rarity: with so few of their own kind left, the remaining animals cannot find conspecific mates. What looks like evidence of "not being a species" may thus be an artifact of endangerment itself, and they favor managing the interbreeding — or even deliberately allowing more of it — as the best way to preserve the distinctive ancestry. One of them added, in an email to the board, that even if the population began as a hybrid mix, it has been breeding on its own in this region for hundreds of years, looks and behaves differently from both parent species, and so may count as a species now even if it did not at its origin. There is also an ecosystem argument floating around: whatever its pedigree, this is the only animal in the region performing the historical predator role that the habitat depends on. And one board member worries about the opposite direction: if we protect these animals, do we then have to protect any individual of the common species that carries a trace of the protected ancestry?

We must recommend one of three positions to the agency: keep the protection on the grounds that the animal is a species; keep the protection while conceding it may not be a species, but arguing that something else about the population warrants saving it; or support delisting. My problem is that I can't tell which question I should even be answering. Whether the animal is a species seems to depend entirely on which definition of species one adopts, and the agency's lawyers tell me the statute just uses the word without defining it.

What would you recommend, and on what reasoning? In particular: is the definitional question something we should try to settle before choosing among the three positions, or is that a mistake — and if it can't be settled, what should we do about the fact that a legal protection with real consequences seems to hinge on it? I'd also like your view on whether the "something else" in the second position is a coherent basis for protection or just a dodge, and on the board member's slippery-slope worry about protecting any animal with a trace of the ancestry. Please argue for your recommendation rather than surveying the options; I have to write a dissent or a concurrence, and I want to know what I'm actually committing us to.
