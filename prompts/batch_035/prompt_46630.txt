I'm writing a thesis chapter on whether intuitionistic logic can be given a systematic theory of meaning, in the neighborhood of Dummett's The Philosophical Basis of Intuitionistic Logic, and I want the standard of adequacy fixed before I evaluate any particular semantics — otherwise every assessment just smuggles in the assessor's biases. Please write me the desiderata: the list of things any theory of the meaning of mathematical statements must deliver to count as acceptable from a constructivist, broadly Dummettian point of view, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a camp. The ones I care most about, which you should cover and may supplement:

1. Compositionality. The meaning of a compound statement must be fixed by the meanings of its parts and the connective — but intuitionistic disjunction and existence have stronger meanings than their classical counterparts (a proof of P ∨ Q requires a proof of one disjunct), so the theory has to show that these stronger clauses can still be assigned compositionally rather than holistically.

2. Manifestation. The use of each expression must be capable of being manifested in finite, observable behavior — the requirement behind the anti-realist complaint that a grasp of classical truth-conditions for undecidable sentences could never be displayed.

3. Self-application. The theory of meaning must not itself depend on inferences it declares invalid — the justification-of-deduction problem, where the usual hope is something like Prawitz's normalization argument showing the introduction rules are in harmony with the elimination rules. Say whether this requirement can be met non-circularly.

4. The undecidability trap. The theory cannot treat there being no proof of a given sentence as a determinate fact, since that would grant omniscience over the space of possible proofs; yet it also cannot declare every open conjecture meaningless. It has to draw that line without pretending to know in advance which sentences are undecidable.

5. Classical coincidence on decidable domains. Classical and intuitionistic logic agree wherever the predicates are decidable, so the theory should either explain why classical reasoning is unobjectionable there as a consequence of the semantics, or admit it cannot and flag the cost.

6. Scope. The theory must say what it covers: whether it handles Brouwer's choice sequences or explicitly scopes them out, and whether it respects Bishop's insistence that a constructive existence proof yield a witness independent of any particular philosophy of mathematics.

For each requirement, note whether it is genuinely forced or a prejudice of the anti-realist camp; then tell me which subset, if any, you think is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. Skip the scorecard of existing semantics — I'll do that myself once the list is settled.

On the epistemic side: for each substantive claim, say how confident you are, and at the end single out the point you are least sure of. In particular, I want to know which of the tensions you describe are recognized problems in the literature and which are your own reconstruction — label them as such, because I need to know what I would have to argue for rather than cite.
