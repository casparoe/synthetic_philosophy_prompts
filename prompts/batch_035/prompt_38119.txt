I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and a colleague from my running club — she's doing a philosophy degree part-time — has drawn me into an argument about when several things add up to one further thing. Her verdicts so far are firm: a living cat is one object, and so is a living oak, and so is a single bacterium; a duffel bag containing a glove, a water bottle, and a headlamp is just those items and not a third thing over and above them; two rocks in contact are still two rocks; a brick wall is not an object, just bricks arranged wall-wise; a honeybee colony, however coordinated its foraging, is never one thing, only many bees; and — this is the one that surprised me — a dead oak, still standing with every branch and leaf exactly in place, is no longer one object: the matter now composes nothing at all. Three new cases are on the table for next week's run: a slime mold, where thousands of formerly independent single cells have fused into one streaming mass that crawls, forages, and re-forms when cut in half; a coral head, which is thousands of polyps budded from a single larva, genetically identical, linked by shared tissue, depositing a shared skeleton; and — this is what started the whole argument — a distributed system I built at work, thousands of processes on separate machines that collectively detect failures, rebalance load, and present a single service, with no process in charge.

Predict her verdicts on all three, and lead your answer with just those three predictions. Then give me the rule she seems to be running. Something like "the parts compose something exactly when their activity makes up a single life" fits everything she's said, but so might "exactly when they form one genetically uniform, developmentally integrated body" or "exactly when they jointly act as one autonomous agent" — and I genuinely can't tell whether these rules even disagree about the slime mold, the coral, or my cluster. So: what each candidate rule predicts about the three cases, your best guess as to which rule she's actually running and why, and the further case, whatever it is, that would best separate the rules that agree on her verdicts so far — I want something to spring on her at mile five. Keep the whole answer under 350 words.
