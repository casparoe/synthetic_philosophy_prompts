You are to act as a senior philosopher specializing in axiology and population ethics who has spent a career translating ethical theories into tools that grant-makers, regulators, and review boards can actually use. Please think carefully and work in stages: begin by brainstorming several ways the fund's commitments (stated below) could be operationalized, noting where the commitments pull in different directions, then commit to the design you think best survives the constraints, and be explicit about what your chosen design sacrifices.

Context: I am a program officer at a small private fund that makes grants exclusively on animal welfare. Our charter commits the fund to the following five positions, and I need them turned into something usable:

(1) Reducing suffering takes strict priority over creating or enhancing happiness whenever the two compete for the same budget.
(2) Suffering below some intensity threshold T may be traded against other goods — including pleasure — using roughly standard utilitarian arithmetic: numbers of animals, duration, intensity.
(3) Suffering at or above T is lexically prior: no amount of happiness, for any number of beings, can offset preventing or ending it.
(4) The fund does not know where T sits; the charter is silent, and our board members disagree sharply about how intense something must be to count.
(5) Even below T, pleasure and pain are not straightforwardly commensurable; a "hedonic exchange rate" between them is at best a modeling convenience, and the procedure should not pretend otherwise.

I need a written decision procedure my team of three officers can actually run each quarter. Practical constraints: we receive around 80 proposals per cycle, each officer has roughly one hour per proposal, and the final sign-off comes from a board with no philosophy background that must be able to see, from the paperwork alone, why a proposal failed. The procedure should therefore specify: the questions to ask about a proposal, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which answers return an automatic "no"; and which situations must be escalated to the board because the open question is one of values rather than facts — especially threshold placement, which I suspect cannot be proceduralized away and should be handled honestly rather than buried in a formula.

The procedure must also tell my officers what to do about three recurring trouble spots. First, proposals whose entire expected benefit is positive-affect enhancement — enrichment for animals whose baseline welfare is already decent — since the charter is unclear on whether these are permitted, discouraged, or forbidden. Second, "many-mild versus few-intense" comparisons: a large herd of chronic, low-grade malaise against a small number of animals in acute agony, where the arithmetic below T points one way and the lexical commitment above T points the other. Third, proposals whose intensity estimates cross T only under the optimistic end of the applicant's own numbers.

Then run the procedure on three representative proposals, in the form of worked examples with the reasoning visible at each step: (a) subsidizing a switch of a region's broiler flocks to slower-growing breeds, which would reduce mild chronic malaise across tens of millions of birds per year while modestly increasing mortality for some; (b) a surgical street-animal rescue service that treats roughly eight hundred animals per year in acute, T-crossing pain — this project is our founder's favorite, so be honest about what the procedure yields; (c) installing perches, substrate, and foraging devices for a zoo population of elephants whose welfare indicators are already reasonably good.

Finally, close with a candid statement of where the procedure misfires: which classes of proposals it will systematically mistreat, and which of the fund's five commitments does the most damage when forced into an operational form. Do not give me a reading list or name-drop the literature; I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
