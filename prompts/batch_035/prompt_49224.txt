My close friend Dev has spent months trying to decide whether to have a child, and the argument that broke out about it at her birthday dinner was easily the most interesting part of the evening. Maya opened by saying the decision cannot be made rationally at all: becoming a parent is transformative in the sense that you cannot know what it is like without actually becoming one, and any expected-utility calculation smuggles in the current self's valuations for outcomes that will be lived by a future self whose values cannot now be predicted — so the deliberation is structurally broken, not just hard. Ben replied that this proves far too much: every significant choice changes you to some degree — moving cities, taking a first job, marrying anyone — so if self-change destroyed rational choosability, nothing significant would ever be rationally choosable, which is absurd; the sensible reading is that rational choice breaks down only past some threshold of self-change, and parenthood need not be over it. Maya answered that the threshold is illusory, since Ben cannot identify which values are "core" and preserved without already knowing how they will change; and she located the real asymmetry elsewhere: for ordinary choices you can lean on testimony and imagination, but for transformative ones testimony fails in principle — being told what it is like is not knowing what it is like — so the deficit is categorical, not one of degree. Ben's comeback was that the deficit does not do the work she thinks, because you can rationally choose an experience for its discovery value alone: valuing finding out is a preference you have now and does not require predicting the outcome's phenomenology, just as no one thinks you cannot rationally taste an unfamiliar wine because you cannot imagine the taste; besides, people choose experiences precisely in order to become someone — the medical student enrolls partly to acquire a doctor's tastes — and we do not call that irrational. Maya's last word was that discovery-value reasoning is fine for small, reversible gambles but ruinous for irreversible ones: discovering might mean discovering you have destroyed what you now care about most, and the medical school analogy fails because the student can quit, whereas parenthood is the rare commitment with no exit — so what is really being gambled away is the option itself, and it is the loss of exit, not the unknown phenomenology, that makes the decision intractable.
