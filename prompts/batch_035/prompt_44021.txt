can you write me a parody piece? i want an FAQ — like the kind HR hands you on day one at a new job — from a fictional megacorp that has gone completely all-in on nudging. the premise: upper management read Thaler and Sunstein and decided the *entire* employee experience is now designed by the newly created "Choice Architecture Office." about 650 words, formatted as numbered Q&As, in that relentlessly cheerful corporate-onboarding voice, deadpan all the way through — the company believes in itself 100% and never winks.

the jokes have to come from the actual substance of the paternalism/nudging debate, not just generic "company bad":

- the office's core claim that there is no neutral design anyway — there's always a default, so choosing it for you isn't taking anything from you, it's just... architecture
- "libertarian paternalism" and its promise that opting out is always cheap and easy, which should visibly fray (you CAN take the stairs instead of the default elevator, it's just that the stairwell door is painted to look like a wall, for aesthetic reasons)
- transparency: the FAQ cheerfully insists every nudge is fully disclosed, and the disclosure document is located... somewhere. sub-basement 2 maybe
- at least one nudge that openly works by exploitng employees' cognitive biases (loss aversion, social proof, a leaderboard of who took the default option) and gets defended as "helping you help yourself"
- the question of who audits the Choice Architecture Office given that its members presumably have the same biases as everyone else, answered with total confidence
- one Q where an employee asks "isn't this just manipulation?" and gets a very self-assured nudge-vs-shove distinction that does not survive the paragraph it appears in
- and ideally the funniest bit: the company's stated goal is employee welfare, where welfare turns out to be defined, circularly, as whatever the default option is

so the target is the real argumentative terrain — the no-neutral-design move, easy opt-out, transparency about nudges, welfare vs autonomy justifications, the slippery slope worry — and a specialist should feel the author actually engaged with that literature and its critics, not just skimmed a TED talk about defaults. but it also has to be readable and funny for someone who's never heard of any of it. no winking at the reader, and the satire should stay fair — exaggerate real features of the view rather than invent dumb ones. end it on some corporate sign-off that quietly lands the reductio.
