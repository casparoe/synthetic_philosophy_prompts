I teach an intermediate course on game theory and rational choice for philosophy students — eighteen of them, philosophically sophisticated but viscerally allergic to anything that looks like algebra — and we've just finished the prisoner's dilemma and Nash equilibrium. Next week I want to spend our single 75-minute session on Hofstadter's superrationality, and rather than lecturing on it I want them to be burned by it before they can name it. The vehicle should be a live, in-class replica of the Luring Lottery he ran through his Scientific American column in 1983, where readers could submit as many contest entries as they wished on a single postcard, the prize was a fixed pool divided by the total number of entries received, and one entry was drawn uniformly at random to take the prize — so every additional entry anywhere in the pool raised its submitter's chance of winning while shrinking the prize for everyone, submitter included. Here is the classroom mechanics I have in mind; please refine it if you see a flaw, but keep the core incentive structure intact: each student privately writes a nonnegative integer on a slip (their entry count), I pool the slips, draw one entry uniformly at random, and the winner receives 1,000 lottery dollars divided by N, the total number of entries submitted, convertible to extra credit at a rate I announce in advance. If everyone submits exactly 1, each person's expected payout is 1,000/324 lottery dollars; if everyone submits 100, expected payouts collapse by two orders of magnitude; and here is the trap I want them to fall into and then dig themselves out of — a student who believes the other seventeen will together submit M entries maximizes her own expected payout by submitting exactly M entries, a best-response rule that, applied by everyone simultaneously, has no rest point and drives the pool toward worthlessness.

I'd like you to design this as two rounds with an interlude. Round one runs cold, with the rules explained but no discussion of strategy permitted. In the interlude, students should receive a short anonymous "open letter to all entrants" — I'd like you to draft this letter, about 150 words, in the voice of a reader trying to talk the whole pool into submitting exactly 1 entry, using only arguments the students themselves could reconstruct, and without ever using the word "superrationality" or gesturing at mind-control; the letter should get the logic right, since I'll ask them to attack it afterward. Before round two, students should also have the option of a brief open caucus where they may publicly pledge to submit 1 — but the actual slips must stay anonymous, so that trusting the pledge is a genuine epistemic gamble. Design the sequencing so the interesting moment arrives on its own: the reveal of N after each round, ideally with the slips counted aloud, so the class hears the pool inflate and then, maybe, contract.

Beyond the two rounds, please provide a full lesson plan: rules, materials, timing, the letter itself, and a debrief that does the expected-value algebra on the board, shows why the best-response rule produces escalation with no symmetric equilibrium, and only then names superrationality — framing it carefully as the claim that perfectly rational agents facing a symmetric problem will decide identically, so each should choose as if choosing for all, and distinguishing it from both altruism and wishful causation. The debrief should also connect to the historical outcome: Hofstadter's readers, having just been told about superrationality, nevertheless submitted entries of astronomically large size (including at least one googolplex), rendering the prize a minuscule fraction of a cent — a result worth dwelling on as a failure of the epistemic premise rather than of the logic. I'd also like an honest comparison to the prisoner's dilemma: unlike defection there, no strategy here is dominant, and students should be pushed to say whether that makes superrational cooperation more or less credible when it's actually your own payoff shrinking. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I already anticipate a joker writing a number with a hundred digits, so give me a principled rule for handling absurd entries and whatever else you foresee — and a candid note on what the exercise cannot reproduce (real money, a pool of 700,000 anonymous strangers rather than eighteen classmates who share a dorm, the impossibility of post-lesson retaliation), so I know which parts of the lesson I'll have to supply by fiat. Begin your response with the design itself — no preamble, no restating of this request — opening with a one-sentence summary of the plan, then the details.
