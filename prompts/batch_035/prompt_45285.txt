I'm on the steering committee for our city's participatory budgeting program — real money, a few million a year, and residents vote directly on which infrastructure projects get funded. Historically the vote has been open to anyone 18+ living within city limits, with pretty light verification (utility bill or lease, that kind of thing; citizenship has never been required). This cycle two things blew up at once. First, a council member wants to open the vote to 16- and 17-year-olds. Second, and way more contentiously, one of my fellow committee members proposed what she calls a "stakeholder ballot": anyone who can show they're affected by the projects — daily commuters who work downtown but live in the next county over, business owners, even the seasonal crowd at the lake — could cast a ballot weighted at half a vote, on the theory that the pot comes from sales tax revenue and these people pay into it. Someone else immediately countered that if we're really going by affectedness we should go all the way: the projects are supposed to last decades, so let's add a "future residents" ballot, maybe cast by high schoolers on behalf of the people who will live here in 2050. And a third faction mostly wants to keep residents-18+ but add incarcerated folks who list a city address as their home.

The committee agreed everyone should circulate a written opinion before next month's meeting, and I'm honestly shakey on where I land. I keep flip-flopping between "everyone subject to the decisions gets a vote," which seems right but also quietly supports the non-citizen residents we already include and maybe the 16-year-olds too, and the all-affected view, which feels fairer to the commuters but seems to expand forever — why stop at a half vote, why stop at this metro area at all. And I can't tell whether the future-residents thing is a clever reductio or a genuinely good idea.

Draft the opinion memo I'll circulate. It needs to take a concrete position on each of the four proposals (16-year-olds, the half-weight stakeholder ballot, the future-residents proxy, incarcerated voters) — not just lay out options and shrug — and it should treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly, since I have to keep working with these people afterward. Please structure it as: "Bottom line" (one short paragraph with my recommendation on each of the four), "What this vote is for" (about two paragraphs on the underlying question of who belongs in this particular demos and why), "The proposals one by one" (four short paragraphs), "The strongest objection to my view" (one paragraph, steelmanned), and "Can we actually run this" (short — practical notes, because I don't want us to endorse something the clerk's office can't administer).
