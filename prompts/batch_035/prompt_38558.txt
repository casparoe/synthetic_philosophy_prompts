Large software companies now route product decisions through continuous experimentation: features ship behind randomized A/B tests, releases are gated on pre-registered success metrics and guardrail metrics, and there is an explicit norm against overriding the numbers — no shipping because the highest-paid person's opinion says so. Treating this as a candidate case of institutionalized Deweyan experimentalism: for Dewey, inquiry is the controlled transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate one; valuation is itself a kind of experimental judgment rather than a report of antecedent preferences; and democracy is the arrangement in which those affected by the consequences of a course of action participate in settling it. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the experiment regime. Which of its features genuinely presuppose Deweyan commitments — does the guardrail-metric structure, for instance, presuppose that ends and not just means are being experimentally tested, or only a ban on unmeasured hunches — and which are underdetermined, equally at home in crude inductivism or in an ordinary Bayesian statistics department? Then locate the sharpest divergence from Dewey: is it that the success metrics are fixed antecedently, that the users being experimented on are objects of inquiry rather than participants in it, or that revision never reaches the question of what the product is for at all? Finish by saying what a genuinely Deweyan version of the practice would have to change, and whether the result would still be recognizable as an A/B-testing regime or would have become something else — public deliberation, say — wearing its instrumentation.
