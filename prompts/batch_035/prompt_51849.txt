I dropped out of school early and mostly got into philosophy by reading on my own, so bear with me. Over the last year I've settled on a view I've since learned people call dogmatism: if you genuinely know something, you're entitled to just dismiss evidence against it instead of treating it as a reason to investigate. Like, I know that touching a hot stove hurts, so if some website claimed it doesn't, I don't owe the website a fair hearing. I got here from the old Moore thing — if I know I have hands, then when someone tells me "you might be a handless brain in a vat," I can apparently just ignore that. And there's the arithmetic version too: someone insists my multiplication is off, but I've checked it twice and I know it's right, so the challenge can be waved off without doing it a third time. It always seemed to me like this has to be right, because otherwise you'd spiral — every challenge would demand a new investigation, every investigation could be challenged, and so on.

Here's the twist I've added on my own, which I havent seen anywhere: I think the permission to dismiss is much stronger when the challenge comes from testimony than when it comes from my own senses. If a guy on the street tells me the bridge ahead is out, but I can literally see the bridge standing there, I can wave him off without a second thought. But if I look ahead and the bridge seems to be gone, even though I know I crossed it an hour ago and know bridges don't just vanish — that feels different, like I'm owed at least a look before I dismiss my own eyes. If I'm right about that, dogmatism comes in degrees depending on where the challenge comes from, and the flat version (dismiss everything equally) is too strong.

So my question. What would actually count against this view — mine, or the plain one, take them seperately if it matters? Not just "it sounds arrogant." I want the concrete stuff: cases the view can't describe, weird consequences, results it collides with, places where it predicts something its rivals don't. Which of these would a committed dogmatist have to admit is a genuine problem, and which would they brush off, and is brushing it off legitimate there or just stubbornness? And the thing I keep wondering: between the "only when you know, not merely believe" clause, my source-of-challenge clause, and whatever other patches people have added — has the view been qualified so much that it can't lose anymore? If so, is that fatal for a view of this kind, or is that just how epistemology goes? If you can, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
