I am grading a set of take-home exam answers for a graduate course on rational choice and formal epistemology and want an independent assessment of one of them before I finalize marks.

The exam question was:

State van Fraassen's Reflection principle. Explain the diachronic sure-loss (Dutch book) argument for it. Then discuss what you consider the most serious objection to treating Reflection as a requirement of rationality, and what you take to be the best response available to a defender of the principle.

The student's answer reads as follows:

Reflection says that if you now know that at some later time t you will assign a proposition A credence r, you should assign A credence r now. Van Fraassen's argument for this is a diachronic Dutch book: if your current credence in A differs from the credence you are certain you will have at t, a bookie who knows both numbers can sell you a package of bets, some now and some at t, that yields her a profit in every outcome. This is exactly the same argument, with the same force, as the synchronic Dutch book argument for the probability axioms: in both cases incoherence means sure loss, and sure loss means irrationality. Indeed, Reflection is best understood as simply conditionalization applied to your own future opinions — since you are certain what you will believe later, you should update on that fact now, so anyone who accepts conditionalization is already committed to Reflection. Moreover, someone who violates Reflection is in a position to assert something inconsistent, such as that she is certain that tomorrow she will be 90% confident of A even though A is false; this Moore-style inconsistency shows that violating Reflection is not merely imprudent but formally incoherent.

The most serious objection comes from anticipated irrationality: cases in which you know in advance that your future self will be drunk, hypnotized, or the victim of information loss, so that deferring to that future self would be reckless. The right response is to restrict Reflection to future selves you currently regard as rational: the principle binds you only with respect to opinions you trust, so these counterexamples leave the core requirement intact. Still, one might worry that even the restricted principle overreaches, because the Dutch book argument was first given by de Finetti, and his result establishes only that static incoherence is exploitable; there is no comparably tight argument connecting diachronic incoherence to irrationality rather than mere imprudence.
