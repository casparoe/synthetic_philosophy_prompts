You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in social choice theory, democratic theory, and the logic of collective decision-making. I need your help drafting a formal document, so please think carefully and rigorously before you write: begin by reasoning through the situation I describe, identify the exact assumptions in play, and only then compose the memo.

I am a professor of philosophy and, this year, chair of the ad-hoc Committee on Voting Methods for our university's Faculty Senate. The Committee must recommend, in time for the April plenary, a single voting rule for electing the next Dean of Humanities. There are three serious candidates — call them A, B, and C — plus the possibility of write-ins. Our current rule is simple plurality, and the committee has just had an experience that has made everyone newly nervous about it.

At a non-binding straw poll of ninety-nine faculty last month, preferences split into three nearly equal blocs: roughly a third ranked A over B over C, roughly a third ranked B over C over A, and roughly a third ranked C over A over B. That is, of course, a textbook Condorcet cycle — each candidate beats one rival pairwise and loses to the other, so there is no Condorcet winner. Worse, when we experimented on paper with different rules applied to the same profile, we got different winners: plurality hands the election to A; Borda rewards whichever candidate has the fewest last-place rankings; sequential pairwise voting gives the chair of the agenda — currently a person, not a procedure — the effective power to decide the winner by choosing the order in which candidates are voted on; and instant-runoff makes the outcome hinge on which bloc's second preferences get counted when the last-place candidate is eliminated.

The committee has split into three factions, and the memo must engage all of them:

1. A mathematician colleague argues that Arrow's impossibility theorem proves that every ranked-ballot method with more than two alternatives is arbitrary, so we might as well keep plurality for the sake of simplicity — a position that conveniently coincides with his own agenda-setting power as Senate chair.

2. A political scientist urges us to switch to approval or score voting, on the ground that Arrow's theorem applies only to methods that aggregate ordinal rankings, so eliciting graded or cardinal information about candidates escapes its grip.

3. A philosopher colleague insists that any method violating independence of irrelevant alternatives lets a fringe candidate spoil the result, and demands a rule that at least satisfies IIA in the case of two clear front-runners — or, failing that, a principled explanation of why we should tolerate its violation.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the plenary. It should:

- Recommend one concrete, implementable rule — specify the ballot design, the counting procedure, and a tie-breaking mechanism — so that the Senate can vote on it as written.
- Engage Arrow's theorem honestly and precisely: state its actual assumptions (unrestricted domain, ordinal preference aggregation, at least three alternatives, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship), assess which of them actually bind in our situation, and reach a verdict on the mathematician's claim that the theorem condemns our choice whatever we do.
- Evaluate the political scientist's escape route carefully — including whether cardinal ballots really stand outside Arrow's framework, and whether approval voting itself can fail IIA when a non-winning candidate withdraws or is added.
- Treat the philosopher's demand for IIA with respect but without pretending it can be fully met: given our cyclic profile, show what trade-offs are forced on us and which ones the recommended rule accepts.
- Address a question the committee has been circling but not facing: whether the cycle in the straw poll reveals a defect in our voting rule or in the faculty's preferences themselves — that is, whether a voting method should be understood as discovering a pre-existing collective preference or as constructing one.
- Note, briefly, what May's theorem guarantees if the race narrows to two serious candidates, and what it cannot guarantee before that point.
- Give the strongest consideration on whichever faction's side loses a fair and serious hearing — the memo should show the losing side why they were beaten, not merely that they were outvoted.
- Optionally, comment on whether the choice of rule is itself strategically manipulable — whether a committee with a known favorite could recommend a method for reasons the memo can name.

The memo should be roughly 900 to 1,200 words, written in the formal register appropriate to a Senate document, with a clear recommendation stated up front. Assume the Senate consists of intelligent academics with no prior background in social choice theory: use the technical apparatus where it earns its keep, but explain it on first use. The document must be self-contained and actionable — no citations, no references to the literature, just the reasoning the Senate needs in order to decide.
