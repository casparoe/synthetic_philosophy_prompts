I spent thirty-two years in numerical weather prediction, the last fourteen of them in charge of forecast verification at a national meteorological service. We lived by scoring rules: the Brier score for probability forecasts, rank histograms for ensembles, reliability diagrams for calibration. A forecast system that was systematically overconfident got recalibrated or retrained, not scolded. Since retiring I have been reading epistemology in the evenings, and I was genuinely delighted to discover accuracy-first epistemology, which treats graded beliefs the way we treated forecast probabilities: as readings on an imperfect gauge, to be evaluated by a strictly proper scoring rule rather than by anything pragmatic. It read like home.

My daughter, a graduate student in philosophy, looked over an argument I put together from my reading and told me flatly that it proves too much and that I should work out where it goes wrong before I embarrass myself. I have not been able to. I set it out as carefully as I can.

(1) An agent's credences are rationally impermissible if they are accuracy-dominated: if some alternative assignment of credences would be more accurate than the agent's in every logically possible circumstance, then the agent's credences are irrational. (2) Every probabilistically incoherent credence function is dominated in this way; this is the celebrated dominance argument for probabilism, and I found it completely persuasive — it is the piece of philosophy that made me think the subject could be as exact as metrology. (3) The domination in the argument is computed over all logically possible worlds, and what is logically possible is fixed by logic itself, not by what any particular agent can recognize as possible. The space of worlds is not trimmed to fit anyone's cognitive reach; the proof of (2) does not consult the agent. (4) Now take any logical truth L, however obscure — say, a tautology whose shortest proof runs to a thousand lines, or a combinatorial identity I have no hope of checking. If my credence in L is 0.9, consider the rival assignment identical to mine except that it gives L credence 1. Since L is true in every logically possible world, that rival is at least as accurate as mine everywhere and strictly more accurate overall. So by (1) my credences are irrational. (5) No actual agent — human or machine — recognizes every logical truth, so every actual agent has credence below 1 in some logical truth or other. (6) Therefore every actual agent is irrational, full stop; rationality is an asymptote no one can occupy. (7) Worse, by running the same reasoning on the negation of a contradiction, any agent with any credence above 0 in any contradiction it fails to detect is irrational, which covers everyone. (8) I cannot accept that. The careful forecaster whose credences are coherent as far as she can possibly trace the consequences of her evidence does not seem irrational in the way the incoherent gambler does.

There is a related thing I want sorted out. When our forecast system was miscalibrated, we did not declare it irrational; we recalibrated it. It seems open to someone to say that an agent with a known blind spot about logic should be recalibrated in the same spirit — adjusted toward coherence — without the pre-adjustment state counting as irrational. But I suspect this analogy is doing something it shouldn't, since recalibration in my line of work was justified by improved future scores, and I understand accuracy-first epistemology wants its verdicts about rationality to be independent of such practical payoff. I would like to know whether the analogy survives or whether it smuggles pragmatism in.

Tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs. I will admit my preferences: I am very attached to (1) and (2) together, since rejecting (1) seems to take probabilism down with it, and rejecting (3) looks ad hoc unless someone can show me a principled restriction of the space of worlds that still saves (2) — though I notice that any such restriction makes coherence relative to what the agent can see, and I do not know whether two agents with different blind spots could then each be perfectly coherent while disagreeing with each other and with logic itself. If the honest answer is that the argument is valid and (8) should go instead, I want to hear exactly what I am paying by biting that bullet.
