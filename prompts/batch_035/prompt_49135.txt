I'm a science journalist and I've been reporting an anniversary-ish piece on the Anthropocene saga — the whole thing where the geologists' commission voted down the formal epoch proposal in early 2024, a few years after Crutzen's famous outburst at that meeting in Mexico kicked the term off. Last week I had coffee with two of my go-to sources, a Quaternary stratigrapher I'll call M and an environmental historian I'll call R, and they got into it about what should happen to the concept now. The exchange went roughly like this:

M said the vote settled it, and rightly: an epoch needs a golden spike, a single reference layer in physical rock or sediment, and the plutonium fallout layer at Crawford Lake was the only candidate with anything like a clean, worldwide signal. Dating it to agriculture or colonial-era land use means there's no layer at all, just a smear — that's poetry, not stratigraphy.

R said this is gatekeeping. Crutzen coined the term to make a point about the scale of human planetary impact, not to satisfy the International Commission on Stratigraphy's rulebook. You don't get to inherit a word other fields built, run it through your procedures, and then declare it dead when it fails them. And anyway, he said, every boundary geologists ever ratified was chosen by a committee of humans weighing competing desiderata — the dates are decisions dressed up as discoveries, so "who decides" is the actual question, and quietly handing the decision to stratigraphers was itself a political move.

M came back: if there's no agreed boundary, the word is just shorthand for "humans now dominate Earth systems" — fine as shorthand, but then the mistake was ever proposing it as an epoch. Use it, don't formalize it, and stop pretending it picks out a date.

R said that's where he thinks M's position does its real damage: a word without a date can't do any of the work people need it for. If the Anthropocene is 70 years old it's basically about industrial capitalism and the bomb; if it's 10,000 years old it's about humanity as such, sedentary life, farming. Those imply completely different stories about responsibility and blame, and geologists waving it off as "not stratigraphy" doesn't make the choice go away — it just hides who's making it.

They kind of trailed off there and asked me what I thought, and honestly I couldn't referee it. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about which point — not just who wins overall? Also tell me if there's something both of them are missing. And since I'd be putting my byline on whatever I take from this, please flag how confident you are in each verdict and single out the point you're least sure about.
