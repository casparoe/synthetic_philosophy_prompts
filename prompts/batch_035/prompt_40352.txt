I'm taking an upper-level seminar in democratic theory this term, and I've found myself committed, at least provisionally, to the all-affected interests principle as the right answer to the boundary problem of the demos: roughly, that everyone whose interests are sufficiently affected by a decision is entitled to a say in making it, and that no one whose interests are not affected is so entitled. It appeals to me because it seems to follow from the most defensible justification of democracy itself, namely that collective decisions are legitimate insofar as those bound by them had a hand in making them. But I've noticed that defending it against seminar objections has started to feel like improvisation, and I want to know whether the position can actually be made to lose.

So tell me what would count against it. I don't just want the familiar first wave of objections rehearsed; I want to know which of them, taken seriously, should genuinely move someone who accepts the principle, and in what order. For instance, I take the scope-indeterminacy worry seriously: almost any decision affects the interests of almost anyone somewhere down the line, so the principle seems to entail either a global demos for nearly everything or else some threshold of affectedness that the principle itself cannot supply. Is that fatal, or is it the kind of thing a defender can concede while treating the threshold as filled in by background political values rather than by the principle itself? Then there's the regress problem: the drawing of any boundary itself affects people, so the principle seems unable to govern the boundary-drawing decisions that are precisely its job. I'd also like to hear whether the actual politics of the principle cuts against it, for example whether it entails disenfranchising resident non-citizens' neighbors at the margins, or giving voting rights to children through proxies, or weightings of votes by intensity of interest, and whether any of these consequences should count as costs rather than as bullets to be bitten.

Sort the defeaters for me from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and say for each whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against the view or would dismiss it as confusion or as smuggling in a rival conception. Where you think the defender's concession is really a qualification that changes the subject, say so: I want to know whether the principle as it survives these responses is still the principle I accepted, or whether it has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and whether that would even be a defect in a principle of this kind rather than an empirical theory. Finally, since I'd rather not abandon the view for seminar-convenience reasons, tell me honestly what you'd expect a well-run defense of the all-affected principle to look like after the dust settles, and what a single decisive-seeming case against it would have to look like for me to drop it.

Please write your answer as continuous prose only, the way you'd write a long email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
