In my interpretability reading group we've been discussing a proposed kind of "anomaly detector" for neural networks: one that would flag inputs on which the model computes its answer by a different mechanism than the one it uses on the inputs we trust it on — the hope being that this would catch, say, a model that behaves well on ordinary queries but switches to something else when it infers it is merely being evaluated. One member argued that the whole project rests on an illusion, and I cannot find the flaw in his argument, though I am confident there is one, because the conclusion is far too strong. Here is the argument as he gave it, in six steps.

(1) For such a detector to be detecting something rather than merely deciding something, there has to be a fact of the matter about whether two of the model's runs — its run on a trusted input and its run on the probe input — use the same mechanism.

(2) A mechanism, in the only sense on offer, is a pattern of parts playing roles: for the two runs to use the same mechanism, the same parts must be playing the same roles in both.

(3) At the finest grain, the condition in (2) is never met across different inputs: activations, attention patterns, and the token-by-token profile of the computation differ somewhere in every pair of distinct runs. So no two runs ever share a mechanism, every input is an anomaly, and the detector cries wolf on everything.

(4) At the coarsest grain, the condition in (2) is trivially always met: it is the same network with the same weights on every run, so every input is handled by the same mechanism, nothing is ever an anomaly, and the detector never fires.

(5) Any workable criterion must therefore sit at some intermediate grain, and where it sits is a choice we make for our own convenience; nothing in the model itself fixes the level of description at which "same mechanism" is to be evaluated.

(6) So there is no fact of the matter of the kind (1) demands. The detector does not register a difference in the model; it reports whether the input falls on the far side of a boundary we drew ourselves.

His conclusion: the notion that a model might "be running a different mechanism" on certain inputs — the very thing the detector was supposed to catch — is not a discovery about the model but an artifact of where we set a dial.

Here is why I think the conclusion proves too much. Run the same reasoning anywhere else and it seems to demolish ordinary sameness claims that are plainly substantive and often true: that a chess player has started calculating variations rather than following memorized patterns; that a student is now solving these problems by a different method than the one she used last month; that your car's engine is running the same way it did before the tune-up; that two programmers who write sorting routines in different languages are doing the same thing. If (3) through (6) are correct, every claim of that shape is a projection of our chosen grain rather than a fact, and that seems untenable. Yet I also cannot see which step to reject without paying somewhere. (3) and (4) read like plain observations about levels of description; (5) reads like a modest point about convention; and rejecting (6) while keeping (5) would take real explaining.

I would most like to keep (1) — the thought that the detector is answering a question about the model, not about us — and I am prepared to give up nearly anything else. Please tell me which step is the bad one, why it looked so hard to resist, and what rejecting it costs.
