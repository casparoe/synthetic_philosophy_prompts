I hold the etiological (selected-effects) account of biological function: a trait's function is whatever it was selected for doing, so the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping is what hearts were selected for, whereas the heart's incidental production of a chest-sound is not a function at all. I'd like a serious map of what would count against this view. In particular, which of the following standard trouble cases should the view's own defenders concede as counting against it, and which should they dismiss: exaptation, where a trait's current use (feathers for flight) differs from the effect it was originally selected for (thermal insulation); traits fixed in small populations by drift rather than selection; the very first instances of a trait in the earliest organisms, where there is no selection history to appeal to; and proteins engineered into synthetic organisms that perform their intended role flawlessly despite having no evolutionary past at all? For each case, I want to know whether the etiological theorist can absorb it with a principled refinement — for example, appealing to recent rather than original selection history, or to functions conferred by human design — or whether such appeals amount to insulating the view against every conceivable counterexample. And separately: does the etiological account make any predictions or deliver any distinctions that the causal-role account cannot, such that it risks something substantive rather than merely restating how biologists happen to talk?
