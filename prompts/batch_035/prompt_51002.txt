A friend and I hosted a dinner on Saturday that turned into a five-hour argument between two of our guests, and I have been unable to decide who got the better of it. The subject was suffering-focused ethics: whether moral weight of suffering differs in kind, and not just degree, from the moral weight of happiness. I will recount the exchange as faithfully as I can, in eight moves, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

The two guests were Dara, who takes the suffering-focused side, and Marcus, who defends the symmetric, classical-utilitarian picture.

1. Dara opened with an asymmetry. Imagine a world containing only perfectly contented people, and now imagine adding one more contented person to it; the world does not seem better for it. But add to the original world one additional person whose life is wretched, and the world is clearly worse. From this she concluded that adding happiness is at best an optional improvement while adding or allowing suffering is a genuine moral failing, and that the two are not on a par.

2. Marcus replied that her principle makes suffering lexically dominant: a world with a billion lives of deep fulfillment and a single pinprick would be worse than a world with ten mildly pleasant lives and no pinprick, since any amount of suffering, however small, outweighs any amount of happiness, however vast. He took that to be a reductio.

3. Dara denied ever claiming lexical dominance. Her view, she said, is only that suffering counts for more than happiness — perhaps much more — when the two are weighed against each other.

4. Marcus said this leaves her with a dilemma. Either the extra weight is finite, in which case some quantity of happiness does outweigh the pinprick, and she is a classical utilitarian with a skewed exchange rate, so the rest is haggling over a constant; or no quantity of happiness outweighs any quantity of suffering, in which case she is stuck with the conclusion from his reductio. There is no stable middle position.

5. Dara rejected the assumption, buried in both horns, that everything gets weighed on a single scale. Her actual view, she said, involves two separate tallies: one for total suffering and one for total happiness. Increasing the second is optional and welcome; increasing the first is forbidden, no matter how large the gain to the second. Nothing is "outweighed" because nothing is on the same scale to be outweighed.

6. Marcus probed the two-tally rule with prudential cases. On her rule, it is apparently wrong to permit someone to endure a painful surgery that buys decades of good life afterward, or to take bitter medicine in exchange for recovering one's health. Dara answered that these are chosen: when the person who suffers is the person who benefits, and they consent, the prohibition does not apply.

7. Marcus said that if consent is what does the work, then the prohibition she described in (5) was never about the structure of value at all — it was a rule against paternalism, trading one person's suffering for another person's happiness, and her asymmetry from (1) has quietly dropped out of the argument. To test it, he offered a case with no possible consent: a toddler given a vaccination that stings for a minute and prevents years of illness. Forbidden or not? Dara, after a pause, said it is fine because the sting is trivial. Marcus pressed: trivial relative to what? The only way to measure how trivial a suffering is, is to compare it with the happiness it buys — which reintroduces the very single scale she rejected in (5).

8. Dara's last stand was to relocate the whole view. The two tallies, she said, were always meant to operate within a life, not across lives; consent is merely a rough proxy for the within-a-life case. What she actually holds is that no one's happiness may be purchased with someone else's suffering, and that this says nothing about whether, within a single life or a single mind, suffering outweighs happiness. Marcus replied that this is a genuinely different and more interesting view than the one she started with, and that if she had opened with it he would have had far less to say; but he noted it is no longer a suffering-focused view at all, since it is silent on the relative weights of suffering and happiness and concerns only the distribution of sacrifices. Neither of them would concede, and we ran out of wine.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the eight moves, say whether it is sound, what it gets right, and where it goes wrong, if it does. Then say who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall, and identify what both of them missed. As you go, please flag how confident you are in each of your rulings, and single out the one point in your adjudication that you are least sure of and explain why.
