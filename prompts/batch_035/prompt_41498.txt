Ok so context: I'm a political theory PhD student, currently in a seminar on democratic innovations, and I've noticed that every single sortition paper I read eventually falls back on the jury analogy. The move goes: we already trust randomly selected amateurs with no accountability -- jurors can't be voted out, don't face reappointment, aren't experts, and we don't think that's a crisis, in fact we think jury trials are legitimacy gold. So a legislature chosen by lot, the argument goes, is just scaling up something we already accept; anyone bothered by unaccountable randomly selected legislators should be bothered by juries too, and they're not, so the objection is fake. My sortition-friendly friend pushes this hard, especially against my other objection about career politicians -- he says the jury shows you don't need electoral accountability OR expertise, just a well-designed process and ordinary judgment.

I want to actually audit the analogy instead of vibing about it. Can you give me the correspondence table -- what's supposed to play the role of the judge, the trial, the verdict, the lawyers, voir dire, sequestration, the appeals court -- and then tell me which disanalogies actually bite for the legislative case versus which ones are just noise. Some things I keep circling: jurors answer a question somebody else poses (the question isn't itself up for them to decide), they apply a law they didn't write, service is episodic rather than ongoing, and there's a whole professional apparatus around them doing agenda-setting and error-correction. Does the jury analogy end up quietly showing that sortition is only legitimate when it's embedded in a structure run by... a permanent political class of some kind? Which would be a hell of a concession for a movement that's also big on term limits and hates career politicians. And does anything like the appeals mechanism have a workable legislative analog, or is that just where the analogy breaks for good? Basically I want to know whether the "we already accept juries" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was doing the load-bearing work the whole time. If it helps, frame part of it around the accountability objection specifically, since that's what the analogy is usually deployed to answer in the seminar.
