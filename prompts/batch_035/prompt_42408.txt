I chair the ethics review panel at a software company of about 800 people. Once or twice a month we get asked to bless or veto policies before they ship — data practices, hiring rules, marketing claims, referral bonuses, that kind of thing — and our current "framework" is basically vibes plus whoever argues loudest. I've been reading up on Kant's universalizability test (the idea being that an act is wrong if the policy behind it couldn't hold as a law for everyone) and I want to try making it into an actual procedure my panel can run.

Complications I already know about from reading around: apparently the Kant people distinguish two different things you look for when you universalize a maxim — a contradiction in even conceiving the universalized world, versus being able to picture the world fine but no rational agent being able to will it into existence. Also there's the problem that how you describe the maxim changes the verdict: "I'll lie when it benefits me" fails differently than "I'll lie this Tuesday to close the Acme deal," so people can rig the test by over-specifying. I have no idea how much of this is supposed to survive into a usable procedure versus being philosophy-office furniture, so you decide what makes the cut.

Here's what I want:

1. **The procedure** (~700 words) — numbered steps, the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and a step for pinning down the maxim's description so nobody can game it by describing their proposal too narrowly or too vaguely. Say where the procedure terminates in a "no" versus where it punts to a human because the question is a values call that universalizability was never meant to answer (e.g. the maxim is just trivially universalizable, like everyone prefering coffee to tea, and the real issue lives somewhere else). It has to be runnable by five smart non-philosophers in a 90-minute meeting, and the verdict has to be explainable to leadership in a paragraph they'll actually read.

2. **Where judgment lives** (~150 words) — an honest list of which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, so we know when we're fooling ourselves.

3. **Three test cases** (~400 words) — run it on: (a) letting recruiters shade the truth about promotion timelines so candidates don't walk, (b) collecting behavioral analytics on users and monetizing them, disclosed only in a 40-page terms of service that everyone knows nobody reads, and (c) giving job candidates falsely warm rejection feedback so we don't burn bridges. I fully expect to dislike how at least one of these comes out — that's part of why I'm asking — but tell me plainly which ones the test was built for and which ones it just doesn't reach.

4. **What the procedure can't do** (~150 words) — where universalizability is silent or looks like it gives the wrong answer entirely, and what I should stop pretending it can handle.

Keep the Kant jargon out of the procedure itself, but you can use it in sections 2 and 4 so I can follow up on my own. No reading lists or name-dropping of papers — just the procedure and the commentary.
