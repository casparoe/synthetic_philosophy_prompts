Analyze the Pullman Strike of 1894 through Marx's concepts of exploitation and alienation, and then tell me where the theory breaks. The salient facts: after the Panic of 1893, George Pullman cut wages by twenty to thirty percent while holding rents and company-store prices unchanged in the model town he owned outright, so that the wages he paid largely came back to the company as rent and purchases; the strike that followed became a national boycott of Pullman cars under Debs's American Railway Union, was met with a federal injunction and federal troops, and was crushed. Do the mapping carefully rather than repeating the standard company-town narrative. On exploitation: what exactly is the surplus here, given that the deduction is a visible transfer back to the employer rather than extraction hidden inside the production process — does that literalness confirm Marx's account of exploitation as something obscured, or does it show that exploitation can be run through housing and consumption in a way a production-centric theory only reaches by extension? On alienation: the town was built as an answer to the labor problem, complete with behavioral standards and paternalistic control over workers' whole lives — one laborer said workers were "born in a Pullman house, fed from the Pullman shops, taught in the Pullman school, catechized in the Pullman Church, and when we die we shall go to the Pullman Hell" — and its housing was comfortable by the standards of the day. Does a designed totality of that kind count as the perfection of alienation, or as evidence that Marx's category misdescribes a place where the worker's environment was, in a sense, the employer's problem? Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: what evidence about the strike — workers' actual motives, or how rents compared with housing costs in Chicago — would push this toward a grievance story needing no Marxian categories at all, and identify the point in your own analysis where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
