I'd like a short story that dramatizes Nozick's entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain argument, but without the story ever mentioning Nozick, basketball specifically, or any philosophy by name — the ideas should be doing all the work under the surface.

Here's the setup I have in mind: a small island commune of maybe thirty adults that, after some hard early years, has finally achieved a distribution everyone endorses. Not just tolerates — endorses. Every person's holdings are exactly equal, and the islanders genuinely believe the equality itself is what makes their arrangement just (so it's a patterned principle, though the story shouldn't use that word either). One summer a famous aging athlete — let's say a retired sprinter, to keep it from being too on the nose — takes up residence. She's charismatic, and people start handing over a carved shell or a portion of their fish whenever she races the local kids down the beach or gives lessons, entirely because they want to. A year later she holds twenty-five times what anyone else does. The conflict arrives when a delegate from the mainland federation visits, declares the new distribution unjust, and proposes to level things back out. The sprinter, and a good chunk of the islanders, push back: every single transfer was voluntary, so if the starting point was just and each step was just, how can the endpoint be unjust — unless the federation is willing to coercively interfere with free people's choices to preserve its precious pattern.

I'd like the story to track that logic carefully (initial distribution, voluntary transfers, resulting distribution) but also to complicate it somewhere, because I don't want a Nozick-shaped commercial. Maybe one islander paid her shells mostly out of loneliness, and quietly regrets it. Maybe someone notices that the children born after the sprinter arrived will inherit none of the original equality, and asks whether the "just starting point" is something you only get to invoke once. Whatever you do, don't resolve it with a speech — no character gets to win the argument, and the philosophy should live in dialogue and in what the narrator happens to notice, not in exposition. I'm prepping a discussion section for an intro political philosophy class and I figured a story would land better than another lecture, so aim for something I could actually hand to students and ask "whose side is the story on, and is that the right side?" Ambiguity on that question is a feature, not a bug. Somewhere between 1000 and 1800 words feels right but use your judgement.
