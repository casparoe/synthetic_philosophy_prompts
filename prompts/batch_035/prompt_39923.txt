Take on the role of a professional formal epistemologist who's spent a career on epistemic logic, and who's just been roped into drafting the wrap-up document for an adversarial collaboration. Don't rush into it — do a bit of brainstorming first about where each camp would actually be willing to budge, and only then write the thing.

The fight is logical omniscience. On one side, the idealisers: they think an agent who knows all the logical consequences of what it knows is like a frictionless plane in physics — a harmless idealisation, fine for the models we build in epistemic logic and Bayesian epistemology, and that treating it as a scandal confuses the logic of knowledge with psychology. On the other side, the bounded-rationality people: they think that if your framework entails that anyone who knows P and knows that P implies Q must thereby know Q, you've stopped describing anything that could be an agent at all — and worse, you've smuggled in a norm no finite agent could ever meet, so the framework's advice is broken before it starts.

These two have been arguing past each other for a year and have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration. I jsut need the joint statement they could both put their names to, and I need you to get the drafting right — that's the hard part here.

Format it as exactly five numbered sections, no introduction, no conclusion, and keep each section tight (a couple of sentences, or a compact table where that's genuinely clearer):

1. The claims both camps can sign off on — about the formalism itself, about what necessitation and the distribution axiom actually do to the model, and about the difference between a modelling choice and a normative claim.
2. Where they genuinely part ways, with each disagreement phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own position. So nothing like the idealisers calling the other side confused about what a model is, and nothing like the bounded side saying the idealisers simply ignore agents — neutral wording throughout, or it's not a document both would sign.
3. Which parts of the dispute are merely verbal. I suspect a fair chunk is, e.g. over whether the knowledge operator in the logic is even supposed to track anything like ordinary knowledge, but flag it as a suspicion rather than settling it for them.
4. The cruxes, with each side's prediction written down in advance. At least one crux should be the line-drawing problem: everyone concedes that memory limits matter (nobody says an agent who forgets is irrational thereby), so the bounded side wants to know why computational limits get idealised away when memory limits don't — and the idealisers need to either find a principled line or bite the bullet. At least one other crux should be whether a logically non-omniscient agent can be Dutch-booked by a cleverer agent who does see the consequence; both camps accept that case bears on the crux, so record what each side actually predicts about it before anyone runs the case.
5. Whatever both agree would count as real progress on the debate, plus one thing each side concedes it has been getting wrong this whole year.

Two warnings. Don't slant the wording toward either camp — the temptation will be strong, since one camp's whole position is that the other's complaint misses the point. And don't paper over the disagreement with 'it depends' hedging; if the crux predictions in section 4 come out identical, you've fudged the draft and should fix it. If you're unsure which side a claim really belongs to, it's fine to say so briefly, but don't manufacture agreement that isn't there.
