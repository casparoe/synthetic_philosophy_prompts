I'm preparing a prospectus for my dissertation committee (about two pages) and want help turning a half-formed idea into a proper proposal.

The idea concerns luck egalitarianism. The standard distinction between brute luck and option luck classifies a risk as option luck when the agent deliberately took it on. But the largest distributive risks in a life are iterated rather than one-shot — career and location choices, repeated saving and health behavior. In a long sequence of chosen, fully informed, even mathematically fair gambles with compounding stakes, final positions come to be dominated by how the early draws happened to fall: an early loss shrinks the stake in every subsequent round, so two people making identical choices can end up in radically different circumstances. My hunch is that option luck decays into brute luck over time; that the decay rate, not the choice/no-choice line, is the real content of the brute/option distinction; and that familiar institutions can be read as devices for setting that rate — bankruptcy discharge, limited liability, and other fresh-start provisions as scheduled resets that return accumulated option luck to something like a new starting position. If that is right, the familiar charge that luck egalitarianism is too harsh to serve as a theory of justice is partly misframed: the theory's harshness is not fixed but is a policy parameter, and the real question is the timescale of responsibility, not whether to hold people responsible at all.

Please turn this into the prospectus with: a precise statement of the question, determinate enough to have an answer (something like: under what conditions, if any, should the luck egalitarian verdict about a person's holdings at a time depend on how many earlier rounds of choice that person has been through); a fair statement of why the existing positions leave it open — I take it the whole-lives view and the time-slices view each yield principled but conflicting answers, and I want that presented honestly rather than as a straw man; the method, concretely — what I would actually do over two terms, including the stylized model of iterated gambles I would build, the candidate criteria for the brute/option boundary I would test against it, and how I would check the results against actual legal institutions rather than merely illustrate the theory with them; what a result would look like, including the negative result (no non-arbitrary timescale is derivable from the theory's own commitments) and why that would still count as a contribution; and honest conditions under which the project would fail.

Two further requests. First, do not include a bibliography or tie the argument to particular authors — I will insert references myself; describe the positions and arguments in plain terms. Second, I want the proposal to be candid about its own fragility: include a short section on what would change your mind — which consideration, if it succeeded, would lead you to abandon the decay-rate framing outright rather than patch it — and mark, within the plan, which parts you are confident in and which you are genuinely unsure about.
