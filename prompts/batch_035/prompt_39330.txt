I'm in a philosophy of science reading group, and we've just worked through Worrall's 1989 paper on structural realism — the one built on the Fresnel-to-Maxwell story, where the equations governing light's diffraction and polarization apparently survived the collapse of the luminiferous ether that was supposed to carry the waves. The moral one member drew was that theory change is like the history of cartography: Ptolemy's tables, Mercator's projection, modern satellite mapping — successive maps of one and the same coastline, drawn with different projections and different conventions, some of them involving wildly wrong views of what the earth is like, yet all of them getting the relative distances between harbors more or less right. What accumulates across scientific revolutions, on this picture, is structure: the network of relations and the numbers that constrain the phenomena. From this he concluded that we should be realists about structure and stay quiet about intrinsic natures — believe the web, not the things the web is woven around.

The skeptic in our group thought the cartography analogy was doing all the work and attacked it from several directions at once. For one, rival projections don't really disagree about anything: nobody using Mercator thinks Greenland is actually that size, and switching projections is a matter of convenience rather than belief revision — whereas the ether theorist and Maxwell presumably disagreed about what existed, not about how best to represent something neither doubted. For another, a coastline is something you can walk along and check against the map, so the analogy quietly assumes exactly the kind of access to the "structure of reality" that the structural realist is supposed to be arguing for, not presupposing. And he raised something called Newman's objection: that if all you ever know is structure and never the intrinsic character of the things related, the claim collapses into something close to trivia about how many things there are — a map that says only "there are this many points, related somehow." The skeptic's alternative picture was that theory change is less like changing projections on a fixed coastline and more like redrawing what counts as land and sea.

Can you audit the analogy for us? Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the coastline, the projection, the cartographer's conventions, the scale, the purposes of navigation — then lay out where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually damage the structural realist's conclusion and the ones that don't. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: is there a version of "we know the structure, not the natures" that the cartography picture can genuinely support, or does the analogy only look convincing because it borrows exactly what's in dispute? If there's a better analogy for what's meant to be retained across theory change, I'd like to hear it too.
