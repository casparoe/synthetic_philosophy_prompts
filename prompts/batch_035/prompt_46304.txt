I'm a software engineer with no philosophical training, so please forgive any clumsiness in how I put this. A colleague on a part-time philosophy course walked me through an argument about whom to rescue, and it seems to prove far too much. I've checked each step individually and they all look plausible to me, but the conclusion is one I can't accept, and I'd like a proper diagnosis rather than an instinctive recoil.

The case: I arrive first at the scene of an accident. One casualty, David, lies injured on one side of the wreckage; five others lie injured on the other side. I have the time and the strength to reach only one side before it's too late for the people on the other. My colleague says her argument tracks a paper by someone called Taurek, published, she thinks, in 1977, whose title asks whether the numbers should count, and that its author proposed flipping a coin in exactly this sort of situation. Here is the argument as she gave it to me:

(1) Each of the six has an equally strong claim to my aid: each stands to lose the very same thing, his or her life, and nobody's life counts for more than anybody else's.

(2) The strength of a person's claim is fixed by that person's stake, and is neither increased nor diluted by how many other people happen to have the same stake. Five equal claims do not add up to a claim five times as strong, because a claim is a claim owed to a person, and there is no further person to whom some larger total is owed.

(3) When claims are equally strong and not all of them can be met, fairness requires giving each claimant the same prospect of being saved.

(4) A single lottery over the six, with one ticket each, satisfies (3): each person is saved with probability one-sixth.

(5) A coin flip between 'save the one' and 'save the five' also satisfies (3): the one is saved with probability one-half, and each of the five is saved with probability one-half as well, since saving that side saves all five of them.

(6) Both procedures are therefore fair; and once every fair procedure gives each person the same prospect, nothing further may be put in the balance — in particular, the number of lives at stake on either side may not tip the choice. So I may, and perhaps must, flip the coin; saving the five outright, on the ground that they are five, would be unfair to the one.

Here is why the argument alarms me. Run it at scale. If (6) is right, then in a variant where a thousand people lie on one side and one person on the other, fairness gives me the same permission, or the same obligation, to flip: the one gets a fifty per cent prospect, each of the thousand also gets fifty per cent, and the thousand may not be saved outright merely because they are a thousand. Worse still, a bystander who simply reaches the thousand, without any coin at all, would be acting no better — arguably worse — than one who flips; and as I add people to the many side, the expected number of deaths climbs from two and a half to five hundred and beyond without anything I'm permitted to do changing at any point. My colleague accepts all of this rather gallantly, which is exactly what makes me suspect a slipped premise somewhere.

I have one observation that I can't do anything with, so I'll offer it in case it helps. Steps (4) and (5) both give each person 'the same prospect', but the two equalities don't feel like the same kind of equality: in (4) each person has the same chance of being the one selected, whereas in (5) David is selected with probability one-half and each stranger is selected only as part of a bloc. I can't turn that unease into an objection, and I may be wrong that it points anywhere.

For what it's worth, my own preferences: I'd like to keep (1) and (2). The thought that claims don't add up across people feels right to me, and I'd be sorry to learn that holding onto it forces me to endorse the coin flip. I'm prepared to give up (3), or the move from (5) to (6), but I'd want to know what I then owe the five, or David, in the case where I do simply save one side outright. So, please: tell me which step or inference to reject, explain why that step looked innocent to someone like me, and set out what each available rejection costs — including, if the honest answer is that the argument is valid and something in the conclusion has to be swallowed, which swallow is cheapest and why.
