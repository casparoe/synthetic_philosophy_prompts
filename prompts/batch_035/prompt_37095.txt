My great-aunt died last month at eighty-six. She was a retired geodesist who translated physics monographs into Portuguese well into her eighties, and in her sixties she had found Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness and taken it up as an operating manual: she believed happiness depends on passions that make you forget yourself, that disciplined work is the most reliable of those passions, that one's happiness must never be placed in the keeping of other people, and that illusions are not defects to be corrected but supports of happiness, to be cultivated in oneself and left undisturbed in others. Please write her eulogy. The audience is split: half is family, including some who found her philosophy cold — she said at her own sister's funeral that she refused to pay grief's tax on the living, and they will be listening for whether the eulogy finally corrects her on this; the other half is her reading group, who will catch every du Châtelet allusion and will notice if I get the views wrong. The eulogy must comfort both halves of the room without saying anything she would have considered false: in particular, it must not claim that her happiness was really her family all along, must not suggest she is at peace somewhere watching us, and must not expose or dismantle any of the illusions she deliberately kept — while somehow still being honest about a death that has left real holes in real people's weeks.
