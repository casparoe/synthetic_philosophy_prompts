I'm in a little book club that reads pop science stuff, mostly evolutionary biology and psychology, and I keep bumping into the same frustrating move and I don't know if it has a proper name. Someone proposes that some trait exists because it was useful back on the savanna, or because it helped our ancestors survive -- the standard story about why we find certain faces attractive, or why we get disgusted by certain things, whatever. Then a critic says, no, that story is just made up after the fact, you'd have told a story just as happily if the trait had come out the other way, it explains everything and therefore nothing. Fine, good point, I'm on board. But then the critic offers their own account of the trait -- it's a side effect of something else, an accident of how some other system got built -- and, here's the thing, *that* story is also totally unconstrained. Nobody checks it either. If the trait had come out the other way, they'd have a byproduct story for that too.

So the phenomenon I'm asking about isn't just "people invent just-so stories" -- I've seen people use that phrase, it's cheap -- it's that the criticism gets inherited by the critic's alternative without anyone noticing. The anti-story is treated as intellectually superior just because it's deflationary, not because it's better supported. It's like debunking gets you a free pass. Is there an established concept for this specific thing, where the objection to a mode of explanation applies equally to the objection's own favorite explanation? I assume someone in the philosophy of biology literature has had this thought, since the whole fight about "is this trait an adaptation or an accident" seems to live and die on it, but I don't know the field well enough to find it.

For what it's worth I've started noticing it outside biology too, which makes me think the carving might be broader: in economics, someone proposes a functional explanation for why some market feature exists (it's there because it's efficient) and a critic counters with a historical one (it's a leftover from some accident of regulation), and again both stories can absorb any evidence you throw at them. When I try to describe the general shape I end up saying something like "the explanation space is too roomy" but that's not a concept, that's a shrug. I'd rather not get just a name back -- if there's a real literature on this I want to know how the people in it define the problem and what, if anything, they think distinguishes a legitimately supported story from a merely available one. And if there is no established concept, then honestly I'd take a coined term with a careful definition and a few edge cases over a shrug.
