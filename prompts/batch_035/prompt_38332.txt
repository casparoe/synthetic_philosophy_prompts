My two friends, Rob and Sam, had a long argument at dinner last week, and I've been turning it over ever since without being able to settle it. I have no background in philosophy — I watch physics documentaries and think about them afterwards, which is the extent of my training — so I'd be grateful for a careful verdict rather than a gentle one.

It started when Rob, who works as an engineer, said something he had read somewhere: that the laws of nature don't push anything around. Gravity doesn't make the apple fall; the apple just falls, everywhere, always, and the law is only our name for that pattern. Sam, who studied physics at university, thought this was backwards. For her the law is what makes the apple fall: without something enforcing regularity, there would be no reason for the universe to keep its habits, and the fact that it does keep them is the whole mystery.

Rob answered that the enforcement picture merely renames the mystery. Saying things fall because a law makes them is like saying opium puts you to sleep because it has a sleep-producing quality — you have added a word, not an explanation. Sam replied that Rob's picture cannot handle sentences like "if I let go of this glass, it would fall": nothing that merely summarises what happened tells you what would have happened, whereas a governing law does, and everyone, engineers included, relies on such sentences constantly.

Rob said a summary could do that job, because a summary that fitted everything that happened but gave absurd answers about nearby situations would be a poor summary — the best description of the pattern is the one that also gets the would-haves right. Sam said that was smuggling: the pattern by itself doesn't say which situations count as nearby, so something undeclared is doing the work of picking them out.

Rob then tried a different tack: if the universe's entire history had gone differently, the best summary of it would have been a different set of laws — so what the laws are depends on the total arrangement of facts, and whatever depends on the facts cannot be what controls them. Sam said the dependence runs the other way: every instant of that arrangement conforms to the laws, so it is the arrangement that inherits its order from the laws, not the laws from the arrangement. There they stopped, and both looked at me.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — saying who is right at each step and why, not merely who wins overall — and then give your verdict on who comes out more right, together with anything you think both of them missed? Please answer in British English.
