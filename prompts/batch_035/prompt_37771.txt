I teach ancient philosophy, so mid- to late twentieth-century ethics is adjacent territory for me rather than home turf, and I want to be careful not to mishandle a famous line. At a recent colloquium on friendship, a speaker invoked Williams's "one thought too many" — from the essay "Persons, Character, and Morality," where a man who can save only one of two drowning people saves his wife, and Williams suggests that if the man justifies his choice by appeal to an impartial principle permitting him to rescue his spouse, the justification has one thought too many — and then treated that line as settling not just the wife case but the whole integrity objection to impartial moral theory: consequentialism, Kantianism, contractualism, all of it alienating agents from their projects and relationships. I suspect the slogan, as used there, was packing several distinct claims together.

You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in ethical theory and in the Williams literature. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming the candidate readings, then lay them out as a menu I can bring to the next colloquium session. For each distinct precise thesis the one-liner could be expressing — for instance, readings about which thoughts would be motivationally or psychologically corrupting if actually present, about which thoughts the agent would be criticisable for acting from, about which thoughts would disqualify the act as one of loyalty or love, about the limits of what moral theory can demand or supply — say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which of them, if any, would actually support the sweeping dismissal of impartial theory the speaker extracted from it. Distinguish carefully between claims about what the rescuer should think, what he should say, and what may be true though unthought; and tell me whether any defensible reading gets the conclusion that impartial moral theory itself is undermined, or only the far weaker conclusion that such theory misdescribes good people.
