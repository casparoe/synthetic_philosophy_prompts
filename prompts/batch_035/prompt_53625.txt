An analogy I keep meeting in the philosophy of conditionals runs entirely on a betting picture: asserting an indicative conditional is like accepting a conditional bet -- a bet that pays out if the consequent is true and is called off, stake returned, if the antecedent is false. Since the fair odds on such a bet are fixed by your conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, and since our confidence in indicative conditionals seems to track exactly those conditional probabilities (Adams' thesis), the conclusion is drawn that indicative conditionals do not express propositions with truth conditions at all -- they are more like bets, devices for expressing conditional commitment rather than for stating how the world is. Lewis's triviality results are then waved at as confirmation: if the conditional did express a proposition whose probability equalled the corresponding conditional probability in every distribution, probabilities of conjunctions would collapse in plainly impossible ways, so the proposition had better not exist. Audit this analogy. First the mapping: what in the betting picture corresponds to the truth value of the conditional, to embedding one conditional inside another, to asserting a conditional in conversation, and to disagreement between two speakers over the same conditional. Then the breakdowns: a bet can be called off, but a conditional sitting unasserted inside a disjunction or under a negation cannot be; a called-off bet has no winner, but an indicative with a false antecedent still seems to be about something; fair odds can be driven by factors that would not touch a truth value. Say which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which do not, and whether the no-truth-conditions conclusion survives once they are on the table -- or whether the analogy only carries it for simple, unembedded, assertoric conditionals, leaving the embedded cases as an open debt. Also locate precisely where the triviality results attach: to the betting analogy itself, to Adams' thesis as a claim about assertion, or to the stronger assumption that the probability of the conditional equals the conditional probability even after conditionalization. Finally, if there is a better analogy for indicative conditionals than the conditional bet, say what it is and what work it would do better.
