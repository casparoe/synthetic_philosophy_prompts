I am a professor of philosophy, and I need a referee for a dispute I could not settle myself. Last term our program handled a misconduct case, and the argument it produced in the hallway afterward has stayed with me. I want you to adjudicate it move by move, and I should say up front that I have no settled view of my own: both colleagues are people whose judgment I respect.

The case. Twenty-two years ago, early in his career, our colleague Marcus published an article that incorporated, without attribution, long passages from an untranslated foreign-language monograph. He was found out, publicly confessed, and accepted the sanctions. In the decades since, he has run our research-integrity seminar and has been, by all accounts, scrupulous. Last month a doctoral student, Lena, was found to have lifted several paragraphs from an unpublished departmental manuscript into her thesis. Marcus sat on the panel that reviewed it, and beyond reporting the finding he told her, in front of the others, that he found what she had done shameful and that it disgusted him — personal blame, not merely enforcement. Whether he was entitled to that register is what my colleagues fought about.

The exchange, as I heard it:

(1) Deb opened: Marcus's blame is worthless. A plagiarist blaming a plagiarist is hypocrisy, and hypocritical blame is void; Lena is entitled to dismiss it outright.

(2) Sam answered that this was too fast. The hypocrisy that disqualifies blame is doing the very thing while blaming it — the colleague who chastises others for checking their phones in seminars while doing it himself under the table. Marcus is not plagiarizing anyone now. If anything, his blame is evidence of reformation, and reformed wrongdoers are often the best blamers: ex-smokers do not hesitate to warn the young, and we want them to.

(3) Deb replied that his past does not evaporate upon reformation — and that she was not talking about his current conduct but his current register. Shameful, she said, spoken in front of the panel: that is not protest, it is a performance of distance from his own past self. He is asserting a commitment to integrity that his own record violates. That is hypocrisy of the committed kind, and it defeats standing just as surely, even if he never copies another word.

(4) Sam: a commitment his record violates in the past tense. On Deb's principle, anyone who has ever fallen short of a value is forever barred from blaming its violation — only the blameless could ever blame. That is not our practice and should not be: we take the chronically impatient person's blame of lateness seriously, and rightly so.

(5) Deb: we take it, but we discount it. Look who's talking is not a fallacy in these exchanges; it is a challenge to the speaker's right to the currency the exchange runs on. Blame is an address between equals: it claims the addressee owes an answer. As a panelist, Marcus has every right to the finding and the sanction — call that role-standing. But the personal register of his remarks trades on a further standing he does not have; the moment Lena says look who's talking, the exchange simply ends, and it ends in his favor only if we forget his record.

(6) Sam concluded that, on Deb's telling, the problem is the register and not the history. Had Marcus said only that the conduct violates the code and carries consequences, Deb would have no complaint. So standing reduces to voice hygiene: blame in your administrative voice and you may blame; slip into the personal voice and you may not. That, he said, cannot be the deep story about hypocrisy and standing.

Neither would budge, and — to my irritation — I could not referee it, because each move seemed to me to contain something right. I would like the following from you.

First, before committing to any verdicts, brainstorm briefly in a short paragraph: what general principle is each of them implicitly relying on, and at which step exactly do those principles come into conflict?

Second, adjudicate the six moves in the order given. For each, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and justify the rating — do not simply announce a winner.

Third, tell me what both of them are missing. I suspect something is missing, since neither ever asked whether standing, once lost, can be regained by amends, and neither considered what Marcus's very public self-blame twenty-two years ago does to the analysis — but do not let my suspicions steer you; they are hunches, not findings.

Fourth, close with an overall verdict: who is more right, Deb or Sam, and what the correct resolution of the case is. Confine yourself to the substance of the considerations; no literature surveys and no name-dropping. I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.

A note on your role: you are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of blame, standing, and responsibility, and you are being asked to referee, not to mediate — so take the numbered moves exactly as made, do not attribute to either party arguments they did not give, and do not soften the verdicts. If a move is bad, say so plainly. Think carefully before you write; the value of your answer will lie in the precision of the distinctions, not in the confidence of the tone.
