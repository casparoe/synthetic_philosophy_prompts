I teach a semi-intro philosophy of language course, and I'm building a unit on conditionals. The Oswald/Kennedy example has been done to death — my students have literally seen it in three seperate textbooks by now. Here's what I want instead: a fresh, one-paragraph scenario, set in ordinary life (workplace, family stuff, a trip, whatever — no trolleys, no sci-fi), where (a) the indicative conditional "If A, B" comes out clearly true/assertible, (b) the matching counterfactual "If A had happened, B would have" comes out clearly false, and crucially (c) the divergence must NOT lean on the antecedent being known-false like in the Kennedy case. I want an open antecedent — nobody in the scenario knows whether A — so the trick can't just be "we know not-A, so the material conditional is vacuously fine." The indicative's truth should come from epistemic reasoning that fails to track what would actually happen if A. No omniscient narrators, and no stipulating the verdicts: the case has to generate them from its own details.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough. Next, give the counterattack: how would someone who thinks indicatives are just material conditionals with Gricean or Jackson-style pragmatics layered on top explain the same data with a single semantics instead of two? And don't waffle on the ending: given your own case, which treatment do you actually think wins? Take a side and defend it, even if it costs you something elsewhere.
