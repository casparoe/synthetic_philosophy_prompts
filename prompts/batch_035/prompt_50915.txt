I am building teaching materials for a seminar on the ethics of influence, and I want a purpose-built pair of cases to stress-test the leading theories of what distinguishes manipulation from legitimate persuasion. The accounts I have in mind are (1) the reason-bypassing account, in the tradition of Joseph Raz, on which manipulation influences the will without engaging the target's rational capacities; (2) covert-influence accounts, of the kind Cass Sunstein and Matthew Noggle have defended, on which what makes influence manipulative is that it operates in the dark — the target would not, or would be upset to, learn of it; (3) Gorik's pressure account, on which manipulation motivates by imposing non-rational costs and incentives rather than by giving reasons; and (4) exploitation accounts, on which manipulation works by preying on defects or vulnerabilities in the target's psychology.

Here is what I need constructed: a matched pair of one-paragraph hospital scenarios. In the first, an oncologist steering a patient toward a treatment option must do so in a way that is fully transparent, involves no threats or incentives, and engages the patient's own reasoning — the influence should run entirely through a value the patient avows, such as her self-image as a fighter — and yet the episode should still be intuitively manipulative, in a way that survives the test of the patient learning of the tactic later and still feeling used. In the second, a physiotherapist must influence a patient covertly, through an environmental cue that never touches the patient's rational deliberation at all, and yet intuitively not manipulatively — the patient, told of it later, should not mind. Constraints on the pair: a realistic hospital setting; no deception, no threats, no withholding of information, no framing tricks of the 90%-versus-10% variety; and the two cases must be symmetric in stakes and in the agent's benevolence, so that the only differing variable is the psychological route of influence.

Please organize your response under the following headings, in this order and roughly these lengths:

**The pair** (about 180 words) — the two scenarios as specified.

**Verdicts by account** (about 300 words) — for each of the four accounts, say what it classifies each case as and why, so that it is visible where the theories cross rather than merely disagree.

**The discriminating variable** (about 100 words) — name the single psychological feature the pair turns on, and why it separates the accounts in the way shown above.

**The strongest reply** (about 150 words) — take whichever account gets the pair most badly wrong and construct its best defense, rather than its weakest.

**Build audit** (about 120 words) — an honest check on whether the cases generate their verdicts from the theories rather than stipulating them, and what happens to the pair if the discriminating variable is removed from either scenario.

Please note that a case that only *seems* manipulative because the agent has bad aims does not count; the oncologist and the physiotherapist should each act in what they sincerely take to be the patient's interest. The manipulation, where present, must be in the method, not the motive.
