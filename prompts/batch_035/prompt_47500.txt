I'm a student in an upper-year seminar on collective decision making and social choice, and for my final project presentation I want to hand out a one-page decision tree that sorts people into a position on the discursive dilemma — the case where a committee of three votes on premises A, B, and the conclusion C which follows logically from A and B, each member is individually consistent, a majority accepts A, a majority accepts B, and yet a majority rejects C. The tree's job is to take someone from the judgment that this pattern is genuinely problematic to a named view about how a group's collective judgments should be formed.

Please build the tree so that every internal node is a question someone can answer before knowing which position it leads to — things like "must the group's collective judgments be logically consistent whenever all members' judgments are?", "is it acceptable for a collective to endorse a conclusion no individual member endorses?", "should responsiveness to members override consistency when the two conflict?", and "does the right answer depend on whether the group is meant to track truth together or to fairly compromise between interests?". The leaves should be the standard aggregation positions: majoritarian responsiveness, premise-based aggregation, conclusion-based aggregation, some form of restricted or supermajoritarian rule, and the outright skeptical option that there is no fact of the matter about what the group judged. If you think an oligarchic or dictatorial leaf belongs in the tree, include it, but only via a node that someone could reach honestly rather than by caricature.

Two specific requirements. First, I want the tree built from the logical space rather than from the roster of famous names: derive the nodes from the independent commitments (consistency preservation, responsiveness to majorities, universality across agendas, whether collective judgments must be grounded in member judgments at all), then locate the actual positions as combinations of answers, and explicitly note any combination the tree leaves unoccupied — and say whether, in your view, nobody holds it because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or just because no one has bothered. Second, I want the tree to be tested against two concrete cases beyond the standard one: a hiring committee that must issue a single ranked shortlist when individual members' pairwise majority preferences cycle, and a safety review board that votes on "the design meets standard X", "the design meets standard Y", and "approve the design", where approval logically requires both standards. If premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation give the same verdict on one of these cases, flag that the tree cannot actually separate them there, rather than pretending the cases do the work.

Keep it formal enough for a seminar handout but plain enough that a classmate with no background in the formal literature could use it in five minutes before the next meeting. You may name individual thinkers where the position is strongly associated with them, but the tree must not depend on knowing anyone's name to be used correctly. At the end, add two or three sentences telling me which node you think is doing the most hidden work — the question a user could easily answer one way while intending the other.
