My friend and I got into a two-hour argument at the bar last night about whether you can explain anything with "culture." It started because I brought up Weber's Protestant ethic thing — the claim that Calvinist anxiety about predestination helped produce a certain ascetic, this-worldly work discipline that fit early capitalism like a glove — and he said that's not an explanation at all, it's just redescribing what you want to explain, and that if capitalism had shown up in Catholic Florence first we'd all be reading a book about Catholicism and the spirit of capitalism instead. It got heated. Somewhere in there he accused me of "explaining with values," which I think he half-remembers from somewhere.

Anyway, karma: I somehow volunteered to run a session on exactly this for our little discussion group. They're smart but mostly non-specialists — engineers, a nurse, one guy who read a lot of pop econ books. One hour, whiteboard, no assigned homework. I don't want a lecture on Weber; I want a plan for how to walk them through the thesis as a *causal* claim, so they can see both why it's tempting and exactly where the causal story gets slippery. Specifically:

- What has to come first in the sequence? My instinct is the predestination psychology has to be on the table before "the spirit of capitalism" means anything to them, but maybe I have it backwards — tell me if the order should go the other way and why.
- What's the one carrying example? I was thinking Benjamin Franklin's time-is-money advice, since Weber leans on Franklin himself, but tell me whether that's the right anchor or whether it smuggles in too much, and what the example needs to show vs. what it must NOT be allowed to prove.
- The misconceptions: I'm betting at least one person will hear "Protestants invented capitalism," someone else will hear "religion causes prosperity, full stop," and one guy will absolutely bring up that Italy had banks and long-distance trade centuries before Geneva got strict. Where in the sequence do I head each of these off, and which one is actually dangerous rather than just wrong?
- The big one: my friend's objection — that culture-as-cause is unfalsifiable storytelling, where any outcome can be read back into the values. Where does that fit in the hour, and is there a serious version of it the group should sit with versus a cheap version I can just dispatch?

Also a check for understanding I can do at the end — some question that would tell me they grasp the causal-claim problem itself, not just the historical details.

One more thing, and this matters to me after the arguement: please keep straight what's actually settled versus what's still genuinely fought over among people who know this literature. I don't want to present some particular critic's demolition of Weber as if it's the scholarly consensus, and I also don't want to hand the group a tidy "Weber was right" verdict if the specialists never really settled it. If part of the plan depends on a contested reading — of what Weber even meant, or of the counter-evidence — flag that as contested rather than baking it in quietly.
