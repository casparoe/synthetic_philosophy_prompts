Next term I'm teaching a second-year course on epistemology in non-Western traditions, and the Jainism unit lands on anekāntavāda — roughly, the doctrine that reality is many-sided, so no single flat judgement can capture it, and every categorical claim is true only from a standpoint, in a respect, for a time. The Jains pair this with a sevenfold scheme (the saptabhaṅgī): of any object you may say, in some sense, it is; in some sense, it is not; in some sense, it is and is not; in some sense, it is inexpressible; and then three more that stack the earlier ones with the inexpressible — it is and is inexpressible; it is not and is inexpressible; it is, is not, and is inexpressible. On paper my students read all this as either obvious ("well, duh, context matters") or as mushy relativism ("so every view is equally right"), and neither is the target. I want a 90-minute activity for about 16 students where they run into the need for the seven forms before anyone names them.

The shape I'm imagining: small groups each get restricted and different access to one object or system — I'm open to what exactly, could be a physical object they can only probe through a single channel each (one group weighs it, one taps it and listens, one sees only its shadow, one reads a spec card with half the lines missing), or it could be partial data sheets about some ambiguous event. Each group then has to commit, publicly and in writing, to a set of categorical claims. Then a plenary where the claims collide and the whole room has to draft a single joint statement that does justice to all the evidence at once. And here's the bit I want help engineering: I want to constrain their drafting language so the moves they naturally reach for turn out to be the sevenfold predication in disguise. Something like giving them only approved sentence templates — "the object is...", "the object is not...", "the object is and is not...", and a fourth that lets them gesture at "in some sense it can't be said" — so they discover that the weird "inexpressible" slot is actually load-bearing: it's the point where two partial truths about the same thing refuse to fuse into one flat sentence without losing something.

Then I need the debrief: mapping which moments in the game correspond to which of the seven forms, and crucially separating anekāntavāda from both fallibilism ("we'd get it right with more data") and relativism ("each group's truth is as good as any other's") — the Jain claim is stronger than either, since each standpoint captures something real, which is why the object itself, not just our reports of it, is many-sided. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I assume one group will try to game its probing channel, and someone will smugly announce that "this is just the blind men and the elephant" as if that settles it — how do I answer that, given the parable is genuinely in the tradition and doesnt settle anything? And be honest about which part of the doctrine no classroom exercise can reproduce. My guess is the metaphysical half — the bit about the object rather than our access to it — but confirm or correct me, and tell me how the facilitator should supply that bit by hand in the debrief without just lecturing.
