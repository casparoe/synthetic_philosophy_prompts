So in my sociology of religion seminar we're on Weber and the Protestant ethic stuff, and this quote keeps coming up (it's from his "Social Psychology of the World Religions" essay I think, somewhere around p. 280 in the Gerth and Mills collection): "very frequently the 'world images' that have been created by 'ideas' have, like switchmen, determined the tracks along which action has been pushed by the dynamic of interest."

The thing is, the professor and one other student are using it in completely different ways and neither seems to notice. The professor uses it to say Weber thought culture/ideas genuinely cause things -- like, the Calvinist idea of predestination actually set the tracks for capitalism, so you can't reduce everything to material conditions (his push against the crude Marxists in the room). But the other student pointed out that the sentence right before that one, which she dug up, is "Not ideas, but material and ideal interests, directly govern men's conduct" -- which sounds like it's saying ideas DON'T directly cause anything, they just steer pre-existing interest-driven behavior. Which would make Weber almost the opposite of an idealist.

I got assigned to write up "what the switchman line actually claims" for next week and honestly I can see at least four different things people could mean by it and I don't trust myself to sort them out:

1. a totally boring reading where it just says "ideas matter too sometimes" -- a platitude no one would dispute
2. some kind of selection/filtering idea where the engine is always interests (material or "ideal") and ideas only pick WHICH of several interest-compatible outcomes happens, like picking between multiple equilibria, so ideas matter but only at historical junctures where interests underdetermine the outcome
3. something stronger where ideas are independent causal forces that can move people with no interests pushing from behind at all
4. and then maybe a reading where "ideal interests" already smuggles values INTO the interests, so the dichotomy between the professor's version and the other student's version collapses because "interests" was never purely material in the first place

Can you lay these out properly? For each reading I want: what exactly it claims, whether the first sentence ("Not ideas, but material and ideal interests...") is consistent with it, and whether it's actually defensible or just something people say. And then tell me which reading the professor probably needs for the Protestant ethic thesis to count as an explanation of capitalism rather than just a decoration on top of an economic explanation -- because that's the thing our seminar argument is really about. A list I can basically read out in class would be ideal, I don't want to editorialize too much since I have to be in a room with both of them for ten more weeks.
