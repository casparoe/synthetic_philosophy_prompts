Over the years I've accumulated a set of verdicts about which animals are conscious — which ones there is something it is like to be — and I'd like to know whether they add up to a principle, or whether some of them have to go. My verdicts: a mouse that withdraws from a hot surface and then guards the paw: conscious, no hesitation. An octopus introduced to a new object in its tank, which it explores, carries around, and seems to return to: conscious, and among my most confident verdicts for any non-mammal — I'd even credit it something like curiosity. A crow that caches food, notices it was watched, and returns later when unobserved to move the cache: conscious. A zebrafish: conscious, though with noticeably less confidence than the mouse. A honeybee that stops grooming and rubs at a spot after a minor injury: here I go back and forth, but I currently lean yes. A lobster dropped alive into boiling water: yes — I flinch at this one, and I say it while still unsure about the bee. A nematode worm with three hundred and two neurons and no central brain, which reliably reverses away from a hot plate: not conscious — the avoidance looks like something happening for it, but I'm fairly confident nothing is. A patient in a persistent vegetative state, with sleep-wake cycles but no sign of awareness: I lean no, but without any of the confidence I have about the worm — and this despite the patient showing far less behavior than the worm. And a robot that navigates a room, avoids obstacles, and rights itself when toppled, with behavior I stipulate to be as rich as I like: no, flatly, no matter how rich the behavior gets. My tentative principle is that behavior counts as evidence only in proportion to how centralized and integrated the creature's nervous system is — which explains the worm, the bee, and the octopus, but I can see it straining at both ends: it seems to make my lingering uncertainty about the patient a mere failure of nerve, and I can't tell whether it licenses the flat refusal I feel about the robot or simply assumes it. Please test the tentative principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and commit to a single final principle rather than offering me a menu of candidates. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts the final principle cannot accommodate, and which of those you would overturn and why. Then tell me what verdicts it delivers, and how confident I should be, in four cases I haven't mentioned: a crab, a fruit fly, a human under general anesthesia, and an artificial system of your choosing that you take to be the strongest current test case.
