I'm a retired evolutionary biologist (thirty-odd years working on behavioral ecology, mostly bird mating systems) who has gotten seriously into philsophy over the last few years, and I now run a weekly discussion group at our community center called Science and Ethics. The attendees are mostly other retirees — a former nurse, a high school chemistry teacher, an accountant — smart folks but with no philosophy background, and every few weeks somebody brings in a news story claiming scientists have explained where some moral belief comes from and declares that this proves the belief is an illusion. Last month it was a piece arguing that our condemnation of incest is just inbreeding avoidance dressed up as a rule, so the rule isn't "really about anything." Before that it was someone claiming that since evolution shaped our sense of fairness to manage cooperation in small bands, our ideas of justice are just adaptations with no claim on us. I can feel there's a real distinction here — explaining why we hold a belief versus showing the belief isn't true — and I gather philosophers like Sharon Street and Richard Joyce have pressed versions of this worry about whether evolution undermines objective morality, but our discussions always end in people talking past each other. What I'd like is a decision procedure I can actually run at the whiteboard: a short sequence of questions to ask about any "science explains your moral belief, therefore debunked" claim, with a rule of thumb for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, so the group can work through a claim together in fifteen minutes or so. Somewhere in there the procedure needs to say explicitly when the question stops being a scientific one and becomes a values question that no amount of evolutionary data can settle, and also where the procedure can misfire — I don't want to hand the group a false sense that any of this is mechanical.

Once you've laid out the procedure, please run it on three claims of the sort that actually show up in our group. First, the incest one: the taboo is traced to inbreeding avoidance, so the moral condemnation is supposedly just an adaptation we should see through. Second, a finding that people's purity judgments track pathogen-avoidance instincts — the way people moralize things just because they find them gross, food taboos being the classic example. Third, the one that I expect will cause trouble: someone tries the same debunking move against the belief that factory farming is wrong, on the grounds that we only care about animal suffering because empathy was useful for raising offspring, so we should drop the moralizing. I suspect the procedure should give an answer some of my members won't like whichever way it goes on that last one, and I'd like to see that happen rather than have the procedure dodge it. The hardest case my procedure absolutely has to handle: one member keeps objecting that by the same logic, evolution debunks our belief that evolution is true, or our perceptual beliefs generally, and yet we still trust perception, so why does morality come out worse — the procedure should either answer that or flag honestly where it can't.

A few constraints on the writeup. No jargon, or at least any jargon immediately translated into plain words — nobody in that room knows what a non-naturalist is, and frankly neither do I yet. It has to survive being run by me rather than a professional philosopher, in a ninety minute session where we do two claims at most. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text or tables, the way you'd write a long letter to a colleague, in American English. Take whatever space you need.
