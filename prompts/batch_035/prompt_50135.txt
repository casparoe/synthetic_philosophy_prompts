I run the philosophy society at my sixth form, and for our end-of-term session I want to build one of those online personality quizzes, except the personality it sorts you into is a decision theory. The theme is choices where some predictor or adversary seems to already know what you will do — the family of puzzles that begins with Newcomb's two boxes and includes the one where you meet Death in Damascus and reason that, since he seemed to have been expecting you tomorrow in Aleppo, you had better not go to Aleppo after all — until it dawns on you that wherever you decide to go, Death will have anticipated it. My classmates mostly don't know any of the jargon, and I want the quiz to sort them by what they would actually do in concrete cases rather than by which label they find flattering, so I'd like you to write it to the following specification.

Write a quiz of eight to ten questions. Every question is a concrete case with two or three answer options, and no question may use the name of any theory or theorist — only situations and choices. Build the quiz from the logical space first, then locate the positions in it: think about which combinations of answers are possible, choose the questions so that different positions give different combinations, and only afterwards attach names. Please make sure the following kinds of case appear, each described from scratch in plain language: the two-boxes case with a predictor who has already filled one box based on a forecast of your choice; a version where the contents of the boxes are visible to you before you choose; a case where an action is correlated with a bad outcome only through some background factor you cannot change by choosing it (say, a gene that both inclines you to smoke and causes illness, while the smoking itself is harmless); a case where the coin that decides whether you gain or lose a large sum was flipped before you were born, and you are only offered the choice in one branch; a case where your payoff depends on what a simulation of you was predicted to decide; and a case like Death in Damascus itself, where a pure strategy seems self-undermining and flipping a coin looks tempting. Include also one twist on the Damascus setup of your own devising — for instance, a version in which Death announces where he will be tomorrow, so that staying put rather than randomising is what saves you — so that the quiz can tell apart people who like randomising for its own sake from people who liked it only when unpredictability was doing the work.

The scoring key at the end should map answer patterns onto four leaves: the position that recommends whatever causally brings about the best outcome, treating any predictor's prior forecast as a fixed fact about the world; the position that recommends whichever option is best news, in the sense of conditional expected value; the family of views on which you should choose as the output of your decision procedure that you would most want your procedure to have output, given that the same procedure determined the forecast (these views are known under several names, and if you cannot separate them with my questions, present them as one leaf and say exactly which questions failed to do it); and the view, sometimes called ratificationism, on which you should pick a strategy that is stable once you have settled on it, which may be a genuine mixed strategy — flipping a coin — when every pure option undermines itself. Before presenting the key, list the possible answer combinations, note which combinations no real position occupies, and diagnose each empty region in one or two sentences: is it empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or just because nobody happens to have written it down? Also flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot genuinely separate — for example, readers who take the best-news option simply because its expected payoff is larger — rather than pretending the key discriminates more finely than the questions allow. Finally, tell me what I should say to a classmate whose answers land them in two different regions, since I expect several of those.

Two formal requests: please write everything in British English, and please have the response begin with the first quiz question itself — no preface, no restating of my request — with the scoring key and commentary following afterwards.
