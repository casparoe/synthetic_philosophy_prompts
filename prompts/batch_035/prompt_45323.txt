I teach an upper-level seminar in decision theory and formal epistemology, and I am drafting the final exam. Write me one essay question on Good's theorem on the value of information, designed so that it cannot be answered by reciting the theorem and its usual gloss. The question should be answerable in roughly ninety minutes and should have three parts.

Part (a) should ask the student to state the theorem precisely and prove it for the case of two available acts and finitely many states, requiring them to be explicit about the premises the proof relies on: a fixed utility function defined over act–state pairs, updating by conditioning on the actually observed signal, evaluation of the informed strategy by ex ante expectation, and a single agent facing a non-strategic environment.

Part (b) should present three putative counterexamples, each written up as a concrete scenario within the question text itself, so that every student works from the same cases. The three I want are: (i) a patient deciding whether to order a diagnostic test whose result will be painful to know, such that the knowledge itself lowers the utility of every subsequent act by a fixed amount, whatever the patient goes on to do; (ii) a firm that can commission a perfectly informative market study, but whose rival will see the findings too, so that learning the state of demand destroys profitable opportunities the two firms had while both were ignorant; (iii) an agent whose credences are given by a set of priors rather than a single prior, who chooses by a worst-case rule over that set, and who would do strictly better by committing to an act now than by observing a signal and then choosing by the same worst-case rule applied to the updated set. For each, students must identify which premise of the proof it targets, say whether the case dissolves once we insist on careful re-modeling or instead marks a genuine limitation on extending the theorem, and state what the correct formulation becomes once the case is handled properly.

Part (c) should ask for a short concluding discussion of where the popular slogan that a Bayesian should always accept free information overshoots the theorem, including the distinction between the ex ante claim the theorem actually licenses and any ex post claim about how the agent fares under each possible realization of the signal.

Alongside the question, supply a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme out of 20 marks built on five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically lose them. The marking scheme should make the difference between an A and a B answer concrete: an A answer must correctly diagnose which counterexamples dissolve under re-modeling (for instance, by folding the signal into the description of the outcome) and which instead break a substantive premise (for instance, by moving to a strategic or non-standard-probabilistic setting), whereas a B answer merely restates the theorem and gestures at the cases. In the model answer, keep clearly separated what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature: the theorem itself, within the Bayesian expected-utility setting it is stated in, should be presented as settled mathematics, while the right way to define the value of information for agents with sets of priors or non-expected-utility preferences, and the seriousness of the limitation in strategic settings, should be flagged as open or actively debated — do not present any particular resolution of those issues as if it were the consensus view.
