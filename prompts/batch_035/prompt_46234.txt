I am drafting an essay for a graduate seminar on scientific method and would like comments on the draft below. Please lead with your overall verdict in your first sentence — whether the essay's central claim survives your objections or should be abandoned — and then give your reasoning; do not restate the essay back to me or open with general remarks about thought experiments.

The working title is "Equivocation in the Tower: What Galileo's Falling-Stones Argument Actually Proves." My central claim is that the famous argument in which Salviati, in Galileo's Two New Sciences, refutes the Aristotelian view that heavier bodies fall faster — the one where a heavy stone and a light stone are tied together, so that the composite must supposedly fall both faster than the heavy stone alone (being heavier) and slower (being dragged by the light one) — is not an empirical discovery smuggled into armchair form, as the standard philosophical debate would have it, but something more mundane and, I want to argue, more interesting: an unmasking of an equivocation. Aristotle's "heaviness," I claim, is ambiguous between a per-unit dispositional property (how fast a given bit of matter tends downward) and an extensive quantity (total downward tendency summed over parts). The contradiction arises only because the Aristotelian is never told which of the two his theory commits him to. If heaviness is per-unit, the composite falls at the heavy stone's speed; if it is extensive, the light stone tied on should slow the heavy one down; either answer is internally coherent, and the thought experiment has not ruled out either world — it has only revealed that "heavier" was doing double duty. The knowledge it yields is semantic, not physical.

In the second half of the essay I generalize: what thought experiments yield is not simulated empirical information (the mental-simulation camp) nor merely disguised arguments (Norton's position), but the forced, simultaneous activation of two tacit background commitments that a theory's defenders had never been made to hold at once. On my picture the thought experiment is a stress test on a concept rather than a simulation of a world. To support the generalization I discuss Einstein's elevator (the equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass read as forcing the concept "mass" to disambiguate) and Schrödinger's cat (read as forcing a commitment on whether "superposition" applies to composite, macroscopic systems).

My questions. First: is the Galileo reading accurate — in particular, is my per-unit/extensive distinction a fair reconstruction of the Aristotelian position, or am I strawmanning it? Second: does the generalization to Einstein and Schrödinger help me or drown me? My worry is that the cat doesn't fit — it seems to generate a genuinely new interpretive problem rather than expose a pre-existing ambiguity, and if so it is a counterexample to my generalization and perhaps to the whole account. Tell me whether to keep it, rework it, or cut it. Third: is the central claim about equivocation genuinely different from Norton's view that thought experiments are just disguised arguments, or is my "stress test on a concept" merely his reductio with different packaging? If it is different, I need to say more sharply how; if not, the essay collapses into exposition of Norton and I would rather know that now. Finally, and less importantly: the seminar mixes philosophy and history of science, so flag anywhere the draft rides roughshod over historical detail — including whether the tied-stones argument actually appears where and how I say it does.

I would like a committed response: take a definite position on each of the four questions and tell me concretely what to change, rather than laying out options and leaving the choice to me.
