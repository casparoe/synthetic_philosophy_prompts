Leonard Savage died in '71, so he lived just long enough to see Ellsberg's 1961 paper poking holes in his sure-thing principle but not to see the aftermath -- Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility, the whole smooth ambiguity literature, behavioral econ fully embracing ambiguity aversion as a thing people have rather than an error they make. Suppose we brought him back today and showed him all that. Would he fold, or would he dig in?

Here's what I want you to actually work through: Savage's whole program was that rational degrees of belief must be probabilistic, full stop -- the representation theorem was supposed to show that if you don't obey the axioms you're being inconsistent in a way that leaves you open to sure losses. So when modern ambiguity theorists say that a preference for the known-urn bet over the unknown-urn bet is perfectly reasonable and just needs a broader theory, what's the most Savagean response available? Something like "you're still exploitable, you've just changed the bookie"? And conversely, where would he have to concede that the Ellsberg-type preferences don't actually expose you to any Dutch book, so his consistency argument doesn't touch them?

I'm also curious about the pragmatist streak in him -- he cared about small worlds and admitted the axioms were meant for idealized situations. Maybe he'd say ambiguity aversion is fine as a description of how people behave in large worlds but has no place in normative theory, basically quarantining it? Which of these reactions do you think is most likely for the actual historical Savage, based on what you know of his personality and his later defensive moves around the Allais paradox (where he reportedly revised his own choices on the spot)?
