For over a decade I have been teaching the dispute between higher-order and first-order theories of consciousness, and every year the pattern is the same: the higher-order theorist insists that a mental state's being conscious consists in its being taken as an object by a suitable higher-order representation, the first-order theorist counters that phenomenally conscious experience carries its entire character at the first level with no monitoring required, and the familiar objections -- the possibility of misrepresentation, the lack of any plausible inner sense or monitoring mechanism, and the question of whether the various retreats to dispositionalist or same-order versions preserve anything distinctive of the original view -- are traded back and forth without either side losing ground. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not disagreeing about a single property but are using the word 'conscious' to pick out two different explananda: a functional posit of cognitive science on the one hand, and a phenomenon fixed by how things seem from the subject's point of view on the other. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive question -- perhaps an empirical one about which states of the brain are accompanied by reports, perhaps a formal one about representation -- of which the visible dispute is merely a proxy? Assess the structure of the disagreement by reference to what each side actually claims, and tell me candidly what, if anything, would count as progress: a neuroscientific result, a sharper conceptual distinction, a change in the question, or nothing at all.
