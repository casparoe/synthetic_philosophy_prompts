keep running into the line "democracies don't have famines" — most recently in a podcast where the host used it to argue that humanitarian food aid is basically a waste of money, because what actually prevents famines is elections and a free press, so the real fix is political, not logistical. Pretty sure this traces back to Amartya Sen and his work on the Bengal famine, where his point was that famines happen with food available, because people lose the ability to command food — landlords, wages, whatever. But the slogan itself, as people repeat it, could be doing a lot of different jobs and I want to sort them out.

Like, which of these is it actually claiming?

1. A bare empirical regularity — no famine has ever occured in a functioning democracy. Checkable in principle, but checkable against what counts as a "famine" and what counts as a "functioning democracy"?
2. A causal mechanism — competitive elections plus a free press give rulers an incentive to intervene before mass starvation, so famine is an accountability failure, not a food-supply failure.
3. Something almost definitional — if a country let a famine happen we'd say, in hindsight, it wasn't really a functioning democracy, which makes the claim unfalsifiable and kind of trivial.
4. A weaker statistical claim — democracies just have smaller, rarer famines, not zero.

And separately, which reading did the podcast guy need for his "aid is useless" conclusion? Because that conclusion seems to require the mechanism reading to be true AND to be the only thing that matters, which feels like it's smuggling in way more than the slogan gives you.

Also, two test cases I keep going back and forth on. The Great Leap Forward: China wasn't democratic, so it supports the slogan, fine. But the Irish famine is where I get confused — Britain had elections and a press and all that, yet a million people died on its doorstep. My friend says Ireland doesn't count as part of the democracy (Catholics couldn't vote or sit in parliament, so the people starving had no electoral pull), but that seems to just push the problem around: does the slogan then need a condition like "the affected population must be enfranchised" to survive, and if so, is that a real finding about democracy or just a carve-out to save the thesis? And do democratic famines like that (or the Bengal one, under wartime British rule with an elected government at home) show the slogan needs to be stated much more carefully than people state it?

Lay the distinct readings out as a numbered list with a verdict for each — trivial, substantive-but-plausible, or false — then tell me which reading the aid-is-useless argument actually needs and whether it gets it. Don't end with mush, I want your actual assessment of whether the slogan, on its best reading, is true.
