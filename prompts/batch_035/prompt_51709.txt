I'm on the standards-and-practices side of a mid-sized streaming service — we're the people who handle viewer advisories, content categories, that kind of thing. Last year we greenlit a six-part docuseries built around a charismatic author pushing a Fomenko-style "new chronology": the idea that ancient history as taught is a late construction, that big chunks of it are duplicated copies of medieval events, and that you can prove this with statistical parallels between royal dynasties plus supposedly ancient astronomical records that don't match the sky. The show is gorgeous, it rates well, and it's framed like an investigation: the author tours sites, and every episode ends with a mainstream historian getting maybe forty seconds to say we have mountains of evidence, followed by the author shrugging that absense of evidence isn't evidence of absence.

A couple of archaeologists' associations are now publicly annoyed at us, and internally we're split four ways. One faction wants a short card at the start saying the series presents one perspective on historical method. A second faction says that wording is worse than nothing, because it puts the chronology consensus and the new chronology on the same shelf as two equal perspectives — which is exactly the framing the show is engineered to produce. A third faction wants a card that actually says the series contradicts established methods of historical dating, and the business side says any card at all reads as a lecture, audiences will rally to the underdog, and we should just quietly recategorize the thing as entertainment in the app and leave it be.

We meet Friday and I'm supposed to circulate a recommendation before hand. Can you draft the memo? It needs a concrete bottom line — which option, and roughly what the card should say if there is one — but also the reasoning, because what I keep snagging on is what each option implicitly claims about how historical knowledge works. The "one perspective" card seems to presuppose that settled historical conclusions are just interpretations among others; the "contradicts established methods" card presupposes there's a demarcation between scholarship and speculation that we can actually state in two lines on a screen; and doing nothing presupposes the show's format is neutral, when maybe the whole "investigation" framing, with the fringe guy as the detective and the experts as the obstacle, is doing the persuasive work before anyone says anything factual. I want the memo to take the no-card faction's best argument seriously too, and not just the ratings version of it — the epistemic version, that sticking a "fringe" label on a view can make it more credible, and that we're a streaming service, not a ministry of education. There's also a consistency problem worth addressing: we already run true-crime series where guilt is heavily implied under a "some details have been dramatized" card, and nobody internally agrees on what our cards are even for — a warning, a disclaimer, or a genre label. Keep it short enough that people will actually read it before the meeting.
