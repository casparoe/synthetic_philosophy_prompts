So I'm drafting a paper on advance directives and I want a second pair of eyes before I send it anywhere. The core idea: most defenses of advance directives lean either on the autonomy of the earlier self (the "then-me" gets to rule because it's still me) or on precedent autonomy (the earlier self's choices bind in something like the way a promise binds). I think both are wrong, or at least incomplete, and I want to argue for what I'm calling the "epistemic trustee" view. When I sign a directive while competent, I'm the only party with reasonably full access to my own values — the later, demented self is in some sense a stranger to them. So the directive works less like a command from a sovereign and more like instructions a trustee leaves for a caretaker: authoritative, yes, but authoritative because of who has the relevant knowledge, not because of who owns the future.

The notes I have so far, roughly:

1. The identity route fails for the radical dementia case. If the later self genuinely doesn't remember or endorse the earlier values, "it's the same person choosing" does less work than people assume. Worse, identity cuts both ways: the later self is "me" too, so identity alone can't explain why we honor the directive over the protests of the confused patient sitting in front of us.

2. Precedent autonomy handles that better, but it over-generates. It would keep the directive binding even when genuinely new information arrives that would have changed the earlier self's mind — say a new treatment makes the anticipated condition far less bad than the earlier self feared when signing. Insisting the directive binds regardless makes the practice look irrational.

3. On the trustee view, the directive is defeasible guidance, not a command. Its weight is proportional to how well informed the earlier self's values were, and it can be overridden when the surrogate has strong evidence the earlier self would have decided differently under the new circumstances. And notice this is roughly what good clinicians already do quietly in practice, whatever the official forms say.

What I want from you: mainly, tell me whether the trustee framing actually earns its keep or whether it's just precedent autonomy with a fresh coat of paint — a colleague said it's the latter and it stung. Also please poke at point 3, since the slide from "the directive is defeasible" to "clinicians already treat it that way" feels like it smuggles in an is-ought move, and I can't quite see how to patch it. Finally, is there an obvious case or thought experiment I should be engaging with that I'm missing? Don't give me references or a bibliography — just tell me the ideas, if any.

Be honest. I'd rather hear it's bad from you now than from reviewers in six months.
