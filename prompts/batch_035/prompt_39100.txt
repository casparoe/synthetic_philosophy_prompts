Here is the proposition: I want your credence that the correct semantics for ordinary de re modal claims about persons is transworld identity — that is, that when I say I could have become a professional violinist, what would make that true (holding fixed the actual facts about me: I never learned an instrument, I have the job I have) is that there is a single person, me, existing in some other possible world, who took up the violin there. This is to be weighed against Lewisian counterpart theory, on which the claim is instead true in virtue of some world containing a violinist who is my counterpart: someone sufficiently resembling me in the respects that matter, related to me by comparative overall resemblance rather than identity. By "correct semantics" I mean the best regimentation of the truth-values and inferential behavior of ordinary modal claims — including iterated ones, like claims about what I could have been able to do — not ontological parsimony for its own sake.

Please decompose your credence. I would expect terms at least for the following, each with a probability and a one-line justification, though add others if you think they belong:

Note that if worlds are instead ersatz constructions or merely representational aids, transworld identity stops looking like a strange metaphysical commitment — an overlapping mereological or causal-historical relation between world-stages arguably comes cheap — so this term and the next interact.

(b) Whether the Humphrey-style objection — that counterpart theory makes a claim like "Humphrey could have won the election" be about someone other than Humphrey, someone who merely resembles him, when it is obviously a claim about Humphrey himself — is a genuine cost of the theory or a confusion about what the theory is doing (a paraphrase that preserves truth-values rather than a re-description of what we had in mind).

(c) Whether counterpart relations can handle the hard cases symmetrically: cases where a single world contains two equally good candidates for being my counterpart (the one who took up the violin and the one who took up the cello), and cases where what I care about is what happens to me rather than what happens to someone like me — and whether the theory's need to let the counterpart relation shift with context (resemblance in career-relevant respects, origin-relevant respects, etc.) is a feature or an instability.

(d) Whether iterated and nested de re modality — "I could have been someone who could have been a different sort of person altogether" — favors either side, since this is where the two semantics are most likely to come apart in truth-value.
