I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a module on social epistemology, and I also serve as the student facilitator for our department's weekly peer-review workshop, attached to a writing-intensive seminar with about thirty students. Each week the students exchange essay drafts and write structured feedback on one another's work before the seminar meets; my job is to read every comment sheet before it reaches the author, and decide what happens to it: send it back to the reviewer for rewriting, edit or soften it myself, pass it through unchanged, or escalate it to the tutor. The tutor has asked me to ground my triage in what our module has covered — Miranda Fricker's distinction between testimonial injustice (a speaker's word given less weight than it deserves because of who they are) and hermeneutical injustice (a gap in shared concepts leaving someone unable to make their experience intelligible to others, or to themselves). But she wants a procedure, not a theory: something I can actually run on a comment in two or three minutes, with about thirty sheets to get through on a Thursday evening before Friday's seminar.

Please write me that decision procedure. It should set out the questions to ask of a comment in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and the outcomes available at each point — back to the reviewer, edited by me, passed through, escalated to the tutor. Somewhere in it you'll need to make the theory usable by someone who hasn't read it, so please spell out which distinctions must survive the translation: for instance, the difference between a comment about whether a claim is true and a comment about whether this author can be believed to have made it; or between a gap in an essay caused by the author's inexperience and one caused by the absence of any established vocabulary for what they are trying to say. Please say which parts of Fricker's framework you deliberately dropped in making it operational, and why the drops are safe. Mark clearly the points where the procedure runs out and a human judgement about values has to take over — I don't want to pretend the checklist is doing work it can't do.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish comment sheets from last term.

First: a reviewer wrote, on a draft by the only mature student in the group, who returned to study after fifteen years in care work and writes in long, plain sentences, 'I don't buy that you've engaged with the literature — this reads like it's patched together from lecture notes.' The draft in fact quotes three sources closely and argues with one of them.

Second: a reviewer wrote, on an essay by a student describing the experience of being the only person in her family to go to university and what that did to how she could talk about ideas at home, 'This is just anecdote. Cut it and make a proper argument.' The passage in question is arguably doing conceptual work that no ready-made vocabulary in the course covers.

Third — and here's the case where I expect the procedure to give me an answer I won't like — our workshop has a rule that every comment sheet must open with something positive. Last term a reviewer opened with 'This is really impressive work for someone at your stage,' followed by two pages of substantive and, frankly, correct criticism of another student's argument structure, from a reviewer widely believed in the group to look down on the author. I'd want to sanction that reviewer and save the author from the criticism. If the procedure instead says the substantive criticism goes through and the opening compliment is the part I should act on, so be it — but show me how the procedure separates the two, because on my gut reading the compliment is the kind thing in the sheet and on the procedure's reading it may be the injury.

Finally, please be honest about what the procedure cannot do: what it will miss, when it will misfire, and which of its failures I should expect rather than try to patch. And please write your answer in British English.
