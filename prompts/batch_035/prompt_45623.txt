Six months ago I was made an associate editor at a mid-tier interdisciplinary journal, and because my cover letter mentioned that I had once taught a seminar on the adaptationism debate, I have become the default handler for the steady stream of evolutionary psychology submissions we receive — four to six a month, mostly on mate preferences and coalitional reasoning, occasionally on disgust or the evolutionary origins of religious belief. My own training is in social psychology, so I can evaluate the statistics and the design, but I have no framework for the prior question: is a given paper doing science, or is it constructing a just-so story — a narrative about the Pleistocene that could comfortably accommodate any result the data happened to deliver? My reviewers split predictably: the evolutionarily trained ones wave nearly everything through, the others dismiss nearly everything wholesale, and neither camp gives me anything I can actually put in a decision letter.

Please turn what the philosophy of biology actually knows about this — the adaptationist debate, the difference between a hypothesis that generated a prediction in advance and one that retrofits an explanation onto a known finding, the alternatives to adaptation that must be ruled out (byproduct, exaptation, genetic drift, culturally transmitted learning), and the evidential weight of comparative, cross-cultural, and developmental data — into a decision procedure I can realistically run: about twenty minutes for triage, up to an hour for a full report. I need the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure outputs reject, request major revision, or send out for review; and where it must hand the decision back to me because the question is one of judgment rather than checkable fact. Since I write the decision letters myself and they go to the authors, each stop on the checklist should be expressible as a sentence to an author rather than a verdict in the margin.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions. First, a study finding that women's stated mate preferences shift across the menstrual cycle in a way the authors say fits an ancestral mating logic. Second, a cross-species comparative paper using phylogenetic methods to argue that a cognitive trait appears in exactly those lineages where a particular foraging ecology would have selected for it. Third, a paper with no new data that explains a well-documented sex difference in some behavior as an adaptation, arguing that the observed pattern is precisely what the ancestral account would predict. I'd like at least one case where the procedure yields a verdict I am inclined to dislike — I came to this job sympathetic to the skeptics — and I'd like you to tell me where that sympathy is most likely to distort the application.

Two further requests. First, be plain about what the procedure cannot do: which hard cases in this literature it leaves genuinely undecided, and how a submission can pass every check and still be wrong. Second, since parts of this will be judgment dressed up as procedure, tell me what would change your verdicts — which consideration, if an author or reviewer pressed it, would move a submission from one bucket to another — and identify the step of the procedure where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write everything in American English.
