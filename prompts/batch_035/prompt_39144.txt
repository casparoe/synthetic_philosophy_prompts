I teach an intro-level environmental ethics course (about 25 students, one 75-minute session), and I want to design an in-class game about intergenerational justice and climate change. The thing I'm wrestling with: last weekend I got into a long argument with a friend of mine who teaches economics, and he kept insisting that climate policy can't really be said to *wrong* future people, because the people who would exist in a low-carbon world are literally different people from the ones who would exist if we keep burning fossil fuels — different conceptions, different timelines, different individuals. My first reaction was that this was a debating trick, but apparently it's a serious position (something like Parfit's non-identity problem), and it's made me realize my students hear "we owe it to future generations" as obvious and never confront the weirdness underneath it.

So here's what I want: a multi-generation resource game where the students in each "generation" make policy choices that both (a) deplete or preserve a shared resource for the next generation, and (b) secretly change *which* future students exist — like, the composition of the next generation gets reshuffled depending on what the current one does, so the students who benefit or suffer from your choices aren't the ones who would have been there had you chosen differently. I'd rather the identity-shuffle not be announced up front; I want them to discover it partway through and feel the ground shift. Materials should be cheap (cards, tokens, a whiteboard), no real money, and the payoff scheme should make short-term consumption genuinely tempting so it's not a strawman where everyone cooperates for the aesthetic of it.

Then I need a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the actual philosophical positions: the person-affecting view vs. impersonal ones, whether "harming" requires making someone worse off than they otherwise would have been, and whether the identity-matters move actually lets my economist friend off the hook or just relocates the problem. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume someone will figure out the shuffle early, or generations will collude across rounds and break the structure), and be upfront about which part of the problem the game just can't reproduce — I suspect it's hard to make students feel that the *other* possible generation was real in any way, since the counterfactual people are, well, nobody in the room. If you know a fix or a workaround for that, I'd love to hear it, but don't paper over it if there isn't one.
