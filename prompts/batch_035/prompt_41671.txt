The proposition I want a credence on, stated as precisely as I can: in a society with a functioning legal system, a regulatory regime that permits compensated gestational surrogacy under strong safeguards — independent counsel for the surrogate, psychological screening, escrowed compensation, a right to withdraw mid-pregnancy with the fee pro-rated, no specific performance of the contract — is ethically preferable, all things considered, to a blanket prohibition. Please decompose rather than merely assert. I want a rough probability and a line of justification for each of the following sub-questions, plus any further terms you think belong in the decomposition:

1. Commodification: is gestation the kind of service that can be permissibly contracted for, or does paid gestation differ in kind, not just degree, from other physically demanding paid work, such that money changes the meaning of the act rather than merely compensating it?
2. Consent across time: a surrogate signs before the cycle starts, but the pregnancy runs on a drastically altered hormonal regime, so the person who might want to withdraw in month seven is, in a real sense, not the person who signed.
