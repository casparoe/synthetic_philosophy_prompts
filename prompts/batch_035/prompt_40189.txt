I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on ethics and public policy, and this past week's discussion turned to whether moral progress is real and, if so, whether we should expect it to continue. The phrase everyone in the room kept reaching for was the "expanding circle" — the picture, associated with Peter Singer and popularized further by writers like Steven Pinker, that history shows humanity's circle of moral concern steadily widening: first beyond the family and tribe, then to strangers, to people of other races, then (arguably) to women, and perhaps now to animals and to people not yet born. Our seminar instructor used the image to argue that extending concern to animals and future generations is simply the next lap of a process already underway, and that we should both expect it to continue and help it along. One of my classmates pushed back that the whole framing is a metaphor doing the work of an argument, and since then I haven't been able to stop wondering whether she's right.

Could you audit the analogy in the way we've been taught to audit models in my philosophy of science course? First, I'd like the correspondence made explicit — a table is fine: what plays the role of the circle itself, the center, the expansion, the inside and the outside. Then I'd like to see where the mapping breaks. It seems to me the picture may quietly import at least three things: that there is a single center, when it's not obvious whose vantage point the circle is centered on or whether different people's circles expand at different rates; that expansion is monotonic, when the historical record may include contractions — periods in which previously included groups were re-excluded, or concern thinned out; and that the boundary is sharp, when actual moral concern looks graded — distant strangers are "inside" the circle in some sense, yet we owe them far less than we owe neighbors, which suggests something more like concentric rings of diminishing intensity than a single boundary that either includes or excludes.

Finally, please sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the conclusion my instructor drew — that further inclusion is the natural continuation of an ongoing process — and those that don't, and then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the problematic ones are on the table. If it does survive, I'd like to know which premises are carrying it once the circle is gone; if it doesn't, I'd like to know whether there is a better analogy available, or whether the case for extending moral concern has to be made without any image at all. I'm hoping to write my term paper on this, so I'd be grateful if you could flag which points are genuinely contested in the literature and which are more or less common ground.
