I've been puzzling over Bertrand's chord paradox for a while, entirely on my own, and I've reached the point where I want an actual number rather than more hand-waving. The setup, as I understand it: draw a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle, and ask for the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord is longer than the triangle's side. Depending on how you pick the chord — random endpoints on the circumference, random midpoint along a fixed radius, random midpoint in the disk interior — you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4. All three look like faithful applications of the principle of indifference: in each construction, you treat some parameter as uniform because you have no reason to prefer one value over another, and the three answers contradict each other.

So here is my question. What is your credence that the principle of indifference, as a rule for assigning probabilities from ignorance alone, can be salvaged as a coherent principle — that is, that there is some correct formulation under which "choose a chord at random, with no further specification" has a single right answer?

I want the decomposition, not a bare number. The sub-questions I've come up with myself, which you should feel free to correct, supplement, or drop: (a) whether "at random" is simply incomplete language that always smuggles in an unspecified selection procedure, so the problem is linguistic rather than mathematical; (b) whether requiring invariance under symmetry transformations picks out a privileged measure, and whether that requirement is principled or just another arbitrary-seeming constraint; (c) whether the failure is specific to continuous domains and the principle remains fine for finite cases, so that what's needed is a patch rather than a replacement; (d) whether maximum-entropy-style rules dissolve the problem or merely relocate it. Give each term a rough probability with one line of reasoning, combine them, and state which single term the final number is most sensitive to.

One more thing: my own tentative view is that (a) is nearly certain, but that this counts as *saving* indifference rather than abandoning it — the principle becomes a rule for reasoning about specified procedures, not about raw ignorance, and a rule so transformed is still doing real work. If you think that reframing changes what the question is really asking, tell me, and give your credence for both readings if they diverge enough to matter. Finally, what consideration, argument, or kind of case would move your number the most?
