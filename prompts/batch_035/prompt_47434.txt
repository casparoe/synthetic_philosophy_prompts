In April 1922, during Albert Einstein's first visit to postwar Paris, he and Henri Bergson met at the Société française de philosophie to debate what relativity had done to the philosophy of time. The salient facts can be given briefly. Bergson, then arguably the most famous philosopher in the world, had studied the mathematics of relativity carefully and prepared at length. He granted the theory everything within physics, but insisted that its "time" is a constructed, clock-based, physicist's time, and that it leaves untouched — and cannot touch — la durée, time as lived. In particular, he argued that the celebrated asymmetry of the traveling twin does not hold for lived duration: that once the whole journey is taken into account, the traveler has lived no less than the one who stayed, and that the discrepancy appears only when one mistakes clock readings for time itself. Einstein's reply was terse: he distinguished physical time from psychological time, allowed no third, philosophical category, and remarked that "The time of the philosophers does not exist." Bergson expanded his position into a book that same year, Durée et simultanéité, and the verdict of physicists was that on the twin case he had simply blundered; the episode is remembered as philosophy's rout at the hands of physics. Privately, Einstein's sympathies ran further than his public dismissal: in a 1955 condolence letter to the family of Michele Besso, he wrote that "People like us, who believe in physics, know that the distinction between past, present, and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion."

Analyze this episode through the lens of the presentism–eternalism debate, and be disciplined about the mapping, because it is not the obvious one. Bergson was not a presentist: he did not defend a privileged global present and raised no objection to the relativity of simultaneity as physics; his quarrel was with the spatialization of time — with treating the four-dimensional manifold as all the time there is. Einstein, in public, did not so much assert the block universe as refuse the question. So the first task is to say what each man actually held, where the presentism/eternalism lens genuinely illuminates the exchange, and where it misdescribes both parties. The second is to trace the modern descendant of the dispute: the argument from the relativity of simultaneity to eternalism (in something like its Rietdijk–Putnam form), which infers from Minkowski spacetime that all events are equally real. Bergson's charge, translated into this idiom, is that the argument smuggles an ontology into an operational theory — that conventions about clock synchronization are made to carry metaphysical weight they cannot bear. Assess whether that reconstructed charge has force; note where presentism, A-theoretic views of flow, and Bergsonian duration part company (a defender of flow need not be a presentist, and vice versa); and say what the episode shows about the theory — whether it is a confirmation of the relativity-based argument for eternalism, an anomaly for it, or a case that argument never anticipated, because the dispute was never really about the ontology of the manifold at all. Where the lens is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
