I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long essay about what, if anything, dreams are. In my reading I've come across a dispute that I think deserves a central place in the essay, but I'm having trouble pinning down exactly what the two sides disagree about, and I'd appreciate a careful comparison before I structure the piece around it.

On one side is Norman Malcolm, in his book Dreaming, arguing in the spirit of Wittgenstein that the concept of a dream cannot be the concept of an experience had during sleep. His reasoning, as I understand it, is that the dream report made on waking is the only route to what was dreamed, and that such a report could never be checked against the dreaming itself — not because our instruments happen to be too crude, but in principle. If there is no conceivable way to verify that the sleeper was having the experiences the morning report describes, then treating dreams as experiences that occur during sleep is not a false theory but a piece of nonsense: it posits a private object to which even the subject has no independent access. Dreams, on this view, belong to the grammar of our waking lives — a report that a sleeper gives in the morning, full stop — and the question of what happened while they slept doesn't arise.

On the other side is Daniel Dennett, in a reply whose central move I want to make sure I have right. He accepts, at least for the sake of argument, that we can't verify whether the experiences reported on waking match anything that happened during sleep — but denies that this makes the idea of dreaming-as-experience meaningless. Instead he offers a picture on which the composition of the dream could be distributed over time in ways the report can't discriminate: the content might be worked up during sleep and edited like tape, or composed entirely in the moment of waking and inserted, like a cassette loaded and played back on demand. The verificationist facts are the same either way, and since they can't settle the matter, they can't show that one of the options is nonsense. If they could, Dennett suggests, the same style of argument would threaten the idea that our waking experiences are experiences of a real, independently existing world unfolding in time.

Please compare the two positions with an eye to three questions I need answered for my essay:

1. Do Malcolm and Dennett actually disagree about dreams, or do they disagree about philosophical method — about whether conceptual analysis can rule out an empirical question in advance of the science? It matters for my framing whether Malcolm's conclusion is about the concept of dreaming or about what dream researchers will find.

2. What would each side say about lucid dreamers — sleepers who, in REM sleep, become aware that they are dreaming and can signal to observers with prearranged patterns of eye movements while the dream is still going on? My instinct is that this breaks the heart out of Malcolm's verificationist argument, since we now have reports issued from inside the dream, contemporaneous with the experiences they report. But I want to know whether that instinct is right, or whether Malcolm's argument was never meant to be vulnerable to this kind of evidence — whether it concerns the reliability of memory rather than the impossibility of signaling.

3. Does Dennett's cassette-and-editing model actually answer the question of whether dreams are experiences, or does it relocate the question — replacing the mystery of dreaming with a mystery about when and where content gets composed, which may be just as resistant to the verificationist's tools? If the latter, is that a cost for Dennett or a demonstration of his larger point?

Please don't structure your answer as a list of reading suggestions or a survey of the literature; what I need is the dialectic itself, worked through, so that I can decide how to present the disagreement fairly and where, if anywhere, it is genuinely unresolved.
