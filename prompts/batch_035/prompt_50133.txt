I lead a philosophy of science reading group — mostly grad students in other fields, no logic beyond a first course — and in a single forty-minute session I have to introduce the problem of verisimilitude: whether it even makes sense to say one false theory is closer to the truth than another, and if it does, how to define that. I don't want the explanation itself; I want a plan for delivering it. Please cover the following.

First, the dependency structure: what has to be established before the problem can even look like a problem? My instinct is that they'll need to see why "all theories are false" doesn't settle the matter — why it seems obvious that a theory predicting 7 planets is better off than one predicting 100, when the truth is 8 — and then discover that this obviousness is exactly what resists formalization. Tell me what order to present the pieces in and why, including where the Miller–Tichý-style negative result belongs: I assume it must come late, but I'd like your view on whether to preview the failure early so the definitions land as attempted rescues rather than as idle machinery.

Second, the carrying example. It should let the group compute or at least eyeball closeness for a handful of simple false statements against a known truth — a finite toy world, ideally with two or three statements that the naive definitions rank in embarrassing or contradictory ways (each of two statements coming out more verisimilar than the other, or a false statement beaten by one of its own false consequences). Tell me what the example must contain and what it must avoid — I suspect infinite or continuous domains are out, and I'd like to know if you agree.

Third, the misconception map. The ones I anticipate: that verisimilitude is just a disguised probability or confirmation measure; that adding a true conjunct to a false statement obviously makes it closer to the truth; that the whole enterprise collapses if there is no single "whole truth" to be near; and — from the more philosophically literate — the worry that any closeness measure depends arbitrarily on the language chosen to state the theories. For each, tell me where in the sequence it is best defused rather than merely rebutted, and which ones I should let surface on their own.

Fourth, what to refuse to do at this level: I assume the actual formal apparatus of the post-Popper literature — content measures, min-max similarity, the structuralist approaches — is beyond forty minutes for this audience. Say what to wave at with a name and what to skip entirely, so that the group leaves knowing the shape of the problem without believing they've seen the solutions.

Finally, one question I can pose in the last five minutes whose answer would reveal whether the group has actually grasped the problem rather than the vocabulary.

One request about your own stance: if you think the session as I've framed it can't be done well — that the negative result can't be made to bite in forty minutes without formal apparatus, or that my ordering instinct above is backwards in a way that matters — say so plainly and adjust the assignment rather than producing a plan that papers over the difficulty.
