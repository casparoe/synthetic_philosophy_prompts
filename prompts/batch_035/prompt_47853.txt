I'm TAing a philosophy of science section (16 students, one 80-minute class, desks we can push into tables) and next week we hit a unit on prediction, falsification, and when it's legitimate to keep a theory alive. The case I want to build the whole session around is Malthus: the population/geometric-vs-arithmetic food story, the fact that his doom date came and went roughly on schedule in England, and the question of whether he was wrong, right-but-early, or doing something that isn't really prediction at all. They'll have read Popper before this session and will get Lakatos the week after, so the activity has to happen in the gap where they've got the falsificationist reflex but not yet the vocabulary of research programmes and protective belts.

What I want is a game they play before any of that gets named. My rough idea: a multi-generation village simulation where table-groups make choices (fertility, how much to plant vs consume, whether to expand onto marginal land) for points, and one student each round plays "the prophet" who has to publicly commit to a collapse prediction with stated conditions. I (the facilitator) secretly control a deck of event cards -- new crop varieties, opened land, a blight -- and the deck is stacked so the prophet's collapse fails to arrive for several rounds in a row, then a crunch round where it lands. The payoff scheme should make it sting: people who hedged against the prophet lose something when he turns out right, and following his advice the earlier rounds cost them. The thing I most want is for the *students themselves* to start saying stuff like "well obviously the prediction held, it said unless new land opened up, and it did" -- the rescue move should come out of their mouths before I put a name on it. Then a second run of the game where they know the deck composition, so they can feel the difference between being right in advance and being right in hindsight, which I suspect is where "right but early" either earns its keep or falls apart.

So: design the whole thing. Full rules, deck composition (how many dud rounds before the crunch, and why that ratio), what stays hidden and when to reveal it, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, the role cards, a timeline that fits 80 minutes with the debrief included. The debrief should map specific rounds onto ceteris paribus clauses, ad hoc rescues vs. legit auxiliary assumptions, and hindsight bias, in a way that sets up Lakatos without name-dropping him yet. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing students infer the deck is rigged and start optimizing around me instead of the game, or the prophet role becomes comedy), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce -- the real timescale, and the fact that historical critics were writing before they knew the outcomes -- and where I have to just say so out loud. Also flag whether you think the setup unfairly stacks things toward vindicating Malthus, and if so how to rig at least one variant where the lesson cuts the other way. Answer in American English, and don't hold back on detail -- I'd rather cut than improvise.
