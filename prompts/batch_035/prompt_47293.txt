I'm teaching a unit on Galileo's actual position on Scripture and science, centered on his 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, and I want to pair it with Brecht's Life of Galileo. My worry is that the play and the letter tell two almost opposite stories, and I'd like a reading that faces that instead of smoothing it over.

The background my students will have: in the letter, Galileo argues (1) that Scripture accommodates itself to the understanding of common people and so should not be read literally on physical questions, citing the standard patristic practice of taking passages about God's hands or anger non-literally; (2) that God is revealed equally in the book of nature and the book of Scripture, so demonstrated physical truths cannot be false, since God cannot contradict Himself; and (3) that Scripture should only be reinterpreted on astronomical matters once the truth is established by rigorous demonstration — which, conveniently, he thought Copernicanism had nearly reached. The letter also contains the famous epigram he attributes to a cardinal of the highest rank, to the effect that the Holy Spirit teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go.

Brecht, by contrast, gives almost none of this. His Galileo is no careful hermeneutic strategist but an Epicurean hedonist who recants to save his own skin and comforts, and who late in the play indicts himself for betraying his profession — for pursuing knowledge under social arrangements he never tried to change, and for believing that the point of science was to make life easier rather than to end misery. Brecht revised the play after Hiroshima, hardening Galileo's guilt, so the play's target is arguably not the Church at all but the scientist's responsibility under any authority that wants his knowledge. My students will read the letter as a study in theological subtlety and then watch a play in which theology is mostly offstage and the interesting moral question is Galileo's own.

Please give me a scene-by-scene account of which of Galileo's three commitments from the letter the play actually dramatizes, distorts, or quietly replaces — and in each case say whose version is more illuminating. Three anchor points I want you to treat in detail: the scene with the little monk, where the argument from the needs of the common people is put in the mouth of a peasant-born cleric and Galileo answers it with contempt for pious poverty rather than with his own accommodation doctrine; the recantation scene, where you might argue Brecht stages exactly the political limit of the letter's reasoning that truth need only defer to demonstrated proof; and the late scene where Andrea visits the blind Galileo in custody and the Discorsi is smuggled out, which Brecht frames as partial redemption rather than vindication. I'm also curious whether the play, read against the letter, exposes something real in Galileo's position — that the demonstrated-truth escape hatch hands final interpretive authority to whoever counts as having demonstrated something, which in 1633 was not him.

If you think one of the three commitments from the letter matters most for teaching the play, or the play matters most for teaching the letter, say which and why. And throughout, flag for me where your reading leans on a contested interpretation of the play or of the letter, and what would most plausibly make you abandon the reading: a better account of Brecht's revisions, a different reconstruction of Galileo's actual strategy, or something internal to the texts that cuts against the pairing. I don't want a tidy synthesis if the honest answer is that the letter and the play are simply about different Galileos and the tension is the point.
