I work on knowledge representation, and I have become convinced that the frame problem is the site of one of the longest-running non-arguments in cognitive science. The dispute has stalled so thoroughly that the two camps barely read each other anymore, and I want a proper diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is.

Here is how the dispute looks from where I sit. When McCarthy and Hayes introduced the problem in 1969, it was a technical annoyance within the situation calculus: if you represent the effects of an action by writing what changes, then implicitly or explicitly you must also represent the unbounded number of facts that don't change, and a naive encoding of that collapses under its own weight. The knowledge-representation community treats this as solved in the essentials: McCarthy's circumscription, Reiter's default logic and successor-state axioms, and the broader inertial-reasoning toolkit made compact persistence reasoning a routine engineering matter. On this reading, the frame problem is a bookkeeping problem about the common-sense law of inertia, and treating it as philosophically deep is a category error.

The philosophical camp disagrees, and its strongest version deserves better than the caricature it usually gets. Dennett, in his 1984 paper on cognitive wheels, granted everything the KR people say about compact representation and still insisted the real problem was untouched: how does a system come to know which of the indefinitely many consequences of what it knows are worth computing at all, without first computing them to check? His parable of the robot that reasons its way to pulling the wagon-bomb into the room with its spare battery is meant to show that the difficulty is not representational bookkeeping but the unformalized notion of relevance. Fodor pushed this further, arguing that the frame problem is a genuinely new problem in epistemology — roughly, the problem of how rational belief fixation constrains search without knowing what it is looking for — and that it reveals something about abduction that no classical computational account of the central cognitive architecture can deliver. Shanahan, in his 1997 book and later survey work, tried to referee by arguing that much of the controversy rests on sliding between the narrow logical problem and several broader readings, including one that shades into the problem of induction and one that shades into the problem of relevance.

And now a third act has opened: people claim that large language models either solve the frame problem or dissolve it, on the grounds that statistical regularities over training text deliver something like relevance for free, without any explicit inertial machinery; others respond that this is wishful relabeling, and that brittleness and confabulation out of distribution are just the old problem wearing new clothes.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is mostly verbal. The narrow frame problem was genuinely solved or shown tractable; the broad frame problem, as Fodor and Dennett posed it, is not well-posed, because relevance is always relevance-to-a-goal and no one has specified what the goal-relative function is an answer for — so the two camps are respectively talking about a solved engineering problem and a non-problem, and never actually disagreed about anything. But I can't quite make myself believe this, for the awkward reason that something like the abductive phenomenon they point at is manifestly real and unexplained.

So here is what I want from you. First, diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, a hidden empirical question, a substantive disagreement about what a theory of cognition owes us, or two research programs with incommensurable success criteria that happen to share a term? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, engaging the strongest version of the relevance reading rather than a straw one. Second, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper decomposition of the concept, a formal result, some class of systems, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. In particular, adjudicate the LLM claim — is it a genuine test case for the philosophical reading, a relabeling, or a red herring that changes the subject from rational relevance to statistical salience? And if you think the broad problem is ill-posed, say concretely what a well-posed replacement for it would look like.
