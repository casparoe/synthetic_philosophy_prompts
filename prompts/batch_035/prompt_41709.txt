My friend and I spent the better part of Saturday evening arguing about theodicy, and we eventually realized we were talking past each other because we disagreed about what would even count as success. She kept pressing me on whether any explanation of evil could justify it; I kept insisting that justification and explanation are different projects. Before we go another round, I want the terms of the argument settled, and I'm asking you to write them: the desiderata an adequate theodicy has to meet, where adequacy is fixed by a specific purpose — not converting skeptics, but being something a reflective theist can sincerely maintain in front of a parent who has just lost a child, without embarrassment or evasion.

Include, at minimum, the cases the theodicy has to cover: moral evil done by free agents, natural evil like earthquakes and pandemics, the billions of years of animal suffering that predate any human soul whose formation could be at stake, and the distribution question — why the misery falls so unevenly and so rarely tracks desert. Include the distinctions it must respect, such as theodicy versus defense and explaining versus justifying, and the consistency constraints: it must not imply that we do wrong to fight suffering (a soul-making story on which relieving pain steals goods from the sufferer has failed), must not make evil itself good, and must not rely on compensating goods so vague that they would justify anything. State the case behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular theological camps, and flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, demanding that God's reasons be humanly intelligible while also demanding that they cover horrors we cannot imaginably redeem. Tell me which single requirement excludes the most popular candidates. Then, briefly, run free-will theodicy, soul-making, and skeptical theism against the list, noting where the last one dissolves rather than meets the requirements. Please write in American English.
