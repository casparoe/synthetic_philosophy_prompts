I'm a high school senior taking an elective called "Ethics and the Environment," and for my final paper I have to analyze a real environmental episode using one of the frameworks from the course. I've chosen the collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery off Newfoundland, and I want to run it through the tragedy of the commons, but I'm running into a complication I can't resolve on my own, and I'd like help thinking it through.

Here's the story as I understand it, so you don't need to look anything up: for centuries, cod was fished largely by small inshore crews using relatively simple gear, and the fishery was the economic backbone of Newfoundland's coastal villages. In the decades after the Second World War, increasingly industrial fishing arrived, first from distant foreign fleets and then, after Canada extended its fishing limits to 200 miles in 1977, from Canadian factory trawlers and draggers, often encouraged by government subsidies. Catches stayed deceptively high even as scientists and many inshore fishermen — who noticed their own catches thinning — warned that the stock was in trouble. In 1992, Canada declared a moratorium on northern cod fishing that was supposed to last a couple of years and instead ended a way of life, throwing tens of thousands of people out of work in hundreds of outport communities.

The textbook reading is straightforward: individual fishers each gained the full benefit of every additional fish taken while the cost of depletion was spread across everyone, so rational self-interest drove the stock to ruin, exactly as the tragedy of the commons predicts. But the more I read, the less tidy this gets. The fishery wasn't a free-for-all at the end — it was regulated by a federal department that set quotas, and the quotas were simply too high, partly because of political pressure and faulty stock assessments. Meanwhile, the inshore fishermen, the people the tragedy story supposedly indicts, were among the loudest voices calling for restraint, and their local knowledge turned out to be more accurate than the official science. And the proposed "solutions" to commons tragedies — privatizing the resource through individual transferable quotas, or imposing top-down state control — sit awkwardly with the fact that top-down state control is arguably what failed here.

So here's what I'd like you to do. First, map the episode properly onto the tragedy-of-the-commons framework: who counts as a user of the commons, what the incentive structure actually was, and which parts of the collapse the framework genuinely explains. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks — whether this is better described as a failure of open access, a failure of centralized mismanagement, or a perverse interaction between government subsidies and the commons logic, and how much each of these matters. Third, bring in the counter-tradition associated with Elinor Ostrom, which holds that communities can govern common-pool resources themselves under certain conditions (clear boundaries, rules made by the users themselves, graduated sanctions, and so on): ask which of those conditions were present or absent in Newfoundland, and whether the inshore fishermen's traditional practices constituted a commons governance system that industrialization and state management effectively dismantled. Finally, address the twist that's been bugging me: whether the moratorium itself can be analyzed as a public goods problem — restraint as a contribution everyone benefits from, with each fishing community tempted to quietly defect — and whether the political difficulty of keeping it in place for so long supports or undermines that analysis. If the honest answer is that some part of this question is one the theory simply has nothing to say about, I'd rather know that than have the theory stretched to cover it, since my teacher grades hard on forcing frameworks where they don't fit.
