Write a short dialogue between a utilitarian (in the spirit of Harsanyi) and a Rawlsian, both stuck together behind the veil of ignorance, arguing about whether they're allowed to use probabilities while there. The utilitarian's line: I don't know who I'll end up as, but it seems rational to treat that as an equal-probability gamble over every citizen's life and maximize expected utility — which, once you crunch it, just is average utilitarianism. The Rawlsian's line: that's precisely the move the veil is supposed to block. There is no single gambler back here who might wake up as anyone; there are just separate persons, and the separateness of persons means one person's loss can't be written off against another's gain — so the expected-value calculation is a fiction, and we should be looking at how the worst off fare instead (maximin). Make them actually trade blows: the utilitarian should demand to know why refusing to average is anything more than letting some people be much worse off so that others can stay comfortable, and the Rawlsian should explain what exactly makes a gamble across persons different from an ordinary gamble within a single life, where we all think expected value is fine. Neither side should be a pushover, and dont manufacture a tidy compromise at the end. Afterwards, add a short note sorting out which moves in the dialogue would be more or less settled among people who know this literature and which are genuinely open — and dont present either side's position as the consensus one.
