After a documentary on the Bengal famine of 1943, two members of my history discussion group got into an argument about what category famines belong in: natural disaster or political crime. I'll reconstruct the exchange as fairly as I can.

R opened by classifying famines as political crimes. In Bengal, she said, total food supply in 1943 was not dramatically lower than in the preceding years; what collapsed was particular people's ability to command food — wartime inflation, panic hoarding once rice from Burma stopped arriving, and the government's denial measures (seizing boats and destroying rice stocks along the coast in anticipation of a Japanese landing) stripped rural laborers and fishing communities of the means to buy or catch food, while officials in Delhi were slow, complacent, and preoccupied with the war. She invoked Amartya Sen's later work on famines, which treated them as failures in people's ability to acquire food rather than shortfalls in food availability, and his observation that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a competitive press and opposition parties. The Irish case, she said, fits the same pattern: the potato blight was natural, but the famine was policy — doctrinal reliance on the market, food continuing to leave a starving island, and relief that was late, stingy, and punitive. If a government could have prevented mass death at tolerable cost and did not, that is a crime, and the natural-disaster label launders it.

D accepted most of the history but rejected the category. His first move: crime requires a culpable agent, and the clearest policy-made famine, the Great Leap Forward, looks less like a decision to starve people than like monumental self-deception — local cadres inflated harvest figures to survive and to please, grain was requisitioned on the basis of fictional yields, and the reporting system was too broken and too frightened to tell the truth until millions had died. Catastrophic negligence and ideological dysfunction, he said, are not criminal intent, and the word crime smuggles in a mental-state claim the record does not always support.

R answered that omissions can be crimes where there is a duty: a lifeguard who watches a swimmer drown is guilty without wanting the swimmer dead. A state with the capacity to prevent famine at tolerable cost has that duty, and Britain in the 1840s was the richest state on earth.

D replied that the analogy proves too much: it works only if the state knows what is happening, and the Great Leap is precisely the case in which the system destroyed its own knowledge. And if mere capacity plus mass death constitutes a crime, then every government that develops too slowly is criminal, which flattens the category until it covers nearly every poor country — and erases the contrast that made R's opening point sharp.

R's last move: she never claimed every famine is a crime. She claimed that the classification is a choice, and that natural disaster is the label that keeps getting chosen by the parties who benefit from the blur.

Neither would budge, and the rest of us could not referee it. Please go through the exchange move by move: at each step, say who is right, and identify exactly where the losing argument overreaches. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them missed.
