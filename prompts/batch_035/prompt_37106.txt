I sit on the governing board of a national prize for popular science writing — in my day job I cover science and ideas for a broadsheet, and the board leans on journalists and critics precisely because we are supposed to have read widely and judged for ourselves. We are mid-crisis. Submissions have grown past two hundred titles a year, and our chair has proposed a protocol under which each longlisted book is read in full by exactly one member of a screening panel, who files a two-page assessment; the final jury then selects the winner on the basis of those assessments plus a chapter of excerpts, reading only the top five titles cover to cover. A second board member has offered an amendment she calls the chain-of-custody rule: testimony about a book's merits is admissible only if it comes from someone with first-hand acquaintance with the book, and any secondhand characterization — an assessment of an assessment — is inadmissible. A third bloc dismisses the whole dispute, noting that our screeners are seasoned critics whose judgments are more reliable than most jurors' anyway.

Before our vote on the protocol, I need the written opinion I will circulate to the board. It should recommend a concrete revision — or acceptance of the chair's protocol as it stands — and defend it in terms my fellow board members can actually engage with. In particular, I want it to take seriously the question lurking underneath the procedure fight: whether the panel can meaningfully award the prize for excellence when its belief that the winning book is excellent arrives by report rather than by acquaintance, and whether something over and above accuracy — the jury's shared exercise of its own judgment, its standing to praise in public — is lost when aesthetic evaluation travels secondhand. At the same time, it should give the critics' efficiency argument its full due, including the point that some of what makes a book good (structure, rigor, clarity of explanation) may transmit by description better than the skeptics allow. It should also say what the chain-of-custody amendment gets right, if anything, and where it draws its line badly. Please keep it under 1,200 words, written as a formal board memorandum, with the recommendation stated up front.
