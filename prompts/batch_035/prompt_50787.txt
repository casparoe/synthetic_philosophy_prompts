I chair the Retention and Student Success Committee at a mid-sized public university, and, as the committee's one philosopher (I teach philosophy of science), I have been assigned the task of drafting its recommendation memo. The committee must decide the fate of a voluntary summer bridge program piloted last year, and although the dispute presents itself as statistical, it has in my view become conceptual: the committee cannot agree on what "the effect of the program" even is, because our data support different conclusions under different partitions of the same students.

The facts. 240 students enrolled in the pilot, all by choice; for comparison we have the roughly 1,800 non-participants from the same entering class. The headline figure: 66% of participants were enrolled in good standing at the end of the first year, against 78% of non-participants. On that basis the provost's chief of staff calls the program a demonstrable harm and wants it discontinued. Disaggregated by entering preparation — below or above the mathematics placement threshold — participants fared better in both strata: among underprepared entrants, 62% versus 54%; among prepared entrants, 88% versus 83%. Since nearly all participants were underprepared entrants, the aggregate comparison is dominated by composition, and the program's faculty conclude that the headline number is an artifact and the program should be expanded. A third colleague then disaggregated the same data by residence — on-campus versus commuter — and found no advantage for participants in either group; she now suspects the stratum-wise benefits are artifacts as well, and proposes that we keep slicing until we find a partition everyone accepts. The committee is at sea about which partition is "the real one," and colleagues have begun saying that no purely statistical rule can tell us which partition is relevant. If that last thought is correct, I want the memo to say so — and to say what does the work instead.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the April meeting. It should: (1) recommend one of renewal as-is, expansion with changed targeting, or discontinuation, together with a concrete evaluation plan for next year; (2) state criteria the committee can actually apply when partitions disagree, including an account of why the preparation partition and the residence partition cannot both be decisive, if that is our view, and of what is wrong with the proposal to keep slicing until a partition satisfies everyone; (3) treat seriously the chief of staff's strongest consideration — that the headline aggregate is the university's reality so far as the trustees are concerned, and that our within-stratum comparisons rest on voluntary enrollment rather than random assignment, so that even the favorable partitions may be confounded by unobserved motivation; and (4) be explicit about what observational data of this kind can and cannot establish about the program's causal effect, so that the recommendation does not pretend to a rigor the evidence lacks.
