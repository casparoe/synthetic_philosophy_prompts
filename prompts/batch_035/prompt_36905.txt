A senior colleague on our clinical ethics consultation service has, over the years, given consistent rulings on when a patient counts as dead, and we now have three new referrals on which we would like to predict her verdicts — and to understand the rule she is implicitly running. Her track record: a man with total and irreversible loss of all brain function, including the brainstem, whose heart still beats on a ventilator — dead. A woman whose heart stopped under a do-not-resuscitate order and who was declared dead after five minutes without circulation — dead. A patient in a documented permanent vegetative state who breathes unaided and shows sleep-wake cycles — not dead. A locked-in patient who communicates entirely by eye movements — not dead. A newborn with anencephaly who breathes spontaneously — not dead. A donation-after-circulatory-death donor whose ventilator was withdrawn and who was declared dead two minutes after the heart stopped, even though the heart might have resumed had the ventilator been reconnected — dead.

The new referrals are: (a) a woman at sixteen weeks' gestation who has suffered total brain death and whose body is being maintained on somatic support so that the pregnancy can continue — six weeks of gestation have already passed; (b) a man in irreversible asystole, with resuscitation abandoned, whose circulation is nonetheless fully maintained by ECMO and in whom no brain activity can be detected; (c) a patient whose brainstem is intact but whose cortex has been completely and permanently destroyed, so that consciousness will never return.

Please predict her likely verdict on each referral, setting out the candidate rules that could underlie her earlier rulings (noting that the obvious consciousness-based criterion seems to be excluded by the vegetative-state and anencephaly cases, so the interesting question is what remains), saying which rule best explains the track record, where the surviving candidates diverge on the new referrals, and giving your best guess with reasons. Then identify the further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the candidates. Throughout, please distinguish what is settled in law and mainstream clinical practice from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and clinicians — for instance, on whether brain death is the death of the organism as a whole or merely of the brain — and avoid presenting any contested position as the consensus.
