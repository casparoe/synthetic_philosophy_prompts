I referee manuscripts in the foundations of physics for a mid-tier journal — perhaps six to ten papers a year, and a noticeable fraction of them hang their central claim on a thought experiment: the author constructs a scenario in the head, declares it possible or impossible, and draws a substantive conclusion about the laws or about some interpretation of quantum mechanics. My fellow referees oscillate between two reflexes: "it's just intuition dressed up, physics needs math and data, reject," and "Galileo and Einstein worked this way, accept." Neither reflex is a review. I want a procedure I can actually run in about an hour of close reading per manuscript, with written comments due in ten days, one round of author response, and the editor making the final call.

Please take the role of a philosopher of science who has thought hard about how thought experiments can yield knowledge at all, and who has done practical refereeing, and build me a decision procedure for reviewing this kind of paper. Before writing it, sketch the taxonomy you will use. My understanding is that there is a real, live disagreement here: one camp holds that a thought experiment is only ever a disguised ordinary argument, so its apparent special force must be an illusion; another camp holds that thought experiments can do something arguments cannot — give a kind of direct insight into what the laws permit. Where that literature is genuinely split, the procedure should route around the split rather than quietly take a side, and if it cannot route around it, it should say so and flag the step as contested. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who know this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.

The procedure itself should give me: the questions to ask of a manuscript, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; the points at which the procedure outputs a recommendation (roughly: the thought experiment is doing genuine argumentative work / it should be converted into an explicit derivation before publication / it is intuition trading on mental pictures and should be rejected as such); and the points at which it must stop and hand the call to me, because the question is one of judgment or taste rather than something with a right answer. It should contain a step that tests for the standard failure mode — a thought experiment whose intuitiveness comes from the very theory it is meant to support — and it should tell me how a referee can test for that in practice, since none of us can introspect our own theoretical training.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions:

1. A Maxwell's-demon variant in which the demon has bounded memory, and the author appeals to the thermodynamic cost of erasing information to argue that the second law survives the demon.
2. A paper arguing, from a scenario in which a perfectly isolated observer measures a quantum system and then reverses the unitary evolution, that a prominent interpretation of quantum mechanics is committed to a claim its own defenders deny.
3. A paper that dismisses a rival's thought experiment as physically impossible while itself assuming a dynamical law that no experiment has confirmed — both sides trading in possibilities licensed by nothing.

For at least one of these, the procedure should deliver a recommendation I will flinch at — pick whichever case you can make most uncomfortable — and then say plainly why my flinch is not a reason to override the procedure. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: which questions about a thought experiment it leaves to the editor regardless, and where it will predictably misfire — for instance, on thought experiments whose real work is done by a diagram or by the rhythm of the prose rather than by the possibility of the scenario, or on ones whose significance only shows up decades later in mathematics.
