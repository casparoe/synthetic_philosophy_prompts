I'm a third-year PhD student in economics writing a dissertation chapter on the value of information in strategic settings, and I've talked myself into an argument I can't get out of. The bone of contention is Good's theorem — the result attributed to I. J. Good that, for a Bayesian agent in a decision problem, a costless signal can never lower the achievable expected utility. The proof is disarmingly simple: because the agent is free to ignore the signal, every act that was available without the information remains available with it, so the informed optimum can't fall below the uninformed one. I've always loved this result, and my chapter was going to be organized around the policy it seems to license — never turn down free information.

The problem is a game I built as a worked example and now can't dismiss. An entrant and an incumbent move simultaneously. Demand will be strong or weak with probability 1/2 each, common knowledge. The entrant chooses Launch or Shelve; the incumbent chooses Restrain (stay in the premium segment) or Expand (build for the mass market). Only the entrant's payoff depends on the state, and the incumbent receives no signal in either version below. Entrant's payoffs, written (strong, weak): Launch/Restrain (12, −8); Launch/Expand (3, −1); Shelve (0, 0). Incumbent's payoffs, state-independent: Launch/Restrain 4; Launch/Expand 1; Shelve/Restrain 6; Shelve/Expand 12. Uninformed version, where neither player learns the state: launching yields the entrant 2 or 1 in expectation against restraint or expansion, versus 0 for shelving, so launching strictly dominates; the incumbent, facing certain entry, restrains (4 > 1). Iterated strict dominance gives a unique equilibrium, and the entrant's expected payoff is (12 − 8)/2 = 2. Informed version, where the entrant privately learns the state before moving and this is common knowledge: "launch iff strong" now strictly dominates every other contingency plan for the entrant (6 versus 2/−4/0 against restraint; 1.5 versus 1/−0.5/0 against expansion), and the incumbent, facing entry only in the strong state, compares restraint at (4 + 6)/2 = 5 with expansion at (1 + 12)/2 = 6.5, and expands. Again unique by dominance, but the entrant's expected payoff is now (3 + 0)/2 = 1.5. So a costless signal about a state of nature — nothing in its content concerns anyone's choices — lowers her payoff from 2 to 1.5, while the incumbent's rises from 4 to 6.5. The information is valuable in the game, just not to the person holding it.

Here is the argument I can't beat. (1) Good's theorem: within a fixed decision problem — prior, feasible acts, utilities — a costless signal never lowers maximal expected utility, because the agent can ignore it. (2) A player in a game faces, from her own vantage point, an ordinary decision problem: the co-player's behavior is just part of the environment, inducing a distribution over the consequences of her acts. (3) My signal is costless and reports a state of nature, not the incumbent's choice, so it is exactly the kind of signal the theorem covers. (4) Therefore the entrant cannot be worse off informed: at worst she ignores the signal and secures her uninformed payoff. (5) But she is worse off, by a dominance computation I've now redone five times. Each premise looks fine to me in isolation, and the conclusion is something I can't accept as a matter of arithmetic, not taste. I have a nagging feeling the trouble is in how (2) and (4) fit together, but every time I try to pinpoint the bad move, all the steps still look individually defensible.

Act as a senior decision theorist, equally at home in Bayesian decision theory and noncooperative game theory. Before committing to a diagnosis, please re-derive both equilibria yourself and either confirm my numbers or correct them — I want the arithmetic checked, not taken on faith — and then work through at least two candidate places the argument could break before settling on one. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs: in particular, what survives of Good's theorem once your diagnosis is in, and what the never-turn-down-free-information policy really amounts to in strategic settings as opposed to the lone decision maker. I'd rather keep (1) than anything else in the argument, but I want the honest price of that, not a rescue.
