I teach a philosophy of history survey and I'm writing up my own position statement on cyclical vs. linear time so the students can poke at it — but I'd like a check first, because I suspect I'm holding views that don't fit together and I can't see where. Here's the set:

(1) Polybius's anacyclosis is basically right as a schema: kingship decays into tyranny, that provokes aristocracy, which decays into oligarchy, which provokes democracy, which decays into mob rule, and then someone strong restores kingship and it starts over. (2) Ibn Khaldun is also basically right: states run on group cohesion (asabiyyah), cohesion decays mechanically with luxury and succession, and a dynasty rarely outlives three or four generations. (3) I take these to be the same cycle observed at different scales, not two rival theories. (4) Spengler is organism-mysticism — civilizations do not have life-courses, and I want nothing to do with that talk. (5) Toynbee's challenge-and-response mechanism, on the other hand, is sound and explains why some societies buck the cycle for long stretches. (6) I'm a committed methodological individualist: no historical explanation may appeal to supra-individual entities with needs, aims, or fates of their own. (7) Modern constitutional democracies with dense institutional record-keeping have exited the cycle — not permanently, but as a difference in kind, not degree.
