Six weeks ago my wife and I had our first child, and with her return to work I have become the person who plans our household's meals for what will presumably be the next two decades. I have settled on a principle for the meat we buy, and I want it stress-tested before it hardens into habit. The principle: where a choice between meats exists, choose beef over chicken. My reasoning, pieced together from reading I've done since the baby arrived: by my arithmetic, boneless chicken costs roughly one bird's life for every three or four pounds, while a finished steer's death is spread across four or five hundred pounds of beef — a difference of more than a hundredfold in deaths per pound, even after amortizing the breeding herd across all the offspring it produces. Moreover, a good part of a beef animal's life is spent converting grass nobody could eat into calories, whereas a broiler spends its entire six weeks eating corn and soy grown on land whose cultivation — the tilling, the pesticides, the combine at harvest — kills whatever wild animals were living in the field. My brother-in-law, who has been involved in animal welfare advocacy for years, tells me this is a rationalization dressed up as arithmetic. I would rather have the question taken seriously than be reassured. So: what would count against the principle? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward — which empirical premises, if they turned out false, would sink it; what it predicts that its rivals do not (consider "choose chicken because cattle grazing ties up more wild habitat," or the plainer view that one should simply reduce all meat in equal proportion); which objections a committed defender of the principle would concede as genuinely damaging and which they would be tempted to wave off; and an honest verdict on whether the principle can be qualified into unfalsifiability, always finding some neglected harm somewhere that happens to favor beef. I am also unsure how to treat the pasture's own inhabitants — insects, mice, ground-nesting birds — and whether they belong in the ledger at all. For what it's worth, what I would personally find most persuasive is evidence about how the lives of the animals themselves compare, not merely how many of them there are.
