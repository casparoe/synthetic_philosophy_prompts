I've been reading Friston-adjacent stuff for years now (LessWrong threads, the more skeptical takes from people like Anil Seth) and I still can't pin down when the free energy principle is actually doing explanatory work vs. just redescribing whatever happened. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of applications — is there a principle behind them? Bacteria doing chemotaxis up a glucose gradient, described as minimizing expected free energy: seems genuinely illuminating, like it unifies perception and action into one story even for critters with no brain. The specific active-inference models of saccades and attention that make quantitatve predictions you can actually fit to behavior: obviously real science, whatever you think of the grand metaphysics. Calling a thermostat a free-energy minimizer: I say word games, it isn't representing anything. The dark room objection, plus Friston's reply that your priors say you're a creature that eats and explores, so hiding in a dark room forever would be surprising: the reply works, but it feels like it works by stuffing everything into the priors, and then I can't see what could ever count against the theory. Addiction modeled as misweighted precision on priors: honestly this feels like it captures something that plain reward-prediction-error stories miss, but I can't explain why it doesn't have the same stuff-it-in-the-priors problem. And a guy on a forum telling me religions spread because they minimize free energy at the cultural level: pure vibes, at that scale the principle seems to collapse into "systems that persist are the ones that persist". So: is there a principle that separates the load-bearing applications from the redescriptive ones, and can it get my dark-room verdict and my addiction verdict right at the same time? If not, tell me which verdicts I should give up, and what the principle says about cases I haven't listed — a plant turning toward light, say, or the biosphere as a whole. Also please say how confident you are in the overall principle and especially which part of it you'd least bet on.
