I'm a retired biochemist -- spent the better part of thirty-five years running a lab that worked on protein folding, so my instinct when I hear a claim is to ask what would falsify it. Since retiring I've got properly into philsophy, and I've fallen down the Wittgenstein rabbit hole, especially his remarks on religious belief. I help organise the interview evenings at our local philosophy club, and next month our guest is a philosopher who defends a broadly Wittgensteinian line on religion: the idea that religious utterances take their meaning from their role in a form of life, and that the believer's words function more as an expression of commitment, or a picture that regulates a whole life, than as an empirical hypothesis being put on the table for testing.

I'll be honest, to my old lab-rat nose the whole thing smells faintly of moving the goalposts -- if belief isn't a claim about how the world is, what exactly is it? But I want the questions to be fair, and to press where the view is strongest, not just where it looks wierd from outside. Draft me eight to ten interview questions. I want at least one each on the following:

1. What the view says about the content of religious belief. Is the believer asserting anything at all when she says God exists, or is it closer to what you'd call a confession of attitude -- and can something really be a belief if there's nothing in the world that would make it true or false?
2. Wittgenstein apparently wrote in his notebooks that religious belief is something like a passionate commitment to a system of reference, rather than belief in a set of propositions. What does our guest think hangs on that distinction, and does it survive contact with believers who insist that they do believe propositions -- virgin birth, resurrection, the lot -- very literally?
3. The empirical-believer problem. Plenty of religious folk treat intercessory prayer as something that could in principle shift outcomes in the world, and some of them fund young-earth geology expeditions. Are those people doing religion wrong? Are they doing the same thing with different confidence settings? Or does the Wittgensteinian line simply not apply to them -- and if it only applies to the sophisticated minority, isn't that a bit convenient?
4. The comparison with the hinges from On Certainty. If my certainty that the earth existed long before my birth isn't held on evidence but functions as a hinge that the whole game turns on, is religious certainty the same sort of thing? And if so, does that dignify religion, or does it cheapen science by making its certainties look like commitments people just happen to have?

For each of those four, include a follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give. And finish with one question about what would count as evidence against the Wittgensteinian view itself -- I mean something the guest's own framework could register as a bad result, not just "well, some believers disagree with each other".
