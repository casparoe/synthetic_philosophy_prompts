I'm a junior in high school, and for a term paper in my ethics seminar I set out to defend deontology — the idea that some things are just wrong to do, whatever the consequences — against an objection my teacher raised that I now can't answer. Here is her argument as best I can lay it out in steps. (1) In the case where a surgeon could kill one healthy patient and transplant his organs to save five dying patients, the deontologist insists the surgeon must refuse, even though she knows her refusal means five deaths. (2) Any moral rule must be grounded either in facts about the outcomes of the acts it forbids or in facts about the agents bound by it, since those are the only two kinds of fact there are to ground a rule in. (3) A rule against killing cannot be grounded in outcomes, because one death is a better outcome than five. (4) So the constraint must be grounded in a fact about the agent, namely that she herself would be doing the killing rather than allowing the dying. (5) A surgeon who, seeing five deaths coming, refuses to act just so that she herself will not have killed, is treating the condition of her own hands as worth more than four lives. (6) Nothing about one's own conduct could rationally be valued above four other people's lives; a person who did value it that way would be morally self-indulgent, almost vain about her own purity. (7) Therefore deontology is either secretly outcome-based after all, in which case it is not deontology, or it is moral vanity dressed up as principle.

My teacher also anticipated the reply I most wanted to give, which was that the constraint is not about the surgeon at all but about the patient: his right not to be killed and used as a source of spare parts. She responded that if the constraint is grounded in the patient's right, and all five dying patients have that same right too, then five rights outweigh one, and the surgeon's refusal lets five rights be violated instead of one — which is worse by the deontologist's own lights, since it is worse by the standard of rights she says she cares about. So rights-talk, she says, just re-runs step (3), and we get pushed back to the agent after all. I have a feeling there is a slide in there somewhere, perhaps between a right being violated and a particular person violating it, but I cannot articulate it well enough to put it in the paper.

Here is what I would like from you. Tell me exactly which step of the argument to reject and why that step looked fine in the first place, and then tell me what rejecting it costs, both for my paper and for the deontology I am trying to defend. If you think the argument is actually valid all the way through, say so, but then tell me which premise is least costly to give up, because I would most like to keep (1) — I really do think the surgeon must refuse — and I would rather give up something in the middle than give up either the refusal or my grip on why it is right. My teacher also mentioned something she called moral residue, the idea that the deontologist can refuse to kill and still be genuinely sorry about the five, and I suspect it matters here; please explain whether it does, and whether it helps with her objection about rights stacking up. Please write your entire reply as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
