Please explain how Daniel Dennett's crane-versus-skyhook distinction in Darwin's Dangerous Idea (1995) relates to his contrast between greedy and good reductionism. I can state each distinction on its own — skyhooks are explanations that import intelligence or design from outside the natural order, while cranes are mindless, algorithmic processes that build complexity gradually from simpler foundations; greedy reductionism is the vice of leaping straight from a phenomenon to its lowest-level description, skipping the intermediate machinery, whereas good reductionism patiently builds that machinery. What I'm unsure about is how the two distinctions map onto each other. Is greedy reductionism a covert form of skyhook-seeking — wanting the reduction accomplished by fiat rather than by the slow crane-work of actual mechanisms — or is it a distinct failure mode that only afflicts people already inside the naturalistic picture? Relatedly, how does this play out in Dennett's dispute with Stephen Jay Gould, for instance over the charge in Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper that adaptationists traffic in untestable just-so stories? Dennett seems to hold that even where that critique lands, it shows only that some crane-building was done sloppily, not that cranes are insufficient. I'd like a clear account of why he thinks that, and of whether there is any version of the spandrels-style objection that, on Dennett's own terms, would point at a genuine need for something more than cranes.
