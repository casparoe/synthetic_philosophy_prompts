I'm evaluating whether coherence arguments actually succeed in establishing that rational agents maximize expected utility, and before assessing any particular argument I want the specification it will be graded against. Please write out the desiderata that any adequate coherence argument for expected utility maximization must meet.

By a coherence argument I mean any argument of this general shape: if an agent's credences or preferences violate certain structural conditions (probabilism, transitivity of indifference, the von Neumann–Morgenstern independence axiom, and so on), then the agent is vulnerable to some defect — she accepts a Dutch book of bets, or can be money-pumped through a cycle of trades, or is dominated in every state of the world — and since the defect suffices for irrationality, violating the conditions is irrational; hence rationality requires compliance with the conditions, which in turn delivers expected utility maximization. The specification should cover arguments of this shape whatever the particular defect invoked.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or more of a preference characteristic of one camp in the literature. The desiderata I expect you to consider, though you may add others or argue that some on my list are not genuine requirements:

1. The conclusion must bind the agent's actual choices, not merely bets she would accept in the abstract. The motivating worry: an agent who takes no bets, or limits her stakes, seems to escape the standard formulation, and an argument that can be avoided by refusing to play is not an argument for a norm on decision-making generally.
2. The premises must not presuppose the conclusion. A Dutch book argument against violating probabilism whose setup already assumes the agent's degrees of belief obey the probability axioms, or a representation theorem whose construction of the option space already encodes the utility structure it is meant to derive, would beg the question.
3. The argument must work in one-shot cases. It cannot lean on the agent facing the bookmaker repeatedly, since the law of large numbers does no work when the exposure occurs exactly once, and rationality had better not depend on how often one happens to be exploited.
4. The defect must be shown to be a genuine rational failing, not merely something that looks bad. That the agent is guaranteed to lose some money must be argued to be irrational even when the amounts are trivial and the agent knows the whole episode is a one-off; similarly, being money-pumped must be shown irrational even when the agent can simply refuse the later trades, since a pump that depends on the agent's own future cooperation is suspect.
5. The argument must say something about cases where compliance and intuition pull apart. If preference patterns like Allais's violate independence without any visible incoherence — no sure loss, no cycle, no domination — the argument owes us an account of why the structural condition it enforces is a condition of coherence rather than a substantive taste about risk that a fully rational agent might or might not have.
6. It should respect the distinction between what holds of an idealized agent and what follows about ordinary agents, since a demonstration that no logically omniscient, perfectly reflective agent violates the axioms does not by itself show that our failures are irrational rather than limitations.

Then, the part I most want to see: identify any pair or subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say why — if the list itself is unstable in that way, that is a result about coherence arguments, and I want it stated plainly rather than papered over. Finally, in a paragraph each, assess de Finetti's Dutch book argument for probabilism, the von Neumann–Morgenstern construction, and Savage's representation theorem against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases: the scorecard is for calibration only, and the real thinking is in the requirements.
