Ok so my friend Dave and I have kept a shared note on our phones for like three years now where we log predictions and then check who was right -- sports stuff, whether people we know will break up, work drama, election-ish things, whatever comes up. It started as a joke but at this point we each have a couple hundred scored calls, and Dave is ahead, though honestly not by much. Anyway somewhere along the way I talked myself into this principle: when two people have thought about something equally hard and know roughly the same stuff and they still land on different answers, the right move is for each of them to slide toward the other one, like meet in the middle or at least partway. Split the difference. I found out later there's a whole philosophy literature on this and my view is apparently called conciliationism, though most of that reading went over my head honestly.

But here's what's bugging me. First, Dave thinks splitting the difference is itself dumb -- he's a "if you've thought it through, hold your ground" guy, and it feels like the fact that we disagree about disagreement itself should do something weird to the whole picture, like the rule pointing a gun at itself or whatever. Second, even though we're close on the score, we're not literally tied, and if he's even slightly more reliable than me, am I even his "peer" in the way the rule needs? Third, I can feel how I might be protecting the view: whenever splitting feels clearly wrong (say, some guy who's flipped a coin disagrees with me), I just say "well he's not my peer so the rule doesn't apply," and I worry I've defined the rule so it only ever fires when it looks fine. Kind of like a bet you can never lose.

So lay it out for me: what would actually count against the split-the-difference view? Not just "here's an argument someone made," but what kinds of situations or observations would make me want to drop it -- cases where the intuition against splitting is strong enough that the view should take a real hit, whether something like our track-record data could undermine it, whether the self-disagreement problem with Dave is fatal or just a curiosity. Tell me which of these a person commited to the view would concede really hurts versus wave off, and rank them from view-killers to merely annoying. And be straight with me about whether the "equal peers" escape hatch makes the whole thing unfalsifiable, and if so whether that's actually a problem for this kind of view or just how it has to be. I'm more interested in the cases than the citations, I can't follow the papers anyway.
