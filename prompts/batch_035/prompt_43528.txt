I teach a small seminar on social choice (14 students, one 80-minute session), and I'm building a session around Sen's liberal paradox — the result that you can't have both a weak Pareto condition and even a minimal amount of individual liberty in a social decision function, i.e. having at least two people each decisive over at least one pair of social states. The students will have seen Arrow and Condorcet cycles before, so the cycling itself won't shock them. What I want them to *feel* is the specific strangeness of the liberal paradox: that respecting everyone's right to decide their own stuff, plus respecting unanimous preferences, lands you in an outcome the whole group agrees is worse, and that the escape hatch is always someone's own private choice — the very thing the liberty condition was supposed to protect.

Here's roughly what I've been imagining, but you should design it properly. Split the class into "flats" of 3 or 4 students. Each flat is deciding a few binary personal matters — think: does Ravi practice trombone at 7am in the shared flat, does Mila record her podcast in the living room on Sunday mornings, does Jonas put his fermenting kimchi in the shared fridge. Each student gets a preference card assigning them a full ranking over the combinations (not just their own matter), and the cards are engineered so that (a) each person's ranking over their own matter conflicts in a specific way with how it enters everyone else's rankings, and (b) the outcome where everyone exercises their right the way they personally prefer is Pareto dominated by another combination, reachable only through someone choosing against their own preference. The flat's "constitution" has two rules: personal matters are decided by their owner alone, and anything the whole flat unanimously prefers must be adopted. Then they settle in, and see what happens.

Design the whole thing: the exact scenario and the preference cards (I need a concrete worked example of the rankings so the cycle actually appears — please write out the actual tables or rankings, don't just say "design the preferences so a cycle occurs"), the sequence of moves or rounds, any materials, how to run it in 80 minutes, and how to handle the students who don't get a card (I'm thinking some of them play an external "review board" that only enforces the two constitutional rules, but do whatever works better). No money payoffs — these are philosophy-adjacent econ students, the outcome of the flat's week is payoff enough, but if you can think of a cheap scoring scheme that makes people care, go for it.

Then the debrief: map the moments in the game back onto the formal pieces — which house rule was the liberty condition, which was the Pareto condition, where exactly the cycle showed up, and how this relates to but differs from a plain Condorcet cycle. I want them to see that the paradox isn't really about voting at all.
