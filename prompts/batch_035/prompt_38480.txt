So I'm on the hook for something kind of terrifying. I'm a philosophy postdoc who got invited to give the ethics grand rounds talk at the medical school's palliative care department -- the audience is their joint clinical ethics / hospice staff. My paper is the Epicurean line, but a specific version of it: death is not bad for the one who dies -- not just not painful, not bad for them at all -- because there is no subject left for it to be bad for, and the standard deprivation reply (death is bad because it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had) smuggles in a comparison to a life that was never yours to lose. I lean on the symmetry point, that nobody thinks the eons before their conception were bad for them even though those also "deprive" them of goods, though I admit that argument has soft spots. To make room for the obvious reactions I do grant that the process of dying can be bad, that anticipatory fear is understandable even where its object is a mistake, and that death is often terrible for the bereaved -- so the harm gets relocated to the living and to the dying-process, not denied, and funerals and grief counseling stay perfectly rational.
