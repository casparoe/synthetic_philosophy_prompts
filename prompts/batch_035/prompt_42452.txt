Consider the following examination question and a student's answer to it.

**Examination question (30 minutes):** Egalitarians agree that people should be equal, but disagree about the metric of equality: what exactly is it that should be equalised? Set out the main welfarist and resourcist answers, and explain the expensive-taste objection to welfare egalitarianism. Then assess whether the objection forces a move from equality of welfare to equality of resources, making reference to at least one further metric discussed by Sen or his critics.

**Student's answer:**

Egalitarianism says people should be equal, but equality is only intelligible once we specify the respect in which people are to be made equal. Welfare egalitarians say the relevant equalisandum is how well people's lives go — utility, or the satisfaction of their preferences. Resourcists say what should be equalised are the means people have available, such as income, wealth, and liberties; Rawls's list of primary goods and Dworkin's auction of resources are the two best-known versions.

The expensive-taste objection targets welfare egalitarianism. Suppose someone freely develops a taste for pre-phylloxera claret and vintage champagne. Satisfying those tastes costs far more than satisfying ordinary ones, so perfect equality of welfare requires transferring extra resources to the person with expensive tastes. Most people find this unfair: the person chose the tastes, and compensating them for their own choices amounts to subsidising indulgence. The objection is essentially a special case of the adaptive-preferences problem, since tastes are expensive only relative to the circumstances in which a person was raised.

Dworkin's alternative is equality of resources, tested by the envy test: a distribution is equal when nobody prefers anyone else's bundle to their own. Combined with the hypothetical insurance market, this holds people responsible for their ambitions while insuring them against bad brute luck. Cohen accepts the spirit of the objection but refines it: we should compensate only for tastes one does not identify with, such as involuntary cravings, and not for tastes one embraces.

Rawls complicates this. Since the difference principle requires improving the position of the worst-off group, and positions are ranked by how well off their members are, Rawls is best understood as a welfare egalitarian with a maximin twist.

Finally, Sen's capabilities approach is a refinement of resourcism: a capability just is a resource plus the functioning it enables, so the approach adds nothing to the resource metric. And since interpersonal welfare comparisons cannot be made with any precision while resources can be counted, the resourcist metric is the right one.
