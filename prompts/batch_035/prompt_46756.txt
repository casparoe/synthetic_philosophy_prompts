As a professor of philosophy, I want a careful disambiguation of a piece of epistemological shorthand I heard deployed last week. At a departmental colloquium, a junior colleague of mine ended discussion of a graduate student's paper — one sympathetic to William James — by invoking Clifford's principle from The Ethics of Belief, roughly that believing anything on insufficient evidence is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, adding that James's rejoinder, that we are sometimes entitled to believe ahead of the evidence when the option is live, forced, and momentous, had been answered a century ago. The student, who works on the epistemology of religious faith, was visibly deflated, and I suspect the dictum did more work in that room than its content licenses. I would like you to act as an expert epistemologist with deep knowledge of both Clifford and James, and to think through the possible precisifications before committing to your list. Please give me exactly five distinct precise claims that the Cliffordian dictum, as my colleague used it, could be advancing — ranging from the near-platitudinous to the strong and controversial — presented as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion, where each item is one or two sentences stating the reading and delivering a verdict on whether it is plausible and, crucially, whether it would actually suffice to dismiss the student's Jamesian position.
