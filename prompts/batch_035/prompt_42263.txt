The question was: "State Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge. Explain how it handles Gettier cases. State the principle of epistemic closure, explain why Nozick rejects it, and evaluate his response."

Here's the student's answer:

"Nozick's tracking theory says that in addition to believing P and P being true, you know P when your belief tracks the truth of P, which means: if P were false, you would not believe P. So knowledge is a belief that would go away if the fact went away. This handles Gettier cases because in those cases the belief is true only by luck, and the luck shows up as a tracking failure: the person would have believed P even in close situations where P was false. For example, in fake barn county, if the barn façade in front of you had been a real barn you would still have believed you were seeing a barn, so you don't know there's a barn there. The closure principle says knowledge is closed under known implication: if you know P, and you know that P implies Q, then you know Q. Nozick rejects closure because of skeptical hypotheses. On his theory you know ordinary things like 'I have hands,' but you don't know 'I am not a brain in a vat,' because if you were a brain in a vat you would still believe you have hands, so that belief doesn't track. So you know P and you know P implies Q but you don't know Q, and knowledge is not closed. Nozick thinks this is a price worth paying, since the alternative is skepticism about everyday knowledge, and sensitivity captures what's important about knowing. In my opinion denying closure is too expensive: if I know P and I competently deduce Q from P, then it would be irrational for me to refuse to accept Q, so it seems I must know Q after all."
