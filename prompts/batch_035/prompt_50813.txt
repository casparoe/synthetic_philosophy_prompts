I run a reading group on AI alignment — 16 people, a mix of CS grad students and a couple of philosophers, 90 minutes, a room with tables we can rearrange. We keep hitting the same wall: someone cites an eval score, someone else says that's an average-case number and tells you nothing about what an adversary gets to do with the system. Everyone nods, but I dont think they feel how different the two claims are, or that choosing between them is a real decision with costs attached rather than a mood. So: design a session where they run into it themselves, before anyone names the distinction.

Things I want in it:

- The same set of system behaviors gets scored under two regimes. In one, the evaluators get a fixed budget of random probes; in the other, a red-team player gets to inspect the deployed system first and then choose what to throw at it. Same artifacts, different guarantee — and I want the moment where a team that "passed" gets wrecked to be viscerally annoying to them.
- Red team is a rotating role, and an exploit only scores points if you're the first to find that particular one. I want the tail to feel sparse: most inputs are fine, the catastrophe lives in a few narrow places, and finding them is the whole game.
- A caution dial. The teams playing the "assistant" (role-play is fine, students acting as a policy, or cards, whatever you think holds up) can trade usefulness for safety, and they should actually have to pick points on that curve and then see the curve laid out in the debrief afterwards.
- Somewhere in there, one deployment context where the average-case strategy wins and one where it loses. The lesson should not be "always demand worst-case" — that's the part I most want them to leave with. The guarantee you need depends on who gets to pick the input, and that's a fact about the deployment, not about the math.

No real money — points or candy is fine. Don't name the distinction until the debrief. In the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto the actual quantifiers (the for-every versus the on-average), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce. My guess is the biggest gap is that a real adversary gets compute and does search, so it finds weird corner cases a tired human red-teamer never would; also that the "average" in average-case is over a distribution nobody ever hands you. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it too — I'm braced for the teams gaming the rubric, but give me the rest.

Also, no laptops or software, and prep has to fit in one evening — cards and printouts is about my budget, and that's for me, the facilitator.
