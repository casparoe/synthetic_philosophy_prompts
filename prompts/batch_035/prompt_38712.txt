I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, twenty years in, and I've spent my career inside a working epistemology that never calls itself that: the two-source rule, the distinction between on-record, off-record, and deep-background attribution, FOIA fights, our dependence on press officers and stringers, corrections policies, the track-record reasoning by which a desk decides whether to run a story on an unnamed official's word. I have no philosophy training, but I've been reading the epistemology of conspiracy theories lately — enough to know the landmarks without being fluent in them — and I keep having the same thought: my trade runs a rival enterprise on exactly that terrain, with a completely different toolkit. So here is my request.

Restate the central positions of that debate in my working vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. Specifically, translate four things. First, the generalist claim that conspiracy theories are defective as a class — presumably the counterpart would be some blanket editorial policy of suspicion toward an entire genre of story, so tell me what that looks like from the desk and what it costs us. Second, the particularist rejoinder that each theory stands or falls on its evidence, given that actual conspiracies — Watergate, industry cover-ups — do get uncovered, sometimes by reporters using exactly the sourcing practices the generalist distrusts. Third, the diagnosis of conspiracy-mindedness as an intellectual vice — gullibility toward insider claims, closed-mindedness toward official ones — and how it does or doesn't match our folk concept of the reporter who falls in love with a thesis. Fourth, the charge that such theories are self-sealing, that errant data is itself read as confirming the cover-up; I suspect this lands close to home, since a story built on deep-background sourcing can be self-sealing in the same way — a denial becomes stonewalling, the absence of corroboration becomes a measure of how deep it goes — and I want to know whether the two-source rule is best understood as our culture's anti-self-sealing device, or as something else.

Then the report itself: what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge to make it fit, and what has no expression in newsroom vocabulary at all. Two candidates for the third column that I want you to check rather than accept: underdetermination, the philosophers' point that the official story and the conspiracy story can both accommodate all the available evidence, and the category question of whether "conspiracy theory" marks an epistemic kind at all. Can my vocabulary say either of those? And guard against the temptation to smuggle journalism's norms in as though they were neutral standards — treat our sourcing rules as one epistemic culture's habits, not the metric against which the philosophers are being measured.

Finally, commit. I don't want the balanced conclusion that each side has something to learn; I want you to take a definite position on whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary, which we should patch, or a sign that the philosophical disagreement is less substantive than it looks, and to defend it. And as a practical addendum: if exactly one piece of philosophical vocabulary is worth stealing into newsroom practice, name it and say why that one rather than the others.
