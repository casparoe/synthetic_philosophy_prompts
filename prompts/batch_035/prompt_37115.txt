I'm a philosophy of biology grad student, and next month I've been invited to give a talk in an animal behavior department's seminar series, which is basically enemy territory for someone like me. My thesis, in short: the textbook version of the handicap principle -- signals are honest because they're costly, low-quality individuals can't afford to fake them -- gets the logic half backwards. What enforces honesty isn't cost as such but differential cost: honesty is stable wherever the signal is differentially expensive across quality, including cases where it's nearly free for high-quality signalers. So the peacock's tail and the gazelle's stotting can be perfectly honest without anyone being "handicapped" in the sense of suffering net fitness loss from signaling per se. I want to recast the whole thing in terms of receiver skepticism: signal costs exist to convince recievers who have no independent way to verify quality, and the cost is essentially an entrance fee whose height is set by how much the receiver can be bothered to doubt, not waste demanded by the signaling logic itself.

The audience is mostly empiricists who work on birds and fish and know the handicap principle mainly as "Zahavi's idea that males show off by wasting resources," plus one theoretician who models signaling equilibria and I strongly suspect thinks Grafen settled this whole debate around 1990, so anything that smells like re-litigating it from a philosophy department will annoy her. There's also a postdoc who studies carotenoid-based coloration and loves pointing out that in her system the cost is in obtaining the pigment from the diet -- and the pigment does other jobs, like immune function -- not in growing the ornament at all, which she takes to be a headache for simple cost stories. And there's a professor emeritus who was around for the original Zahavi versus Maynard Smith spats in the journals back in the 70s and reacts to anything that sounds like "Zahavi was right" with a kind of tired contempt.
