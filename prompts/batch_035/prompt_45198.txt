For our seminar on late-antique moral thought, my professor has been reconstructing Augustine's doctrine of lying from his two treatises on the subject, and she summarized his position as a series of verdicts on cases: first, someone who asserts what he sincerely believes, though it is in fact false, does not lie at all — he may be rash or credulous, but he is not a liar; second, someone who asserts what he believes to be false lies even if, by luck, what he says turns out to be true, because the liar is judged from the state of his own mind, not from the way the world happens to be; third, a lie consists in having one thing in mind and expressing another, in words or in any other kind of sign, so that the specific evil of lying is the will to produce a false belief in the hearer — the double heart; fourth, jesting falsehoods delivered in an evident joking register, where the tone itself announces that no deceit is intended, were never counted as lies in the first place; fifth, lying is never permissible, no matter the stakes — martyrs may not deny their faith to persecutors, Catholics may not feign heresy to infiltrate heretical congregations, and one may not lie even to preserve someone's temporal life; and sixth, the Hebrew midwives were rewarded for their mercy toward the Hebrew infants, not for their lie, which remained a sin, merely a pardonable one. She stressed that these verdicts are meant to cohere, and that the whole doctrine hangs on getting the definition exactly right.

Her exam question presents three new cases and asks what Augustine should say about each, given the reconstruction above. In the first, a doctor answering a dying patient's direct question gives a series of statements each of which she believes to be true, selected and arranged so that the patient will come to believe he is not dying — she never asserts anything she believes false, but the false belief in the hearer is exactly what she is cultivating. In the second, a householder is asked at the door by an armed pursuer whether the fugitive he is hunting is in the house, and answers that he is not here — every part of her mind knows how the pursuer will take this, and she is relying on him taking it that way, though she privately means it in a narrower sense. The third is meant as a control: an actor on a stage recites a scripted falsehood before an audience that knows the whole play is invention.

I would like you to act as a careful historian of ancient and medieval moral philosophy and work this out rigorously before the exam. Please begin with some brief brainstorming about which candidate rules or formulations fit the professor's list of verdicts equally well — I suspect at least one rule makes lying turn on asserting what you believe false, while another makes it turn on the will to induce a false belief regardless of what is asserted, and I would like to see how each of these handles every item on her list, including the joke verdict and the doctor's and householder's cases. Then give me your best-guess predictions for the three cases, with your reasoning about what Augustine's own definition commits him to, and a statement of how confident you are for each and why. Finally, identify the one further case, described concretely, that would most sharply discriminate between the candidate rules you identified, and spell out what each rule would predict about it — my professor said she would credit whichever of us could design the case that forces the rules apart, so please take particular care with that part.
