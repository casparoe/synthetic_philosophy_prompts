Quick backstory: I'm a journalist, I write mostly about science and tech, and I'm trying to get my head around something for a piece I'm drafting. The practice I want you to unpick is the disclosure norm around AI. Basically everywhere you look we're building rules that require AI to identify itself: chatbots are supposed to tell you they're chatbots if you ask (in a few jurisdictions that's actual law now), AI-generated images and text are getting labeled, there's a whole compliance industry growing up around proving content wasn't machine-made. Meanwhile the mirror rule doesn't exist for humans. Nobody has to prove they're human to post anything, anonymity is treated as basically a right, and asking a person to prove their personhood comes off as rude at best. And then there's the flip side, the captcha, which forces everyone, humans included, to pass a little test that's mostly about pattern-matching cleverness — which is wierd when you think about it.

A while back I read Dennett on counterfeit people. His idea, as I took it in, was that a fake person is like counterfeit money: it passes all the surface tests while lacking the real value, and the real value in a person — the part that can't be faked — is being an accountable agent. Someone who owns what they say, who can be credited, blamed, trusted, committed to things. A smooth-talking system with nobody in the accountable driver's seat is the danger, not the smooth talking itself.

So here's what I want from you: what does the disclosure-and-labeling practice actually presuppose about what makes a person a person? To me it looks like it presupposes that the difference is provenance — where the thing was manufactured — since disclosure is required no matter how the system behaves, and no human ever has to pass any kind of accountability test to get the personhood presumption. Is that reading right, or could the exact same practice be justified from Dennett's accountability angle too, in which case I've set up a false opposition? And if you rebuilt the rules on Dennett's criterion instead of provenance, what would concretely change — would the labels go away entirely, maybe replaced by something like whoever-deploys-it-owns-what-it-does? Also, I honestly can't tell whether the captcha belongs in this analysis at all or whether it's just a security mechanism that carries no view about persons, and I'd like a straight answer on that rather than a hedge.

One more wrinkle I keep stumbling on and can't resolve: the way we talk about AI in public — the "deceptive AI" framing, the "it's pretending to be human" stuff — locates the fakeness in the behavior, in the pretending. But the rules police the origin, not the pretending. It feels like the practice and the folk commentary are pulling in opposite directions, but maybe that's actually fine and I'm overthinking it? Tell me if so.
