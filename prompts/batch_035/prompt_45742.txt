For a seminar on whether contemporary AI systems can literally be said to have beliefs, I've assembled five considerations that each appear to bear on whether attributing beliefs to a large language model is genuine or merely a useful predictive shorthand. Please rank them from most to least probative for settling that question, with one sentence per item explaining its placement. Then identify the single item whose evidential value is most illusory — the one that seems to settle something but, on reflection, does no real work — and say in a sentence what the illusion consists in.

1. The system's behavior is better predicted by a model that posits beliefs and desires than by any model that does not (the intentional-stance test in its classic form).
2. Probing the network's internals reveals features that track states of the world and persist even in contexts where the system asserts the opposite.
3. The system's stated confidences calibrate: when it reports 70% confidence, it is right roughly 70% of the time.
4. Fine-tuning can revise the system's "beliefs" wholesale through feedback from trainers alone, with no intervening change in the world or in the system's evidence.
5. Whether or not we attribute beliefs to the system makes no difference to what we can get it to do; the attribution only affects how we talk and write about it.
