my dad was diagnosed with early-stage alzheimers in march, and ever since I've been re-reading Dennett's Consciousness Explained, which I only half understood back in college. the part that keeps grabbing me now is the self as a "center of narrative gravity" — his idea that the self isn't a little person inside your head but an abstraction, like a center of mass in physics: nothing you could point to, not made of any stuff, but perfectly real and useful for predicting and organizing everything. a posited point around which the story of a person spins. it was an fun idea to argue about in a seminar at 19; now I'm watching my dad lose whole chunks of the narrative and the question of whether there's still a "him" that's more than the accumulated story feels a lot less academic.

I want to write a poem for him, something I can read out loud at thanksgiving. here's the part I need help with: I've decided it should be a pantoum, because the form seems made for this. in a pantoum each stanza is built from the second and fourth lines of the stanza before, and the final stanza circles back to reuse lines from the first — so the whole poem is literally constructed out of its own retellings, old lines coming back in new contexts. that's what I want the form to do philosophically: the self on this view gets re-authored out of whatever material is lying around from previous tellings, so the repetitions shouldn't just be musical, they should mean something slightly different each time a line returns, and the ending — where the opening lines arrive again — should land on Dennett's actual point rather than a cartoon of it. he is NOT saying "there was never anyone there, your dad is already gone, it's all just machinery." the center of gravity is real, it's just not a thing. so the ending should feel like the story holding together, or holding him together, precisely through the recycling — the narrator not discovered behind the poem but constituted by it.

some constraints: 6 or 7 quatrains, loose iambic but I don't care about perfect meter, no rhyme required if it fights the sense. and please don't let it tip into either a greeting card or a lecture — no name-dropping Dennett inside the poem, and no cheap consolation either (no "he'll always be with us"). if someone who actually knows the theory read it, they should be able to tell it's about a center of narrative gravity and not just about memory loss, and they definately shouldn't wince. it can be sad, it should be, but the sadness should come from the situation and not from me instructing the reader to feel it. a couple of the recycled lines changing valence from hopeful to factual, or from factual to hopeful, would be exactly the kind of thing I mean.
