Before I pick a side in the whole "does consciousness science need first-person data" fight, I want the actual spec: what does a method for studying consciousness have to deliver to count as adequate? Write me the desiderata list. I'm thinking things like: it has to handle the cases where subjects' reports and their information processing visibly come apart (blindsight-type patients who guess above chance while denying seeing anything), it can't take reports as infallible data but also can't just discard them, it has to say what counts as a report at all (is a button press a report? eye movements in no-report paradigms?), it has to work for subjects who can't give reports at all like infants, animals, or locked-in patients -- that one seems to bite hard. And it has to deal with the fact that training subjects in introspection changes the very thing they're reporting on. Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, flag which ones you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (if any), and say which single requirement does the most to knock out competing methods. Then briefly run Dennett's heterophenomenology against the list and tell me where it scores worst -- I suspect it's the nonverbal-subjects requirement but convince me either way.
