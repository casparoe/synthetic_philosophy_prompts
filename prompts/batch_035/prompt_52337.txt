I spent thirty-five years in population genetics before retiring, and have lately been reading philosophy of science in earnest — Hume on induction, Goodman's new riddle, and then, quite by accident, the no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning, which I gather go back to David Wolpert in the mid-1990s. It struck me that the theorem and Hume's problem are the same problem in different dress: averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm generalizes better than any other, so the fact that our everyday and scientific inductive practices work at all appears to demand an explanation. My former department runs a monthly discussion group of retirees who enjoy this sort of thing, and I would like to hand them a decision tree before our next session.

Please write it so that it starts from the single premise "averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm outperforms any other" and leads, by a series of branch questions, to a named position on the justification of induction. Each node should be a question a thoughtful person could answer without knowing which position it leads to. The questions I would like to see appear, or analogues of them: whether the theorem says anything at all about our universe as opposed to the space of all possible universes; whether a prior that favors simple functions is an a priori truth, a fact about the actual world, or merely a pragmatic convenience; whether the uniformity of nature is something we discover or something we must presuppose in order to discover anything; and whether induction is even the right thing to justify, or whether the whole enterprise should be replaced by something else. The leaves should include at least the Humean skeptic who takes the theorem as confirmation, the Bayesian who answers with an Occam-style prior, the Popperian who denies that induction is the target, and something in the neighborhood of Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication. Please flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and diagnose any region of the logical space your questions carve out but no one occupies — is it empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?
