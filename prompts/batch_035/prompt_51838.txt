I am a PhD student in health psychology; my dissertation concerns death anxiety — its measurement, and whether it responds better to cognitive restructuring than to acceptance- or exposure-based approaches. At an interdisciplinary end-of-life seminar, a philosopher on the panel invoked Epicurus to dismiss half of my proposal: the old line that death is nothing to us, since, in the translation he read out, "when we exist, death is not yet present, and when death is present, then we do not exist." If I understood him, his claim was that once this is granted, fear of one's own death is straightforwardly a cognitive error — a false belief that death is bad for the one who dies — so the appropriate intervention is philosophical argument rather than psychological technique, and my comparison conditions amounted to ways of managing a reaction that should simply be corrected. I lacked the standing to push back at the time, but I do not believe the one-liner was doing all the work he wanted it to do.

Could you lay out, as a list I can bring to the next seminar, the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing? For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false; and say whether that thesis, if true, would actually license the inference my colleague drew from it — that fear of death is a cognitive error to be corrected rather than a reaction to be managed. I would be grateful if the menu were honest about two matters in particular. First, anticipatory fear: my participants are alive while they dread their future deaths, so a thesis about death's not being bad when it arrives may leave entirely open whether dreading it in advance is irrational, and I have noticed that Epicurus himself seems to argue separately that anticipating a painless thing needlessly causes pain — I want to know whether that is a distinct premise or part of the same slogan, and what it adds. Second, the gap between death's not being bad for the one who dies and fear's being unwarranted, given that fear might be warranted by something other than the badness of its object. Where a single word ('bad', 'for', 'fear') does different work across readings, please flag that rather than papering over it.
