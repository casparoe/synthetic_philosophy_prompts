My closest friend from graduate school has spent the last decade as a professional forecaster — she is paid, mostly by insurers and pension funds, to estimate how long institutions, firms, and regimes will last, and her party trick is a confident forecast on anything you name within ten minutes. I've started keeping notes on her calls because her reasoning fascinates me, and lately it has started to matter to me personally. Her track record as I've recorded it: she predicted divorce for her cousin, who married at twenty-three after an eleven-month courtship, on the base rate for first marriages — she said nothing she knew about the couple seemed to her to update it. She predicted her aunt's first restaurant, a lifelong dream funded with borrowed capital in a tourist town, would close within three years, and it did; she cited the failure rate of restaurants and called her aunt's passion a selection effect pointing the same direction. When our university launched an interdisciplinary master's program with a famous founding professor and dedicated external funding, she predicted closure within a decade, remarking that famous founders leave, soft money expires, and the base rate for new degree programs is merciless. She predicted that a certain personalist ruler, twenty-six years in power and the survivor of two coup attempts, would be gone within ten years, citing the tenure distribution of such regimes and a decaying patronage economy — though I genuinely could not tell whether the coups counted for her as survivorship evidence or as symptoms. And she predicted that the hundred-and-ten-year-old firm that dominates the film stock my photography studio depends on will be absorbed or gone within fifteen years, on the fate of dominant incumbents after technological transitions.

Here is the case I actually care about. I have been offered a partnership that would require a thirty-year mortgage on a farm in the wealthiest province of a federation that has held together for two hundred and twenty years — a multiethnic federal union bound partly by a shared commercial elite, currently experiencing serious secessionist agitation in that very province. My savings, the farm, and possibly my citizenship arrangements all turn on whether the federation remains a single polity for the next thirty years. When I put this to her next month, what will she predict? As I reconstruct her implicit rule from the notes, at least two candidates fit everything above: she may be taking the base rate of the broadest category the case falls into and treating case-specific facts as noise or selection effects, in which case the graveyard of multiethnic federations and empires, most of which eventually broke apart, often when a wealthy core sought exit, points toward dissolution; or she may be choosing the reference class matched to the outcome's causal drivers, treating the fact of two centuries of survival — with whatever crisis absorption and sunk coordination that represents — as the strongest evidence available, in which case a federation already 220 years old plausibly faces a much lower thirty-year hazard than the naive base rate suggests, secessionist agitation notwithstanding.

Predict her verdict, with your reasoning and an honest statement of how confident you are. Identify the candidate rules that fit her track record, say which of them fits it best and why, and be explicit about where the rules agree on her past calls and where they would come apart on this one. Then tell me the single further question I should put to her that would best discriminate between the rules — I'm thinking something like a probe case with survival history but no live stressor, or a live stressor but no survival history, but you judge. Finally, be self-critical: say where your inference is most fragile, what evidence about her or her past calls would flip your prediction, and whether any of her recorded verdicts could be read as already inconsistent with one of your candidate rules in a way I've missed.
