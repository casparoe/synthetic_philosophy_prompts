I record a small ethics-of-everyday-life podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent years arguing that manipulation is not a distinct moral category at all. His line, as he's put it in print, is roughly this: whenever we call something manipulation, we are just labeling an ordinary channel of influence — flattery, guilt, framing, selective emphasis, emotional appeals — with a word that signals our disapproval, usually on behalf of a choice we wish the target had made instead. If the persuasion succeeds because the target was irrational or emotional or tired, that's a fact about the target's psychology, not a further wrong done by the persuader, and it applies equally to a salesman and to a friend who talks you out of quitting your job in anger. He is happy to grant that lying is wrong and coercion is wrong; his claim is that once you subtract the lying, the coercion, and the disapproval, nothing of independent moral weight remains to be called manipulation.

I want to take him seriously rather than ambush him, so I've been reading around and want to pose questions that engage the strongest machinery on the other side. Two threads in particular. First, the family of views on which manipulation is essentially the bypassing or exploitation of the target's deliberative capacities — roughly, getting a person to act by working on the causes of her behavior in ways that don't route through her appreciation of reasons, treating her decision-making process itself as a lever rather than an audience. Second, the cluster of test cases people raise against my guest: benevolent manipulation (a nurse who calms a panicking patient by distracting him rather than by argument), self-manipulation (setting an alarm across the room, or pre-committing Ulysses-style to resist later influence), and cases where the "manipulated" outcome is one the target would endorse on calm reflection. He needs a story about each.

Draft me a sequence of ten to twelve interview questions, ordered so that they build on one another rather than firing off independently. Each main question should be paired with a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give, given the position I've described. At least one question should force him to say whether the bypassing account, if it were right, would make manipulation wrong in a way that is different in kind from mere inefficacious respect for autonomy, since he'll want to say the difference is illusory. Include one question about whether his deflationary view generalizes to gaslighting — whether that's just disapproved-of influence plus lying, or whether the sustained subversion of someone's trust in her own epistemic faculties is a distinct wrong he hasn't accounted for.

Please structure the response in three sections, in this order. First, a short section titled "Where the guest stands" (~100 words) laying out his position as charitably as the material above allows, so I can check my framing before the interview. Second, the main section titled "Questions and follow-ups," containing the numbered questions with their anticipated-replies follow-ups (~700–900 words).
