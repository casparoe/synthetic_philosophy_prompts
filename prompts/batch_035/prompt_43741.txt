I'm editing a small collection of stories where each one dramatizes a problem from philosophy of physics, and the next piece is about Einstein's hole argument. In case you don't have it on hand, the setup is roughly this: suppose you think of spacetime as a kind of canvas whose points have an identity of their own, independent of whatever events happen on them. Now take some empty region of spacetime — the "hole" — and smoothly shuffle which events sit on which points of the canvas, keeping everything outside the hole fixed and keeping the fit between the events themselves exactly as it was. On the canvas view you've produced a genuinely different universe, since every event in the hole is now attached to a different point; but every possible observation, every relation between events, is untouched. So the question the story should live inside is: did anything happen? Is there a fact of the matter about which point of the canvas is "here" at all?

Write a short story, roughly 1500-2500 words, set in a far-future Bureau of Coordinate Standards — the officious agency that maintains the Official Chart of Where Everything Is, issuing updates whenever anything moves. The protagonist is a night-shift cartographer who receives an anonymous memo claiming that during "routine maintenance" the previous night, everything inside her assigned sector was permuted in exactly the manner described above: every object smoothly reassigned to a different location-point in such a way that all relative distances, angles, and motions came out identical. Her instruments confirm nothing, her supervisor insists the chart is by definition correct since it only records relative positions, and a retired colleague keeps telling her that the canvas was never real in the first place. She becomes obsessed with finding a way to catch the difference — filing requests, designing a measurement that would only work if the points themselves, not the events, have identity. I'd like the ending to land on her holding two charts that are indistinguishable in every observable respect, and having to decide what kind of thing her job was even tracking all along.

Two constraints: don't have any character lecture the reader about Einstein or about substantivalism vs. relationism — the argument should be felt through the bureaucracy, the memos, and her obsession, not explained. And keep the physics loose and literary rather than rigorous — it's her world's official cosmology, so you can invent its jargon (the "maintenance," the "re-registration of emplacements," whatever fits). A small typo or awkward phrase in her internal monologue would actually help make her feel real and tired. The story should leave the reader genuinely uncertain whether she has discovered something, lost something, or was always tracking nothing.
