At a dinner following our philosophy-of-language reading group, two friends of mine spent the better part of an hour on a sentence the rest of us had never thought to question: "It's raining outside, but I don't believe it is." Theo opened by saying the whole fuss around it is misplaced — the sentence is plainly consistent, since someone could easily be in exactly that situation, wrong about the weather two feet from the window; so it is a contingent truth like any other, and at most it's socially odd to say. Lena said consistency is the wrong bar entirely: no one can sincerely assert it at all, and the interesting question is what kind of failure that is — it isn't the Liar, which resists being assigned a truth value; this sentence has one and still can't be said, and that needs explaining. Theo offered an explanation: asserting something carries the conversational implicature that you believe it, and the sentence cancels its own implicature; but cancelling implicatures is usually harmless — you can say someone is brave while denying you intended any contrast with his being English — so at worst we have the mild oddity of someone explicitly withdrawing his own grounds. Lena replied that this treats belief as a detachable add-on when it is closer to the norm of assertion itself: a sentence that asserts the rain while removing the speaker's own entitlement, more like saying that one is not speaking, or that one does not exist, than like an implicature cancellation.

Theo pushed on the analogy: those self-defeating sentences are false whenever anyone utters them, whereas the rain sentence can be true in the very mouth of the person saying it, so they cannot be the same phenomenon; and besides, if assertion necessarily expressed belief, actors, ironists, and anyone reading the forecast aloud would be constantly incoherent, which they are not. Lena said that contexts which openly suspend the normal felicity conditions hardly show the conditions don't exist. Then Theo played what he called his trump: the version about Sam — it's raining, but Sam doesn't believe it — is perfectly fine, so the problem must somehow involve the first person; yet the past-tense version about yourself — it was raining yesterday, but I didn't believe it — seems fine too; and the reversed version, where you assert your belief and deny the weather, sounds every bit as bad as the original, even though there you're avowing a belief whose content you yourself reject rather than denying a belief you're committed to. If the whole thing were a norm of assertion, he said, the person restriction and the tense facts wouldn't be nearly this fussy. Lena muttered that present-tense, first-person belief reports might not be reports at all — that saying you believe it's raining is less a description of your mind than a hedged way of saying it's raining — and by then the plates were being cleared with both of them unmoved and the rest of us lost. I couldn't referee this and didn't try.
