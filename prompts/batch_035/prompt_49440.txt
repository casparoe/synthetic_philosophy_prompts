I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts on which of my own beliefs survive learning about their evolutionary origins, in order to work out how far I think evolutionary debunking arguments actually reach, and I'd like help extracting the principle behind the verdicts, if there is one. My belief that ripe fruit is sweet: I learn that the taste system was selected because sugar is calorically valuable, and the belief feels entirely undamaged — if anything the genealogy explains why it's reliable. My aversion to brother-sister incest: I learn it was selected because it kept ancestors clear of inbreeding depression, and here I do feel the debunking — I think the aversion loses its authority as a moral guide in cases where the genetic risk it was tuned to is absent, say a sterile couple raised apart who meet as adults. My belief that pain is bad and to be prevented: I learn that pain-avoidance was selected because it got organisms away from tissue damage, and the belief feels completely undamaged — pain really is bad, not merely fitness-enhancing to flee. My belief that my children matter more to me than strangers' children do: I learn kin-selected partiality, and while I notice the genealogy, I don't feel debunked in the same way I do about the incest case. My beliefs in elementary arithmetic and modus ponens: presumably these capacities were also shaped by selection, but I can't make myself feel any pressure at all. My belief in objective moral facts — that some things are wrong independently of what anyone thinks: this is where I feel the pressure most. And my belief that the suffering of distant strangers matters exactly as much as my own: this one I feel pulled in both directions, since impartiality looks like a thin cultural layer over evolved parochialism, yet I trust my impartial convictions more than my parochial ones. The tension I keep hitting is this: the incest verdict and the pain verdict seem to demand opposite principles — the aversion whose content tracked a real fitness-relevant fact gets debunked while the aversion whose content tracked fitness even more directly does not — and the arithmetic verdict and the moral-facts verdict seem to demand opposite principles too, since selection favored logical and mathematical reliability for the same reason it favored useful moral attitudes, whatever exactly that reason was. My tentative principle is that a belief survives its genealogy when the evolutionary explanation works by rewarding organisms whose beliefs were true, so that the selection pressure itself requires a truth-connection; but this seems to rescue the incest aversion, which I wanted debunked, and I can't tell whether it rescues pain's badness or merely presupposes it. Can you state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precisely enough to be tested; tell me which clause each of my verdicts is supposed to flow from; and tell me what the best candidate says about cases I haven't listed — the reliability of my memory, my confidence that the sun will rise tomorrow, my belief that torturing an animal for fun is wrong, and my belief in the soundness of the debunking argument itself? I would also like an honest accounting of which of my verdicts, on your best principle, has to go.
