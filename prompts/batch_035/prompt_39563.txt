I teach an intermediate phil mind seminar and every year the unit on self-deception goes the same disappointing way: students learn the labels (intentionalist, deflationist, divided-mind, whatever) and then just pick whichever name sounds coolest, without noticing that the labels paper over the actual fault lines. So this year I want to force them to commit to cases first and get a position second. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on belief, irrationality, and the mechanics of motivated reasoning, and think carefully before you start -- maybe begin with a little brainstorm of where the positions genuinely come apart, then build from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names.

What I want is a decision tree that starts from the central paradox of self-deception -- that the self-deceiver seems to have to both know something and not know it at the same time -- and walks down to named positions at the leaves. Each internal node should be a question a student could honestly answer without having any idea which position it leads toward, and the questions should be about cases and commitments, not terminology: e.g., whether you think a person can simultaneously believe p and believe not-p, or whether one of the two isn't really a belief at all (maybe an avowal, an alief, a pretense, a wish that gets reported as a belief); whether the self-deceiver needs something like an intention or a strategy, or whether the whole thing can happen to you via subpersonal mechanisms you'd never endorse; whether the "self" that gets deceived has to be a whole agent or can be a partition; and whether failing an intervention -- like the person who won't look at the test results even though refusing to look is itself information -- counts as part of the phenomenon or just a cause of it. I'd like at least three or four levels deep, with roughly six to nine leaves, and at each leaf put the position's name where one exists (deflationist views in the style of Mele, divided-mind views in the vicinity of Davidson or Pears, expressivist or concern-based accounts like Barnes and Thagard's, Gendler-style accounts where the state in question isn't a belief at all, plus any respectable leaf I haven't thought of). Crucially, where two positions can't actually be separated by any node in the tree -- where they agree on every case but differ only in bookkeeping or rhetoric -- say so explicitly instead of pretending the tree distinguishes them. And if some combination of answers has no occupants, tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. The tree is for students, so keep the node questions concrete and case-based (the wife who "doesn't notice" the affair, the smoker who "doesn't really believe" the studies, the hypochondriac who believes the diagnosis is fine but keeps checking) rather than asking them to self-apply labels.

One important request: I don't want a confident sales pitch for one position. Please keep separate what is more or less settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested -- my sense is that almost nothing here is settled, but if some points are (e.g. that paradigm cases don't require deliberate intention, if that's even right), mark them as such, and flag wherever you're presenting a contested reading as if it were consensus. I'd rather the tree came with an honest disclaimer about its load-bearing choices than that it looked authoritative. Also feel free to note anywhere that the case-based questions I suggested are leading or badly framed -- I threw them together and you're the expert.
