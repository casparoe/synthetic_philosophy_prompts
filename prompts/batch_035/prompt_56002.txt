I've been on a biopic kick lately (watched a run of the Oscar-bait ones back to back after reading a bunch of takes about one of them on the usual blogs), and the whole legal/moral setup around them has been bugging me in a "there's a hidden philosophy underneath this" way. Here's the thing: in English law, and pretty much everywhere else, you cannot defame a dead person — the claim dies with them — so a filmmaker can, in principle, invent a whole scene where a long-dead historical figure commits some vicious act they never committed, market the film as a true story, and face zero defamation exposure. Meanwhile the same legal systems, in some jurisdictions anyway, let estates inherit and enforce a right of publicity in the person's name and image — contract and property law treating the dead celebrity's persona as a descendible, tradeable asset. And the moral norms among audiences and critics run on a third track entirely: everyone shrugs at compressed timelines and composite characters, but let a biopic invent a specific act of cruelty that never happened and people say the film *wronged* the dead person, not merely that it misled viewers. Studios even pay estates for cooperation, which looks alot like the market pricing in a reputation interest that the law officially says doesn't exist.

So what I want to know: what theory of posthumous interests does this arrangement actually presuppose, if any? Does the defamation bit presuppose a flat Epicurean line — no subject left, no harm done, nothing to compensate — while the publicity-rights bit presupposes something closer to inherited property, and can those even be held together coherently, or is it fine because they're answering different questions? Alternatively, can the whole thing be reconstructed on the assumption that all the real interests at stake belong to the living (descendants, fans, society's stake in an accurate historical record), with "wronging the dead" just being a confused but harmless way of tracking harms to the living? The standard rationalist answer of "the dead don't have experiences, next question" feels too quick here, precisely because the practice is so internally differentiated.

And if we did adopt a theory on which the dead can genuinely be harmed by misrepresentation — what would actually have to change? Would we need estates to get some kind of veto over portrayals, or a mandatory end-credit sequence specifying which scenes were invented, or is there a reason a system that takes posthumous harm seriously still wouldn't want to hand control of a historical figure's image to their heirs (who, after all, have their own reasons to want a hagiography)? I'd love a proper reconstruction of the implicit theory here, including an honest account of where the practice is underdetermined — i.e., where several different views about the dead's interests would all license the existing setup as-is — and where it genuinely commits us to something.
