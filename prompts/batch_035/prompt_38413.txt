I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of history seminar on counterfactual reasoning, and I keep coming back to It's a Wonderful Life as maybe the most famous counterfactual experiment in American film, though nobody in the historiography literature ever seems to mention it. George Bailey gets to run the delete-one-variable thought experiment on his own life: Clarence the angel shows him Bedford Falls minus George, and it's Pottersville -- his wife an old maid, the pharmacist Mr. Gower a ruined convict, the whole town a sleazy casino strip run by Potter. My instinct is that the film is doing something philosophically interesting and philosophically suspect at the same time, and I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the actual debate about counterfactuals and inevitability seriously.

The questions I'm circling: Clarence's counterfactual has a ceteris paribus clause doing enormous hidden work -- it removes George but holds everything else fixed (the Building & Loan still exists, everyone else is still in town) and then lets the effects cascade out from the deletion. Is that remotely how a historian would run the counterfactual "what if George Bailey had never been born," or is it rigged so that exactly one causal chain -- the ones where George personally intervened -- gets to propagate? The structuralist or inevitabilist reading of history would presumably say Potter's Bedford Falls was coming anyway and one decent banker couldn't have stopped it; the film seems to smuggle in a full-blown great man theory of a small town while pretending it's only demonstrating causal dependence. And there's a rhetorical trick on top: the counterfactual is run by an angel, at the worst moment of a man's life, specifically to talk him out of suicide, and it shows only the worst-case Pottersville -- which feels like the cherry-picking that makes historians distrust counterfactuals as a genre. Can you defend the film against that charge, or does its conclusion (George infers his life had value from the fact that the world would be worse without him) depend on the cherry-picking being legitimate?

Where I'd especially like your help is the ending of the essay: whether the film adds something to the inevitability debate that the arguments alone don't get at. Historians worry that counterfactuals reward fantasy and neglect structure, but the film is (I think?) aware it's cheating -- George never gets to see the counterfactual where the world without him is merely different, not hellish, and the film frames the whole Pottersville sequence as a kind of divine rhetoric rather than sober history. If the film knows its counterfactual is a one-sided persuasion tool, does that make it more or less useful as a case study? I want the essay to say something about what fiction can do with these questions that argument can't, not just diagnose Frank Capra of bad methodology. Also, minor thing, but if you think the better comparison point is a different film or story that runs counterfactuals more honestly (Ebenezer Scrooge's ghost tours in A Christmas Carol are an obvious cousin), I'd take that too.
