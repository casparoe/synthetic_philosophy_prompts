I'm a high school junior, and over the past year I've quietly stopped doing almost anything for my classes unaided. My history essays start as chat transcripts, my physics problem sets get a first pass from the model before I look at them, and my Spanish is now half flashcards and half asking for translations I never attempt myself. My grades have gone up. But last week a teacher asked me, in class, to explain a concept that was all over my latest essay -- how the reinsurance market spreads risk, roughly -- and I discovered I could not say a single sentence about it. That gap between what my work shows and what I can do unaided is what worries me, not the grades.

I don't want to swear off the tool, and I don't want a lecture about cheating. I want a decision procedure I can actually run, in under two minutes, in the moment before I open the chat window: a fixed list of questions to ask myself, in order, with rough criteria for what counts as a yes or a no, and a clear stopping point that either licenses the help or forbids it. It has to work across subjects -- math, languages, essay writing, lab reports -- without me having to become a learning scientist to apply it. It should say explicitly where the procedure can't decide and hands the call back to my own judgment, and it should be honest about where it will misfire, not pretend to be airtight.

I've also started sketching my own taxonomy of what the tool does for me, and I'd like the procedure to use, correct, or discard it: I think of it as a butler (does the task while I wait), a tutor (walks me through the reasoning step by step), a sparring partner (attacks my draft or my proof and forces me to defend it), and a searchlight (tells me what I don't know I don't know, like the name of the concept I'm fumbling toward). My guess is that butler-mode is what rots my understanding and sparring-partner-mode is what builds it, with tutor-mode ambiguous depending on something I can't name -- but I'm making this up, and I want to know how much of it survives contact with what's actually known about learning: things like the difference between getting something right and being able to regenerate it, the finding that people routinely overestimate how well they understand a mechanism until asked to explain it, the idea that some difficulties in practice are desirable rather than wasteful, and the general phenomenon where offloading a process to a tool lets the skill underneath decay without your noticing.

Then run the procedure on three real cases from my week. First, the reinsurance essay, where I already know the outcome I don't like but want to see whether the procedure would actually have caught it. Second, tomorrow's physics problem set, where I can solve maybe half the problems unaided and my custom is to paste in all of them. Third, Spanish class, where I've been having the model produce example sentences with new vocabulary and my quiz average has actually improved -- a case where I suspect the procedure should let me keep going, but I want to see whether that's what it really says or whether that's just what I want it to say. If the procedure can't distinguish these cases, say so plainly, and tell me what a tenth-grader with no training in any of this theory would need to add or drop to make it work.
