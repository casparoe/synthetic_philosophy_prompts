I'm a PhD student in decision theory and formal epistemology, entering my dissertation year, and I need to decide in the next two months what my thesis will be about within the literature on imprecise probabilities — sets of probability functions instead of a single precise credence, of the sort developed in response to Ellsberg-style ambiguity and pursued by people like Isaac Levi, Peter Walley, and their successors.

What I keep noticing is that the field's energy goes into the decision rules: what to do when the credal set fails to pin down a choice — maximin, E-admissibility, minimax regret, and the rest — and into the axiological fight over which rule satisfies which plausibility constraints. But I can't tell whether that's the genuine bottleneck or a symptom of something upstream that was never settled. My half-suspicion is this: the field never agreed on what a credal set is about. On one reading it models a fact about the agent — a rational agent can be genuinely undecided between precise distributions, in something like Levi's sense of unresolved conflict, and the set is the correct description of her state of mind. On a second reading it models a fact about the evidence — any of several distributions is compatible with what she knows, and the set belongs to her epistemic situation rather than to her. On a third, closer to robust statistics, it is a device for making inferences insensitive to modeling assumptions, and talk of credence at all is loose. These readings seem to make different decision rules look natural, to change whether dilation of the set upon learning is a pathology or just honesty, and to change whether the whole apparatus is even a rival to precise Bayesianism as ordinarily understood.

So I want a prioritization, not a survey. Please rank the main open questions in this area for someone in my position — about three years, a background in statistics and formal epistemology rather than metaphysics, and a preference for work that would eventually change how people model uncertainty in actual practice (expert disagreement, model ensembles in science, decisions under deep uncertainty) rather than tidy up internal formalism. The candidates as I see them, though I may be missing some: (1) the choice and justification of decision rules under indeterminacy; (2) the updating problem — generalized conditioning, its failures, and what to do when learning shrinks or splinters the set; (3) the semantic question I sketched above, of what the credal set represents; (4) whether imprecise representations can be collapsed into precise ones by going up a level — treating the set as a precise distribution over distributions, or as an attitude toward one's own credal state — and whether that collapse would be a refutation or a harmless re-description; (5) the transfer question, whether the machinery developed for ordinary empirical uncertainty applies to moral or normative uncertainty, where there may be nothing like a probability distribution to be imprecise about.

For each item you keep in the ranking, tell me why it outranks what's below it: which criteria are doing the work — how much other work depends on it, tractability for someone with my training, neglect, and whether settling it would matter outside the seminar room. Be explicit about the trade-offs between these criteria where they conflict, and say which item you'd actively deprioritize and why — including whether the decision-rule dispute, which absorbs so much attention, is downstream of the semantic question such that working on the rules first is a mistake. Finally, tell me what would move your ranking: what would have to happen, in the literature or in my own situation, for the top item to drop.

Don't give me a bibliography or a list of who said what; I'll assemble the references myself. What I need is the ordering and the argument for it.
