I'm taking a philosophy of mind course this semester (called 'Mental Content', basically a survey of how meaning could possibly fit into a natural world) and we just hit the teleosemantics unit. I get the basic pitch: you want beliefs to be about things without smuggling 'aboutness' in by hand, so you say a state's content is fixed by what the mechanism producing it was selected for, and you get the possibility of error for free (the frog can misrepresent, snap at something that isn't a fly). But in the last seminar my prof raised what she called the indeterminacy problem, and it's been bugging me all week. The setup: frogs in the lab will snap at BB pellets, little dark moving dots, whatever. So was the snap-detection mechanism selected for detecting flies? For detecting flies-or-BBs? Or maybe not even flies, but 'small dark moving thing within strike range', since that's what the selection process actually rewarded? It looks like selection histories are too coarse and too gerrymandered to pin down one determinate intentional object, and the problem seems to generalize everywhere — hearts got selected for pumping blood but incidentally also for making thumping sounds, so which of these show up in contents downstream of interoception?

Separately, I also have a confusion about Swampman — the lightning-created molecule-for-molecule duplicate with no evolutionary history. If content supervenes on selection history, Swampman has no beliefs at all, which seems pretty crazy given he's behaviorally identical to the original. When I asked about this, my TA said the Swampman objection and the indeterminacy problem pull in *opposite directions* — one complaining that history fixes content too strictly, the other that it can't fix anything determinate — and that this tension is supposed to tell us something about teleosemantics, but I honestly didn't follow how the two complaints combine. Do they jointly show the view is broken, or do they somehow cancel out?

Could you act as a professional philosopher of mind and take some time to think this through carefully? Specifically I want: (1) why indeterminacy is supposed to be a *special* problem for teleosemantics rather than for naturalistic theories of content in general (the same gerrymandering worry shows up for causal theories of reference too, right?); (2) the main strategies for squeezing determinate content out of selection history — my prof mentioned 'narrower-than vs. broader-than' style moves, and something about letting the consumer side of the system, or the whole organism-environment interaction, do the determining — and whether any of them actually works or they just relocate the indeterminacy; and (3) whether the Swampman and indeterminacy objections really do pull in opposite directions and what follows if they do. Please don't just give me the standard textbook runthrough — brainstorm a bit first, and include the stuff nobody puts in textbooks, like whether some people just bite the bullet and say content is genuinely indeterminate and that's a feature not a bug.
