I'm one of three people on the grants committee of a small philanthropic fund (a family office that spun off, about $2M a year to give away), and I've realized our process for evaluating weird proposals is basically vibes. Most of what we see is normal enough -- bed net distributions, local school programs -- but a couple times a year we get proposals that are structured like: very small chance of astronomically large payoff, please give money. Examples we've actually sat through: a guy who wants to fund a study of whether particle collisions could trigger some vacuum-decay scenario, someone proposing to preserve "at-risk" knowledge by etching it onto nickel plates in a salt mine, and a group arguing that since we might be living in a simulation, we should fund research into not annoying the simulators. I've read enough decision theory to know the naive answer is expected value: multiply the probability by the payoff, biggest number wins. But I also stumbled on this thing called Pascal's mugging, where someone threatens to torture an absurdly huge number of people unless you hand over your wallet, and naive EV says pay him, which is obviously broken. So now I don't trust the math I half-remember and neither does the committee.

What I want is a decision procedure we can actually run in an afternoon, per proposal, written for people who are not decision theorists (one of our members, bless him, refuses to take seriously any probability below 1%, another one basically wants to always fund anything involving space). Concretely: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure outputs a "no" and stops, and where it has to punt back to us because the question is about values rather than math and can't be settled by the procedure itself. It needs to say what to do when the probability attached to a proposal is not just small but obviously made up -- the applicant pulled 10^-30 out of the air -- and whether there's a principled way to set a "probability floor" below which we ignore payoffs no matter how big, or whether that's just an irrational flinch at large exponents. It should also handle diminishing returns (our $50k is a rounding error in some of these schemes) and the possibility that the "payoff" is itself questionable, not just the probability.

Then run it on three worked cases: (1) our usual bed net grant, (2) the vacuum-decay-study proposal, and (3) the simulation-simulators one -- and I want at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I'm going to hate, since those are the tests of whether it's real. Finally, and this matters to me: be honest about which parts of this are actually settled among people who study this stuff and which parts are genuinely contested. I can never tell whether things like bounding the utility function, or discounting tiny probabilities, or refusing to aggregate across astronomical numbers of lives are the consensus view or just one school yelling louder, and I'd rather see the disagreements flagged than have them hidden inside a confident-looking formula. We meet quarterly, so if the procedure needs a spreadsheet or some crude numbers that's fine, but it can't require anything we couldn't do ourselves in a couple hours.
