A friend of mine who has been reading popular philosophy summarized something called the doomsday argument for me. My sense is that parts of it are right and parts quietly overreach, but I don't know the literature well enough to say where the seams are. Here is his account, as close to verbatim as I can recall:

"If you treat yourself as a random sample from all the humans who will ever live — which is just the Copernican idea that we shouldn't expect to occupy a special position — then the fact that you happen to be roughly the hundred-billionth human tells you something. A random sample from a sequence is very unlikely to fall in the first few percent, so you can be about 95% confident that fewer than twenty times as many humans will ever be born as have already been born. And since a future in which humanity survives for millions of years, let alone colonizes space, would mean trillions upon trillions of births, the upshot is that there's a 95% chance humanity is extinct within about two hundred years. The reasoning itself is mathematically airtight; the only premise anyone could question is whether you're allowed to treat yourself as a random sample at all, and practically everyone accepts that, since it's the same principle as estimating the total contents of an urn from one blind draw. Philosophers eventually wrote the argument off anyway, because you can run the same trick on any grouping you like — I'm probably in the last 95% of the existence of my bowling league — and that's obviously silly, so the whole thing is obviously silly."
