I've been following the mechanistic interpretability and AI-control threads for years now, and the topic I keep circling back to is what people in that space call anomaly detection: the idea that before we trust a model in a new deployment, we check whether it is running the same mechanisms on the new inputs that it ran on the inputs where we evaluated it. The presupposition that bothers me is the word "same." Everyone gestures at "same mechanism," but the positions hiding under that phrase are wildly different, and I suspect that half of the disagreements I see in comment threads about whether a given safety case actually works are just people silently operating with different individuation criteria for mechanisms without noticing it. So rather than another post arguing for one criterion, I want a diagnostic handout: a decision tree that takes someone from their concrete reactions to toy cases to a named (or explicitly unnamed) position on what it takes for two episodes in a network to count as the same mechanism.

Please build the tree from the logical space first, then locate the positions in it, rather than starting from a list of schools and reverse-engineering questions. The branching questions should be ones someone can answer without knowing where they lead, and they should be anchored in cases rather than abstractions. Some distinctions I'd want to see represented as nodes, roughly: whether mechanism identity is fixed by the weights and parameter-level implementation, by the computational graph in some canonicalized form, or by an abstract functional role that many different implementations could share; whether two mechanisms that are behaviorally indistinguishable on every distribution you can actually test are thereby the same mechanism, or whether there can be untestable implementation facts that break the identity; whether a mechanism must be a localized, intervenable component (something you could ablate or steer), or whether a global property of the whole network can count; and whether the individuation should be sensitive to what the mechanism is for — i.e., whether a circuit repurposed to drive a different downstream behavior counts as new. Seed the tree with concrete cases at each node, for example: a checkpoint fine-tuned such that the same behavior is re-derived through different weights; a circuit that is structurally identical to a benign in-distribution one but now driving planning-like behavior; two implementations of the same algorithm with different internal connectivity, one of which is interpretable to us and one of which isn't; and the adversarial case of a model deliberately presenting a familiar-looking surface behavior. Aim for something like ten to fourteen leaves, with a one-sentence statement of each leaf's most awkward commitment (the consequence its defender has to bite). For combinations of answers that nobody plausibly holds, say so and hazard a guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And please flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate — I'd expect the functionalist-about-algorithms and the straight behaviorist-under-testability to end up uncomfortably close, for instance — rather than pretending the nodes do more work than they do.

Audience is forum regulars who know what a circuit, a steering vector, and a honeypot eval are, but who have never read anything in the metaphysics of science literature on mechanisms, so keep the vocabulary operational rather than academic. To make the handout usable as a quiz, phrase each node so it reads as a concrete case plus a forced choice, and make sure every path from root to leaf is answerable start to finish by someone who has never heard the position labels. Finally, close the whole thing with a machine-readable enumeration: a final line of the form \leaves{...} containing the leaf labels in tree order, semicolon-separated, so I can diff handout versions as I revise the tree.
