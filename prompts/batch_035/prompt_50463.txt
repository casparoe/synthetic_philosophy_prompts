I am at work on a literary novel whose central figure is a retired actuary turned utilitarian philanthropist, a woman who has spent her life pricing catastrophes and who becomes quietly obsessed with the Pasadena game—the St. Petersburg-style construction from Nover and Hájek's 2004 paper, where a coin is flipped until the first heads and the payoffs grow without bound while alternating between gains and losses, so that the expected value is a conditionally convergent series whose value depends on the order of summation and is therefore, on the standard treatment, undefined. Her crisis of rationality is the spine of the book: a woman who has made every choice by computing an expectation now confronts a case where there is nothing to compute. To keep her consistent from scene to scene, I need to know precisely which resolution of the paradox she would actually endorse, and I have discovered that I cannot answer that question myself without first having the logical space mapped honestly rather than by slogan.

To that end, I would like you to construct a decision tree that takes a reader who knows the setup of the game from the bare starting point—I accept that the payoffs and probabilities of each outcome are perfectly well defined, but I do not know what the game is worth to me—to a specific position on the Pasadena paradox. Each branch node should be phrased as a concrete question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads toward (for instance, about whether they would pay more to play the game once or ten thousand times, whether an ordering of summation is a fact about the game or a fact about how we describe it, whether real-world impossibility of astronomical payoffs is philosophically relevant or merely an engineering detail). The leaves should include at least the following: Easwaran's appeal to weak expectations; Colyvan's line that the paradox exposes a structural limitation of expected-value theory, so that comparisons between such games must proceed some other way; Buchak-style moves to risk-weighted or otherwise amended decision theories; treatments that assign values via non-Archimedean numbers; views that cap utilities or probabilities so the pathology never arises; and the pragmatic dismissive view that expected utility theory simply was never meant to govern such games.

Please aim for roughly eight to ten leaves, build the tree from the questions and their permissible combinations first and only then locate the named positions within it, and note explicitly any combinations of answers that no one has bothered to occupy—together with a sentence each on whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag candidly any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate (positions that would answer every diagnostic question identically and differ only in what they call themselves), since a character who embodies one but is described in the language of the other is exactly the kind of incoherence a careful reviewer would catch. The audience is an intelligent reader of literary fiction with no decision theory beyond what the novel itself supplies, so the tree should be self-contained and free of anything that presupposes access to the literature.
