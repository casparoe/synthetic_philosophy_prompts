For about a decade, a few of us in the department have kept a standing lunch game in which we pose one-off pairwise choices between sure amounts and simple gambles — all gains, dollar amounts, no repeated play, no compounding — and we hold each other to consistency across the years. I keep the ledger, and I have become mildly obsessed with the record of a senior colleague, Professor R., whose choices are always delivered in seconds, as though she were applying a rule rather than computing anything. Her track record: she took $45,000 for sure over a coin flip paying $100,000 or nothing; she took an 80% shot at $100,000 (nothing otherwise) over $50,000 for sure; she took the $100,000-or-nothing coin flip over $25,000 for sure; she took $65,000 for sure over the same 80% shot at $100,000; and she took $25,000 for sure over a coin flip paying $60,000 or nothing. All of these are framed as one-shot decisions against a fixed and comfortable background of wealth — no mortgage coming due, no liquidity constraints — and we have never caught her revising a choice on reflection.

Next week I am running three new cases past her, and I would like predictions rather than a mere entry in the ledger. Case A: an 8% shot at $100,000 versus a 10% shot at $65,000. Case B: $65 for sure versus an 80% shot at $100 — the dinner-money version of one of her earlier choices. Case C: a coin flip paying $210,000 or nothing versus $80,000 for sure. I have half convinced myself that her record fits a Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility rule with a linear utility function and a suitable risk function; a game-theorist friend of mine insists that plain expected utility maximization with a suitably concave utility curve does exactly the same job; and a third friend claims she is running nothing so principled — something like a rule of thumb requiring a comfortable expected-value premium before she'll give up a sure thing. Rather than argue over coffee, I want the question properly analyzed: which rules actually fit the five recorded choices, what does each of those rules predict for A, B, and C, and which further choice would best pull the surviving candidates apart?

Please return a single JSON object with the following fields. "candidate_rules": an array, one element per rule you take seriously (at minimum the three above, plus any other that fits the record), each an object with "name", "statement" (one sentence, precise enough to compute with), "fits_record" (a boolean with a one-sentence numerical justification referencing her actual choices), and "predictions" (an object with keys "case_A", "case_B", "case_C", each giving the option the rule selects, and where the rule requires parameters, the parameter values you used). "my_prediction": an object with the same three case keys, each giving the option you predict Professor R. will take plus a one-sentence reason. "best_guess_rule": the rule you consider most likely to be the one she is actually running, together with your reasons for preferring it. "discriminating_case": a description of one further pairwise choice — specify amounts and probabilities — that would best separate the surviving rules, with a sentence on what each predicts for it. Finally, "anomalies": any recorded verdict that your best-guess rule accommodates only awkwardly, stated honestly. Please make sure every number you cite in "fits_record" and "predictions" actually computes — I will check the arithmetic.
