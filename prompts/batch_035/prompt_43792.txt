I've spent years in the LessWrong-adjacent anthropics threads, and one distinction gets glossed in every one of them: the difference between my future selves in Everett branches and my counterparts in David Lewis's concrete possible worlds. Both pictures tell me that "other versions of me" are fully real: on the Everett reading they are later stages of people who share my entire causal history up to a measurement event; on Lewis's, they are distinct people in distinct concrete worlds, related to me only by qualitative resemblance. Commentators slide between the two whenever the subject turns to quantum gambles, quantum immortality, or "would you accept the same odds if the coin were quantum." But the two relationships are structurally different — one holds within a world, the other only between worlds — and I have yet to see a case built to force them apart and test whether the difference does any work.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The scenario must be a decision problem an ordinary person could actually face: one paragraph, plausible stakes (nothing astronomical, nothing trolley-shaped), and no exotic machinery. Quantum physics may enter only through an ordinary quantum random number generator — the kind one can buy — so no demons, no perfect predictors, no simulations, no uploads. The case must be engineered so that a rational agent's best choice comes out differently depending on whether it treats (a) future selves in other Everett branches and (b) counterparts in merely possible Lewis-worlds as the same kind of beneficiary — same weight, same claim on one's self-concern — or as two fundamentally different kinds. It must not merely stipulate this; the divergence has to be generated by the theories' own machinery, the way counterpart theory and the Everett interpretation actually work. A natural shape: an agent comparing two gambles of equal everyday expected value, one whose randomness is quantum and one whose randomness is classical, where in each the "losing outcome" leaves someone who is you-in-some-sense badly off — but only one of the two puts that someone in this world's future. The design must make the prescriptions diverge rather than cancel.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough. Show the verdict each of the three positions delivers — strict Lewisian modal realism with counterpart theory; Everettian branching with care weighted by the Born measure; and the collapse view, on which a rational agent should weight all concrete "other me"s identically, branch or world — and explain why each gives the verdict it does. Then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work (my guess: shared causal history, that is, being one world rather than two), what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed, and how a Lewisian would attack the case. For that last part: Lewis himself, if I recall correctly, allowed in a late short piece on the Everett interpretation that branching, were it real, would threaten ordinary identity in a way merely possible worlds never do — note it if you believe I have misremembered this, but do not let the answer rest on it.

Finally, take a stand. Do not lay out the options and leave me to pick: commit to a definite answer — either self-concern properly extends to branch-selves and counterparts on the same footing, or the two relations ground genuinely different kinds of concern — and defend it. If the case as built cannot support a stand, say so plainly and identify what a better case would need. And since any such case leans on contested assumptions (chiefly the no-collapse reading of measurement), close with a candid note on where the design breaks if those assumptions fail.
