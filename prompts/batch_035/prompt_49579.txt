I'm in a philosophy of language reading group that has been working through the material on reference, necessity, and Twin Earth-style thought experiments, and a dispute over a single sentence has left me unsure what to think. The sentence is "water might have turned out not to be H2O." One member, who is very much on board with the Kripkean picture, said the sentence is simply false: if "water" rigidly designates H2O, then "water is H2O" is necessary if true, and while we had to discover it empirically, it couldn't have failed — so there's no "turning out" it might have done. A second member pushed back: before chemistry, it was a genuinely open question what the clear, drinkable stuff in rivers and lakes was; it could have turned out to be some other compound entirely, so the sentence is true, and the first member is confusing what is necessary with what could be known in advance. Then a third member tried to intervene with something he called two-dimensional semantics: he sketched the idea that a term like "water" can be assigned two different intensions depending on whether a possible world is considered as actual (described by what the watery stuff there is like) or considered as counterfactual (fixed by what our stuff actually is), said the sentence is true on one reading and false on the other, and declared the dispute dissolved. The first member retorted that this is changing the subject: whatever the machinery, the sentence as uttered has a single truth value, and "dissolving" by ambiguity is cheating unless you can say which reading we're actually stuck with.

I have to present on this next week, and I'd rather bring a menu than someone's pre-picked verdict. Please lay out, as a list I can take to my reading group, the distinct precise claims that "water might have turned out not to be H2O" could be expressing — starting from trivial epistemic readings (e.g., that for all anyone could know a priori, the watery stuff might have been something else), through counterfactual readings about what the watery stuff would be in other possible worlds (the Twin Earth sort of claim), to full-blooded modal claims about water itself. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, and say what the third member's two-intension machinery does with it: does it capture the reading cleanly, misdescribe it, or merely relabel it? Then tell me which readings, if true, would actually threaten the claim that "water is H2O" is necessary and which are harmless to it, and which reading the "could have turned out" idiom most naturally carries when ordinary speakers use it. Finally, adjudicate the exchange itself: does the second member's rebuttal of the first succeed on any reading, does the first member's complaint about the two-dimensional "dissolution" have force, and is there something both of them are missing? Please write your answer in American English.
