I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Ethics and Governance of Autonomous Systems," and I'm writing this year's take-home exam. I want one essay question on responsibility gaps — the phenomenon where an autonomous system causes harm but no agent seems to satisfy the usual conditions for bearing moral responsibility, because no individual foresaw the outcome, no individual controlled the system's behavior in the moment, and the causal history is distributed across many contributors. I do not want a question that can be answered by reciting the standard literature on the topic; the question should force students to apply the conceptual machinery to a concrete, unfamiliar proposal.

Please design the exam question around the following hypothetical statute, which you should present to students as part of the question text: a legislature responds to a string of accidents involving autonomous delivery vehicles by enacting a law under which (a) victims are compensated from a fund financed by a levy on all firms deploying such systems in the jurisdiction, and (b) when a court finds that no identifiable natural person possessed the foreseeability and control normally required for moral responsibility, the court must issue a formal finding that the harm was "morally unattributable," which bars public criticism of any individual engineer, manager, or executive in connection with the incident, though firms remain liable to the fund. The essay question should ask students to evaluate whether this statute dissolves the responsibility gap, merely re-describes it, or transforms it into something new — and to consider, among other things, whether the statute exploits what has been called the "moral crumple zone" by shifting blame toward or away from human operators, whether moral responsibility could intelligibly attach to collectives or to design decisions made years before the harm, and whether the bar on public criticism presupposes a contestable picture of what moral responsibility is for.

Alongside the question, please provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that takes a clear position rather than surveying views neutrally, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks total. The marking scheme should be organized around five specific moves a first-class answer must make — for example, distinguishing blame-liability compensation, or explaining why a finding of "moral unattributability" might itself change the moral landscape going forward — with the marks for each and a one-line note on the characteristic way answers fall short of each move. Where a move depends on a substantive interpretive choice (say, adopting a quality-of-will view versus a control view of responsibility), the marking scheme should credit either choice as long as the student applies it consistently and notices its costs.

Two constraints: do not structure the marking scheme as a generic rubric with bands like "excellent / good / adequate" — I want it to name the concrete philosophical content that separates a top answer from a merely competent one. And do not ask students to cite or attribute specific papers, since this is a closed-book exam and I'm testing understanding, not recall of the literature.
