I'm a third-year PhD student in political philosophy, and my department runs a year-long working group in which two faculty members — a committed liberal nationalist and an uncompromising cosmopolitan — have spent two semesters arguing about whether compatriots have special claims on one another. They have finally agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I've been tasked with drafting the document. Please write the joint statement the two of them could both sign.

Some context on where their dispute has gotten stuck, so the statement can be genuinely in the weeds rather than a bland restatement of the literature. The cosmopolitan holds that the arbitrariness of birthplace is on all fours with the arbitrariness of native talent — if luck-egalitarian reasoning constrains what people are entitled to claim on the basis of unchosen circumstances, national membership should be no exception. The nationalist's central move is institutionalist: the state is a coercively imposed cooperative scheme that demands sacrifice, compliance, and contribution, and only within such a scheme do duties of justice (as opposed to duties of humanitarian aid) arise. Over the year, three sub-disputes have crystallized out of this exchange, and I'd like the statement to treat each with real precision rather than lumping them together:

First, the family analogy. The nationalist leans on the thought that unchosen associations can ground real obligations — we do not think a parent wrongs strangers by preferring her child — while the cosmopolitan insists the analogy fails because states, unlike families, involve administrative exclusion backed by force.

Second, what I've privately started calling the levels problem, though they never use that term: if special obligations track shared coercive institutions, it is not obvious why the state is the right-sized unit. Rich regions within states, provinces, and even municipalities run cooperative schemes of their own; the nationalist owes us a principled stopping point, and the cosmopolitan has yet to show that the sliding argument stops at the globe rather than somewhere intermediate.

Third, the practical crux: whether compatriot priority is defensible in immigration and refugee policy specifically, or whether whatever special obligations exist are fully consistent with open (or nearly open) borders — the nationalist wants to say compatriot claims justify exclusion, the cosmopolitan wants to say they justify at most redistribution.

The deliverable should have three parts: a numbered section of claims both parties can sign, including about what they jointly take themselves to be disagreeing about and what they are not; a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; and, for each genuine crux, a concrete case, policy, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If any part of their dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive — I have begun to suspect the dispute over whether compatriot claims are claims of justice or of something else might be — please flag which part and why, and note how the collaboration should reword things so the parties are no longer trading blows on a question of labels. Please do not include a reading list; the two of them have read everything already.
