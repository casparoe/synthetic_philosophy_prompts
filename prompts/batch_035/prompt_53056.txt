I am a trustee of a mid-sized medical research charity, and our board must decide this Thursday whether to approve a fundraising appeal our director has organised. Its headline reads: "Every pound you give goes to research." Strictly speaking this is true: donations go into a restricted fund that pays only for research, while our running costs are met from a founder's endowment. But money is fungible — every donated pound that "goes to research" frees an endowment pound to cover running costs, which come to about a third of our total income — and the appeal is plainly written so that donors will conclude our running costs are nil. Two trustees say the statement is a lie in all but name, since its entire function is to plant a false belief; two reply that it is not a lie at all, because it is true, and that at most it is misleading, which is standard practice in charity appeals and not something our policy forbids. I have to write the memo the board will vote on. Please draft it: recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and ground the reasoning in the real philosophical divide here — whether the wrong, if any, consists in asserting what one believes false, as the first group's objection assumes, or in deliberately engineering the reader's inference while keeping every sentence true, as the second group's defence assumes. Address whether that distinction should govern our policy even if it marks a genuine moral difference. Please also take seriously the strongest point on whichever side loses — which, for at least one side, will be that a technically true statement engineered to be misread is in some respects worse than an open lie, because it profits from the donor's trust in the very practice of truthful assertion. The memo should be a document the board can act on, with a clear bottom line, and please write it in American English.
