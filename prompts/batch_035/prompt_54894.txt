My buddy Elena is a neuroscience postdoc and free will is basically her party trick -- every barbeque, somebody brings it up and she hands down verdicts like a judge. I've actually started writing them down. Verdict 1: the old Libet experiment where people flick their wrist whenever and the brain's readiness potential starts a third of a second before they report feeling the urge -- she says no free will there, that's just your brain running you. Verdict 2: when somebody brings up that Libet himself thought the conscious veto saves it, she waves it off, says the veto is also just brain activity you didn't consciously start, so no rescue there either. Verdict 3: some follow-up study where a brain scanner could guess which of two buttons you'd press a few seconds before you felt like you decided -- same verdict, if anything more confident. Verdict 4 is the one that got me: there's work arguing the readiness potential isn't really a decision forming early, it's background noise that occasionally drifts over a threshold, and crossing it triggers the move. I figured she'd soften on that one, since her whole thing was "the decision happens before the awareness." Nope -- she said the noise version is if anything worse, you're a geiger counter, not an author. And once, on the thing where stimulating a spot in the brain makes people feel an urge to move and they swear it was their own idea, she took that as more of the same: introspection can't tell the differance, so it counts for nothing.

Here's why I'm writing. Three new cases came up at the last barbeque and she went quiet on them (her kid needed the bathroom, then we all got distracted), and I want to know what she'll say before the next one. Case A: those brain-computer interfaces where paralyzed people type by thinking of letters, except the decoder is fast enough that it locks in each letter slightly before the person says they intended it -- but there's a working cancel button, and people use it all the time to stop wrong letters before they go through. Case B: slow decisions, like me taking three months and two spreadsheets to pick between two job offers -- no single flick moment anywhere, the deliberation is conscious the whole way through. Case C: a hypothetical somebody floated, where you deliberately inject noise into the motor system so the outcome is genuinely unpredictable from any prior state -- the person does something they didn't plan and says afterwards it felt impulsive, not chosen.

So: what will her verdicts be on A, B, and C? More usefully, what rule is she actually running? I can see a few that fit everything so far: "no free will unless you consciously start the action," or "no free will if the choice is predictable from brain states before you're aware of it," or my own pet theory, which is that she's just a flat determinist and Libet is decoration, and literally no experiment could come out in a way that changes her answer. Give me your best guess per case with reasons, tell me which of the rules that fit her track record actually diverge on A, B, and C -- or whether they all give the same answer everywhere, which would be its own kind of finding -- and design the one extra case that would most cleanly split the rules apart, so I can spring it on her at the next barbeque.
