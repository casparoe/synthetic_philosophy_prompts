I'm a novelist, and my third book is being translated into five languages, none of which I read. My translators send queries weekly — this character's surname is an anagram of the word for her central delusion, this gesture means something unfortunate in the target culture, readers there have never encountered the institution my opening chapter satirizes — and so far I have handled each one ad hoc: a footnote here, a glossary entry there, a quiet cut the third time. This is becoming untenable, and I want a system instead of a temperament. Some years ago I read Gödel, Escher, Bach, and what has stayed with me is the chapter arguing that a message carries three distinguishable layers: a frame message, which tells a receiver that there is something here to be decoded at all; an outer message, the implicit set of instructions for how to decode it; and an inner message, which is the thing I thought I was writing all along. I'd like this turned into a procedure I can run on each incoming query from a translator: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure issues a verdict (annotate, rewrite, leave alone, or refer the matter back to the translator because it is a matter of craft rather than of meaning), and where it admits it cannot decide and hands the question to me. My constraints are these: I can verify nothing in the target language, so the procedure must tell me what can be decided from the English side alone; my publisher's editors want a written record of every decision and its rationale; and each additional revision round costs us roughly a month, so the procedure should tell me when the cheap fix is a real fix and when it is a dodge. One worry I'd like the procedure to address rather than dodge: I don't see how a footnote can ever tell a reader how to read, since the footnote itself must be read, and presumably in the same spirit as the thing it explains; if that suspicion is right, much of what I've been doing for a year is motion rather than progress, and I'd rather know. Please then run the procedure on three live cases. First, the surname that anagrams into the word for the narrator's delusion, where a second character's noticing of the anagram carries a plot turn. Second, the scene whose emotional weight rests on a schoolyard rhyme — the meter and the plodding, institutional dullness of it are the point, while the actual words are nearly incidental. Third, and here I suspect I am asking for the answer I will like least: my first chapter is written in a deliberately flat, administrative register that I intended as a signal to readers about what kind of novel this is, and one translator reports that in her language it reads not as a signal but simply as bad prose. If the procedure tells me that chapter has to be re-signaled per language, or rewritten outright, say so plainly. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter to a colleague whose time one respects — and close with what the procedure cannot do, said honestly.
