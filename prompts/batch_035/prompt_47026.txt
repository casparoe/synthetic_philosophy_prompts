My brother-in-law (huge econ blog guy, thinks he's Walter White of finance) was over for dinner last week and basically lectured me the whole evening, and now I can't stop thinking about whether he's right.

Some context: I've been saving for a house down payment and recently sold a chunk of my tech ETFs and moved the money into short-term bonds, because I'll need the cash in about two years and I don't want a bad market year right before I need it. My brother-in-law says this makes me irrational, or actually that it makes me one of the "suckers" the theorem protects you from being.

Here's roughly how he put it (I wrote it down after because he talks fast): "Decades ago economists proved that if two people are rational, start with the same beliefs, and each knows the other is rational, then they can never trade. For a trade to happen, both sides have to expect to gain, and if you know the other side — who is rational — is still willing to trade with you, that fact alone is information that you were wrong to want the trade in the first place. So the willingness itself kills the trade. This is also why rational people can never disagree: they keep updating until they converge. Bottom line: any trade that actually happens shows at least one side is irrational, and whenever you buy or sell a stock you should assume the person on the other side knows something you don't. So the rational move is never to trade at all, which is why index funds beat everyone and why your rebalancing is you thinking you know better than the market."

Part of me wants to just say he's full of it, but honestly it sounds like he's describing real results, just maybe stretching them? The thing that bugs me is my reason for selling has nothing to do with thinking I know something the buyer doesn't — I just need the money soon and can't afford a 30% drop. He says "a trade is a trade," if I'm selling someone's buying, and per the theorem that means one of us is being dumb. Is that actually what the theorem says? Can you go through his little speech and tell me which parts a careful economist would nod along to, which parts are the real result stretched past what it says, and whether his bottom line — that I'm irrational for rebalancing — actually follows from the accurate parts? Also he claimed this is why markets can't really aggregate information, which seems weird to me since prices obviously move on news, but I don't know enough to say where he goes wrong there either.
