A book I've been reading on twentieth-century ethics condenses Ayn Rand's position on altruism into a paragraph I would like checked. As the author presents it, Rand used altruism in its everyday sense, as meaning any concern for the interests of others, and attacked that concern wholesale; her ideal of the selfish man was one who follows his own desires and feelings wherever they lead, unburdened by guilt; on her view giving to charity is always wrong, since any transfer of value to another person treats one's own life as less important than theirs; her real quarrel was with forced redistribution — with the welfare state — rather than with a moral doctrine about how individuals should live; and she took herself to have refuted altruism empirically, by observing that its most consistent practitioners throughout history have been totalitarians. The author concludes that Rand's ethics, so understood, leaves no room for ordinary decency.

I am inclined to think Rand's view is largely wrong, but I also suspect this rendering is uncharitable in places — I recall that she somewhere insisted she was not against helping others as such, only against treating it as a duty. Please go through the five numbered claims above in order, in a numbered list of exactly five items, each item one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each claim, say whether a careful expositor of Rand would accept it as accurate, and if not, what she actually held and precisely where the distortion lies. Where the author's concluding remark about ordinary decency depends on a distortion, note that within the relevant item rather than in any closing remarks.
