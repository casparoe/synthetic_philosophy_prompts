A genuinely uncomfortable argument broke out at my weekly drinks thing, and I — someone who covers science and ideas for a living — could not referee it. Maybe you can.

Background: a friend of mine, also a journalist, wrote a piece a few years back that he'd half forgotten. Someone dredged it up online and accused him of basically being cruel to his subject. Instead of rereading the piece himself, he sent it to a friend whose moral judgment he trusts completely, laid out the situation, and asked point blank: was what I did wrong? She read everything and said yes, genuinely wrong, you should apologize publicly and mean it. So he said okay, and did — still without ever rereading his own article.

At the bar, Marcus said (1) this was totally fine, arguably admirable. We defer to trusted judgment all the time — doctors, plumbers, wine critics. His friend knows him, knows the norms of his profession, and has a great track record. Refusing to defer would be a kind of vanity, like diagnosing yourself because you want the authentic experience of illness.

Dana said (2) no — moral judgment is different in kind. If a doctor tells you to take a pill and you take it, nothing's gone wrong; if a friend tells you an apology is owed and you apologize on her say-so, something creepy has happened. Part of being a morally grown-up adult is that your apologies are *yours*. Outsourcing it is like having someone else write your love letters.

Marcus came back (3) that Dana is running two things together: believing on testimony versus acting on testimony. He'd grant you shouldn't believe moral claims on just anyone's word — but he now genuinely believes his piece was cruel, on excellent evidence, namely a trusted friend who actually read it. Given that belief, apologizing is the obvious move. So where exactly is the creepiness?

Dana answered (4) that the creepiness is right there: he formed the belief on her word and never even rereading the thing he's apologizing for. He's apologizing for wronging someone without ever confronting the wrong himself. And she said: picture someone who does this their whole life — defers to the same saintly friend on every moral question, always ends up doing the right thing, never once reasons about right and wrong themselves. Perfect behavior, and yet that person is missing something essential.

Marcus's last word (5) was that we all outsource chunks of our moral lives and nobody flinches: people adopt their parents' politics wholesale, follow their union's voting recommendations, take their therapist's word on whether to cut off a relative. Either all of that is creepy or none of it is, and Dana can't say where the line falls.

They both turned to me expecting a verdict and I had nothing. Please go through their exchange move by move — who's right at each of the five steps, not just who comes out ahead overall — and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
