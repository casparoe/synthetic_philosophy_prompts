Before I take sides in the argument between the deflationary view of thought experiments — John Norton's claim that they are nothing over and above picturesque argumentation — and the Platonist alternative associated with James Robert Brown, I want to fix the standard of judgment. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of how scientific thought experiments yield knowledge must deliver, with the motivating case behind each requirement, a judgment as to which are non-negotiable and which are merely desiderata of particular camps, and a note on which pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

The list should be built from, at least, the following hard cases and contrasts; you are welcome to add your own, provided the cases are ones the theory must actually face rather than illustrations of one camp's hobbyhorse:

1. The novelty problem. Galileo's falling bodies appear to teach something new about the world with no new observations — his chained unequal weights, which must fall neither faster than the heavy one alone (else the light one drags) nor slower (else the pair as a whole is a heavier body), so the speed cannot depend on weight. Any adequate theory must say where the knowledge of the world comes from, and it cannot be from the armchair reasoning alone, on pain of making physics a priori. Make this the anchor case.

2. Success and failure in the same medium. Thought experiments demonstrably mislead. Einstein's photon box was intended to defeat the time–energy uncertainty relation and was instead refuted by Bohr, using general relativity's own redshift of clocks in gravitational potentials. Maxwell's demon initially appeared to threaten the second law of thermodynamics and was eventually tamed only by bringing entropy-bearing background theory to bear. A theory that explains only the triumphs is not a theory of the method but of its survivors.

3. The compression contrast. Stevin's wreath of spheres on the inclined plane — the chain of balls draped over a triangular prism, which cannot rotate or it would circulate forever, so the loads on the balanced sides must be proportional to the lengths along the planes — seems, unlike Galileo's case, to be reconstructible as a fully explicit argument with no epistemic residue. If some thought experiments are compressible into argument and others are not, say whether this marks a real epistemological division or merely a difference of expository style, and what the theory owes us for each kind.

4. Revision across generations. Newton's bucket convinced nearly everyone of absolute rotation and absolute space; Mach's later reanalysis recast the very same scenario as evidence only for motion relative to distant masses, and Einstein's elevator flattened the distinction between gravitation and acceleration for good. The same scenario, different verdicts. The theory must explain how a thought experiment's import can be reopened by theory change, and what it was doing in the meantime.

5. Idealization. Thought experiments traffic in frictionless planes, infinitely rigid rods, perfectly sealed boxes, and instantaneous signals; they purport to teach us about a world containing none of these. Say what licenses the extrapolation and where it fails — or admit that it sometimes does not, as with the special relativistic ban on rigid bodies that quietly undermines some classical scenarios.

6. Overgeneralization. Philosophers' thought experiments — the violinist, the trolley, Mary's room — seem to run on the same machinery but under different norms of refutation. A theory of scientific thought experiments owes an account of whether the method is unified or disjunctive, and if disjunctive, where the line falls.

Additionally: flag explicitly which requirements are motivated by the nature of the case and which by a prior epistemological commitment to empiricism, Platonism, or naturalism — I want to be able to see which desiderata are doing real work and which merely encode a verdict about the Norton–Brown dispute reached in advance. Then, and only briefly, say how the argument view, the Platonist view, and the mental-simulation view each fare against your list, and which single requirement is fatal to which.

Please answer in American English, in a reasonably formal register. I am far more interested in the requirements and their motivating cases than in the final scorecard, so weight your answer accordingly.
