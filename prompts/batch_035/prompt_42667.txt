I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy background who got into stoicism through the internet, and I keep choking on the "dichotomy of control" whenever I try to actually use it. The idea that some things are up to me and some aren't sounds clean until I'm in an incident review where the outage was 40% my code, 30% an upstream dependency, and 30% the on-call who didn't read the alerts. Basically nothing at work is fully in or fully out of my control, so the dichotomy never fires. Also, the version I mostly see online splits things into three buckets (fully in my control, partially in, not at all), and I have a feeling that's a self-help addition rather than what Epictetus actually had in mind -- if the original handles partial-control cases differently, I want the procedure grounded in that, not the watered down version, but explained in normal person language, no Greek without a translation.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run in about two minutes when something stressful happens at work: what to ask myself, in what order, what counts as an answer, and where it stops being procedure and starts being judgment I can't outsource. It has to work mid-incident, not just in calm reflection afterwards, and it should say what to do about the middle bucket -- the stuff I can influence but not control -- instead of just labeling it. Then run it on three cases: (1) a postmortem where my bad deploy caused the outage, (2) a reorg that puts my project on ice, decisions all above me, and (3) my name coming up for promotion where my manager likes me but the calibration committee is a black box. Make sure at least one case gets an answer I won't like.

Also please tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire -- e.g. does it quietly become "stop caring about stuff" when I run it repeatedly -- and what would change your verdicts: is there a reading of Epictetus (or a known objection to this whole way of operationalizing him) under which the procedure is doing the wrong thing entirely? Answer in American English, casual is fine.
