Write a dialogue between two characters debating whether weakness of will is just a kind of time preference.

Some background: I'm a high school student working on an extended essay about akrasia, and I've gotten stuck on a dispute I found between a psychology-influenced view and a more traditional philosophical view. The first character should defend the idea that what the ancients called weakness of will is really just hyperbolic discounting — the empirical finding that people devalue rewards steeply as a function of delay, so that smaller-sooner rewards can beat larger-later ones even when the agent "knows better." On this view, akrasia is not a distinct failure at all; it's just preference reversal caused by discount curves that cross: from far away you prefer the larger-later reward, but up close the smaller-sooner one dominates, and there is no mysterious extra step where judgment is "overpowered." The character might even suggest that willpower, on this picture, is a bookkeeping trick — bundling many similar choices together so that each individual lapse looks costly enough to keep the whole bundle intact.

The second character should push back that discounting cannot be the whole story, because akrasia as classically understood involves acting against one's better judgment at the very moment of action, not merely having preferences that change over time. The second character can appeal to the distinction between an unconditional judgment (this is what I should do, full stop) and a judgment about which option is best all things considered given my current desires, and argue that only the former is the kind of thing weakness of will can betray. They can also point to cases that discounting seems unable to explain neatly, such as feeling compelled by a temptation one does not even want (enjoying the temptation not at all while giving in), or regretting the lapse before, during, and after it — which suggests a stable judgment that was never reversed.

Both characters should be equally competent and should genuinely engage with each other's points, not talk past them. Neither should be a strawman, and the dialogue should not end with an artificial truce — let them reach a real, substantive point of disagreement about whether "irrationality" here means anything beyond shifting preferences.

Title the dialogue, and at the very end, on a final line beginning "Verdict:", state in a short phrase which character you think made the stronger case (e.g., "Verdict: the discounting theorist" or "Verdict: the better-judgment defender").
