It's finals season, and I'm dreading it, because for the last few years the pile of essays on the leveling-down objection from my upper-level distributive justice seminar has been more or less identical: recite the telic/deontic egalitarianism distinction from Parfit's "Equality or Priority?" lecture, announce that telic egalitarianism is sunk, stop. I want to write one essay question this year that they can't answer by naming the views, and I have a specific angle in mind.

Here's my hunch, which a colleague accused me — over coffee, half-joking but only half — of building my hobbyhorse into the exam. Students constantly run together two claims: (a) that there's something impersonally good about pure leveling down, i.e. lowering the better-off while helping nobody, which is what actually sinks telic egalitarianism; and (b) that it's objectionable to let someone's relative standing do moral work at all. Standard prioritarianism escapes (a): on the concave-transform picture, lowering anyone's welfare never makes the outcome better, so there's no leveling-down good for the prioritarian to awkwardly affirm. But (b) is where the priority view gets caught anyway — the Otsuka/Voorhoeve point about how a prioritarian must prefer a tiny benefit to the much worse off over a huge benefit to someone slightly better off, which smells like the same complaint in different clothes. So the real question isn't whether the leveling-down objection refutes prioritarianism (answer: no, not as standardly stated) but whether the prioritarian's escape is genuine or whether the objection just changed shape and followed it out the door.

So, three deliverables. First, the essay question itself. It should set up a concrete pair of cases — one pure leveling-down case on which the egalitarian and the prioritarian give different verdicts, and one case where they agree — and then ask whether prioritarianism survives the objection as stated only by walking straight into its cousin. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 words. Third, a marking scheme worth 20 marks total, built on five must-hit points, where for each point you say what an A answer does that a B answer doesn't. I specifically want the top answers to actually work through the value functions informally — what happens to the sum when you lower one term, why concavity alone doesn't get you the verdict students keep attributing to it — since I've designed the two cases so that you can't really construct them, or see why they diverge, without doing that arithmetic. And keep the question and model answer free of any literature beyond Parfit's lecture and the Otsuka/Voorhoeve point: it's a closed-book exam, and I don't want the marking scheme to secretly reward citation memory. Also, if you could note for each must-hit point the one-line way candidates typically botch it, that would save me a lot of time at the grading table.
