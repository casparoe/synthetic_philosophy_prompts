I'm a high school senior taking a philosophy elective, and we've just spent two weeks on the surprise examination paradox, which I found by far the most disorienting thing we studied all year. Our teacher told us to imagine a class where the teacher announces: there will be an exam one day next week, Monday through Friday, and on the morning of the exam it will be a surprise — you will not be able to deduce in advance which day it's on. One student then reasons: it can't be Friday, because if we got through Thursday with no exam, we'd know Thursday evening that it had to be Friday, so it wouldn't be a surprise. But once Friday is out, it can't be Thursday either, because by Wednesday evening we'd know — and so on backward until every day is eliminated and the student concludes the announcement is incoherent and relaxes. Then the teacher gives the exam on, say, Wednesday, and it is a genuine surprise. For our final we're allowed to submit a creative project instead of an essay, and I'd like to write a sestina about it.

Here's the design I've been sketching, and I'd like you to write it within these constraints. The six end-words should be Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, and surprise. I want the reasoning itself to be carried by the form rather than just described: the middle stanzas should enact the student's backward elimination in reverse chronological order — the Friday stanza first, then Thursday, and so on — so that someone who knows the paradox could reconstruct the deduction from the pattern of the end-words alone. But I've noticed something about the sestina that seems to me exactly right for this: the form is constitutionally unable to delete an end-word. Friday is "ruled out" in the first reasoning stanza and then compulsively returns at the end of some line in every subsequent stanza, which is precisely the gap in the student's argument — the deduction removes Friday from the space of possibilities but not from the world. I'd like the poem to exploit that rather than fight it.

The envoi should be where the exam actually happens. Since the envoi conventionally buries some of the six words mid-line while ending its lines on the others, I want the day the exam falls on to be one of the mid-line words — arriving where it isn't structurally expected, the way Wednesday arrives where the argument said no day could. The poem should be narrated by the student, whose tone can start out smug and should end somewhere between grudging admiration and dread, since what's been proven is that their logic is airtight and also useless. And two final quality checks: someone who teaches this paradox should read the poem and find that the structure genuinely tracks the argument rather than name-dropping it, and someone who's never heard of it should still get a good poem about an overconfident student and a teacher who keeps her word.
