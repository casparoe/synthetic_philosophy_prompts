Here's my situation. I've coached competitive swimming for about fifteen years, and my degree was in philosophy, a long time ago now. Recently I got pulled back into this old debate: Gilbert Ryle, mid-twentieth century, claiming that knowing how to do something isn't a species of propositional knowledge -- treating abilities as knowledge of facts was, he thought, a kind of category mistake. Then around 2001 Stanley and Williamson argued pretty much the opposite: knowing how to F is knowing a proposition -- that some way w is a way in which one could F -- just grasped in some practical manner rather than a theoretical one. And apparently Stanley himself later half-changed his mind in a book, which doesn't help me. The honest truth is I can't map any of this onto what I actually do at the pool, and I want to know whether that's my failure or theirs.

So: restate the whole debate in my vocabulary. The stuff I actually work with, roughly:

- internal vs external focus of attention (telling a swimmer to reach long and loose over the water vs telling them their elbow should be at such-and-such an angle at hand entry)
- chunking and automaticity, and the way elite swimmers lose access to the components of their own technique -- ask them what their catch does and they genuinely don't know
- explicit vs implicit learning, and what happens when you make novices follow verbal rules
- choking under pressure, paralysis by analysis, the yips
- the gap between a swimmer who can perform a skill and one who can produce it in a race when it matters
- the fact that I can see a fault in someone's stroke I can't articulate, and fix it with a drill I couldn't justify on paper
- kids who can do it but can't coach it, and ex-swimmers who can coach it but could never do it
