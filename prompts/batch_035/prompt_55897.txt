You are a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and the mathematics of infinity, and you are used to diagnosing arguments rather than just judging their conclusions. Before answering, think carefully and do some quiet brainstorming about where the pressure points are; then commit to a diagnosis and defend it.

I am a high school senior. This year I got excited about two unrelated things at once: effective altruism, which I heard about on a podcast, and Cantor's theory of infinite cardinal numbers, which I found in a library book. I put them together and produced the argument below. My teacher says it seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the exact defect. Here is the argument:

(1) Morality, at bottom, is about how our actions affect the welfare of everyone.
(2) To compare two available acts, total the welfare each would produce across everyone affected, and choose the act with the larger total.
(3) For all contemporary physics can tell us, the future may contain infinitely many people: nothing rules out that time continues forever with people in it.
(4) If the future is infinite, then whatever I do, the total is one and the same countably infinite cardinal: adding any finite amount — saving a life, ending one, giving away a fortune or burning it — leaves the cardinal untouched, since a countable infinity plus any finite number is still just a countable infinity.
(5) So for all we know, every act available to me yields exactly the same total, and no act is better than any other.
(6) If no act is better than any other, then nothing I do matters morally: I might as well not donate, not vote, and not pull the drowning child from the pond.
(7) But some acts plainly do matter. So consequentialism — or at least step (2) — is false.

What makes me suspicious is not only the conclusion but how far the reasoning travels. The same steps seem to show that a doctor shouldn't bother saving patients, that reducing extinction risk is pointless, and even that my friends who reject consequentialism are in trouble, since any view that cares at all about how the future goes must apparently compare outcomes somehow, and if (4) and (5) are right, every such comparison comes out a tie. An argument that empties every theory that weighs the future, not just one of them, looks like it proves too much.

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I will be honest about my attachments: I would most like to keep (1) and (7). My friends all point at (2), but I have also read that infinities behave strangely in ethics in ways people don't anticipate, so I have a hunch the trouble is hidden somewhere between (4) and (5) — some quiet change in what "total" means — and I want it named precisely if it is there. If instead the argument is valid and I really must give up a premise, lay out the candidates, say which is least expensive to abandon, and explain why. Walk me through your reasoning rather than just announcing a verdict.
