I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading about conditionals, and I can't shake the feeling that natural-language "if" is doing something my guard clauses never have to do. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, both first-rate, neither a strawman, each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other.

Callum holds a disunified view, in the tradition of Adams and Edgington: indicative conditionals have no truth conditions at all; they are assertible to the degree to which one would accept the consequent upon supposing the antecedent. Counterfactuals, by contrast, do have truth values, determined by which alternative histories are closest to actuality.

Ines holds a unified view, in the tradition of Stalnaker: both moods receive the same kind of semantics, in which an if-clause restricts a modal or selection operator, and the felt difference between the moods comes only from the ordering of worlds — for indicatives, the closest worlds are those compatible with what we know; for counterfactuals, they are the worlds that deviate as little as possible from the actual causal facts.

Have them work through the following test suite, with each case genuinely argued through rather than mentioned in passing:

1. "If Shakespeare didn't write Hamlet, someone else did" sounds fine, while "If Shakespeare hadn't written Hamlet, someone else would have" sounds wrong. Ines's best move is presumably that the closest worlds in which Shakespeare didn't write the play are worlds of misattributed authorship, in which someone else did — making the counterfactual true and its wrongness a pragmatic effect. Callum predicts falsity, since the closest alternative histories are ones where the play simply goes unwritten. Whose explanation of the contrast holds up better, and is either move ad hoc?

2. An open-future pair about the very same event: "If it rains tomorrow, the match will be cancelled" versus "If it were to rain tomorrow, the match would be cancelled." Since both concern the same future rain-worlds, Ines's two orderings threaten to collapse into one; yet the would-version somehow suggests the speaker takes rain less seriously. Callum, meanwhile, must say what probability he is even reporting. Who strains more here?

3. A three-step sequence, run first in the counterfactual mood, then in the indicative mood: "If you had struck the match, it would have lit" — "True, but if you had struck it and it had been soaked, it wouldn't have lit" — "Still, if you had struck the match, it would have lit." The final reassertion sounds acceptable. Run the same sequence indicatively ("If you strike it, it will light" / "But if you strike it while it's soaked, it won't" / "If you strike it, it will light"), and the final reassertion now sounds shifty. If this asymmetry is real, it favors Callum, on whose picture indicative judgments update like probabilities while Ines needs contextual reordering of worlds; make sure Ines gets her best reply.

End the dialogue with each character naming, concretely, the kind of case or observation that would make them abandon their view. No forced consensus, and no cheap victory for either side.
