Design a 90-minute activity for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar (16 students, one session, no prior reading on the topic) that makes the extended mind thesis felt rather than merely stated. The activity should stage something like a cognitive offloading task: for example, half the participants carry notebooks, phones, or index cards that they are required to use for all task-relevant information, while the other half must rely on unaided memory, with the two groups then facing a common challenge that stresses their information stores. Midway, introduce a disruption — confiscating or corrupting the external aids — arranged so that participants naturally describe what happened in terms like losing a faculty, not losing a tool; the rules should do the work of generating that reaction, not the facilitator. Then a debrief that maps what participants experienced onto the standard conditions normally appealed to in this debate (constant availability, easy access, automatic trust, past endorsement), with participants themselves deciding, criterion by criterion, whether their aids met them — and hence whether the disruption counts on reflection as an intervention on the mind or merely the loss of a convenient prop. Please include rules and materials, a timeline, a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (including the likely result that the offloading group finds the aids not yet transparent enough to count as cognitive), and an honest statement of which feature of the thesis the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion — presumably the temporal dimension, the way trust in an external store is built over years rather than minutes. At the end, note how confident you are that the activity produces the intended reaction rather than merely an amusing demonstration, and identify the step in the design you are least sure will work as intended.
