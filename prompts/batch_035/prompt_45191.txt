My ethics seminar spent three weeks on Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" — the case where you can save David, or save the five strangers, but not both — and ended, as these discussions usually do, with the coin flip. Our study group then tried to extract what we took to be the core of his reasoning and extend it beyond the one-versus-five case, and the extension now strikes all of us as proving far too much. Here is the argument we constructed:

1. A person's claim on you to save her is grounded in the loss she would suffer if you did not — and nothing about that loss is made greater by the fact that others would suffer the same loss; no one is worse off merely because others also die.
2. In the choice between David and the five, each of the six would suffer the same loss — the loss of his or her life — so no one's claim is stronger than anyone else's.
3. Where claims conflict and none is stronger, the bare fact that more people stand behind one option cannot itself make that option the one morality favors; to let it do so is to "count the numbers," treating several persons' claims as if they merged into one louder claim.
4. None of this depends on the persons being present, identified, and facing me in a single dramatic moment; it concerns only how individual claims are grounded, and so it applies wherever each person at stake stands to lose the same thing.
5. Nearly every life-affecting decision — triage, allocating scarce vaccines or ventilators, funding one safety program rather than another, setting a pollution standard — is a decision among options under which each person at stake stands to lose the same thing: her life, or the same injury.
6. So in nearly all such decisions, the number of lives at stake on either side cannot itself determine which option morality favors; the numbers have no moral weight, only whatever else discriminates between the options does.
7. And where nothing else discriminates, preferring the option that saves more is counting the numbers, which is exactly what (3) forbids — so preferring it is no better justified, morally, than preferring the option that saves fewer.
8. Therefore a health ministry that prefers a program projected to prevent a thousand deaths to an otherwise identical program projected to prevent one death is giving weight to something morally irrelevant; its preference is on the same moral footing as the reverse preference would be.

I cannot accept (8), but I also cannot see where the argument goes wrong, and I've stared at it long enough that every premise still looks individually defensible. I half suspect the trouble is in the quiet expansion between (4) and (5) — something seems to change when we move from a choice I make about particular persons to a rule an agency makes about statistical lives — but I can't articulate what, or whether that distinction can bear the weight. Tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked fine to us, and what rejecting it costs: does the cost land only at the level of policy, or does it also infect Taurek's original verdict about the coin flip? For my part, I'd most like to keep (1) together with the coin flip in the one-versus-five case; if you think the argument is actually valid and the honest move is to bite the bullet on (8), say so and defend that, but then explain what a ministry official is supposed to say when asked why she chose the thousand-death program.
