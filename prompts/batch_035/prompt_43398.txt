I'm taking a seminar on Kant's ethics and writing my term paper on the formula of universal law, and I've gotten myself stuck. My friend (a committed consequentialist, very smug about it) claims the universalizability test is a parlor trick: any maxim can be gerrymandered until it passes, and the ones that fail only fail because you described them tendentiously. I don't buy that, but when I try to spell out my own view I keep going back and forth on cases, and I want to figure out if there's a coherent principle underneath my gut or whether my friend is half right. So here's what I want: you're a Kant scholar, think carefully before answering (maybe brainstorm a couple candidate principles first and then test them), and tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts.

The verdicts. (1) Promising to repay a loan you know you can't repay, just to get the cash: clearly wrong, and I'm fine saying that's because a world of universalized false promising is somehow self-undermining. (2) Letting your natural talents rust -- you can play piano well but just never bother -- I also think that's wrong, but it doesn't feel self-undermining the way false promising does; a world of lazy people is sad but coherent. Someone in seminar kept pushing a distinction between a maxim being literally impossible to universalize vs. it being universalizable but irrational to will, and my hunch is that distinction is doing real work here, but I don't fully get it. (3) Making false promises only to strangers in cities you'll never visit: my gut says this is exactly as wrong as (1), but universalizing it doesn't seem to break anything -- the practice of promising where I live is untouched. (4) Accepting help from others whenever you need it but never helping anyone: wrong. (5) Refusing to ever accept help AND never offering it -- total self-reliance: I actually think that's permissible, even though it's like (4) with the hypocrisy removed, and I can't explain why removing the hypocrisy makes it fine. (6) A maxim with the cheating built into the description -- say, making a false promise only when you're exactly $43 short on a Tuesday -- seems to slide through universalization, but I think it's as wrong as plain false promising.

So: is there one principle that gets all or almost all of these? Does the two-kinds-of-contradiction thing I mentioned in (2) actually sort the cases, and where does the maxim-individuation problem (3) and (6) fit in -- is my friend right that it sinks the whole test, or is there a principled way to describe maxims that doesn't just bake the answer in? Also tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't made up my mind on: parking in the accessible spot at 3am in a completely empty lot, and keeping a promise to someone you've since learned is a serial promise-breaker (with a third condition if you can't: be honest about which of my verdicts above can't be saved, and which one is just a disguised version of another). Don't be wishy washy -- if my (5) is secretly indefensible, say so. im trying to have a defensible paper, not a comfortable one.
