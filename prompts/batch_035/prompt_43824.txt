Three weeks ago my oncologist read my scan and declined to say which of two kinds of disease I have; over the coming months it will declare itself. The two regimens on the table — an aggressive one with a punishing recovery, or watchful waiting — do not, as far as anyone can tell, change the outlook within either kind. She offered one further frank observation: the patients who insist on the aggressive regimen turn out, disproportionately, to be the ones with the aggressive kind, presumably because the disease itself breeds urgency. So my pull toward the aggressive choice is, I cannot help noticing, evidence about the very thing I am choosing in ignorance of.

My brother-in-law, a retired actuary, has presided over our Sunday dinners long enough to have become the family's standing referee on puzzles of judgment under uncertainty, and since my diagnosis we have taken to posing him hypotheticals to learn where he comes down. His rulings so far, each case stated as it was put to him:

The tea. A bitter tea that does nothing at all to the body; still, the people who drink it are mostly the healthy ones, because the constitution that makes a person healthy also gives them the taste for it. His ruling: drink it and take the pleasure; the news will take care of itself.

The man who pays for greed. An old man who predicts choices almost perfectly places a million dollars in an envelope for you if and only if he predicted you would take that envelope together with a second, sealed one holding a guaranteed thousand. His ruling: take both — the million is in the envelope already or it is not, and the thousand is mine regardless.

The notarized version. The same envelopes, except that an auditor certifies in advance that the million is inside. His ruling: take both.

Next Sunday someone means to put to him the case decision theorists call Death in Damascus, and I would like to predict his ruling before he gives it. As it will be put to him: he is in Damascus, planning to stay the night and finish his work there, as he always does; his habits are settled and known. Death appears and tells him that tomorrow at noon he has an appointment with him in whichever city he is then standing in; Death predicted long ago which city that would be, stationed himself accordingly, has never once been wrong, and will not disclose the city. Staying in Damascus is comfortable; the ride to Aleppo is punishing, though there is a friend there; both matter a little, and dying rather more. A bookie who has overheard the whole exchange offers, for one dollar, a ticket paying eleven dollars if he is alive a week later. In the story the man rides, and dies in Aleppo; the appointment had been in Aleppo from the start, and it was the fleeing that carried him there.

What will he rule — on the city, and on the ticket? I care less about the answers than about the rule he is running. I can see at least two that fit every ruling he has given so far: one on which only what a choice brings about counts, so that he would never let what his choosing tells him weigh against it; and one on which the news a choice carries counts in its own right. I suspect a third, which treats his own deliberation as precisely the thing Death has already modeled. Lay out the rules his record fits, say what each predicts for Damascus and for the ticket, make the best guess you can and argue for it, and tell me which single further hypothetical would settle, before Sunday, which rule he is running. Then, if you can, say whether my own choice is the same shape as the man's — no one predicted me; I am simply the kind of person my kind of tumor makes — and which way his rule cuts for me, or whether my case is a different shape altogether.
