Please describe the most important implications of Parfit's hitchhiker for how an ordinary person should design their own commitments. In the case, a stranded traveler gets a ride out of the desert only after promising to pay the driver on arrival in town; once there, keeping the promise is no longer in the traveler's interest, so a perfectly rational and transparent traveler never gets the ride at all. The lesson usually drawn is a kind of rational irrationality: being disposed, here and now, to act against your interests later can be what makes the present situation go well. What I want are the practical implications of that lesson for everyday commitment devices — non-refundable deposits, publicly announced deadlines, automatic transfers to savings, deleting an app rather than resolving to use it less, telling a counterparty that you never renegotiate. For each implication, state what it follows from in the case, and distinguish which implications depend on others being able to observe or predict one's disposition, and which hold even when the binding is entirely private. Note also that the hitchhiker's problem is not temptation but the shift in what is rational once the ride is secured; so tell me where the implications for self-binding against weakness of will end and the implications for binding against a foreseeable change in incentives begin — or say whether the case supports both equally, since self-help advice tends to run them together. Finally, flag any popular takeaway from the case that you think is wrong or overextended, for example that it generally pays to make oneself visibly unable to reconsider, rather than inflating the list of implications.
