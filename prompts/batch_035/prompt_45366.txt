I teach philosophy of science to a mixed crowd (18 students, mostly sophomores, one 75-minute session) and I'm fed up with how the Goodman week goes on the page -- everyone nods at grue, nobody feels why its a problem. So I want a game instead. Here's the mechanic I've been toying with: students are traders in a little futures market. Each round I draw a stone from a bag, show it, and put it back; every stone drawn so far has been green. Before each draw the traders place bets on the next stone, and the market pays out according to each trader's private payoff card. The hook is that I hand out two kinds of cards. One just says green pays 10. The other defines the payout predicate in a time-indexed way -- green before round 8, blue after round 8, pays 10 -- phrased plainly so it doesn't look like a trick, but it also never lies to anyone holding it. So after round 8, half the room is betting on identical evidence through a different language, and that's where the fun should start.

What I need from you: full rules I can actually run (turn structure, how bets get placed, a payoff scheme in points or a small amount of extra credit -- no real money), the exact wording of both card types so the time-indexed one sounds boring rather than loaded, and advice on how to seed the bag. That last part is where I keep getting stuck: if the stones actually switch behavior at round 8, haven't I turned the new riddle into a boring surprise about my bag instead of a puzzle about induction? But if nothing changes, what do the time-indexed traders even experience? Figure out something that keeps the game honest to the symmetry point.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the game onto projectibility, onto the fact that both hypotheses fit all the evidence so far, and onto why nobody adopted a grue-ish predicate unless the card forced them -- without naming Goodman or grue until the very end, and with no lecture up front. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the real riddle no classroom game can reproduce and I'll just have to supply myself in the debrief.
