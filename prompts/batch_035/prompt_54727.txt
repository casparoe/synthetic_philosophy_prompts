I've spent the last few years failing at the same New Year's resolutions, and I recently read a popular-science article claiming this all comes down to something called hyperbolic discounting: the idea that we don't discount the future at a constant rate, the way compound interest works, but much more steeply for rewards that are close at hand. Because of this, the ranking of two rewards can flip depending on where you're standing in time — the big payoff in July beats the snacks and the couch when you're planning on Monday, but on Thursday evening the couch wins. The article presented this as the explanation of weakness of will: my Monday self genuinely prefers being fit, my Thursday self genuinely prefers relaxing, and the flip between them is just how the math works out.

The more I turn this over, the less satisfying it seems, and I'd like someone to talk me out of my worry. It feels like this story explains weakness of will away rather than explaining it. If the person on Thursday evening simply has a different preference from the person on Monday morning, then Thursday-evening me isn't weak at all — he's wholeheartedly doing exactly what he wants. There's nothing he's giving in to. But weakness always seemed to involve a genuine failure of some kind: standing in front of the fridge at midnight while some part of you is yelling to stop, and losing anyway. If each momentary self is internally consistent, where has the weakness gone? Maybe the failure only makes sense if the agent is something stretched out over time, a single thing that can betray itself — but then it's not obvious the discounting story captures that.

Please argue for the position that hyperbolic discounting genuinely explains weakness of will rather than merely redescribing it as a sequence of different but self-consistent preferences — in other words, argue that the temporal flip in what you prefer really is what akrasia amounts to, and not just a disguised way of saying "different times, different agents." Along the way, please address the worry above: that "weakness" seems to require an internal conflict or a lapse of self-control, not just a succession of preferences that disagree with each other. I'd especially like you to say something about which self should count as the agent — the momentary one, or the extended one — and whether an extended agent can even be said to betray itself when its preferences at different times simply point in different directions.
