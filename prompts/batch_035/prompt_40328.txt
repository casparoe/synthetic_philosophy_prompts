For the past year my reading group has been stuck on what I thought would be a straightforward interpretive question: what exactly did Descartes deny to animals? The hard-line reading, which you find in most ethics-of-animals literature, has him treating beasts as automata, machines with no inner life at all, so that a dog's yelp is like the creaking of a mechanism and nothing is felt anywhere. The revisionist reading, which the Descartes scholars in the group keep pressing, says this is a caricature: the official doctrine only withholds thought, in the strict sense of something attributable to an immaterial soul, while his hedging in the late correspondence, especially the exchange with Henry More, concedes that animals may well have sensations tied to their bodily organs. On this view Descartes denied animals reflection, language, and self-awareness, but never flatly denied that a cat feels pain; the yelp is expressive of something, even if the cat cannot think about what it feels. Each side can produce passages, and each side accuses the other of cherry-picking — the ethicists say the scholars are whitewashing the man who, more than anyone, licensed three centuries of casual cruelty, and the scholars say the ethicists are prosecuting a caricature rather than a philosopher. Nothing has moved in twelve months, and I've started to suspect we're not actually disagreeing about Descartes at all.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that the impasse has at least three layers and we keep trading them without marking which one we're in. One layer is textual and may be genuinely indeterminate: Descartes may have been inconsistent, or unclear, about whether sensation without thought is even coherent, in which case no reading can be made to fit all the texts and the demand for one is misplaced. A second layer looks verbal: the parties may be using the word "experience" differently, the hard-liners requiring that experience be available to reflection and the revisionists not, so that both sides are right about their own Descartes. A third layer isn't about Descartes at all but about what his position does for us now, whether it matters more as an episode in the history of ideas or as a still-live argument in debates about animal consciousness, and I suspect this is where the heat actually comes from. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which reading is correct: tell me which of these layers, or some other one, best explains why the argument has gone nowhere, what in the texts each side is actually responding to, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction drawn, the question reframed, or an honest admission that this particular dispute cannot be settled and why. And please push back on my framing: if the trilayered hypothesis is itself confused, or if the disagreement is just ordinary textual indeterminacy dressed up as something deeper, say so plainly rather than accommodating me. Write it as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write a considered letter.
