You are a philosopher of physics, equally at home in the foundations literature — Bell's mature notion of local causality, the Jarrett–Shimony decomposition of factorizability, superdeterminism, the memory loophole — and in the daily reality of quantum optics and solid-state labs. You write with the discipline of someone who knows that the bridge between a mathematical theorem and a piece of apparatus is a fragile thing that has to be rebuilt rather than assumed. Think the mapping all the way through before you write any of the deliverable — decide what exactly corresponds to what, and where the theorem simply has nothing to say — but present only the finished sections named below. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliography; reason from what you know.

The episode: the 2015 loophole-free Bell tests. To fix the facts. For four decades every Bell test left at least one escape route open: the 1972 Freedman–Clauser photon experiment had such lossy detection that it needed a fair-sampling assumption; Aspect's 1982 time-varying-analyzer experiment tightened the locality conditions but left detection and switching issues; Weihs's 1998 Innsbruck experiment enforced strict Einstein locality but not efficient detection; efficient-detection tests (Rowe in 2001 with trapped ions, and photonic tests in 2013) closed the detection loophole but on tabletop scales. In 2015 both major loopholes were closed simultaneously: the Delft experiment entangled electron spins in diamond 1.3 kilometers apart via heralded entanglement swapping, achieved efficient spin readout, chose measurement bases fast, and violated the CHSH inequality over 245 heralded trials, with the local-realist null hypothesis assigned at most 3.9% probability even allowing for memory in the devices. Within weeks, two photonic tests — one at NIST, one in Vienna — reported loophole-free violations with entangled photons and high-efficiency detection. Later "cosmic" variants chose the settings using light from distant stars and quasars, to push any common cause of the settings further into the past.

The lens: Bell's theorem read as a constraint on explanation rather than a discovery of influences. On Bell's later formulation, local causality is a factorization condition on conditional probabilities, and deriving the inequality additionally requires the statistical independence of the settings from the relevant hidden variables. Here is the claim I have come to suspect and want taken apart: the 2015 experiments are best described not as showing that nature is nonlocal, but as raising the price of every remaining way of keeping a local explanation of the correlations. Detection and locality loopholes were genuinely closed, so the surviving escape route is only the independence assumption — superdeterminism, or more generally correlations between settings and hidden variables from a common cause — and the experiments did nothing to close it, perhaps could not have. And the wrinkle I most want examined: in several of these tests the settings were chosen by quantum random number generators, so the assumption that the settings are independent of the hidden variables is partly underwritten by the very theory whose predictions are being vindicated — quantum-predicted correlations and quantum-predicted randomness are both doing load-bearing work. Is that a vicious circularity, ordinary scientific bootstrapping, or something in between?

6. "Verdict on my claim" (about 150 words). Was I right that the honest headline concerns the unavailability of local explanations rather than the presence of nonlocal influences, or does that undersell what 2015 actually established?
