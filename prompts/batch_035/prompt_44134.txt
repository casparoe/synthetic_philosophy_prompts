My brother, who is doing a master's in behavioral economics, and his girlfriend, a philosophy PhD student, spent most of a family dinner arguing about whether weakness of will actually exists, and I've been deputized to referee the rematch. The exchange went like this. (1) He opened by claiming that akrasia is a pseudo-problem that economics has dissolved: people who discount the future hyperbolically exhibit preference reversals as a matter of course — the self who at 10 p.m. resolves to go to the gym at 7 a.m. genuinely prefers gym to sleep, and the self who hits snooze at 7 a.m. genuinely prefers sleep to gym, and both are fully rational at the time. No mysterious faculty of "will" fails; two successive selves simply have different tradeoff rates, and noting that exponential discounting wouldn't produce this instability shows the effect is specifically about the shape of the curve, not about anything normatively interesting. (2) She replied that this changes the subject: akrasia is acting against one's own better judgment, and the hyperbolic model explains incontinent-looking behavior by denying the phenomenon — on his picture the snoozer isn't weak-willed, he just has different preferences than the person who set the alarm, which is exactly what Socrates claimed in the Protagoras when he argued no one errs knowingly and everyone does what seems best at the time. Economists, she said, have rediscovered the Socratic solution and announced it as progress. (3) He countered with regret and self-binding: an ordinary change of taste never leads you to pay money to constrain your future self, yet people buy small dessert plates, sign up for gyms with cancellation fees, and tie themselves to the mast like Ulysses — so something real is going on, only relocated: it's an internecine conflict between successive selves rather than a failure of a single will. (4) She answered that once the conflict is between selves, the original problem is gone rather than solved: Davidson's question was how a single agent can freely do what she judges, all things considered, worse — and if you split the agent into a sequence of preference-holders, you've redefined akrasia as a negotiation among parties rather than explaining how one person can act against her own judgment. Moreover, animals and small children presumably discount hyperbolically without being weak-willed, so hyperbolic discounting can't be sufficient for akrasia. (5) He finished by saying this concedes his point: animals also lack all-things-considered judgments, so layer such judgments on top of the discounting machinery and you get the whole phenomenon for free — "will" does no work anywhere in the story, and the remaining dispute is verbal.

They've both asked me for a verdict before the next dinner, and I don't trust myself to give one. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess each of the five claims as clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with your reasons. In particular, I'd like your view on whether the behavioral picture dissolves akrasia, relocates it, or actually preserves the classical phenomenon under a new description; on whether the self-binding and regret phenomena carry the argumentative weight he assigns them; and on whether there's a shared assumption both are getting wrong — for instance, about whether there's a single thing, one phenomenon, that a theory of akrasia owes us an account of, or whether they've been talking past each other by demanding that the word pick out different things.
