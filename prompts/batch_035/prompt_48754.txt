I chair the constitutional review committee of a national student debating federation — roughly four thousand members, an AGM once a year, and a constitution last overhauled in the 1980s. Our current amendment clause is conventional: any article may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the general meeting, with notice circulated sixty days in advance. At this year's AGM a faction will move to replace it with a single omnibus clause, which would read, verbatim: "Any rule of this federation, including this one, may be amended, suspended, or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the general meeting." They argue that enumerating special procedures and protected articles merely invites procedural gamesmanship, and they cite the game Nomic, invented by the philosopher Peter Suber, as evidence that a fully self-applying amendment rule is coherent — in Nomic the whole point is that the rules for changing the rules are themselves subject to change. The opposing faction wants exactly the reverse: an entrenched core of principles (non-discrimination, one-member-one-vote, the secret ballot) declared unamendable, with an explicit provision that even motions to amend the entrenchment itself are void. A third committee member claims the entire debate is confused: our existing clause already applies to itself implicitly, since a two-thirds vote that can amend "any article" can amend the amendment article, so the omnibus motion changes nothing and the entrenchment motion is self-undermining theater.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before the AGM. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the omnibus clause, adopt the entrenchment scheme, keep our current clause, or propose a specific hybrid — and it must engage the genuine paradoxes of self-reference rather than wave at them: whether a rule can confer the authority required to amend itself, or whether such authority has to come from somewhere else and the self-applying clause merely signals it; what happens when two procedures with different thresholds each claim jurisdiction over the other (as when the entrenchers propose entrenching by two-thirds while the omnibus clause purports to keep everything amendable by two-thirds); and whether an explicitly self-entrenched clause differs in any principled way from an ordinary clause that simply happens never to have been challenged. It would help if you worked through the old Alf Ross puzzle from his 1969 Mind article on self-reference in constitutional law — a provision purportedly amended by the very rule it is an exception to — and said whether our dispute is a fresh instance of it or a watered-down version.

Two further constraints. First, please take the Nomic comparison seriously rather than dismissively: if the game demonstrably works in practice, whatever makes it work — convention, tacit consent, the players' shared desire to keep playing — may be doing hidden work that we would want a federation of volunteers to reproduce rather than fake. Second, note where a five-person game night and a four-thousand-member federation with a bank account, staff contracts, and a duty of care to members genuinely come apart. The memo should give our AGM delegates something they can vote on without a law degree, so please end with a short numbered recommendation.
