Each month I lead a discussion evening at my local community center — a dozen or so regulars, mostly working people (a nurse, two engineers, a retired schoolteacher), bright but with no philosophy training — and next month the topic falls to me. For years I have been turning over on my own what philosophers apparently call the gap between "is" and "ought": the observation that no set of purely descriptive premises seems to entail a normative conclusion unless some normative premise is smuggled in along the way. I have never studied this formally, but I have thought about it enough to have a picture of it I trust: that the point is about what an argument needs in order to be valid, not about whether values are real, and that people constantly run this together with the other, older complaint that defining "good" in natural terms leaves an open question behind. My regulars will not distinguish these two complaints on their own; I barely did myself for the first two years.

I have forty minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience that arrives believing one of two things: either that the gap has been debunked because obviously we can argue about values, or that the gap proves morality is just opinion. Both are wrong, and both are wrong at different points in the reasoning, so the order in which I fix them matters to me. My own candidate for the example that could carry the whole session is a chess endgame: from a full description of the position on the board, plus the rules, can you derive what White ought to do? I like it because something like a bridge premise (roughly, that White is playing to win) has to be added before any "ought" appears, and the audience can watch themselves add it. But I have also considered a medical case — a full chart, and the question of what the doctor ought to do — where the bridging premises about goals feel much less optional, which might be a strength or a fatal weakness. I cannot tell, and I would rather be told than guess.

Please do not explain the is/ought material to me; I want a teaching plan for the session. Tell me: the order the pieces must come in and why, given that the validity point has to be in place before the definitional point can be distinguished from it, or whether you would reverse that; which example should carry the session and what it must contain and must not contain to do its job (a "must not" I have in mind: the smuggled premise should be visible when added, not hidden in an ambiguity of the word "good"); the misconceptions my regulars will bring or form — the two I named, plus the one I most fear, that the whole discussion lands as "so science can't tell us anything about how to live," which is not what I want them walking out with — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off; one question I can put to the room at the end that would show me whether the idea landed rather than just generated nodding; and what I should openly decline to take up at this level and say so — for my own part I have concluded that whether the gap, once granted, does anything at all to moral realism is a question I cannot handle honestly in the time, but correct me if that refusal gives up too much.

Please lead your response with its bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict on the plan's shape, in one sentence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of what I have asked for.
