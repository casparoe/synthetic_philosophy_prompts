Quick bit of background: I'm the type director at a small foundry, and we keep having the same argument every time someone pushes a design sketch in a stylised direction. Someone will do a brushy display face where the terminals flare so much the 'a' starts reading as 'o', or a blackletter-flavoured thing where the 'S' cap drifts towards 'f', and half the room says "it's still legible, look at it in context" and the other half says "you can read it because you know the word, that's cheating". We never resolve it because we have no shared way of deciding when a letterform has stopped being that letter, versus when it's that letter in an unfamiliar costume, versus when it's a perfectly fine letter that just doesn't belong in this family.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a single glyph, in about ten minutes, before we commit it to a release. The interesting philosophical bit I want you to actually carry the weight of: what is it that makes every 'a' an 'a' -- is it the skeleton (the underlying topology, the closed bowl plus the right-side stem) versus the flesh (weight, contrast, terminals, the whole stylistic layer)? Or is it more like family resemblance across instances, or maybe it's recognitional, that what matters is what a reader triggers on in context? Whatever you decide, the procedure needs to make that call operational, and it needs to be honest about which bits of it are judgment calls dressed up as steps.

Specific things I want the procedure to handle:

- It should separate the question "is this still an a?" from the question "is this still an a in this family?" -- we sometimes reject a glyph that's a fine 'a' but a terrible fit for the design, and the procedure shouldnt merge those into one mushy verdict.
- It needs a test for the context excuse, i.e. the designer saying "read this sample paragraph, you understood every word". My instinct is that word-level guessing is doing a lot of work there and I want a way to check that, maybe nonsense-string or isolated-glyph tests, but I'd like you to think about which tests actually probe letter identity versus just probing habitability of the style.
- Blackletter is the classic edge case because historically readers who grew up on fraktur genuinely read those forms fluently, so any claim that e.g. a fraktur 'k' is "objectively confusable" needs to be more careful than it first looks. If the procedure has to bite a bullet here, say so.
- Confusable pairs like b/d, l/1/I, rn/m should be treated as a distinct failure mode from "this stopped being an a" -- those seem like different sorts of problem and I suspect the fix differs too.

Then run the procedure on three sketches from our current project: (1) the flare-terminal display 'a' I mentioned, which I privately love and will be annoyed if it fails, (2) a blackletter-inflected capital 'S' in an otherwise neutral sans, which I want killed but for principled reasons not vibes, and (3) a single-storey 'a' in a geometric sans where the team is split because it's totally legitimate in plenty of classic faces but reads oddly next to our double-storey 'g'. For each one, tell me where the procedure outputs a verdict and where it correctly punts to a human.

Also please be upfront about what the procedure can't do: I'm guessing things like how much novelty readers will absorb in a week of exposure are genuinely outside what any checklist can decide, and I'd rather know that than have a procedure that pretends. Keep it usable by a junior designer without any philosophy background, so translate whatever distinctions you use into plain language, but don't lose the actual content while simplifying. Prefer no jargon you haven't explained inline, and dont worry about being nice about my flare-terminal 'a' -- if it fails, it fails.
