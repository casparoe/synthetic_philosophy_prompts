I run a small type foundry that produces fonts for signage and user interfaces, and we have a recurring dispute. A submitted letterform gets called an expressive take on the letter 'a' by its designer and not an a anymore by our QA reviewers, and we have no shared rule for deciding who is right. The conceptual root, as I've come to see it, is this: every 'a' across every typeface — double-story, single-story, geometric, calligraphic — is somehow still the same letter, while other shapes, even quite similar ones, are not; and the very same shape can be unmistakably 'a' in one font and unreadable in another, because of what the surrounding letters do to it. So the question splits in two: what makes a glyph the letter it is, and what makes a font the style it is — and our reviews confuse the two constantly.

I want a procedure our three-person review panel can actually run, glyph by glyph, built on the idea that a letterform has two separable aspects: a structural skeleton, which makes it the letter it is, and a stylistic surface, which belongs to the font as a whole rather than to any single glyph. The procedure should give, in order: the structural checks that establish letter identity, and what counts as passing each; a way to decide when style has distorted or starved a formally correct skeleton so badly that ordinary readers will misidentify it, including a quick reading test we can run when the panel disagrees; the points at which the procedure terminates in a rejection; and the point at which the question stops being one of legibility and becomes a judgment about how far style may go — which, being a matter of taste and policy, must come to me as design director rather than to the panel. Please be candid about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, about what the procedure cannot settle at all, and expect at least one case where it returns an answer the designer will hate.

Then run it on three live disputes from our current release: (1) a single-story 'a' in a geometric sans whose bowl is drawn so closed and circular that the panel worries it reads as an 'o' with a stray stroke; (2) a blackletter-influenced lowercase 'a' in an otherwise humanist face, where the panel splits between period charm and unreadable at phone sizes; (3) a lowercase 'l' that has been straightened, to tighten spacing in narrow interface settings, to the point that at small sizes it is confusable with the capital 'I' and the digit '1'.

For context: we hit roughly one such dispute per ten new glyphs, the panel has at most fifteen minutes per case, none of its members has training in typography theory, and we can occasionally borrow two or three non-designers from the office for a quick reading test, but we have no budget for formal user studies. Please write your response in American English.
