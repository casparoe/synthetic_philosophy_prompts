I work as a backend software engineer, and I've been trying to sort out what I actually mean by "pleasure" by cataloguing my own gut verdicts on cases, most of them from work. The rush when a flaky integration suite finally goes all green after a week of chasing an intermittent failure: pleasure, obviously, and something almost chemical. The first bite of food after a whole day of too-busy-to-eat: pleasure of a completely different, purely bodily kind, but I count it just as fully. The hours-absorbed flow state of a big refactor, where I notice nothing at the time and only look back afterward and think that was a good evening: I'm inclined to say there was pleasure there, even though I can't point to any moment of it that felt like anything. The relief when a 3 a.m. page turns out to be a misconfigured alert rather than a real outage: feels like pleasure even though nothing good happened, only the removal of a bad thing. Quietly being glad that a project I'd be stuck maintaining got cancelled before it started: I count it as pleasure, yet there's no felt quality whatsoever, just an attitude toward a state of affairs. And a case that bothers me: I sometimes replay in my head a design review that I remember going brilliantly, and I get real enjoyment out of the memory, except I've since read the actual notes and it went badly; I misremember. I still think I got pleasure at the time of remembering, but I'm uneasy about that verdict.

Here's my problem. The cases I'm most confident about are the felt ones — the green-build rush, the hungry bite, the relief — which pushes me toward thinking pleasure is a kind of felt tone. But then the unfelt gladness about the cancelled project and the absorbed flow state seem to slip out, and I don't want to say they're not pleasures. Maybe pleasure is instead a certain attitude I take toward a state of affairs, with the felt cases just being the ones where the attitude comes with a distinctive sensation? But that starts to look like it would also count things I'm not prepared to count, and it sits strangely with the misremembered design review, where the state of affairs I'm pleased about never happened. So: is there a single principle here — felt tone, an attitude of enjoyment, the satisfaction of a desire, or some hybrid — that captures my verdicts? Please state the account or the small set of candidates precisely, say which verdicts each clause is there to save and which of my verdicts it can't accommodate (I'd honestly rather overturn one than adopt something baroque), and tell me what the winning account predicts for a few cases I haven't mentioned: taking quiet pride in a service I wrote years ago that's still running fine and that I never look at; the pleasant buzz of being flattered by someone I later learn was insincere the whole time; and the runner's high, which comes with almost no directed attitude toward anything at all.
