In my philosophy of mind course we have been studying pain asymbolia, the puzzling condition (usually associated with damage in or near the anterior cingulate cortex) in which patients say they feel pain — they can locate it, describe its quality, and rate its intensity — yet report that it does not bother them, do not withdraw from the stimulus, and sometimes even seem amused by it. After the seminar, two classmates of mine, D. and M., stayed behind to argue about what the condition shows about the badness of pain, and I could not tell who had the better of it.

(1) M. opened by claiming that asymbolia settles an old question: the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensation itself. The asymbolic patient has everything sensory that pain involves, and none of the badness; so what we mind when we mind pain must be the aversive reaction layered on top of the sensation, not the sensation's felt quality. Pain, she concluded, is not intrinsically bad — it is bad only when accompanied by suffering, and asymbolia displays the two coming apart in a single person.

(2) D. replied that this proves either too little or too much. If the dissociation shows that the felt sensation is neutral, then the same dissociation should make us doubt that the asymbolic patient is in pain at all, rather than merely experiencing some pain-like sensation: you cannot simultaneously use the case to establish that pain can occur without badness and to define pain by the very sensory component you have just demoted. What the patient retains, he said, is like the dull pressure a person under analgesia reports — we call it pain only as a courtesy, by extension from normal cases, and the extension is exactly what is in dispute.

(3) M. countered that the analogy with analgesia ignores the patients' own testimony: asymbolic patients insist that what they feel is genuinely painful, not faint or pressure-like, and they say this while exhibiting indifference. Their first-person reports, made with the concept of pain learned through a lifetime of ordinary pain, deserve to count as evidence that the sensation proper is intact while the affective evaluation is selectively switched off.

(4) D. granted that the reports count for something, but pressed a different worry. Even if the sensation is fully intact and merely devalued, M.'s conclusion does not follow, because the ethical question is not which component carries the badness of pain-as-we-normally-know-it; it is whether we are permitted to cause this state in someone for whom it is not bad. And he thought M.'s line of reasoning points somewhere uncomfortable: if we conclude that asymbolic pain is not bad, we have licensed inflicting it, which is precisely the reasoning that has historically been used to dismiss the complaints of patients whose distress was no longer visible. A state that is not bad for the person in it may still be bad-making in a way that matters for how we may treat them.

(5) M.'s last word was that D. was changing the subject from metaphysics to ethics: the question in the seminar was whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to the phenomenal character, and the cingulate evidence, together with the two-component structure it suggests, supports a negative answer regardless of what follows for permissible treatment. D. said that a dissociation discovered in abnormal brains cannot ground a claim about the essence of a state defined by normal cases, and that if it could, the same method would show whatever we liked about any concept by finding a patient in whom it fragments.

They eventually gave up and left it to me to find someone who could referee. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each of the five steps, say whether it is right, mistaken, or partly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall, and, importantly, identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
