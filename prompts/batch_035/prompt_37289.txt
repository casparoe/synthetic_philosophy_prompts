I am a retired natural scientist — population ecology first, then environmental epidemiology — who has lately been reading philosophy in earnest. In my working life, "cohort" was a bookkeeping device: a set of individuals sharing an interval of birth, useful in a model, nothing more. Now I find journalists, sociologists, and even philosophers speaking of Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, and Gen Z as though these were real things with dispositions — entitled, pragmatic, anxious — as one might speak of a species with a characteristic niche. Some writers treat generational talk as harmless shorthand; others, following Mannheim's old essay on generations, treat cohorts as genuine social units; a few build whole theories of history out of them. Before I read further, I want the specification the argument is being fitted to.

Write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate account of generations-as-kinds would have to meet in order to count as picking out real kinds rather than convenient labels. To show the register I have in mind, here are some candidate requirements drawn from my own trade; restate, prune, or add to them as you see fit:

1. Identifiability. In cohort analysis we face the age–period–cohort problem: since age equals period minus cohort exactly, the three effects cannot be separately estimated without imposing some constraint, and the choice of constraint is arbitrary. An account on which generational effects do real explanatory work owes us an answer to what breaks this symmetry — what makes the cohort effect more than a residue of age and period effects — or an admission that it cannot.

2. Boundaries. Real kinds may have vague boundaries, as species do, but the vagueness is structured by something. Must an account of generations say whether the familiar cutoffs (1946 and 1964 for the Boomers, say) are pure conventions or track something real — and what difference the answer makes?

3. Membership. Is membership fixed by birth year alone, or can one belong to a generation one refuses to identify with? The account should distinguish membership in the kind from identification with the label.

4. Heterogeneity and effect size. In my trade, a between-group difference smaller than the within-group variance is not a finding. Is the account answerable to a comparable standard — must it say something about the magnitude of generational effects relative to within-cohort variation — or is that an objection imported from the wrong discipline?

5. Portability. Do generations travel? Is there a Gen Z in Japan, in Turkey, in Brazil, or is the schema a projection of one country's post-war history onto everyone else's?

6. Non-circularity. The account must not explain a cohort's attitudes by its generational membership where membership, as used in the explanation, was itself individuated by those attitudes.

For each requirement, give the case that motivates it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly, because that would itself be a result. Then, in a single closing paragraph, tell me briefly how three broad contenders fare against the list: generational realism of the strong sort (theorists of cyclical generations included), the deflationary view on which generations are mere bookkeeping devices like five-year age bands, and a middle view on which generations are real but causally thin, like decades or postal codes. Keep the weight on the requirements rather than the scorecard; I will do the scoring myself.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict: <one sentence>" stating either which single requirement does the most to exclude the candidate accounts, or, if you judge some subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable, which subset and why.
