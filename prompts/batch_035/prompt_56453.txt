A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about her dissertation. She is six years into a PhD, and I told her, in the way one does, that the sunk costs are sunk and that she shouldn't throw good money after bad: the six years are gone, so the only rational question is whether her marginal years ahead are best spent finishing the degree or starting something else. She pushed back harder than I expected, and by the end of the evening I was no longer confident that my one-liners had earned the work I was making them do. She granted that the past expenditure shouldn't appear in any accounting of what she has lost, but insisted that the six years were not merely expenditure: they changed her — skills, habits, a self-conception she has partly organized her life around — and they are evidence, since she now knows vastly more than she did at year zero about how good she is at this and how much she can stand it. When I said the sunk costs are irrelevant, I honestly could not say whether I was making a claim about bookkeeping, about decision theory, about evidence, or about her character.

Please take the family of sayings I was leaning on — sunk costs are sunk, don't throw good money after bad, bygones are bygones — and lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses these phrases could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical. Candidates I can vaguely see, but cannot sort out myself, include: the near-tautology that past spending is unrecoverable; the decision-theoretic claim that the ranking of forward-looking options should be independent of how one arrived at the present choice; the evidential claim that what one has already invested carries no information about the expected returns of continuing; a psychological claim about how people in fact deliberate; and perhaps a normative claim that a life is to be evaluated prospectively only. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is defensible at all, and — most importantly — whether it actually supports the use I put it to, namely advising her to weigh the remaining years exactly as if she were choosing fresh today. Then tell me which reading is most plausibly what people intend when they deploy these phrases to end an argument, and whether my friend's defense of honoring sunk costs requires any claim that is simply false, or whether my slogans were papering over a genuinely open question.
