Heres one from my decision theory reading group. The proposition, stated as precisely as I can: money pump arguments establish full transitivity of preference — the weak relation, indifference included — as a requirement of rationality, and not merely acyclicity of strict preference. What's your credence in that?

For context on why it isnt a clean yes: the naive pump is easy to dodge. If I can foresee the whole sequence of trades circling back to my starting option minus my wallet, I just refuse the first trade — roughly what people call resolute choice. And someone with incomplete preferences can flatly refuse trades between incomparable options, so the pump never gets going. The pro-transitivity side therefore runs patched versions: one where I'm charged a fee upfront so the option sequence is never even offered (if I'd rather pay than face it, the intransitivity is costing me money either way), plus the claim that intransitive indifference, not just strict cycles, leaks money under some variants. The anti side says all this smuggles in extra assumptions — about foresight, or a background norm that you must choose by preference at every node — that are doing all the actual work.

In our group the numbers range from about 0.8 down to about 0.2. Give me your own number with the decomposition: whether the foreseeing agent escapes, whether the patched pumps actually work, whether being exploitable is the same thing as being irrational, and whether the requirement attaches to preferences themselves or whether a simple policy of never trading down could do the same job. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, and where the 0.8 person and the 0.2 person most likely disagree.
