I'm a PhD student in political theory, writing a dissertation on Victorian debates about property, and this term I'm TAing a third-year lecture course on nineteenth-century political thought. The final exam included a question on Mill's late views on socialism, which the lecturer framed around the revisions of the Principles of Political Economy across its editions and the posthumous Chapters on Socialism. I've marked most of the scripts, but one answer sits right on the boundary between two grade bands, and I'd like a second opinion before I commit to a mark. Please assess it as I would: give a mark out of 25 and point-by-point comments saying exactly what to credit, what to penalise, and why — not just a general impression of quality.

The exam question read as follows:

"Mill is often described as sympathetic to socialism toward the end of his life, yet he never became a straightforward socialist. Drawing on the treatment of property, distribution, and co-operation in the Principles of Political Economy (especially the passages added or expanded in the later editions) and on the posthumous Chapters on Socialism (1879), explain (i) what Mill found attractive in the socialist ideas of his day, (ii) the reservations that kept him from endorsing a full socialisation of property, and (iii) how he proposed the question between private property and socialism should be settled. (25 marks)"

And the student answered:

"Mill's late views on socialism are best understood as a consistent development of his view that the distribution of wealth is a matter of social institution rather than natural law, so that the existing regime of private property is merely one historically temporary arrangement among possible others. His early engagement with the Saint-Simonian writers in the 1830s already showed sympathy for the critique of unlimited competition, but it was in the later editions of the Principles that he warmed decisively toward co-operative production. Worker-managed associations appealed to him chiefly because he thought they would develop the labourers' faculties of self-government, and he predicted that the relation between a capitalist employer and voiceless wage-dependent workers would in time be displaced by these associations. He even said that, forced to choose between the existing order with all its suffering and communism, he would accept the latter's difficulties in preference. Although he always insisted that the laws of production are just as much within society's control as the laws of distribution, he recognised that production must still be organised efficiently, which is why he thought the choice between systems was an empirical one: no a priori argument could settle whether common or private ownership would prove more productive, and he expected competition between the systems — together with voluntary experiments in co-operation — to show which was superior. His enthusiasm culminated in the Chapters on Socialism, published as periodical essays in the early 1860s, around the time of Utilitarianism. There he endorsed the socialisation of the means of production: since distribution is conventional and private property historically temporary, Mill concluded that the state should assume ownership and direction of industry, his well-known caution about state incompetence amounting only to a tactical point about the class character of existing governments rather than an objection of principle. Finally, he rejected revolutionary seizure as a route to the new order, insisting that any transformation had to come gradually, by persuasion and experiment, if the gains of civilisation were to be preserved."

In your comments, please separate what is settled about these texts from what is genuinely contested among people who work on Mill — for instance, the publication history and doctrines Mill plainly states on the one hand, versus, on the other, how far his socialism really went, and whether the unfinished posthumous chapters represent a late cooling of enthusiasm or simply carry through his earlier position. Where the student's claims fall into contested territory, say so rather than treating them as errors, and don't mark them down merely because they track a less charitable reading; but don't let genuine mistakes pass just because they're dressed in fluent prose.
