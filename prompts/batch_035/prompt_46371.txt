I am putting together a co-taught unit on moral constraints, and my co-teacher and I have spent three years arguing past each other about the so-called paradox of deontology: the complaint that a deontologist who refuses to violate a right in order to prevent several comparable violations of the very same right by others ends up behaving, by the lights of her own theory, in a way that permits more of what her theory calls wrong. Before we teach it again, we want to hand the students the document we should have written long ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two of us. Draft that document.

The two parties are, first, a constraint deontologist who holds that certain acts (killing the innocent, torture, rigging procedures like trials and elections, breaking promises without excuse) are prohibited by side constraints rather than by their bad outcomes, and who takes the constraints to be at least strongly resistant to being outweighed by numbers of the same kind of violation by other agents; and, second, a rights-consequentialist who agrees that rights violations are gravely disproportionate costs, but insists that the rational response to that fact is to minimize the total number and severity of violations, including one's own, rather than to refuse to contribute a small violation to prevent a larger number of the same. Neither of us is a simple maximizer of aggregate welfare, so do not waste space on that strawman; the disagreement is specifically about whether, when all else is equal, an agent may herself perform one violation of a constraint to prevent others from committing several of the same.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both parties can sign — about what each takes constraints to be constraints on, about the badness of violations, about which cases both agree are clear (the coerced surgery case, the sheriff who must frame one innocent to prevent a mob from killing five, and so on), and about what the paradoxal complaint gets right as a description of the deontologist's position; (2) a table of the exact remaining disagreements, each formulated twice, once in wording the deontologist would accept for her own view and once in wording the consequentialist would accept for his, so that neither side's position is stated in the other's preferred idiom; and (3) the cruxes — I take it these include whether agent-relative constraints can be justified to the victim of the prevented violations, whether the distinction between doing and allowing does any work when the constraint is at stake for both options, whether a threshold view (constraints that bend only at very large numbers) is a stable compromise or an unstable halfway house, and whether the paradox is in part merely verbal, trading on an equivocation between "wrong" as a predicate of acts and "wrong" as a predicate of states of affairs. For that last question, give a verdict, with the reasoning, rather than leaving it open.

In addition to the standard cases, the document must engage the following case, which we use in seminar and which I want handled explicitly: an election official discovers that agents of one party will stuff a ballot box in six precincts tonight unless she herself stuffs it in one precinct first, in a way that credibly discredits the entire election and forces a rerun under tighter scrutiny; assume the rerun is clean and that her only alternatives are to let the six stuffings proceed or to resign. Say where each party's principles put this case, whether the parties agree on any description of it, and what each would predict a threshold deontologist must say about it.

For each crux, state what argument, case, or consideration both parties would accept as genuinely bearing on it — not merely as ammunition — and record each side's prediction of what that consideration will show, stated specifically enough that either side could later be shown to have been wrong. End with a short section on what, if anything, both agree would actually move one of us to change position, and flag honestly if some portion of our dispute is, in your judgment, verbal rather than substantive. Write the joint statement so that a neutral reader could tell the parties have understood each other; formulations that neither side would sign are failures, and so are formulations so weak that both sign them without cost.
