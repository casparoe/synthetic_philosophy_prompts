I'm reading Machiavelli's The Prince with a study group and we got stuck on chapter 25, the one about fortune and virtù. Maybe you can help me untangle it.

So as I understand the chapter, Machiavelli says fortune is the arbiter of about half of what we do, but leaves the other half or thereabouts to our own control. Then he compares fortune to a violent river that floods the land, tearing up trees and buildings — but when it's calm, men can build dikes and embankments so that the next flood goes into channels rather than destroying everything. His point seems to be that fortune shows her power where virtù hasn't made any preparation against her. Then near the end he says something about how fortune is a woman, and that she is the friend of the young and bold because they're less cautious, rougher, and command her more audaciously.

Here's what confuses me. The river metaphor seems to suggest that the right response to fortune is engineering: calm preparation, anticipation, building defenses before the flood. That's basically prudence and planning. But then the woman metaphor suggests almost the opposite — that you should be impetuous rather than cautious, that hesitation loses, that boldness masters fortune precisely by not preparing but by striking. In one image fortune is a natural force you channel with foresight; in the other she's something you overwhelm with sheer aggression. Are these two pieces of advice consistent for Machiavelli, or is he contradicting himself within the same chapter?

Also, some members of my group think the whole chapter quietly gives up on the earlier parts of the book. If fortune really controls half of everything, and if the examples Machiavelli uses (like the Italian rulers ruined by relying on others' arms) were destroyed because their virtù didn't match the times, then the book's practical advice seems hostage to a situation you can't control — you'd need to know in advance whether the times call for boldness or caution, and Machiavelli himself admits men can't change their natures to fit the times. Others say no, the chapter is actually the keystone: the point is that most people fail not because fortune is overwhelming but because they act like it's overwhelming, waiting around instead of impinging on fortune. So the half/half split isn't a confession of helplessness but a description of the default that virtù exists to change.

Can you help me think through which reading holds up better? Specifically I'd like to know whether the impetuosity advice actually follows from the river metaphor or is a separate argument grafted on, and whether the famous ending about fortune being a woman is doing philosophical work or is just the era's rhetorical flourish that we should read past. And if you think Machiavelli is genuinely torn here, say so — I'd rather have that than a forced reconciliation. No need for citations, just your own reading of the text.
