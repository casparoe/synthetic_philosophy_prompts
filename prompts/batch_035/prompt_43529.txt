I edit a small philosophy blog aimed at advanced undergraduates and ambitious amateurs, and for the next post I want a diagnostic quiz titled something like 'What do you really think about the lottery and preface paradoxes?'. A friend who teaches epistemology warned me that most quizzes of this kind just ask readers to self-identify with a school ('are you a contextualist?'), which is worse than useless as a diagnostic; I want the opposite. The quiz should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they fancy. Please write eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options phrased entirely in plain language (no jargon in the question stems), together with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the main positions in the literature.

The cases should be drawn from the neighbourhood of Kyburg's lottery paradox and Makinson's preface paradox rather than the usual fake-barn or Grabit material. Please make sure to include: the single-owner case, where you hold the only ticket in a million-ticket lottery and are invited to say you know it will lose; the conjunction case, where you have separately acquired each of the million beliefs that ticket i will lose and must say whether you may conjoin them; the preface itself, plus the asymmetry many readers feel between the author's sober confession that at least one claim in the book is wrong and the lottery player's confidence that her ticket will lose; the elimination case, where closing off 999,999 tickets one by one seems to put you in a position to know that the last ticket will win; and at least one 'second-order' case where you reflect that you will turn out to be wrong about at least one thing you currently believe. You are welcome to add a case or two of your own devising in the same family — an insurance actuary, a student who has memorised every past exam — as long as each one does real diagnostic work.

The scoring key should map onto at least these leaves: Kyburg-style acceptance rules, which accept whatever is probable enough and deny that rational acceptance is closed under conjunction; Lockeanism with a high threshold, together with its own account of why the preface author's position is or isn't acceptable; views that abandon binary belief outright in favour of credences; anti-luck approaches in the safety or sensitivity tradition, which deny that the lottery belief amounts to knowledge while insisting the preface author does know his book contains an error; subject-sensitive invariantism or pragmatic encroachment, on which the stakes of the case partly determine what is known; and the sharp-threshold epistemicist option that simply bites the bullet on where the line falls. For each leaf, add one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot really separate — for instance, whether any of the cases cleanly distinguishes a pure Lockean from a pragmatic encroacher, or a safety theorist from a sensitivity theorist. Please write the whole thing in British English.
