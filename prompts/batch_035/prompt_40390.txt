A friend who's read a bunch of Hofstadter tried to comfort me after a funeral, and summarized his view of the self for me like this:

"The 'I' is just a self-referential pattern the brain spins — a strange loop — so there's no little homunculus in there. Since the pattern is what matters and not the brain it happens to run on, everyone who knew you well has a copy of your pattern in their head: your closest people carry big chunks of your soul. Hofstadter proved that psychological continuity, not physical continuity, is what matters for identity (basically the same as Parfit), and since your psychological traits keep running in other people after you die, death isn't really the end — the pattern persists. Thats why he wrote about his wife still being partly alive in him after she died. So grieving as if the person is entirely gone is kind of a mistake — it should feel more like missing someone who moved far away."

It was really moving at the time, but before I repeat it to anyone else I want to know how much of it is actually him. Go claim by claim: which parts would Hofstadter endorse, which are conflations with other philosophers' views or overstatements of something he says more tentatively, and does the conclusion about grief actually follow from whatever accurate parts remain? In particular I want to know whether the stuff about other people carrying your pattern is meant literally — like, is a pattern running in someone else's brain a copy of your actual loop, or just their model of you, and does Hofstadter adress that distinction anywhere?
