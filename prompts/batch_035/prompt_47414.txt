My book club just finished Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, and the thing everyone keeps circling back to is that the clones never try to escape. Hailsham's gate isn't locked, they get a car and drive across England chasing rumors of deferrals, and at no point does anyone just keep driving. I want to read the novel through Sartre on bad faith. Is Kathy's serene narration the self-deception Sartre means -- taking her facticity (donor, clone) for her essence, the spirit of seriousness where values feel like features of the world, the same move as his waiter playing at being a waiter? Are Ruth's cruelty, or Tommy's childhood rages, moments where a lucid awareness of freedom flashes through? And what about the euphemisms, donations and completion -- bad faith sedimented into the language itself? But be honest with me about where the reading strains: Sartre thinks angish and the possibility of revolt are always available, and Ishiguro's characters seem to show the opposite, a freedom quietly conditioned out of existence before anyone chose anything. Does the novel complicate Sartre, or just illustrate him? Also give us 3 or 4 specific scenes to argue about at the next meeting.
