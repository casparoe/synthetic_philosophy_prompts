I'm taking a seminar on animal ethics, and our term paper asks us to take a defensible stand on invertebrate welfare in aquaculture — not just in principle, but as it bears on what the industry should actually do. My classmate and I have ended up at opposite poles on decapod crustaceans, farmed shrimp in particular, and we've agreed to be honest about numbers rather than gesture at complexity. My credence that a farmed whiteleg shrimp is sentient — that there is something it is like to be one, involving genuinely valenced states rather than mere nociception — sits around 0.75. Hers is around 0.2, and she accuses me of invertebrate sentimentalism: projecting inner life onto animals whose nervous systems are organized almost nothing like ours, with integration spread across a set of ventral ganglia rather than centralized in one brain, and with reflexive-looking behaviors that in vertebrates we'd happily call spinal.

The evidence we keep circling, roughly: shore crabs given shocks in a preferred shelter will abandon it and trade off shelter value against shock avoidance in ways that look like motivational integration rather than reflex; hermit crabs show shock-induced behavior that analgesics and anxiolytics appear to modulate, and they attend to specific wound sites rather than reacting generally; shrimp show protective grooming of injuries and reduced avoidance trade-offs under painkillers. Against all this, my classmate leans on the substrate side: whatever functional story you tell, valenced experience may require a kind of large-scale, centralized integration that a chain of ganglia cannot deliver, and the fact that nociceptors and opioid receptors are evolutionarily ancient cuts both ways — maybe they indicate pain older than fish, maybe just detection older than everything. The stakes are not academic, by the way: by individual count, farmed shrimp plausibly outnumber all farmed vertebrates combined, most are killed by asphyxiation on ice or in chilled brine, and stun-kill methods exist but are barely deployed.

So here is what I want from you: your own credence for the proposition that farmed decapod crustaceans are sentient in the welfare-relevant sense, and more importantly a decomposition of it — the probability that a functionalist rather than substrate-requiring theory of consciousness is correct; the probability, conditional on functionalism, that trade-off and wound-directed behavior are strong evidence of valence rather than sophisticated nociception; whether sentience is better treated as graded rather than binary, and what that does to the truth value of the proposition itself; and whether precautionary reasoning could justify welfare reforms even at fairly low sentience credence, given the asymmetry between the cost of humane slaughter methods and the scale of potential suffering. Then tell me whether you think the disagreement between me at 0.75 and her at 0.2 lives mostly in the philosophy of mind, mostly in the weighing of the behavioral evidence, or somewhere else entirely; and identify the single finding that would move your number the most. If you think one of my decomposition terms is confused, or that the proposition as I've framed it is ill-formed, say so up front and fix the framing before assigning numbers.
