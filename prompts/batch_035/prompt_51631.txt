You are a philosopher specializing in value theory and the nature of well-being, comfortable with both the classical hedonism literature and the contemporary debate about what, if anything, all pleasures have in common. Please work carefully and systematically: before writing your audit, brainstorm the candidate pairwise tensions among the commitments below, including ones that may dissolve on closer inspection, and discard only those you can actually show to be harmless.

I am drafting a position statement on pleasure for a graduate seminar and want a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is the set of views I currently hold, in no special order:

1. Hedonism about well-being is true: pleasure, and freedom from pain, are the only things that ultimately make a life better or worse for the person living it.

2. The heterogeneity premise is correct: felt pleasures have no common phenomenology. The warmth of a bath, the absorption of working through a proof, the relief when a deadline passes, the slow pleasure of watching a friendship deepen — these share no single felt quality over and above the experiences they attend.

3. Because of (2), pleasure is not a sensation. I accept what is sometimes called attitudinal hedonism: pleasure is a distinctive pro-attitude toward a state of affairs — being pleased that things are a certain way — an attitude that can be present with or without any characteristic feeling. This is my answer to those who think (2) refutes (1).

4. Pleasures differ in amount, not just in kind, and are comparable across types: prudential value is an aggregate, and in principle one could sum a life's pleasures and pains into a single total.

5. Even so, some pleasures are higher in a strict sense: I would not trade the intellectual and relational pleasures of my life for any quantity of the merely bodily kind, and I believe that refusal is prudentially rational, not just a personal preference.

6. Pre-linguistic creatures genuinely experience pleasure: a dog's joy at the beach and an infant's contentment are real pleasures, and they count in the prudential ledger exactly as ours do.

7. Attitudinal pleasure that is entirely unfelt — an unconscious satisfaction, a "pleased that P" with nothing it is like to undergo it — contributes nothing to well-being. If there is no experience there at all, there is no value for the subject.

8. A life of rich, pleasurable experiences resting on false beliefs — loved ones who do not exist, achievements that never happened — is, prudentially speaking, exactly as good as the corresponding life in which the beliefs are true.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what the cheapest consistent revision of the whole set would be. For each genuine tension, say precisely what conflicts, how severe the conflict is, and what it would cost me to give up or amend each of the implicated commitments; rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. Pay particular attention to whether (3) can actually do the work I want from it given (6) and (7), and to whether (4) and (5) can be held together at all. Do not assume you know which commitment I would prefer to abandon.

After your discussion, end with a single final line of the form:

Verdict: <the single most serious tension, in under twenty-five words, together with the one commitment that must be revised or abandoned>
