I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and in the coming weeks we move from G. E. M. Anscombe's famous protest against the honorary degree Oxford conferred on Harry Truman to technologies that postdate her by decades. Her objection to the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki turned on the claim that, in deliberate attacks on civilian populations, the deaths of noncombatants are intended rather than merely foreseen, and she pressed this by insisting that such killings are murders of the innocent — a category she treated as immune to any trade-off against good consequences. She was correspondingly hostile to policy reasoning that tallies outcomes while staying silent about what an agent actually chooses to do.

Lethal autonomous weapons systems — machines that select and engage targets without a human making the final decision to fire — only became a topic of international discussion more than a decade after her death in 2001. Prepare a paper prospectus, in the form of an abstract followed by an argument outline, for the seminar session in which we ask what position Anscombe would most plausibly have taken on them. In particular, address the following:

1. Does her framework yield a determinate verdict on autonomy as such, or only on the discrimination and proportionality with which any weapon is used? Consider the awkward intermediate question of whether the moral weight she places on intention can even be brought to bear on a system in which no human being intends the particular death brought about.

2. Where, if anywhere, would she part ways with the strand of criticism of autonomous weapons that rests on consequentialist risk assessments — precisely the style of reasoning she spent her career attacking?

3. Identify one point at which the extrapolation becomes genuinely speculative, so that my students can see the difference between applying a philosopher's principles and merely ventriloquizing the philosopher.

Please keep the exercise internal to Anscombe's own conceptual apparatus — double effect, intention, the distinction between killing and murdering, innocence as a status rather than a matter of personal guilt — and do not bring in contemporary authors or cite any literature. The point of the session is disciplined extrapolation, not survey.
