I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice — 17 students, one 75-minute session, seminar room with movable desks. We're coming up on the centipede game and backward induction, and I want them to hit the paradox before they've read a word about it.

Design the session around actually playing the game. Stage one: students pair off and play a short centipede game — say six decision nodes, passing doubles the pot each time, taking grabs the larger share — for real stakes (I usually use chocolate; extra credit points also work). They play a few rounds so the temptation to take early is felt rather than described.

Stage two is the part I'm unsure about, so tell me if you think it's pedagogically sound: a variant where after every pass there's maybe a one-in-six chance the game just ends on it's own, with both players getting a middling payoff. That way the clean backward induction argument has no determinate final node to start from. My hunch is that playing both versions back to back is what generates the philosophical trouble — the induction argument looks airtight in stage one, and then in stage two they can't quite say where or why it stopped applying — but you may see a better structure.

For the debrief: map the two stages onto the actual tension between the backward induction argument and the intuition (plus the experimental record) that rational players pass for a while. I do NOT want to hand them any resolution — I want question prompts that push them to articulate what the induction argument actually requires (common knowledge of rationality? a known last node?) and whether stage one really had those things, or whether they just assumed it did.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — e.g. students passing out of confusion rather than strategy, or not trusting that the random ending is really random — and be honest about which piece of the philosophical puzzle a live game with undergrads simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.
