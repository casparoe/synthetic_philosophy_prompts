I'm a statistician. For the last fifteen years I've worked in operational hydrological forecasting: I issue probability distributions for river discharge, combine several models into a single predictive distribution, and get scored on it. That is the vocabulary I'm fluent in, so do not translate me into something more familiar. I mean: probability forecasts, proper scoring rules like the Brier score and CRPS, reliability and calibration, linear opinion pools (averaging the experts' probabilities), logarithmic opinion pools (pooling by weighted geometric mean of probabilities, which, if I am remembering the combination literature correctly, is the pooling rule that is externally Bayesian, i.e. pool-then-update on new data agrees with update-then-pool, while the linear pool is not), and combination weights, which in practice we either fix by fiat or estimate from past track records.

Restate the philosophical debate about peer disagreement in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. The question under dispute is what to do when someone you consider an epistemic peer, with the same evidence and comparable competence and no known bias, confidently disagrees with you. The positions I want translated are at least these: the equal-weight form of conciliationism, on which you should split the difference with your peer; the moderate views on which your peer's credence is simply further evidence to be weighed alongside everything else; and the steadfast views, on which your own reasoning can rationally survive the disagreement unchanged.

Then give me the residue report. My working suspicion is that the whole debate is a dispute about pooling weights: equal-weight conciliationism is the equal-weight linear pool, treating the peer's credence as evidence is what I do whenever I ingest another model's output as a combination input, and steadfastness is the degenerate scheme that puts weight one on yourself. In forecasting nobody argues about this, because weights are settled by scoring rules over a portfolio of past forecasts. So my suspicion is that the translated debate dissolves into an empirical question. I want to know whether that is right, and I want the residue taken seriously rather than waved away: which parts of the philosophers' positions translate cleanly, which have to be distorted to fit, and whether anything has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Two residue candidates I can already see: the philosophers seem to think there is a fact about what one ought to believe in the single case, where my vocabulary only ever justifies a combination rule by long-run performance; and they attach some first-personal significance to re-examining one's own reasoning that I cannot state as anything other than a weight choice. Is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, or a sign that the original debate is less clear than it looks?

Please take the role of a formal epistemologist at home in both the philosophy and the statistics, and think carefully before answering. Start by brainstorming: list the positions, list candidate correspondences in the pooling vocabulary, and list candidate residue items, without worrying about tidiness. Then organize the final answer into the translation itself, the distortion report, the inexpressibles, and a verdict on my suspicion.
