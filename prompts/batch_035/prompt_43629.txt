My brother and I have been arguing for months about whether "technically true" is a real moral category or just a loophole people feel smug about using. He's basically Kantian about it: false assertions go in one bucket, everything else is just conversation, and a person who engineers a false belief using only true sentences is at worst rude. I want to push back, so I've been cataloguing my actual gut reactions to cases, but they don't line up the way either of us wants them to, so I'm hoping you can tell me what principle, if any, is actually behind my verdicts.

Here's the list. Misleading the murderer at the door — he asks "is anyone else in the house?" and I say "the kids are all at school," which is true, while my friend hides upstairs — feels completely fine, no residue at all, exactly as fine as lying to him would be. A con man who never says anything false, just carefully lets my grandma connect the dots about a charity that doesn't exist: misleading only, and it feels worse than most lies I can think of. My roommate asking me point blank "did you eat my leftovers?" and me saying "I've been ordering takeout all week" (true — I also ate his food): that feels nearly as bad as flatly saying no, which bugs me, because by my brother's lights it should be way better. A recommendation letter for a mediocre candidate that says only true things — "his attendance was excellent" — feels not fine when the reader trusts me, but fine when everyone in that field knows you read letters between the lines, which is confusing because the sentence is identical in both cases. Telling my nephew the tooth fairy left the dollar under his pillow: I literally left the dollar, every word true, totally fine, and I'd feel weird claiming otherwise. Then the datum that really gets me: comforting grandma with "it was delicious" (a lie) versus comforting her with "I've never tasted anything like it" (true, engineered to mislead) — and the misleading one actually feels a little worse to me, like I'm keeping my own hands clean while feeding her the very same false belief, which is definately not what my brother's picture predicts.

So my reactions seem to track whether someone is relying on me and what they're exposed to, not the assertion-versus-implicature line — yet I still feel there's something distinctively slimy about engineered misleading that doesn't reduce to harm or trust. Can you give me a principle, or a couple of candidates, that fits all of this? If it helps, the case I keep going back and forth on is my partner asking "is there anything going on with you and Dana" and me answering "you know you're the one I love" — all true — and I'd want to know whether whatever principle I should accept says that's fine. I'd also want its verdict on a dying relative I comfort with a technically true thing that gives false hope ("the doctors haven't said anything final") versus telling them a kind outright lie. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — either the principle, or your verdict that no single one fits — and then walk me through the reasoning; no preamble, and please don't restate all my cases back to me.
