I am preparing for a departmental seminar talk in which I will defend the following thesis: Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes is well suited to retrospective rational reconstruction of large-scale scientific change, but it breaks down at intermediate timescales, and it should therefore be explicitly relativized to a stated timescale. A programme can be locally degenerating over a decade of crisis-driven patching while remaining globally progressive across a century, and conversely a flush of novel predictive successes can make a doomed programme look healthy for a generation. My illustrative cases are (a) the protracted late-period patching of classical ether theories, which kept producing "novel" accommodation of experimental anomalies right up to the point where it didn't, and (b) plate tectonics, which looked hopeless on the timescale of its early continental-drift ancestor but progressive when the belt of auxiliary theory was rebuilt around it. I argue that appraisals of "progressive" or "degenerating" are only well-formed relative to a specified timescale, and that failure to state one explains much of the disagreement in the literature about whether this or that real programme was degenerating.

The audience: a philosophy of science department with no card-carrying Lakatosians. It includes a historian of science who regards rational reconstruction as an inherently dishonest genre — imposing a tidy dialectic on what was actually a mess of contingency, priority disputes, and instrumental inertia; a committed Bayesian who thinks theory appraisal should simply be modeled as credence updating and suspects the whole hard-core/protective-belt apparatus is either reducible to that or empty; a Popperian who thinks Lakatos gave away the falsificationist store and that my timescale relativization completes the capitulation; and a handful of graduate students who mostly know Lakatos from a survey course and will ask the textbook questions, some of which are sharper than the faculty realize.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. List roughly eight objections I should actually expect from this specific room, ordered by the damage they would do to my thesis, each phrased in the vocabulary that particular questioner would use — the historian's, the Bayesian's, the Popperian's, and the students' — with a two-minute reply to each and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Where an objection has genuine force, say so and tell me what I should concede up front rather than fight. Also flag the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous and what my least-bad answer is. Finally, tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and what I should do if it doesn't.
