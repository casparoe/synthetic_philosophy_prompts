The question: what must a theory that ranks possible worlds deliver to count as adequate, when the number of value-bearing individuals in a world may be infinite? Take the purpose to be fixed: the theory is good enough only if it can guide real choices between actions whose consequences plausibly extend over infinitely many people or infinitely long futures — the situation faced by, say, a grantmaker deciding between interventions under the assumption that civilization or the universe never ends. Adequacy is relative to that purpose, so any desideratum you propose should be motivated by it, not by taste.

I'll give you the cases I want the list to handle; these are the phenomena, so to speak, and the desiderata should be reverse-engineered from them plus anything else you judge essential:

1. Alpha and Beta. Alpha contains a person at each position 1, 2, 3, …, all with welfare 10. Beta is exactly Alpha, plus one additional person at each position, each with welfare 1. Nobody in Beta is worse off than in Alpha, and Beta contains extra people with lives worth living. But Beta's average welfare is far lower than Alpha's, and both worlds have infinite total welfare, so both "sum to infinity." Is Beta better, worse, or neither? The theory owes us a verdict here that we can justify without hand-waving.

2. Gamma and Gamma-shift. Gamma contains a person at each position n with welfare n (so welfare rises without bound and never totals to anything). Gamma-shift is Gamma with the indices advanced by one: the person at position n has welfare n + 1. Gamma-shift seems to Pareto-dominate Gamma — everyone is better off. Yet Gamma-shift also seems to be nothing but Gamma relabeled: a one-step shift of an infinite sequence with no start maps it back onto itself. The theory must say something about whether "better," "equal," or "incomparable" is right, and why a force that generates an infinite ascending chain of strict improvements (Gamma < Gamma-shift < Gamma-shift-shift < …) does or doesn't collapse.

3. Partial-sum dominance. One intervention yields world A, another world B, where for every finite stage t, the total value accrued in A's first t positions strictly exceeds that in B's first t positions — yet both worlds' values grow without bound, so both are "infinite." A theory that says all infinite worlds are equally good renders the grantmaker indifferent between these interventions. Is any version of "if every finite prefix is better, the whole is better" a genuine desideratum, or a prejudice of those unwilling to give up finitary habits?

Beyond these cases, consider the structural requirements the theory might need: extension agreement (restricted to finite worlds, the theory must reproduce our settled finite verdicts, whatever those are taken to be); permutation and location invariance (relabeling people, or reflecting their positions in space or time, without changing anyone's life, must not change the ranking); transitivity and completeness of the betterness relation, at least within some specified domain; non-arbitrariness (no dependence on an unexplained cutoff, discount rate, or "privileged" generation unless that feature is itself justified); and nontriviality (the theory must sometimes rank — a ranking that returns "equally good" for every pair of infinite worlds fails the grantmaker even if it satisfies everything else, so decide where this belongs on your list).
