Two friends of mine have been arguing for years about the Lucas-Penrose stuff and have finally agreed to an adversarial collab. One is a mechanist (human mathematical thinking is, at bottom, computation, and nothing in Gödel threatens that); the other is an anti-mechanist in the Lucas school (the Gödel theorems show human mathematical understanding outruns any machine). They asked me to draft the joint statement and I want your help. You're a philosopher of logic and mind who has actually read both sides' strongest papers -- think carefully before writing, maybe brainstorm the structure first.

The document should have three parts. First, the claims both sides can sign: what the incompleteness theorems actually establish (about recursively axiomatizable, consistent systems), that no machine has been proven to reproduce human mathematical insight, and that the pop versions -- "Gödel proved the mind isn't a machine" or "a system can't understand itself" -- are folklore neither of them endorses. Second, the residual disagreements stated precisely, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. I especially want them to get exact about the two hardest spots: (a) the anti-mechanist's step requires the human to see that the machine's Gödel sentence is true, which presupposes the machine is consistent -- may the human presuppose it, or know it, and is that even well-defined when no one has told us which machine we're up against (the mechanist is allowed to keep adding axioms, even axioms about Lucas himself)? and (b) human mathematicians have made errors throughout history; can the anti-mechanist lean on an idealized error-free mathematician, or is the idealization doing all the work? Third, the cruxes -- subquestions such that settling one would move at least one side -- and for each, an argument or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction registered in advance. For the fallibility crux, for instance, consider a machine that errs exactly as often and in exactly the kinds of ways human mathematicians historically did: does the Gödelian move still get a grip on it? Record what each side predicts and why.

If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal -- say, over what "seeing the truth of" a sentence amounts to -- flag which part and why. And at the end, please say where you think your own drafting is most likely slanted or just wrong, and what would make you redraw the line between the agreed and the disputed sections.
