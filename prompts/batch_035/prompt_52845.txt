Exam question: "Answer both parts. (a) Computational functionalists hold that whether a system has a mind — and so whether it can matter morally — depends on the computations it performs, not on the material it is made of. Explain this substrate-independence thesis, say why its defenders regard resistance to it as a form of chauvinism, and present one thought experiment standardly used against it. (b) The attribution problem: critics argue that if computation is individuated too liberally, then among the physical systems implementing any given computation we will find absurd candidates for sentience — a wall, an economy, a weather system. Explain why this is a problem for the functionalist and how she might respond, and say whether the possibility that the boundaries of sentience are indeterminate should change how we treat borderline systems."

Student's answer: "Substrate independence says that what matters for having a mind is the functional organization of a system, not its physical composition. If silicon circuitry duplicates the organization of my brain, it duplicates my mind; denying this is like the vitalists insisting that life required an élan vital rather than ordinary chemistry — a chauvinism about substrate. The thesis also explains why speed of implementation is irrelevant: the same computation run a thousand times slower on a clunky machine still implements the same functional organization, since organization is defined over the sequence of state-transitions, not how fast they occur. The strongest objection is Putnam's China Brain: if the population of China were organized to mimic the causal organization of a brain, functionalism would entail that the nation as a whole has a mind, which is absurd. The functionalist's best reply is that the nation would behave exactly like the brain: given the same input-output mapping, it is just a slow, strange implementation, and the intuition that it lacks a mind is more substrate chauvinism. Notice the same reasoning shows that a giant lookup table is conscious, too, since it produces the same behavior. For part (b), the attribution problem is a problem of finding which computations a system actually performs. If we describe an economy's transaction ledger in the right formalism, it may implement the same computation as some nervous system, so functionalism seems to entail sentience where common sense finds none. But this is mainly an epistemic difficulty: the reason we don't know whether the economy feels anything is that we lack the formal tools and instruments to determine what it computes, just as we once lacked microscopes. Once we have the right way of individuating computations, the borderline cases will be resolved the way better microscopes resolved questions about micro-organisms. Since there is always a fact of the matter about what a system computes, borderline cases are cases of our ignorance, so we should treat clearly conscious systems normally and await better science on the borderline ones."
