I'm the on-call incident commander for a county search-and-rescue squad: when a call comes in where we can reach one group of casualties or another but not both before weather or injuries decide for us, I'm the person who says which one we go to. This arises for real perhaps twice a year, and when it does I have minutes, not hours; I'm often at the end of a long shift; and whatever I choose is written down and may later be picked apart by a coroner's inquest, an insurer, and above all by the families. Our standing protocol says to save the greater number, full stop, and the team follows it — but none of us can explain to the family of the person not chosen why the fact that there were three casualties on the other ridge meant their son got nothing. Not a smaller chance: nothing.

I've read enough of the philosophical debate around this — the one going back to John Taurek's 1977 paper arguing that the numbers don't count and that each imperiled person has an equal claim to a chance at rescue, rather than the many simply swallowing the few — to want it as an operating procedure rather than an opinion. Please build me one.

Concretely, I want a decision procedure the duty officer can actually run in the field: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a determinate instruction, and where it must stop and hand the decision back to a human because the remaining question is one of values that no procedure can settle. The procedure has to choose among — and justify choosing among — three candidate cores: (a) equal chances for every threatened person regardless of numbers, the strictly Taurekan coin flip between one and five, or one and fifty; (b) a weighted lottery, in which the lone casualty still gets some real chance but the larger group gets more, a proposal philosophers developed precisely to split the difference between Taurek and the counting view; and (c) straightforwardly maximizing expected lives saved, with the lottery logic dropped. Then tell me explicitly what the chosen core does at the hard edges: whether equal chances survive when the disparity becomes extreme (one swimmer against a tour bus of thirty); whether refusing to count numbers also commits me to refusing to count life-years — a rescued child with sixty years ahead against five rescued patients with months — or whether there is a principled way to keep numbers out while letting duration in; and how the procedure handles asymmetric parties, such as casualties who created their own emergency by ignoring a closure notice, a group that can be saved only all together, or a victim who is already beyond saving when we arrive.

Then run the procedure on three calls:

1. The clean case: one stranded climber on the north ridge, three hikers with comparable injuries on the south, and the helicopter can make only one round before dark.
2. The case whose answer I will not like: a single swimmer in a flooded quarry versus a van of fifteen trapped on a rising road. Whatever the procedure says here, I want to see whether it can actually say it, or whether it breaks and has to admit that at some magnitude nearly everyone abandons the coin flip — and, if so, where exactly the procedure places that cliff and on what grounds.
3. The partiality case: the sole casualty on one side of the mountain is my own brother, a member of the squad; Taurek's view is famous for permitting me to simply go get him. Tell me what the procedure does with that — whether it permits partiality, caps it, or routes it to a marked judgment point — and what I should put in the log afterward.

Be honest about the failure modes: where an equal-chance procedure will often enough produce a worse outcome for everyone that a coroner or jury will read it as negligence rather than fairness; where paperwork pressure will tempt an officer to dress a values judgment up as a procedural output; and what the procedure cannot do at all. If a step can't be completed before the weather decides, the procedure needs a default rule — tell me what the default should be and why. Write the whole answer in American English.
