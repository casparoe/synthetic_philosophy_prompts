I have just started my first term as a teaching assistant, and I am now grading my first set of final examinations for an undergraduate course on Kierkegaard. One answer has me hesitating over the mark, so before I commit I would like a second opinion on how well it actually holds up.

The exam question read: Johannes de Silentio claims in Fear and Trembling that Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac cannot be justified ethically. (a) Explain what the teleological suspension of the ethical is supposed to mean, and why de Silentio insists that Abraham cannot be defended as a tragic hero. (b) Is the suspension, so understood, coherent — can there be a duty that is not a universal duty, and if so, what makes it a duty at all? (c) Briefly, does the suspension leave Abraham able to communicate or justify his act to others?

The student wrote: De Silentio's background assumption is Hegelian: the ethical is the universal, and the ethical person annuls himself as a particular in order to express the universal. Ethics is thus itself teleological — it directs life toward the universal as its end. A teleological suspension of the ethical means that this end can be set aside for the sake of a higher end. God's promise that a great nation would come from Isaac is a higher good than the prohibition on killing, and when the two conflict the higher good wins; the ethical is suspended the way any lower end is suspended when it obstructs a higher one. On this reading Abraham is making a rational bet on divine reward: since God had promised him descendants through Isaac, Abraham had every reason to expect his loss to be restored, and his willingness to proceed was prudent on his own evidence. This is why de Silentio says the tragic hero is the highest expression of the ethical, and Abraham is a tragic hero: Agamemnon, who sacrificed Iphigenia for the welfare of the fleet and the state, is a case of exactly the same structure — the father gives up what is dearest to him for the sake of a greater end, and everyone can understand and admire the sacrifice. The difference between Abraham and Agamemnon is one of magnitude: the state is a great good, but God's promise is the greatest. There is therefore no real paradox. Faith is ordinary heroism at maximum intensity, and the knight of faith is the tragic hero with the stakes raised. Nor is there any problem of communication: because the reason was in principle statable — God's plan — Abraham could have explained himself to anyone, just as Agamemnon could. So a duty to God is simply a higher-order duty that overrides the universal when they conflict, and there is nothing strange about duties that are not universal, since any moral hierarchy already ranks some duties above others.
