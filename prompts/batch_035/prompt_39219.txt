I do research on value learning for autonomous systems, and I find myself genuinely torn between two positions I can't reconcile on my own. To stress-test both, I'd like you to write a dialogue between the two people I keep hearing in my head, and I'd like each of them to be genuinely formidable — neither side a strawman, and neither side allowed to win by accident.

The characters: a researcher who believes inverse reinforcement learning is on track to recovering human preferences from behavior, and a skeptic trained in behavioral economics who thinks the entire project inherits the classic defects of revealed-preference theory. The setting is a lab meeting where they are reviewing one year of data from a home-assistant robot that has been inferring a single reward function from a single household of four — two working parents, a teenager, and a grandmother with early-stage dementia.

I'd like the argument to run through some concrete material rather than abstractions. In particular, the skeptic should press on the following cases drawn from the data. The Tuesday takeout case: the family orders in every Tuesday, not because they prefer takeout, but because both parents work late and the household schedule simply collapses on Tuesdays; the robot has inferred a stable preference where there is only a constraint. The cold-apartment case: the family "prefers" to keep the thermostat at 17 degrees, but only since learning they cannot afford to heat the place properly — the Elster-style problem of adaptive preferences, in which preferences deform themselves around constraints and an inference procedure will faithfully recover the deformed preferences. The aggregation problem: whose reward function is this, exactly, when the grandmother does most of the cooking and the teenager controls the thermostat, so that behavior over-weights some family members by mere accident of chore allocation? And drift: a baby arrived in month four, and the robot treats the year as a single stationary preference.

The optimist, for their part, should score real points. She should defend the Boltzmann-rationality assumption as a principled way to model suboptimality rather than an admission of failure; she should note that the classical identifiability worry — rewards differing by a potential-based shaping term induce the same optimal policy — is a problem for predicting behavior, not necessarily for ranking policies or choosing interventions; she should stress that the robot can query counterfactuals that survey methods never could; and she should argue that, however noisy, a year of fine-grained behavioral data is a better evidence base than anything the social sciences have ever had for what people want.

Crucially, the two must actually engage with each other's points rather than talking past each other. For example: when the optimist replies to the Tuesday case that the robot can simply be given richer state — work schedules, income, the child's school calendar — and will then learn to distinguish constraints from preferences, the skeptic should come back hard with the observation that the constraint/preference distinction is not in the data but in the interpretation, and, more disturbingly, that the robot's own year of presence in the home has partly constituted the routines it now treats as evidence of stable preferences. When the skeptic argues that IRL merely recovers deformed preferences, the optimist should reply that the deformation claim itself presupposes a model of what the family would have preferred absent the constraint — and that, once stated precisely, that model looks suspiciously like the very inference IRL performs, only performed worse and without the data.

Let the dialogue end at a genuine impasse rather than a tidy synthesis. The final stretch should have the two locate precisely what they disagree about — whether "preference" is a behavioral construct that inference from behavior can in principle capture, or a psychological state that behavior systematically underdetermines — and each should name, concretely, what evidence or argument would move them. I want to come away from the dialogue understanding the strongest version of both sides, not knowing whom to side with.
