I'm an applied mathematician — PDEs, numerical analysis, enough functional analysis to be comfortable with sequence spaces, summability methods, and Banach limits — with no serious philosophy background. In my world, a sum that doesn't converge isn't the end of the discussion: you choose a regularization scheme (Abel, Cesàro, zeta), you check which properties the scheme preserves, and you're explicit that the value you get is a property of the scheme as much as of the series. I've recently gathered that utilitarian ethics runs into what looks like exactly this problem when the number of welfare-bearing lives is infinite — infinitely many future generations, or an endless universe — and I want the philosophical debate restated in my vocabulary, honestly, rather than flattened into it.

Translate the main positions one at a time. First, the claim that total utilitarian aggregation simply breaks down when every feasible world contains infinitely much welfare, so that maximization becomes vacuous — including the observation that removing one miserable life from an infinite paradise, or adding one happy life to it, leaves the "total" unchanged though the worlds seem strictly better and worse. Second, the order-dependence worry: that summing welfare over an infinite population or an infinite future depends on an arbitrary enumeration of the lives. Third, the proposed repairs — discounting future welfare until the sums converge, comparing infinite streams by which one's partial sums eventually dominate (the "overtaking" idea), and reaching for finitely additive measures or hyperreal numbers so that infinite totals mean something again. For each, tell me what the position becomes when said in terms of convergence, absolute versus conditional convergence, rearrangement, regularization schemes, and linear functionals on sequence spaces — and keep the translation honest: if the mathematics has to presuppose that lives can be put on a single scale, and that presupposition is itself part of what philosophers dispute, say so rather than smuggling it in.

Then I want the residue report. Which parts translate cleanly — my suspicion is that the anonymity requirement is exactly permutation-invariance of the sum, and the rearrangement theorem then says precisely when anonymity must fail, which would be a nice case of two fields discovering the same wall. Which parts translate only with distortion? And which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? In particular: when two regularization schemes or two extensions of the limit functional disagree on an infinite-welfare comparison, is that disagreement a real moral dispute, or just the familiar fact that Hahn–Banach extensions are non-unique — and if it's the latter, is that underdetermination itself the substantive philosophical finding, the analogue of my having to say "the value belongs to the method, not the series"? Finish by telling me where the residue points: does it show my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers need, or does it show that some of their claims were never well-posed to begin with, and how would someone in either field go about telling those two diagnoses apart?
