I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on moral uncertainty, and we've just finished the unit on how to act when you're torn between moral theories. A coursemate of mine, Dara, has been unusually consistent about her own case across our discussion sections, and I want help predicting her verdicts on some new cases — and, more than that, working out the decision rule she is implicitly running. Please respond as an expert in decision theory and normative ethics: think carefully, start with some brainstorming of the candidate rules before committing to any prediction, and be explicit about your reasoning and your confidence at each step.

Here is Dara's setup and track record. She assigns about 60% credence to a fairly demanding act-consequentialism (maximize welfare) and about 40% to a Kantian view on which lying, promise-breaking, and theft are hard constraints, while beneficence is an imperfect duty: some serious concern for others is required, but the level is left to reasonable discretion, and going beyond it is admirable rather than obligatory.

Her verdicts so far, all cases where the two theories disagreed:
- She would not tell a white lie to spare a host's feelings about a disastrous dish; she deflected with something true instead.
- She kept a promise to help a friend move on a day she could instead have volunteered somewhere she grants the time would have done slightly more good.
- She gives a few percent of her income and helps friends in need, but refuses to raise her giving toward the level consequentialism demands, calling the extra admirable but not required.
- She would not quietly take money from a wealthy friend who would never notice it missing, even though the money, donated, would do far more good than it does sitting in his account.
- She declined to register as a bone-marrow donor, though the risk to her is small and the expected benefit to a stranger is large.

Now the new cases, which I'd like you to predict:
(1) An abusive ex-partner asks Dara where her friend is staying, in circumstances that make it clear he intends to find her and do her serious harm, and the friend's safety depends on Dara's lying. Will Dara lie?
(2) Dara turns out to be a compatible kidney donor for a stranger; the surgical risk to her is small and the benefit to the recipient is large. Will she donate — and will she regard donating as required, or merely as allowed?

In answering, please do four things. First, lay out the decision rules that fit her track record — I'd expect at least a constraint-supremacy rule (the Kantian side's constraints win regardless of stakes), an asymmetric-permission rule (never do what either theory forbids; failing to do extra good is never wrong), and versions of expected-choiceworthiness maximization with adjustable intertheoretic weights — but tell me if I'm missing better candidates, or if some of these collapse into each other, and be honest about the fact that expected-choiceworthiness rules can be tuned to fit almost any track record if the weights are free. Second, say where the rules that fit her record diverge on the two new cases, noting which of the rules speak only to what is permitted rather than to what she will choose, and give your best guess for each case with reasons. Third, say which of her existing verdicts is the closest analog for each new case. And fourth, name the single further case whose verdict would best discriminate among the surviving rules — and explain why that one, rather than a case that merely raises the stakes again.
