As part of our moderation of marks, I am asking a colleague to regrade one tutorial essay before I finalize the class's returns. The essay was written for a second-year paper on philosophy of religion, in response to a question I set on A. J. Ayer's treatment of life after death. Please give it a mark out of 20 and the specific comments that justify the mark — a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression — taking care to distinguish what the student gets right from anything a hasty grader might let slide.

The question I set was:

"A. J. Ayer held that, strictly speaking, talk of life after death is meaningless. Does his argument succeed? And does it apply with equal force to the claim that death is the annihilation of the self?"

The student submitted the following:

"Ayer's argument rests on his verification principle: a sentence is factually significant only if we know what observations would count for or against the proposition it purports to express. Where no possible observations could bear on a sentence, the sentence does not express a genuine proposition at all; it merely looks like one. In Language, Truth and Logic he applies this to claims about God and, by extension, to claims about survival after death. The sentence 'there is a God' is not, for Ayer, false; rather, as he puts it, such a sentence functions like an exclamation — an expression of feeling with no factual content.

I think the argument fails. First, the theist can supply the missing observations: if intercessory prayer were systematically answered, or if the universe displayed features best explained by design, those would be observations counting for God's existence. So God-talk is at least weakly verifiable in Ayer's own terms, and his argument does not get the conclusion he wants. Second, the argument proves too much: by the same reasoning, the claim that death is the irreversible end of experience would also be unverifiable, since no observations could confirm or disconfirm it, which he did not attempt to verify. This is a reductio of the verification principle itself: if it classes the sentence 'there is a God' with an exclamation, it must equally class 'death is annihilation' with an exclamation of horror, and Ayer is in no position to assert annihilation either.

Ayer's conclusion is that all religious language is meaningless. He was eventually forced to soften the principle; as he himself conceded in Language, Truth and Logic, no proposition can be verified conclusively, so 'weak' verification has to be allowed, and the argument collapses. Perhaps talk of an afterlife is best treated as symbolic rather than factual — but then it is not false, as believers intend it, and Ayer's attack misses its target."

In your comments, please engage the substance rather than the style: weigh the accuracy of the exposition of Ayer, the cogency of the two objections, and the treatment of the annihilation side of the question. Please also state how confident you are in each of your main verdicts, and single out the single point in your assessment about which you are least sure.
