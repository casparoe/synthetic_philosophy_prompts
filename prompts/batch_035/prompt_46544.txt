I'm taking an introductory ethics course, and last week we discussed whether the wrongness of factory farming can be established just by pointing at the facts: the overcrowding, the mutilations, the documented suffering of the animals involved. When someone in my section argued that the suffering alone settles the moral question, a classmate cut in with the old line that you can't get an ought from an is: all the facts in the world about what pigs feel, she said, won't by themselves yield the conclusion that we ought to stop, and so the argument was a non-starter. Our professor didn't take a side but muttered something about the slogan "doing several jobs at once," which has stuck with me, because I suspect the classmate and the person making the suffering argument may not even have been disagreeing about anything — the slogan may have been doing work it can't actually do.

Here's what I'd like from you. Take the one-liner and lay out, as a structured menu, the distinct precise claims it could be expressing — I'd guess they run from the near-tautological to the wildly controversial, and I want them separated. For instance, one reading is presumably just a point of logic: no set of purely descriptive premises formally entails a normative conclusion without at least one normative premise. Another might be a semantic or definitional claim in the spirit of Moore — that moral terms can't be defined in terms of natural facts, so that any proposed identification of goodness with, say, pleasure or the satisfaction of preferences leaves an open question. Others might be epistemological (empirical evidence can never justify a moral belief), psychological (no amount of factual information motivates by itself — a reading with Humean flavor about the passions), or even a claim about what arguments in ethics are allowed to look like in practice. I'm not confident I've carved it at the joints, so correct and extend my list.

Then give verdicts. Which readings are trivial but true, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are probably false but doing the real rhetorical work when people repeat the phrase? Crucially, tell me: under any of the plausible readings, does the slogan actually support what my classmate used it for — blocking an inference from facts about suffering to a conclusion about wrongness? It seems to me that the weakest reading is true but idle (no good argument is a bare formal entailment anyway, and the suffering argument could just help itself to an explicit normative premise like "inflicting severe suffering on sentient creatures for minor benefit is wrong," which nearly everyone accepts), while the stronger readings that would do the blocking are exactly the ones almost no one believes. Finally, since the course also keeps invoking Moore's "naturalistic fallacy" as if it were the same point: say whether the is/ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy are genuinely the same complaint, neighboring but distinct complaints, or commonly conflated in a way that matters for this case. I'd like something I can bring to section, so please be explicit about which reading my classmate most likely meant and what I could say to her that isn't just talking past her.
