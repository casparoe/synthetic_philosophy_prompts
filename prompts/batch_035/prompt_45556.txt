so, full disclosure: I write novels for a living — literary historical stuff mostly, two books set in the 1890s, currently one in the 1930s — and last month I got seated next to a historian at a wedding, which was a mistake. she was very nice about it, but her whole vibe was that my job is the thing she has to consciously not do every day: I invent, she reports. I get to decide where the story starts and whose head we're inside; she just... writes down what happened. then a week later I'm at a friend's place and pull Hayden White's Metahistory (1973) off the shelf, and lo and behold, he spends hundreds of pages arguing that historians are basically doing my job in disguise — that they emplot the same pile of facts into tragedy or comedy or romance or satire the way a novelist picks a genre, that the narrative form is the content, and that Ranke's old ideal of showing the past as it actually was is mostly a costume. and now I can't stop thinking about it, because I know exactly how many pure choices go into a novel, and if the same machinery runs historiography, the whole "I report, you invent" thing collapses.

help me audit the analogy. give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of plot, of emplotment (the genre choice), of character, of point of view, and, crucially, of the archive, since that's the thing I don't have — then say where the mapping breaks. I'd especially like to hear from someone who knows the Carr side of this too (the bit about the historian selecting and interpreting being unavoidable) versus the Rankean idea that the facts constrain you. some disanalogies will matter more than others: presumably I can write that my character went to Lisbon on the 4th when the sources say she went on the 7th, and a historian can't — but I can also decide her chapter opens with her mother's death rather than her wedding, and no fact of the matter rules on that, and White's point seems to be that history is full of second kind of decision and pretends they're all the first kind. so which breaks in the analogy actually threaten the conclusion (objectivity in history is a myth, it's literature with footnotes) and which ones don't?

and then the part I care about most, given my day job: does the analogy even do what the sceptics want it to do? it's one thing to say the historian makes narrative choices like I do; it's another to say those choices add up to fiction. I make choices, but my choices don't have to survive a hostile reader checking them against a source base — unless the claim is that "being constrained by evidence" and "being answerable to evidence" are just more plot devices, which honestly sounds like it's smuggling in something about my trade that isn't there. if the novel analogy is carrying more weight than it can hold, tell me what a better one would be — maybe something like reconstruction from fragments, or a translation, or a court judgment, whatever you think actually fits — and where that leaves the Ranke camp and the Carr/White camp at the end. keep it plain, I did not study philosophy, I just eavesdrop on it at weddings.
