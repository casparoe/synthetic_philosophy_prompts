I teach a mixed bunch of sixth formers at a state school in Leeds (15 of them, Year 12, doing classical civilisation, and we can get two 45-minute sessions in the same week) and this term we're on Athenian democracy's car-crash moments. Rather than just lecturing on the Mytilene affair, I want them to actually live through it, and I'm hoping you can design the whole thing. Quick recap in case it helps you get the setting right: in 428/427 BC Mytilene revolted against Athens with Spartan backing, the Athenian assembly voted to execute the entire male population and enslave the women and children, a trireme was dispatched to carry it out, and then a second assembly was held the next day and reversed the decision — a second ship had to be rowed flat out to overtake the first. Thucydides gives us both speeches from the second debate: Cleon arguing that harshness is what imperial security requires, and Diodotus, whose move I find most interesting — he explicitly refuses to argue from mercy, and instead attacks the *procedures* of the debate itself, pointing out that a city which puts speakers under suspicion when they advocate for the defeated will end up hearing only bad arguments.

Here's my problem: my students are nice kids. If I just say "you're the Athenian assembly, vote," they'll vote mercifully on day one and there's no drama, no reversal, nothing to debrief. So the first assembly has to actually go harsh — I need engineered incentives, not hope. Maybe role cards with private interests (generals terrified of looking soft on revolts, families who lost people or money, allies' representatives watching, a few genuine moderates), maybe some payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money, maybe rules about speaking time or who's allowed to speak when. Then in the second session, one or two students need the Diodotus role — the job of flipping the majority not by appealing to compassion but by reframing the question and gaming the procedure, and I want the class to viscerally feel that the same facts plus a different framing turned a bloodthirsty majority around.

So please design: the setup, the role cards, the voting mechanics across both sessions, the payoffs, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the institutional failure modes of direct democracy — deciding irreversible policy in one afternoon, emotional momentum, speakers' incentives to outbid each other, the way accountability after the fact (people being punished for their speeches, which is what happened after Arginusae) makes deliberation worse. Then a short extension showing how the same machinery explains the enthusiasm for the Sicilian expedition or the vote to execute Socrates. Also be honest about what a classroom version can't possibly reproduce — the real stakes, the plague, the genuine fear of losing the empire — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm braced for one kid grandstanding as Cleon for laughs, and for the second debate collapsing into modern moralising about how obviously slavery is bad, which rather misses the point). Oh, and could you write the whole thing up in American English? My TA is American and she'll be adapting it for her own class, so I don't want her having to translate. Sorry for the long brief, and thankyou in advance!
