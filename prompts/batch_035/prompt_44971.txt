I left school at seventeen and have worked in commercial kitchens ever since, but for the past couple of years I've been reading philosophy on my own in the evenings, and the idea that has stuck with me hardest is Grice's: that most of what we manage to say to each other is never actually in the words. A reference letter that reads "she has excellent handwriting and never missed a deadline" condemns the candidate precisely by being true and stopping there. From what I understand, the trick is that conversation rests on assumptions nobody states outright — say enough, say what's true, stay relevant, don't ramble — and because both parties know the rules are in force, a hearer can work backwards from what you did say to what you must have been implying by saying only that. The meaning isn't hidden in the sentence; it lives in the space between what was said and what could have been.

I want to give this idea to my reading group as a poem. Please write a villanelle set in a single scene: someone on a hiring panel reading a reference letter aloud for a former colleague they liked personally but cannot honestly recommend. The two refrains should be the letter's literal praise and the conclusion the reader keeps arriving at despite it. And here is the part I care about most, though I'm not sure I can articulate it well: I want the refrains to change meaning as they return. Identical words, different implication, depending on what the tercets around them have established — because that shift seems to me to be the whole philosophical point, that the same string of words can carry opposite meanings depending on what the speaker could have said and didn't. I'd like the poem to stay serious rather than turn into a comedy about coded recommendations — the reader should feel the letter curdle in their hands, not laugh at it. Please don't name Grice or any of his terms inside the poem; the concept should be enacted, not cited. If it works, a reader who knows the theory should be able to point to where each returning line picks up its new sense and explain what the surrounding lines did to cause it, and a reader who doesn't know the theory should come away with an eerie recognition of conversations they've had themselves.
