At a recent reading-group meeting I sat in on, two members spent an hour arguing about what the color phi phenomenon shows about the timing of consciousness, and I found I could not tell who had the better of it. In the phenomenon, two spots are flashed in succession at different positions, the first red and the second green, separated by a brief interval. Observers don't report seeing two discrete flashes; they report a single spot moving smoothly from the first position to the second, changing color somewhere in the middle. Since the motion and the color change are not present in the retinal stimulus, whatever the subject experiences cannot have been fully determined at the moment the first flash arrived — the brain must have waited for the second flash before settling on the content. The disagreement was about what follows from this.

(1) One member took the Stalinesque line: consciousness runs on a delay. The brain withholds "release" of the first flash until the second arrives, then composes the two into one continuous trajectory; the conscious experience therefore contains the color change from the start, and the subject never experienced a stationary red spot at all. (2) The other member took the Orwellian line: the subject first consciously registers two separate flashes, and the record is subsequently rewritten, so that what the subject remembers — and reports — is the edited file rather than the original broadcast; the rewrite is thorough enough that no trace of the pre-edit experience survives.

(3) The first member then argued that the dispute is empirically empty: any report the subject could give, however prompt, is itself produced after the editing or composing is done, so no experiment could ever distinguish a delayed-then-composed broadcast from an original-plus-rewrite. Since nothing could count as evidence either way, the question of which story is true presupposes something confused — that there is a determinate fact about when a content "arrives in consciousness," as if there were a place and time where the order of arrival matters. The timing question, he said, is like asking at what exact moment during a lecture the audience "got the point": the category doesn't apply at subpersonal timescales.

(4) The second member replied that empirical indistinguishability is not the same as nonexistence: on his account there really is a moment at which the subject consciously registers a stationary red spot, which is then erased, whereas on the first member's account no such moment ever exists — no brief flash of green-then-red experience occurs at any time, experienced or unexperienced. Those are different worlds, he said, even if the subjects can't tell them apart; we don't otherwise accept that unreportable events never happen. He added a concrete point: if the interval between the flashes is stretched beyond a certain duration, the illusion collapses and subjects accurately see two stationary flashes — so whatever process composes the experience operates under a deadline, and a deadline is a straightforward temporal fact about the brain. If the process can miss its deadline when things are slow, why deny that it has real timing when it succeeds?

(5) The first member answered that the deadline concerns when the composing process must finish its work, not when consciousness "occurs": his opponent is sliding from the time at which content is assembled to the time at which it is experienced, and the whole lesson of the phenomenon is that those can come apart — the experienced color change is located mid-trajectory, at a time when no corresponding event is happening in the world or in the cortex. Insisting that the experience itself must nonetheless have a real onset time is just re-importing the assumption the phenomenon was supposed to undermine. At that point they ran out of time, and the rest of us were left with no verdict.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right at each numbered step and why, note where either side equivocates or concedes too much, and identify anything both of them are missing. In particular, I'd like your considered view on whether the Stalinesque/Orwellian disagreement is substantive, merely verbal, or founded on a shared mistaken presupposition — and, if the last, what the presupposition is.
