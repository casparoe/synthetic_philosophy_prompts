For thirty-five years I was an experimental physical chemist, and since retiring I have been working my way through philosophy of the special sciences. I have hit a wall on the concept of utility, and a dispute at our reading group last week confirmed that I am not the only one stuck there. Two visitors joined us that evening, a retired government statistician and a clinical psychologist; I will call them S and P. Their exchange ran as follows, as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

(1) S opened by claiming that utility, as economists use it, is nothing over and above a pattern of choices: to say a person prefers tea to coffee just is to say that she picks tea when both are on offer, and the revealed-preference program associated with Samuelson was designed to define the discipline's key quantity by its measurement procedure precisely so that economics would not traffic in unobservable inner states. (2) P replied that this surrenders the explanatory work preferences were supposed to do: if preference is defined as choice, then the claim that she chose tea because she prefers tea collapses into the claim that she chose tea because she would have chosen tea, which explains nothing; and a preference can exist without any corresponding choice ever being made, as when someone prefers tea but takes coffee only because it is the sole thing available at the station. (3) S answered that P was demanding a kind of access that physics never had either: temperature became a quantity through thermometer readings long before kinetic theory explained it, and mass is fixed by its role in the laws of motion rather than by direct inspection; if choices knit together into stable demand regularities, that is exactly the footing on which other scientific quantities stand. (4) P countered that in physics the independent operationalizations converge — mercury, gas, and radiation thermometers agree — and where they failed to, as with the mercury thermometer against the constant-volume gas thermometer at high temperatures, physics concluded that the faulty procedure was tracking the wrong thing and replaced it, which is how the scale improved; but in the human case the choice-based measure, the self-report measure, and any physiological measure diverge systematically and routinely — retrospective evaluations follow a peak-end pattern insensitive to duration, people's reports of their own satisfaction adapt to circumstances it would be scandalous to endorse, and the addict's choices part ways from the addict's avowed wishes — and there is no available fact of the matter to tell us which instrument measures real utility, so the quantity fragments into scales for different things, none of which is the quantity the theory wanted. (5) S, retreating somewhat, said the discipline can live with ordinal utility alone: a within-person ranking suffices for demand analysis, and cardinal utility and interpersonal comparison can simply be dropped as extras. (6) P closed by observing that this amputates the normative half of economics: cost-benefit analysis, welfare weights, and utilitarian policy all require comparing magnitudes across persons, which an ordinal, intra-personal utility cannot support — so the choice-theoretic definition of utility and the policy-theoretic use of utility cannot both be retained, and the discipline has not noticed that it is running on both.

I could not referee this. At various points each of them reminded me of arguments I once heard in my own field about what it is that a thermometer measures, and I do not trust my sympathies in such matters. Would you adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each of the six steps, state whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing?
