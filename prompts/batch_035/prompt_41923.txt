You are an expert philosopher of law, well versed in the positivism–natural law literature. Please think carefully and work through the argument step by step before committing to a diagnosis — if it helps, brainstorm the possible escape routes first and only then choose among them.

I am a PhD student in philosophy, finishing a dissertation chapter on jurisprudence, and our Hart–Fuller reading group has produced an argument that was presented as a decisive refutation of the separation thesis. Each premise looked unassailable when it was laid out, but I have come to suspect it proves far too much, and I cannot locate the misstep. The argument runs:

(1) On legal positivism, whether an enacted norm is valid law in a given system depends on its sources — whether it was produced through that system's recognized procedures — and not on its moral merits.
(2) The Nazi statutes under which the grudge informer denounced her husband in 1944 were produced through the recognized procedures of the German legal system.
(3) So, on positivism, those statutes were valid law in 1944, and the courts that applied them were identifying the law correctly.
(4) Identifying the law correctly is all that fidelity to law demands of a judge; whatever else fidelity requires, it cannot require a judge to misidentify the law.
(5) The post-war West German court that declined to treat the informer's act as lawful — convicting her instead under the 1871 Criminal Code — therefore either misidentified the law or abandoned fidelity to law.
(6) But that decision is paradigmatically legal reasoning rather than disguised retroactive power, and a theory of law that brands it lawless is not a theory of our concept of law.
(7) Therefore the separation thesis is false: at some threshold of iniquity, legal validity does depend on moral merits.

Here is what worries me. Substitute any other valid but wicked statute — the American fugitive-slave statutes, for instance — and the same reasoning yields that judges who applied them misidentified the law, that judges who refused to apply them were legally correct without possessing any legal basis for it, and that no wicked enactment was ever really law at all. My colleague accepts all of this with equanimity. I cannot, yet I also cannot see exactly where the chain breaks: something feels unstable between (3) and (5), though I cannot name it, and if I deny that link I owe an account of what a judge's duty to apply valid law amounts to on positivist terms.

Which step should be rejected, and why did that step look fine when it was asserted? Before you commit, please sketch what each of the main escape routes — rejecting (4), rejecting (6), or accepting the conclusion and swallowing its consequences for ordinary wicked-but-valid law — has to pay, and then say which bill you would settle and why. I am less interested in which side of the debate wins than in seeing precisely where the machinery that makes the argument prove too much engages.
