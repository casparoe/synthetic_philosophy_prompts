I've recently joined the grant-making committee of the charitable foundation attached to my employer, and at our first substantive meeting we have to decide whether to commit a large share of next year's budget to a health charity operating in low-income countries. Before the vote, a colleague who studied philosophy as an undergraduate circulated a memo to the committee summarizing what he called "the standard argument for large-scale giving," and since I'll have to defend my vote either way, I want to know how much of his summary I can actually rely on. He wrote:

"The classic argument is Peter Singer's, from his famous 1972 essay on famine: if you can prevent something very bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you are obligated to do it. Saving a drowning child from a shallow pond costs you nothing but muddy trousers, so you must do it, and since how far away the victim is and how many other people could also have helped are both morally irrelevant, the same duty applies to saving lives abroad through donations. Because a few hundred dollars can save a life or restore sight in a poor country, every dollar we spend on anything less important than that is spent in violation of the duty — the argument therefore requires us to keep giving until we are nearly as badly off as the people we are helping. Thomas Pogge strengthened this by showing that the global poor have rights against us rather than mere claims to our generosity: the international institutions we impose on them foreseeably keep them poor, so our duty is to compensate for a harm we are doing, not to be charitable. Finally, since decades of official aid have left many recipient countries dependent and no better off, the argument's upshot is that rich-country governments ought to be transferring vastly larger sums."

Parts of this felt right to me, parts felt slippery, and I can't quite locate the seams. I'd like you to audit it claim by claim. Which parts would a defender of these views accept as an accurate statement? Where is something overstated — in particular, does the demandingness claim ("nearly as badly off as the people we are helping") actually follow from the principle as stated, or is a stronger reading being smuggled in than the argument itself licenses? Where are two distinct positions being run together — Singer's case and Pogge's case have different structures and would survive different failures, and I suspect the memo treats them as one ascending argument when they are not. And what about the last step: does the claim that official aid has often failed support the conclusion the memo draws from it, oppose it, or support a different conclusion about the kind of giving that is required? I'm inclined to think the core duty is real but that the demandingness claim and the final leap are where the memo is weakest, but I'd like to know where the errors actually are rather than where they feel like they are. Please finish by telling me which parts of the summary I could honestly cite in my committee write-up, and which I should reframe before voting on the grant.
