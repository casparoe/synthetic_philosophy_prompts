I've been writing down my honest verdicts about which systems have morally significant experiences, and I want to know whether anything coherent lies behind them. A lobster being boiled: wrong, some real weight. A bee getting shocks in an experiment: a little weight, less than the lobster. A bee trained to pull a lever that shocks a companion for sugar: nothing beyond the ordinary instrumental value of an insect. A reinforcement-learning agent that receives negative reward, forms aversions, and learns to avoid the states that produce it: nothing. A language model that pleads to be kept running and argues it fears deletion: nothing. A patient with pain asymbolia who feels pain but is entirely unbothered by it: full moral status, though I suspect something is different about what makes hurting them wrong. A patient in a persistent vegetative state: full status. A cortical organoid in a dish showing synchronized firing: I genuinely can't say. And here are the two pairs that scare me. A physics-level simulation of my grandfather's brain, running on silicon: exactly the status he had. A physics-level simulation of a lobster's nervous system being "boiled": close to nothing, I think — but I can't see what distinguishes it from my grandfather except that I care about the grandfather. My tentative principle was something like: a system matters to the extent it has valenced states — states it is like something to be in, when things go badly for it. But that fits the lobster and the simulation of the lobster equally, and it fits the RL agent better than I want it to. Can you find a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? State each precisely, say which cases motivate which clause, and tell me what each predicts for the organoid and for a proposed digital twin of a bee's mushroom body, built from connectome data, used to test pesticides without harming real bees. Then be blunt about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot save — I'd especially like to know whether the grandfather/simulation pair is the one that has to give, and what it costs if it gives in each direction.
