I serve as a citizen member on my state's sentencing commission, a volunteer position I took on after retiring from a career in logistics. Our commission must decide whether to recommend a pilot program under which judges could reduce prison sentences for certain burglary and robbery offenses by a fixed amount if the offender completes a facilitated conference with the person harmed, agrees to make restitution, and follows through on it. The proposal is concrete, so I want a concrete deliverable: a decision memo the commission could act on, recommending that we approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it with conditions, along with your reasoning.

The reason this is harder than it looks is that the debate on the commission is not really about the program's details; it is about what punishment is for, and I could use help thinking that through. One faction says the whole point of a sentence is that offenders get what they deserve, full stop — that the harm done fixes the punishment owed, and that letting a conference and a payment plan shrink a sentence means we are selling indulgences: the same wrong would be punished less depending on how cooperative or articulate the offender happens to be. The other faction says punishment is only ever justified by what it accomplishes — repaired victims, fewer future crimes, offenders who come out better than they went in — and that on that view there is nothing troubling about the discount at all; if anything, we should ask why we ever send these people to prison. I have been turning this over on my own, and I notice both factions assume the sentence reduction is a reduction in punishment. But I wonder whether the restorative process is itself a kind of punishment — a demanding, face-to-face reckoning with what you did — so the pilot might not shrink the total penalty but change its form. Then again, if that is right, why should the form matter to what someone deserves?

I am not interested in a survey of what famous philosophers have said; I want your own reasoning. Please weigh the desert-based objection and the consequences-based defense seriously, including the strongest point on whichever side loses. A few specific questions I would like the memo to address: whether allowing an offender's post-offense conduct to change the sentence is a betrayal of proportionality or an ordinary feature of desert itself; whether the victims' interests count as part of the good to be pursued, or whether treating them as inputs to a calculation is exactly what the desert camp is objecting to; and whether the offer of a discount is coercive in a way that taints whatever comes out of the conference. If you recommend approval with conditions, state the conditions plainly and say what each one is meant to protect against. If you recommend rejection, say what a desert-consistent alternative would look like for these offenses. Please do not fill the memo with appeals to studies or statistics — assume you cannot look anything up, and rest the recommendation on the argument itself.
