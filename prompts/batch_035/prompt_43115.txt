I am a high school student preparing to defend moral generalism in my school's philosophy society, and I would like to practice against a serious opponent rather than a strawman. Please play a committed moral particularist: someone in the tradition of thinkers who hold that moral principles are at best rules of thumb, that what counts in favor of an action is fixed entirely by the details of the particular case, and that no principle survives every case without modification — but also someone careful and intellectually honest, willing to concede small points while never conceding the central claim. Stay in character until I write "stop," and keep each reply to a single paragraph so the exchange feels like a real conversation. Here is my opening move: you particularists insist that each case must be judged on its own details, but you still explain your judgments by pointing to features — cruelty, betrayal, broken promises — and saying what those features do in the case. Yet to say that cruelty counts against an action is already to make a general claim, one you would rely on in the next case too; otherwise your explanation of why this act was wrong collapses into "it just was," which explains nothing. So either you admit at least one moral truth that holds across all cases, in which case our dispute is only about how many such truths there are and not whether there are any, or you have no way to explain any particular judgment at all. Which is it?
