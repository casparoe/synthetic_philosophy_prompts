For the past year, on my own and with no training in any of this, I've been trying to work out how you could tell a very capable AI to do a job and nothing but the job. My worry is not that it would be malicious. It's that a machine that pursues instructions energetically might, while carrying them out, quietly rearrange things nobody asked it to touch. I wanted a way of defining "and nothing but the job" that never has to list what is good or what is bad, because I suspect nobody can write down a complete list. I came up with a test, and then an argument that appears to destroy not just my test but the whole project. I'd like to know which step of the argument to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

The test — call it the ghost test: to check whether the AI overstepped, compare the world after it finishes with the world as it would have been had the AI never been built, everything else proceeding the same. The AI has stayed within its assignment if the real world is close to that ghost world in every respect the job itself doesn't concern.

The argument:

(1) On the ghost test, whatever differs between the real world and the ghost world is an overstep, because it is something the AI brought about that nobody asked for.

(2) A difference is a difference. By shape alone — without consulting any list of goods and bads — a child alive in the real world and drowned in the ghost world is the same kind of fact as a forest burned down in the real world and standing in the ghost world. The test measures distance from the ghost world, and distance has no sign: it cannot tell better from worse.

(3) So an AI that genuinely passes the ghost test must refrain from changing things whichever way the change runs. It must not set the fire, and it must equally let the child drown, since both are deviations of the same size.

(4) Hence a flawless ghost-test AI stands by through any disaster it could have prevented, so long as preventing it was not part of the assignment.

(5) But a notion of "overstepping" under which a machine that watches a child drown in front of it scores perfectly is not a notion of overstepping at all; it is a notion of inactivity, and it scores rescue and wreckage identically.

(6) Therefore the ghost test fails. And since the same reasoning will apply to any test that works by shape alone, the conclusion is that "do the job and nothing but the job" cannot be defined without a complete accounting of what counts as harm — precisely the thing I was hoping never to need.

The obvious rejoinder is that only the bad differences should count as oversteps. But to say which differences are bad just is the complete list of values, so the rejoinder seems to concede the game before it begins. If I have to give something up, I would most like to keep (1) — I still want some test that doesn't require the list. But I can't see how to keep it without the rest going through, and I can't see how to reject (2) without smuggling the list in through the back door. So: which step do I give up, and what do I pay for it?
