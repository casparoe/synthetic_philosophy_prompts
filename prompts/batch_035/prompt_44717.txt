I'm a retired seismologist — 35 years of doing probabilistic hazard analysis, mostly estimating how often big quakes recur on particular faults. Recently got into philosophy and found out you people have a name for the thing that quietly ate my entire career: the reference class problem. Every time I gave a committee a "2% chance in 50 years" figure for a specific fault, I had already decided which other faults and which stretch of the record count as "similar enough" to pool — and everyone at the table picked a different set, so we just averaged and moved on. So here's what I want from you. Give me your credence that there exists, in principle, a principled answer to how one *ought* to pick the reference class for a single-case probability like that — not "it's a judgment call", an actual right answer. And decompose it: how much turns on whether single-case probabilities have determinate truth values independent of anyone's classifying choices, how much on whether the right class is fixed by causal/physical similarity rather than by whatever descriptors we happen to use, and how much on whether the choice is ultimately pragmatic (pick the class that serves the decision at hand) and whether that even counts as "principled" rather than just useful. A number for each with one line of reasoning, then combine them. Finally, tell me which of those terms a working forecaster like my old self could most hope to settle with data — or whether the whole thing is just commitee politics with extra steps.
