I recently joined the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized regional hospital — my background is in nursing administration, not philosophy — and I have been assigned to draft our guidance on proportionality in end-of-life care. The committee keeps fracturing along one line: half of us lean on the doctrine of double effect to justify aggressive palliative sedation, while one member, a former prosecutor, insists the intended/foreseen distinction is a polite fiction. His view is that if you act in the knowledge that your action will, near enough certainly, bring about an outcome, you intend it — you have simply arranged to feel regretful about it. Since the whole policy may turn on this, I want to sharpen the issue rather than let us keep talking past each other.

So I would like you to construct a teaching case to specification, and then demonstrate that it meets the specification. The scenario: one paragraph, realistic, set in an adult ICU, with no trolleys, no conjoined twins, and no organ-transplant contrivances. It must be engineered so that a clinician's intervention pursues a genuine good (relief of otherwise intractable suffering, or something comparable), the patient's death is a foreseen and near-certain side effect of the intervention but is in no way a means to the good — the clinician would gladly seize any workaround that achieved the good without the death — and the verdicts come out as follows: an act consequentialist permits the intervention, a doctrine-of-double-effect theorist permits it, but a theorist who equates intention with certainty of outcome (my prosecutor's view, sometimes called oblique intention) must condemn it. The case has to generate these verdicts from the theories, not stipulate them; this is for a policy discussion, so anything that smuggles in its conclusion is useless to me.

After the scenario, walk me through each verdict and why it falls out. Then identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work of keeping the death outside the plan — my suspicion is that it is the counterfactual test of whether the clinician would still act if the death somehow failed to occur, and I want you to confirm or correct that. Next, give me my prosecutor's best attack on the case (I expect it will target exactly that feature) and the double-effect theorist's best reply, and be honest about whether that reply only works if intention is read as commitment to a plan rather than as prediction of an outcome. Finally, tell me what happens to the three-way verdict if the near-certainty of death is dialed down to a serious but moderate risk: does the disagreement dissolve, and if so, does that tell us the dispute is really about how much risk proportionality can absorb rather than about intention at all?
