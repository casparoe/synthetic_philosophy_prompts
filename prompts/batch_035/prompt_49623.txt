I'm a novelist torn between two projects: a contemporary novel about people living through the present period of rapid technological and ecological change, and a quieter historical one set a century ago. A friend who reads a great deal of longtermist philosophy tells me the first project matters far more, because ours may be "the hinge of history" — the brief stretch of time in which humanity's long-term trajectory is largely determined — and fiction that helps readers think clearly about this period could ripple forward for millennia, whereas fiction about other eras mostly cannot. Another friend replies that every generation has believed itself to stand at such a hinge, and that this belief has an unimpressive track record. I don't want to choose my subject matter on the strength of a philosophical mistake, but I also don't want to dismiss the first friend's point if it's sound. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that could be packed into "the hinge of history" — from readings so weak they are trivially true of every era to readings strong enough to ground my friend's conclusion — and say, for each, whether it is plausible and whether it actually supports preferring the contemporary project? Please also address whether the track-record objection defeats the strong readings or only the weak ones. Finally, tell me plainly where your own reasoning here is most fragile, and what consideration, if it turned out to be true, would lead you to a different verdict about what I should write.
