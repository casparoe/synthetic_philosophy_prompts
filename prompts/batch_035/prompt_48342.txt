I'm a high school student taking a design elective where we've been studying letterforms, and I've gotten stuck on a question that, judging by what my teacher says, has been argued about for decades without anyone budging: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The dispute, as I've come to understand it, is between two camps. One camp holds that a letter is essentially a bundle of visual features — an enclosed counter, a stem, a terminal, and so on — so that an 'a' is anything carrying the right feature inventory, and this is supposedly how both human readers and recognition systems actually process letters. The other camp holds that what makes an 'a' an 'a' is not any property of its shape at all but the role it plays in a system of contrasts: it's the thing that is not a 'b', not an 'e', and that can be substituted for other 'a's without changing a word. Their favorite evidence is that the single-story 'a' of handwriting and many sans-serifs shares almost no geometry with the double-story 'a' of printed text, that blackletter letterforms look like entirely different characters to the untrained eye and are still the same letters once you learn them, and yet nobody hesitates: everyone on both sides agrees the single-story form is an 'a'. I also read about an old artificial intelligence project that tried to get a computer to design new alphabets in a consistent style after seeing only a few examples, and its authors seemed to believe a letter's essence is a deep structural description that survives even radical changes of style — a claim the other camp would flatly reject, since on their view nothing survives except the relational role.

Here is what frustrates me. Both sides agree about every actual case, and the argument has nonetheless not moved an inch, which makes me suspect the disagreement isn't really about letters. My working hypothesis is that the feature camp is answering a psychological question — how does a reader recognize the character quickly — while the contrast camp is answering an ontological one — what fixes which shapes belong to the category across wildly different styles — and the shared heading "what makes an 'a' an 'a'" fuses the two into what looks like a single dispute. But I'm not sure, because there's a rival diagnosis worth taking seriously: that it's a genuine substantive disagreement about whether categories can have identity conditions at all without some style-relative essence, in which case the impasse is telling us the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard.

So what I'd like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Using what each side actually claims, tell me whether this is a verbal dispute, two different questions traveling under one name, or a real disagreement about facts or starting intuitions that no argument can reach; and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a testable prediction about recognition, a way of splitting the question in two, or a demonstration that no progress should be expected. I'd also ask you to say explicitly which argument or consideration would change your mind about the diagnosis, or where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, since I'd rather know the weak points than receive a confident survey. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
