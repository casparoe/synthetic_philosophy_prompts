Write a satirical FAQ page, of roughly 650 words, in the voice of corporate customer support, for a fictional subscription service called WillCheck™, which offers to purge moral luck from its customers' ethical records. The premise: a customer submits an action they feel bad about, and WillCheck™ issues a certificate re-evaluating the act using only the quality of the customer's will at the time, ignoring everything that happened afterwards. The comedy must come from the philosophy being right: the piece is a reductio of the thought that one could assess an agent by subtracting everything that is down to luck, and it should be funny to someone who knows the moral-luck literature while remaining readable to someone who does not. Do not cite any philosopher by name and include no references or reading lists — a corporate FAQ cites nothing.

So that the piece does not depend on memorised doctrine, here is the background it should draw on. Philosophers distinguish at least four ways luck infects moral assessment. Resultant luck concerns how things turn out: two equally careless drivers, one of whom happens to hit a child. Circumstantial luck concerns the situations one happens to face: the person who would have been a perfectly decent citizen had history placed her elsewhere. Constitutive luck concerns the temperament and capacities one is born with and formed by. Causal luck concerns the fact that one's choices trace back to prior causes outside oneself. WillCheck™ promises to strip all four out, and its pricing tiers should mirror this hierarchy: each more expensive tier removes a deeper kind of luck.

The piece should be deadpan throughout — no answer may admit that the service is incoherent, though every clause should make that visible to an attentive reader. Include at least the following. One testimonial in which a customer reports that after the top-tier "Full Decontamination" she was certified praiseworthy for an act of will she no longer remembers having, and cannot recognise as hers. One answer on whether the service covers remorse, which distinguishes remorse from agent-regret: WillCheck™ cannot process regret, since regret makes no claim about the will and is therefore, the FAQ explains, the residue customers are welcome to keep at no charge. One clause acknowledging the objection that WillCheck™'s own assessments are products of luck — the assessors' standards, the company's history, the customer's capacity for self-scrutiny — and cheerfully deflecting it, for instance by referring the reader to the Terms, which deal with it in exactly the same way. And a final question, in the "we value your feedback" section, in which a customer asks what precisely is being certified once everything attributable to anything has been removed; the answer must concede the point in substance while conceding nothing in tone, perhaps by issuing the customer a complimentary certificate of compliance for an unnamed act of will.

By the end, a reader should have felt the real problem: that subtracting all the luck leaves nothing to evaluate, and that praise and blame, which we cannot do without, never had the purity we demand of them. Keep the register polished, promotional and slightly legalistic, in British English.
