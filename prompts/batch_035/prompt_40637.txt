I sit on what our hospital calls the "boundary requests" committee — the group that reviews cases where a clinician wants to prescribe or do something that's plausibly not treating any disease. We get maybe three to five cases a month: off-label requests from physicians, patient appeals after a denial, that kind of thing. Right now the process is basically vibes. Whoever's in the room that day argues from some mixture of "is this restoring normal function?", "is this medically necessary?", and "would I be uncomfortable defending this in the local paper?" — and different members weight these differently, so we've approved and denied near-identical requests a few months apart. Nobody's happy with it, definately not the clinicians who get inconsistent answers.

What I want is a decision procedure the committee can actually run in a twenty-minute discussion per case, ending in a written rationale that can go in the record and survive an appeal to the medical director. Ground it in the real philosophy on the therapy/enhancement distinction — normal species functioning, restoration vs. augmentation, the whole debate about whether the line even exists — but translated into questions a busy doctor can answer, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each. It needs to say where the procedure gives a determinate verdict, where it stops with a no, and where it hands off to a value judgment the chair has to own in writing instead of pretending the procedure decided it. And it has to handle the known traps head on: prevention looks like enhancement if you're not careful (vaccines, statins), "normal" is doing enormous work and disability scholars have been complaining about that baseline for decades, and something being cosmetic doesn't obviously make it not medicine — we do reconstructive surgery after burns without agonizing.

Then run it on three cases like the ones on our actual docket: (1) parents of a healthy 11-year-old girl, predicted adult height around 5'2", no growth-hormone deficiency, asking for GH because she's being bullied about her height; (2) a 29-year-old professional violinist with no psychiatric diagnosis asking for propranolol for stage fright — she shakes during auditions and has lost her last two; (3) a patient with well-controlled ADHD on stimulants asking for a temporary dose increase to get through bar-exam season. Include at least one case where the procedure lands somewhere I don't like — for what it's worth I think the violinist should get the propranolol, so if the procedure says no there, show it doing that anyway.

Last thing: be upfront about the epistemics. Say how confident you are in each part of the procedure, flag where it's on solid conceptual ground versus where it's papering over a genuinely contested question, and single out the single point you're least sure of. I'd rather know the weak point than have a procedure that pretends not to have one.
