A friend of mine from a seminar on contractualism has been testing her reactions to aggregation cases, and her verdicts so far are: saving five people from each losing a leg beats saving one person from losing a leg; 50,001 severe week-long headaches beat 50,000 equally severe ones; one person's death beats a billion people's mild day-long headaches, however many there are; one person's death beats twenty people's permanent paralysis; and five deaths beat one death. She insists all of this hangs together, and says the non-aggregation verdicts reflect the contractualist thought that one person cannot be sacrificed merely because benefits to others are spread thinly across many lives. Next week's case: one person will die unless four million people are each permanently paralyzed. Predict her verdict. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record — including at least one on which numbers never add up across different severities of loss and at least one on which sufficiently many smaller losses can outweigh a somewhat larger one — say what each rule predicts here, give your best guess at her answer with your reasons for preferring it, and propose the one further case that would most cleanly divide the candidate rules.
