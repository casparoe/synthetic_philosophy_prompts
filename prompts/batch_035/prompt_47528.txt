I'd like your credence that knowledge is finally valuable — that is, more valuable than mere true belief for reasons that go beyond its instrumental usefulness — together with the full decomposition of the reasoning behind that number.

Some framing to fix the terms. In the Meno, Socrates observes that a true belief about the way to Larissa will get you to Larissa just as well as knowledge of the way, so the extra value of knowledge cannot simply be its practical serviceability. His own proposal is that knowledge is true belief that has been tied down, like the statues of Daedalus in the myth, which ran away unless tethered. In the contemporary literature this has been split into the primary value problem (why knowledge is better than mere true belief), the secondary (why it is better than any proper subset of its parts, such as justified true belief), and the tertiary (why it is qualitatively, not merely quantitatively, better than whatever falls just short of knowledge). The main lines of response I have in mind are: the credit view, on which knowledge is true belief arising out of cognitive ability, so that its value is the value of success from ability; the anti-luck or safety programme, on which knowledge excludes luckily true belief; and the knowledge-first line, on which the question is dissolved rather than solved, since if knowledge is unanalysable, perhaps its value is equally primitive. There is also the swamping worry, pressed by Zagzebski with the analogy of the cup of coffee: if the coffee in the cup tastes good, the reliability of the machine that produced it adds nothing to the value of that cup; if a belief is already true, why should the reliability of the process that produced it add any value to that belief?

Here is the disagreement I am trying to locate. My supervisor, who works on virtue epistemology, puts it at roughly 0.9 that knowledge has final value over mere true belief; a friend of mine, a convinced knowledge-firster, puts it at roughly 0.3, on the grounds that the Meno problem is a test that theories of knowledge ought not to have to pass. Give me your own number for the proposition that knowledge is finally, and not merely instrumentally, more valuable than mere true belief, and decompose it into at least the following terms, supplying a probability and a one-line justification for each:

1. the probability that the primary value problem has a genuine solution, as opposed to being dissolved, deflated, or revealing that knowledge simply is not finally better than true belief;
2. conditional on there being a solution, the probability that the credit or success-from-ability line, rather than the anti-luck or safety line, is what delivers it;
3. the probability that the swamping objection can be answered by reliabilists in a manner that does not quietly convert them into virtue theorists;
4. any further terms you think belong, including your handling of the tertiary problem — I would particularly like to know whether you think the qualitative step from justified true belief to knowledge carries value at all, given that Gettier cases are rare.

Then combine the terms into your final figure, say which term the final number is most sensitive to, and tell me which sub-question most likely divides my supervisor from my friend. Finally, identify the single argument or kind of case that would move your credence the most, and state in which direction it would move it.
