After thirty-seven years in physical chemistry — most of them spent designing experiments and reasoning about what they do and do not establish — I retired and turned to the philosophy of causation, reading from the medieval Islamic debate forward through Hume to present-day interventionist accounts. The text that has most unsettled me is al-Ghazālī's eleventh-century polemic against the philosophers, in which he argues, against the Avicennan school, that when cotton burns in contact with fire we observe only that the two events are conjoined, never that the fire possesses any power necessitating the burning; and that anyone who claims to perceive such a necessity is merely reporting how often the one has customarily followed the other. My instinct as a scientist is to dismiss this, but my training will not let me: I know that no measurement ever registers necessity, only regularity. So before I commit to any contemporary camp — regularity theory, counterfactual dependence, interventionism, dispositional powers, occasionalism — I want the specification written first, and I would like you to draft it.

The deliverable is a list of desiderata: what any adequate theory of causation must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each, and a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one school dressed up as requirements. At a minimum, please assess the following candidates for the list (accept, reject, or refine each, and add anything I have missed): (1) it must mark the asymmetry between cause and effect, since smoke advertises fire but does not produce it; (2) it must explain the special evidential value of interventions — in the laboratory I never trusted a correlation the way I trusted a controlled experiment, and I want the theory to say what an experiment teaches that passive observation cannot; (3) it must handle overdetermination and preemption, as when two sufficient poisons are administered and the patient dies of only one; (4) it must accommodate single-case probabilistic causation, since a radium atom decays without triggering and no regularity exists at the level of the individual decay; (5) it must permit omissions to be causes, as with the gardener who does not water the plants; (6) it must not postulate powers or necessities beyond those physics licenses — though I suspect this demand, so phrased, begs the question against al-Ghazālī, whose point is that physics itself registers only conjunction, so please examine whether it can be rephrased non-question-beggingly or should be dropped; and (7) it must either solve or explicitly decline the problem of induction, and if it declines, say whether a theory of causation may legitimately leave induction to some separate theory. Then — and this is the part I most want — identify any subset of the desiderata that is jointly unsatisfiable. I have a suspicion that requiring empirically accessible causes, distinguishing genuine from accidental regularities, and delivering a necessity that covers unobserved cases cannot all be had at once, and if so, the list itself is the result.

Close with one or two sentences each on how a Humean regularity view, a counterfactual or interventionist view, and an al-Ghazālī-style occasionalism fare against the list — briefly, please, since the criteria are the real work and I will do the full scoring myself. To keep your conclusion machine-readable, assign each final desideratum a short label (D1, D2, ...) and end with a line of the form \answer{...} enclosing either the labels of the subset you judge jointly unsatisfiable or, if you judge the full list jointly satisfiable, the single word "none".
