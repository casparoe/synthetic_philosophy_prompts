In other passages it's a volcano or a cold spell in the same role: Tambora blowing its top and doing the groundwork for later unrest, the Little Ice Age quietly finishing off the Norse colonies. It's vivid, but I want to pressure-test it before I tell her it works. Audit the "germ/volcano as historical actor" analogy as she's using it: give me the correspondence table (what plays the general, the campaign, the victory conditions, the spoils of war), then say which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions she draws -- no intentionality, no ability to react to the enemy's moves, the pathogen co-evolving with its hosts rather than strategising, the fact that a "victory" like higher wages only shows up if institutions channel it that way -- and which are just pedentic. So does her story about serfdom and the church survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is it really an institutional story where the microbe is just the initial shock and medieval lords, peasants, and courts are the actual actors? And if the whole framing is sunk, is there a better comparison that keeps the vividness without the fake agency -- a bottleneck, a lottery draw, a demolition contractor who doesn't get to design what's built on the cleared site? Also tell me how confident you are in the historical claims you lean on, and flag the one you'd least want to bet on.
