I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and the thing I can't get past is that the Allais debate basically hasn't moved since the 50s. Allais presents his pairs of choices, Savage concedes he himself has the offending preferences and then says they're a mistake to be corrected in oneself, and seventy years later we've got the common-consequence and common-ratio effects replicated to death, Machina's argument that you can't just save independence with a fanning-out story about utility, Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility which keeps transitivity and quietly gives up independence instead, and the resolute-choice crowd saying the axiom was only ever compelling as a dynamic consistency condition — and still nobody converts. Its the weirdest thing: everyone agrees on the data, everyone can state each others arguments, and the independence axiom keeps getting taught as a rationality requirement anyway.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on whether independence is true. My working hypothesis is that the sides aren't actually disagreeing about one thing: one side is answering a question about what an idealized agent's preferences must satisfy for the representation theorems to go through, and the other side is answering whether a flesh-and-blood person who prefers the Allais B-option is thereby making a mistake. Maybe it's even three questions — a normative one, a formal one, and the psychologists' descriptive one about what people actually do — all living under the single word "rationality." Is that right, or is there a genuine normative disagreement underneath that my framing hides? And if it's real, what kind — bedrock intuitions about what rationality is for, or different standards for what counts as a counterexample to an axiom?

Whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress given it — a formal result, a new experimental paradigm, a distinction no one's drawn — and be honest if the answer is "nothing should be expected, this is bedrock." Also, please say what would change your mind: what argument or piece of evidence would push you toward a different diagnosis, and where in your own reasoning do you think you're most likely to have gone wrong. I've got my own answer sketched out and I'm worried its too tidy.
