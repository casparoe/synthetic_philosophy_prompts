I'm an undergraduate in an upper-level epistemology course this term, and for the past few weeks we've been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty, the notebooks he kept at the very end of his life, along with some secondary literature on what contemporary philosophers call hinge epistemology. Last week, ahead of our seminar meeting, I listened to a general-audience philosophy podcast that covered the same material, and I want to check it against my seminar reading before drafting my term paper — parts of it matched what we read and parts didn't, and I can't always tell which is which.

The episode summarized the view like this: "In his final notebooks, Wittgenstein took up Moore's famous proof that he has hands, and concluded that Moore was right about the conclusion but wrong about the reasoning. Some propositions — that I have hands, that there is an external world — are not inferred from anything but are known directly, with absolute certainty. These certainties are foundations in the classical sense: rock-bottom assumptions on which the rest of our knowledge rests. Wittgenstein called them hinges because the whole system of our beliefs swings on them. Since they are held with total certainty and are shared by every sane person, skepticism is refuted: the skeptic's doubts simply cannot get a grip. And since no evidence could justify the hinges themselves without circularity, we hold them as a matter of decision — a practical commitment, closer to a leap of faith than a piece of knowledge." The episode then drew a moral from all this: because every belief system rests on hinges that its holders simply choose, there is no neutral standpoint from which to prefer a scientist's system of beliefs to a conspiracy theorist's; hinge certainties are arbitrary at bottom, so relativism about justification follows.

My seminar reading suggests some of this is off. I'm fairly confident the leap-of-faith part is not Wittgenstein's, and I half-remember a metaphor involving a riverbed that was supposed to show that hinges can shift over time rather than forming permanent bedrock. But I was struck by how natural the foundationalist framing sounded — we studied classical foundationalism earlier in the term, and it struck me as not something our professor would attribute to Wittgenstein, yet the podcast makes the hinges sound exactly like basic beliefs in the foundationalist sense.

Could you go through the podcast's summary part by part and tell me what a Wittgenstein scholar would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is straightforwardly misattributed? In particular, I'd like to understand whether Wittgenstein thought hinges are known at all — my professor kept insisting on a difference between saying a hinge is certain and saying it is known, and I want to be able to state that difference in my own words, including what hinges do if not support other beliefs. I'd also like to know what role Moore's proof actually plays in the notebooks, since the podcast treats it as Wittgenstein's starting point for refuting the skeptic, which sounded too strong for what we read. Finally, does the relativist moral follow from anything that survives your audit, or does it trade on sliding from unhinged to unjustified to arbitrary? My paper is on whether hinge epistemology, stated carefully, can answer skepticism, so I need to know which parts of the podcast I can build on and which I have to argue against.
