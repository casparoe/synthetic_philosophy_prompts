I write novels, and lately I've become obssessed with Hofstadter's idea that creativity is basically variations on a theme — you take a familiar structure or scenario and twiddle the knobs on it (change the setting, the medium, the protagonist's profession, the century) until something genuinely new falls out, and this copying-with-adjustment is what analogy-making, and therefore all creativity, really is. Please explain the model properly and then dig into the part that always gets glossed over: what determines which aspects of a theme count as knobs one may twiddle and which are load-bearing structure you mustn't touch, and how you could possibly know in advance which is which. I keep testing the idea against my own trade — retelling an old plot in a new genre can read either as a genuinely new work or as a mere costume swap, and it's not obvious to me that the knob picture can explain that difference rather than quietly assuming it. Does the model have the resources for that distinction, or does it break down exactly where it would need to earn its keep? Keep the answer reasonably technical rather than vibes-y, and write it in British English please.
