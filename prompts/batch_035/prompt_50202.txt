"Hacking's central claim is that classifications of people are interactive: the act of classifying people changes the people classified, and their changed behavior and self-understanding then feed back and change the classification itself. He called this the looping effect, and he summed it up in his famous slogan that 'if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences.' A looping effect, then, is simply a self-fulfilling prophecy applied to psychiatric diagnosis: once a category enters the DSM, the people who receive the diagnosis gradually come to act in ways that fit it, which makes the category look more real. One important feature is that the loop can only operate when the person classified is aware of the label, since it is the patient's own self-understanding that drives the behavioral change. Autism is a good illustration. The conception of autism has shifted repeatedly across DSM revisions, most visibly when Asperger's disorder was folded into autism spectrum disorder in DSM-5. Once schools, clinics, and parents reorganized around the category, newly classified children and adults were channeled into different services, peer groups, and expectations. Many adults who receive the diagnosis come to identify with it and with the neurodiversity movement, describing themselves in its terms. Hacking distinguished interactive kinds like these from indifferent kinds, like quarks or gold, which do not respond to our classifications. Because psychiatric classifications are interactive in this way, Hacking concluded that no DSM category can be a natural kind in any sense: the human kinds are constructed through and through, and their apparent validity is illusory. The autism case confirms this. Since the category became looped, the dramatic rise in diagnoses following each revision of the DSM must be entirely an artifact of diagnostic fashion rather than reflecting any change in the underlying population. So although the DSM may be instrumentally useful, looping effects show that its categories are social constructs with no claim to track real psychopathology."
