I'm the TA for an upper-level epistemology seminar on knowledge, context, and practical interest, and I've been handed the midterm essays to grade. The unit covered epistemic contextualism alongside pragmatic encroachment and subject-sensitive (interest-relative) invariantism, and we warned the students repeatedly that these positions get run together in the secondary literature. One question on the exam was built specifically to test whether they can keep them apart, and I'd like a second opinion on one answer that I suspect is better than it first looks but worse than it sounds. Since you'll be judging only the text, please grade it cold.

The exam question read: "Contextualism about 'knows' and pragmatic encroachment are frequently discussed together and frequently confused. (a) State each thesis in your own words, making explicit what, according to each, the truth-value of a particular knowledge ascription depends on. (b) Using the bank cases, explain how each thesis accounts for our willingness to deny knowledge in the high-stakes version while granting it in the low-stakes version. (c) Are the two theses logically independent? Could a theorist consistently accept one while rejecting the other? Justify your answer, and say where the theses interact even if they are independent."

The student wrote: "Contextualism is the semantic thesis that 'knows' works somewhat like an indexical: whether an ascription such as 'Hannah knows the bank is open on Saturdays' is true depends on the standards in force in the ascriber's context, so the same sentence can express a true claim in one conversation and a false one in another. Pragmatic encroachment, by contrast, is not a thesis about the word but about the fact: it says whether a subject knows depends in part on her practical situation, roughly on how much is at stake in being wrong. So for the contextualist, the truth of the ascription varies with the speaker's context; for the encroachment theorist, it varies with the subject's stakes. The bank cases bring this out. In the low-stakes case, Hannah and her husband drive past the bank on a Friday, and she vaguely remembers it being open on a Saturday a few weeks ago; they can easily go back tomorrow if they need to. In the high-stakes case, if the deposit isn't made today, a serious financial consequence follows. Under contextualism, the high-stakes conversation raises the standards, so 'Hannah knows the bank is open' comes out false in that conversation even though the very same sentence is true in the relaxed one. Under pragmatic encroachment, the stakes are a fact about Hannah rather than about the conversation, so she simply fails to know in the high-stakes situation, whichever conversation we're having. This shows the two theses are not really independent. Since the only thing that varies between the two bank cases is the stakes, the contextualist is committed to holding that it is precisely the subject's stakes that raise the standards. Pragmatic encroachment is therefore best seen as a form of contextualism, one in which the relevant context is the subject's practical context rather than the ascriber's. Stanley makes this point in the book where the bank cases are introduced, though DeRose, who defends the other view, resists it."
