I've been teaching intro ethics for awhile now, and every time we hit Williams on internal vs. external reasons I wheel out the same tired examples — the young man who doesn't want to join the army, the person who just doesn't care about their family. This term I want to do something with Huckleberry Finn instead, and I want to get the reading right before I take it into the classroom. You know the scene: Jim has been sold, Huck writes the letter to Miss Watson telling her where to find him, and then, in what the book calls "a close place," sitting there trembling because he's got to decide, forever, betwixt two things, he says to himself, "All right, then, I'll GO to hell" — and tears the letter up. Then comes "It was awful thoughts and awful words, but they was said," and straightaway he feels light and washed clean and resolves to steal Jim out of slavery all over again.

So here's my question, and I want a committed answer, not a tour of the positions: on the internal/external reasons debate, what is Huck's conscience actually putting in front of him in that scene? Is it a putative external reason — a genuine reason to turn Jim in that holds no matter what Huck happens to care about, and which he simply isn't moved by — in which case the novel looks like evidence that external reasons exist but are motivationally inert, which would be a pretty wierd result? Or is Williams basically right here, such that what the scene shows is that the "reason" the conscience offers is a phantom: there is no reason to turn Jim in at all, the only real reasons in play are internal ones flowing out of his friendship with Jim, and the conscience is just internal-reason machinery that's been fed a corrupt moral code? Pick a side and argue it, and then tell me honestly where the reading strains — especially whether Huck's hell-talk is reason-talk in the first place or just plain terror of damnation, and whether that distinction matters for the side you picked. (I know Bennett's old paper, the one that puts Huck's conscience next to Himmler's, but that's about sympathy versus bad morality; I'm after the reasons literature specifically. And please don't land on "the novel supports both readings" — that's the coward's answer, and my students will try it on me too.)
