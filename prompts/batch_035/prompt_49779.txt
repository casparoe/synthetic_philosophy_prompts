I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and the one doctrine I've never been able to shake is anti-mysterianism: no truth is in principle beyond the reach of inquiry. Some feasible continuation of science — by us, our descendants, or our machines — could come to know any given truth. It feels like a load-bearing wall of the whole epistemic culture: the moment you concede a permanent mystery, you've handed the mysterians a blank check.

A friend of mine who works on AI forecasting claims this doctrine, plus the observation that we are not omniscient, refutes itself. His argument:

1. No Permanent Mysteries (NPM): for every true proposition p, it is possible that someone comes to know p. Some feasible continuation of inquiry could deliver knowledge of p. This is meant to be weak — it asserts only possibility, never that anyone actually knows.
2. We are not omniscient: there is at least one truth that no one ever knows at any time in the actual history of the world. Pick your own filler — the exact number of grains of sand on a named beach in 1900, some physical constant to fifty decimals past the last digit anyone will measure — any truth that goes forever actually unknown. Call it U. So U is true and U is never known by anyone: U ∧ ¬K(U).
3. By (1) applied to this truth: it is possible that someone comes to know the conjunction U ∧ ¬K(U) — that someone knows both the filler fact and the fact that no one ever knows it.
4. Knowing a conjunction requires knowing each conjunct. So anyone who knew U ∧ ¬K(U) would know U, and would know that no one ever knows U.
5. But if they know U, then someone does know U, which makes the second conjunct false; and since knowledge is only of what is true, no one can know it. So it is impossible that anyone ever knows U ∧ ¬K(U). Step (3) has delivered an impossibility.
6. Run (1) contrapositive: whatever could never be known by anyone is not a truth. U ∧ ¬K(U) can never be known, so by (1) it is not true. Yet U is true, and ¬K(U) is true of the actual history; a conjunction of two truths cannot be false.
7. So (1) and (2) are inconsistent: either every truth is actually known by someone — the community of all knowers there will ever be is jointly omniscient — or NPM is false and some truths could not be reached by any possible continuation of inquiry.
8. Premise (2) is about as safe as empirical premises get; we are obviously not omniscient.
9. Therefore there are permanent mysteries, and this follows from elementary logic about conjunctions and factivity — not from any claim about the limits of instruments or minds.

He says my load-bearing wall is a load-bearing domino: the moment I admit we don't already know everything, I've conceded unknowable truths. I'd rather keep (1) than accept (9) — agreeing with the mysterians feels like surrendering the whole city — but I can't locate the false step, and I notice two places where I flinch without being able to say why.

First, in step (3): when I signed up for NPM I had plain facts in mind — nothing out there is in principle beyond reach. The conjunction smuggles in a claim about the entire actual history of knowledge, including the knower's own future and everyone else's, and I can't tell whether exempting such propositions from (1) is a principled restriction or an ad hoc escape hatch.

Second, in step (6): I have the sense that a thesis about the reach of inquiry should not get to have opinions about which propositions are true, as though a quantifier or a scope has quietly shifted, but I can't pin it down.

So: tell me which premise to give up, name the step where the argument earns its conclusion, and be explicit about the price of my preferred escape. If the fix is restricting (1) to propositions that don't entail their own unknownness, is that principled or cosmetic? Is there a reading of the possibility operator — possible for some possible agent, rather than possible within the actual history — on which (3) comes out true and (5) comes out false, and does the rest of the argument survive the switch? And give me a straight verdict on the alternative of keeping (1) and rejecting (2): how much of my sanity does the observation that already known spans every knower there will ever be actually buy back? I'd also take it as a favor if you can tell me whether this thing has a name — my friend swears it doesn't, and it has the smell of something somebody worked out long before either of us was born.
