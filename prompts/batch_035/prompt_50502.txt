I am building a comparative philosophy unit on identity over time, and I want a case analysis of a real, ongoing practice rather than another go-round with the puzzle's stock examples. The case is the Shikinen Sengū at Ise Grand Shrine in Japan, and the theory is, of course, the Ship of Theseus -- identity under replacement of parts.

First, the case, so that nothing below depends on facts you cannot be sure of. Ise's main sanctuaries are rebuilt from scratch every twenty years: the form is fixed, but the substance is entirely new each cycle -- freshly cut cypress, joined without nails, thatched roofs, all reconstructed on an empty plot adjacent to the current sanctuary, after which the deity (at the Inner Shrine, the sacred mirror) is transferred to the new building at night and the old building is torn down. The two plots alternate, so the shrine of any given moment stands where its demolished predecessor stood two cycles ago; the currently empty plot is covered in white gravel, with a small wooden hut marking the spot where the central pillar of the old building stood. The cycle has been running, by most accounts, since around 690 CE under the empress Jitō -- the 62nd rebuilding was completed in 2013, and the 63rd is scheduled for 2033. Japanese commentary on the practice often invokes the idea of tokowaka, roughly "perpetual youth": the kami should be ever-new, and rebuilding is the ritual form of that ever-newness. It is also often said that a major motive of the twenty-year interval is transmission of the craft -- a master carpenter trained on one rebuild trains the generation that will build the next -- so that the rebuilding system is simultaneously a device for keeping a set of techniques alive. By most accounts the cycle has lapsed at times, reportedly for well over a century during the civil wars of the late medieval period, before being resumed; I flag this because the exact duration is disputed and I do not want the analysis to hang on it.

Now the assignment. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in metaphysics and the philosophy of art, and who is used to disciplining case analyses: mapping explicitly, running the theory in both directions, and stating where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Before writing the analysis, do some careful brainstorming: enumerate the candidate solutions to the Ship of Theseus on the table (sameness of matter, spatiotemporal continuity, continuity of form or design, continuity of function, best-candidate or closest-continuer principles, conventionalist or stipulationist answers), and for each, ask what it actually predicts about Ise before you decide what Ise shows about it.

Then deliver the analysis proper, along these lines:

1. The mapping. Get explicit about the terms of the original puzzle and where Ise conforms, diverges, or quietly changes the subject. The stock puzzle gives us gradual, piecemeal replacement of a single continuing object, sometimes with a rival reassembly from the discarded parts; Ise gives us wholesale, scheduled, simultaneous replacement with the discarded material destroyed, and no rival candidate. Does that make the case easier -- no competition, so no need for a best-candidate criterion -- or harder, in that the standard resources (gradual replacement, continuity of matter) are simply unavailable? Note also what carries over between cycles: not matter, and not the building, but the design, the craft lineage, the ritual calendar, the ground itself, and the transferred object at the center. Say which of these, if any, the classic literature would count as the relevant continuity relation, and why.

2. The theory tested. Give an honest verdict: does Ise vindicate some existing answer to the puzzle, refute none of them but pressure one or two, or show that the puzzle was malformed from the start -- that it asks about the identity conditions of a kind of thing (an artifact) whose identity is shallow, while Ise's identity is anchored somewhere else entirely, perhaps in the kami's presence, the design, or the practice? Here I'd like you to seriously consider a deflationary reading: that Ise dissolves the puzzle because the question "is it the same shrine?" simply does not arise within the practice -- nobody at Ise is puzzled, and the reconstruction is experienced as preservation. If you take that line, say what becomes of the philosophical question: is it revealed as parochial to artifacts of a Western sort, or does it resurface at one remove, e.g., as the question of what the practice itself is identical to across its reported lapses?

3. The comparative edge, which is the part my unit most needs. Contrast Ise with the treatment of old buildings in the European conservation world, where the received ideal (associated with figures like Ruskin and the doctrine of minimal intervention) treats authenticity as residing in original fabric, and where replacing worn fabric is conceded only reluctantly and documented as loss. It is often said that this collision -- a building renewed by total replacement counting, in the Japanese context, as authentic -- was one of the provocations behind the 1994 Nara Document on Authenticity, which argued that authenticity is judged within each culture's own values and cannot be reduced to a fixed inventory of material properties. Treat that claim as part of the theory under test: does the Ise case show that identity-through-replacement is a coherent alternative paradigm, or does it show that "authenticity" has quietly become ambiguous -- shifting from a property of objects (original fabric) to a property of practices (fidelity of transmission)? Distinguish those two readings explicitly, and say which the Nara-style position needs.

4. The limits. End by saying plainly what the case cannot show: where the analogy with the Ship of Theseus breaks down, where you are guessing about the internal theology of the shrine rather than reporting, and which parts of your analysis would survive even if the reported details (the length of the medieval lapse, the motives for the twenty-year interval) turned out otherwise.

A few ground rules for your answer. Write in American English throughout. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations -- I am interested in the reasoning, not references, and I know you cannot verify sources. Do not quote any text verbatim; paraphrase any position you attribute to a named philosopher, and flag it clearly if you are characterizing a view at a level of detail you are not confident of. Think through the brainstorming stage before committing to conclusions, and prefer an honest "the theory is silent here" to an elegant forced fit. The deliverable is a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory illuminating the practice, and the practice testing the theory.
