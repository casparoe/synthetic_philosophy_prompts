Two of my friends have been arguing about immigration for literally years, and at this point they just recite their greatest hits at each other, so I want to make them do a proper adversarial collaboration, and I need the joint statement drafted for them to react to. One is an open-borders type: freedom of movement is a basic liberty, and turning someone away at a border is a coercive act by the state that needs special justification, with the burden on the excluder. The other is a right-to-exclude type: a legitimate state belongs to its people roughly the way a club (or maybe a family home — they even disagree about which analogy is right!) belongs to its members, so admission is an invitation that is never owed, no matter how badly the outsider wants in.

Draft the document both of them could sign after a good-faith collaboration. Structure it as follows, and please write everything in British English.

1. AGREED CLAIMS: a numbered list of claims both sides could sign. These should cover, at least: what border enforcement physically involves and why that is morally loaded for both of them; the difference between excluding someone who has never entered and deporting someone who has settled; and which empirical questions are genuinely open versus basically settled — state this abstractly, no citations or reading lists, just say what kind of finding would count as settled enough that neither side could reasonably lean on it.

2. DISAGREEMENTS TABLE: columns exactly as follows — "Disagreement" | "Wording the open-borders side accepts" | "Wording the right-to-exclude side accepts" | "Substantive or merely verbal?" — where the last column says which of their disputes are real and which, if any, came down to them using words like "coercion" or "belongs" differently all along.

3. CRUXES TABLE: columns exactly — "Crux" | "Case both agree bears on it" | "Open-borders prediction" | "Right-to-exclude prediction". For the cases, use the two scenarios they keep circling around (tighten them up yourself):

- The Dormitory Nation: a tiny rich state whose entire population descends from migrants votes, 70–30, in a referendum to open the borders, but only on condition that newcomers settle in its half-empty northern provinces for ten years before they may live anywhere in the country. Question both sides must answer: is the settling condition a legitimate exercise of self-determination, or does it grant entry on such unequal terms that it is barely better than exclusion?
- The Inheritance Auction: a state that admits exactly 200,000 people a year, chosen purely by sealed-bid auction for residence permits, with the proceeds divvied up equally among existing citizens and no other route in. Question: does this scheme respect the right to exclude (the people set the number and benefit from it) or violate it (it prices admission in a way that fits neither the club analogy nor the home one)? And separately: is it better or worse than open borders, judged from each side's own view? I'd love it if the predictions here surprised at least one of the two camps.

Also put in the cruxes table the thing they've circled forever but never written down: whether the wrong of exclusion (if any) should be indexed to how desperate the excluded person's alternatives are — i.e. whether turning away someone with excellent options elsewhere and turning away someone with none are the same act, morally. Include a simple case both sides would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction.

4. JSON SUMMARY at the very end: a single JSON object with fields — "substantive_disagreements" (array of strings), "verbal_disputes" (array of strings), "most_promising_test" (string: the single case most likely to actually move one of them), and "would_sign" (boolean: whether, in your honest judgement, each side as described could really sign this without gritting their teeth — if false, say why inside "most_promising_test").

Make sure neither side gets to be a strawman — the restrictionist gets real arguments (self-determination, the "admission is never owed" thought, worries about whom the state is for), and so does the open-borders person. The whole thing must stand on its own: no citations, no bibliographies, no name-drops of specific books.
