Exam question (25 points): Sober and Wilson argue that a good deal of the case against group selection, as pressed by Williams and other critics, rests on an "averaging fallacy." (a) Explain what this fallacy is supposed to be. (b) Explain how the Price equation is used to argue that, in group-structured populations, the "group selection" and "individual selection in context" descriptions are equivalent, and say how Williams's own distinction between organic and biotic adaptation bears on the issue. (c) Explain how Okasha's MLS1/MLS2 distinction complicates the claim that this equivalence deflates the group selection controversy.

Student's answer: The averaging fallacy is when you compute a trait's adaptive value by averaging the fitness of its bearers across all social contexts, ignoring the fact that individuals are non-randomly distributed into groups. Altruists do worse than selfish types within any group, so if you average an altruist's fitness across contexts — weighting each group by its size — you can conclude that altruism is selected against overall. But this conceals that altruists tend to live in groups with other altruists, which have higher group productivity. Williams coined the term "averaging fallacy" in Adaptation and Natural Selection to expose exactly this error, though he himself was skeptical of group selection.

The Price equation shows that the total change in the frequency of a trait in a group-structured population can be decomposed into two terms: a between-group term, the covariance of group mean trait value with group productivity, and a within-group term, the average change within groups. For an altruistic trait the within-group term is negative and the between-group term is positive. Since the same total change is thereby redescribable either way, group selection and individual selection are just two accounting schemes for one process. This is also why group selection is equivalent to kin selection: the between-group covariance plays the role of relatedness. And the equivalence generalizes — species selection, too, is just gene-level selection looked at from another angle, so nothing is really selected above the individual level in any case. Williams's distinction between organic adaptations (benefiting the individual) and biotic adaptations (benefiting the group but not the individual) shows that group selection is precisely the process that generates biotic adaptations, so the equivalence confirms that such adaptations are routinely produced whenever the between-group term is strong.

The MLS1/MLS2 distinction was introduced by Sober and Wilson in Unto Others. MLS1 is where group fitness is defined as the average fitness of the group's members, and MLS2 is where it isn't. The equivalence argument applies to both. Since the Price equation is a mathematical tautology that holds regardless of empirical facts, the upshot is that the group selection controversy is purely verbal: whether we say "group selection" is just a matter of bookkeeping preference, and the debate has no empirical content.
