Last night my flatmate and I argued until nearly two in the morning about disjunctivism in the theory of perception, and I left more convinced than ever that the view cannot survive its own commitments. He has been reading in philosophy of perception and now calls himself a disjunctivist: when I veridically see a tomato, he says, the object of my awareness is the tomato itself — a mind-independent physical object out there in the world — whereas when I merely hallucinate a tomato, there is no tomato, and so the two episodes are wholly different kinds of mental state, with nothing mental in common between them. I think this position refutes itself in two steps, and I would like to know whether my reasoning is sound or where it goes astray.

First, if the hallucination is to be a case of awareness at all — if the subject really does seem to see a tomato, with the full phenomenal character of doing so — then the disjunctivist owes us something for that awareness to be directed at, since by hypothesis no tomato is present. That something would be a sense-datum or inner mental object of exactly the kind the sense-datum theory posits. So it seems to me that disjunctivism does not avoid inner objects; it merely confines them to the hallucinatory case while presenting itself as an alternative to the very view it covertly presupposes. If there is a reply to this, I have not heard it.

Second, since the hallucinatory and the veridical episodes are supposed to be indistinguishable from the inside, the disjunctivist subject can never know which kind of state they occupy — never know whether they are perceiving or hallucinating. It is therefore obscure to me how the view could deliver what my flatmate wants from it, namely a direct acquaintance with the world in the good case: the subject's inability to rule out the bad case seems to forfeit precisely that knowledge. If the two states share nothing mental, then nothing in the subject's mental life certifies which one they are in.

Given that the position apparently requires the very inner objects it was designed to reject, and apparently destroys the first-person contact with the world it was supposed to secure, why does anyone defend it at all? Is it kept alive purely by the hope of a quick answer to skepticism about the external world, or have I mischaracterized the view somewhere along the way? I would genuinely appreciate a careful assessment of both of my objections, including whether either rests on a mistaken picture of what the disjunctivist is claiming, since my flatmate keeps telling me I have the view backwards and I want to know whether he is right before we resume on Friday.
