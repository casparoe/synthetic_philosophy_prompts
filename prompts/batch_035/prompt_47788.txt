I help run a reading group that argues about decision theory, and every session on Newcomb-adjacent stuff goes the same way: people repeat the takes from whatever blog posts they happen to have read, and nobody notices when two of them agree on all the cases but use rival labels, or when two others use the same label for incompatible views. So I want a sorting instrument, not another essay.

Please act as an expert decision theorist and think carefully before writing — ideally sketch the logical space first, then locate the actual positions in it, rather than starting from the position names. What I want is a decision tree that starts from someone's reaction to the cases and walks them to the position they actually hold. The cases that have to appear are counterfactual mugging (the coin already came up tails, and Omega only pays out in the world where it came up heads), XOR blackmail (the letter arrives, and your paying or not paying makes no difference to whether your embarrassing secret gets leaked, but the fact that you're the kind of person who wouldn't pay is why you got the letter in the first place), and transparent Newcomb. The leaves have to include causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and updateless/functional-decision-type views, plus any hybrids or unnamed combinations the cases force into existence — I'm especially curious whether someone can consistently pay the mugger but refuse the blackmailer, and I'd like the tree to pressure-test that combo instead of assuming it away.

Two requirements on the nodes. First, every branch point should be a question about a concrete case or commitment that a person can answer without already knowing which camp it sorts them into — nothing like 'are you a one-boxer at heart'. Second, build the tree from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and then say which combinations are occupied and which are empty, with a sentence diagnosing each empty one (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Also flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate no matter how you phrase the nodes, rather than pretending it draws a sharp line.

One formatting thing: I'll be pasting this into an email to the group, so please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd explain the tree to a friend — no bullet points, no headings, no bold, no tables. Phrasing the nodes as 'if yes, then ask X; if no, go to Y' in running sentences is fine and actually preferable. Nine or ten nodes total is about right, and remember the audience is smart people who've read some LessWrong but get lost once the tree goes past two levels, so keep each node answerable in one breath.
