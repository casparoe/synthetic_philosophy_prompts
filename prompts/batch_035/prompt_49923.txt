I'd like you to write a short structured essay, in your capacity as a professional philosopher with a background in ethics and philosophy of mind, and I ask that you think the issues through carefully before committing anything to the page. The topic is whether the moral considerability of a creature should be understood as coming in degrees that track the sophistication of its inner life — roughly, the view that the richer and more integrated a being's capacity for experience, the more its experiences count morally, but with no sharp line anywhere within the animal kingdom, so that a bee matters, just less than a dog, which matters less than a person.

Here is the controversy I want you to work through. On the graded view, trade-offs between beings are in principle commensurable: a harm to a bee can be outweighed by a modest benefit to a dog, or by a vanishingly small benefit spread across a sufficiently large number of bees, and nothing is categorically off-limits so long as the arithmetic comes out right. Many people feel this gets something badly wrong — it seems to license, at some exchange rate, trading away the deepest interests of a lower creature for the whim of a higher one, which they take to show that the real moral currency is not degrees of status but limits on what may be traded at any rate. The obvious alternative, though, looks worse: any threshold we draw — at beings capable of valenced experience, at vertebrates, at creatures with a sense of their own past — will leave some being just barely below the line, and it seems perverse that a creature nearly indistinguishable from one just above the threshold receives no consideration at all while its neighbor receives full protection. A few seeds you may or may not find useful: it's worth asking whether the choice is really exhaustive, since one might hold that status is equal across sentient beings while the strength of what is owed to them varies, or that some interests are non-negotiable while others may be weighed; and it's worth pressing on what happens at the very bottom of the scale, where the capacity for anything resembling experience fades into mere reactivity — whether the graded view quietly becomes a threshold view down there, and whether that is a fatal inconsistency or an honest admission that the continuum simply runs out.

As for form: give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no concluding remarks — four points that do the real work of the essay, and a fifth that states your verdict and the single strongest reason for it. One request about honesty: if you think the way I've set things up — graded status versus thresholds, with commensurability as the price of grading — is confused, or rests on a false dichotomy, don't answer within my frame just to be agreeable; say so plainly in one of the five points, and say what you would put in its place.
