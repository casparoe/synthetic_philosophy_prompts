I've drifted into holding a strong version of the Kelly/log-wealth view: that rational choice under repeated multiplicative risk is about time-average growth rates of your wealth, not ensemble averages, and that the arguments for maximizing expected log wealth are stronger than people give them credit for -- the ergodicity framing, the almost-sure dominance of Kelly in the long run, the way it dissolves the St. Petersburg-ish cases where naive expected value says bet everything. Call it geometric rationality if you like. I've already absorbed the standard objections and put qualifications on the view accordingly: finite lifetimes (so the asymptotic argument weakens), bets that aren't proportional to wealth (the multiplicative structure isn't always there), and the old line that log utility is just one utility function among many (which I answer by saying I'm not *assuming* log utility, I'm deriving it from growth-rate dominance). My worry is that with all these qualifications stacked up, the view doesn't really risk anything anymore -- it never tells me what to do in a genuinely one-shot, ruin-adjacent situation where I'd be tempted to take a fat-tailed positive-EV bet, and a view of rationality that never bites in the hard cases feels like it's been insulated into unfalsifiability. So: what actually counts against my position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, I want to know which ones serious defenders of the Kelly view would themselves concede (not just what critics say), and I want an honest verdict on whether the qualified version I've described still makes real predictions that expected-value maximization doesn't -- or whether I've quietly turned it into a platitude about liking money and not going broke. If the latter, tell me whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or just what happens to any decision theory once it meets edge cases.
