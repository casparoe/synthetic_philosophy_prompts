the question was:

"Nagel argues that we cannot help judging people for things that are partly a matter of luck. Explain what the problem of moral luck is and why it arises (i.e. the tension it creates), give an example of each of the kinds of luck he distinguishes, and then assess whether our ordinary practice of holding people responsible can survive the problem."

her answer:

"The problem of moral luck is that we judge people differently based on outcomes that were partly luck. Nagel starts from the control principle, that you can only be morally assessed for what's under your control. But basically nothing is fully under your control — your genes, upbringing, even whether the pedestrian steps out while you're driving drunk — so if you took the principle seriously, all moral judgment would collapse, which is absurd. So Nagel's solution is to reject the control principle: he argues it's just false, and once we see that, luck-involving judgment is fine and the problem dissolves.

He distinguishes four kinds. Resultant luck is luck in how things turn out — the classic example is two equally reckless drivers, one happens to hit a child and one doesn't, and we blame the first way more. Circumstantial luck is luck in your circumstances, so really the same thing as resultant — the driver who hits the child was unlucky in his circumstances. Constitutive luck is luck in who you are, your temperament and character. Causal luck is luck in how your actions are determined by prior events.

I think our practice can survive this. The move is that only what was under the agent's will should count: the two drivers were equally bad qua their choice, and the difference between them is just epistemic. Nagel makes basically this same point at the end of the essay. So blame survives, it just tracks the will."
