I've been following the decision-theory threads on LessWrong and the surrounding blogs for over a decade, and one thing that has always struck me about Parfit's hitchhiker is how quietly every discussion assumes the protagonist would prefer to be the sort of person who pays. I want that assumption stress-tested rather than repeated. Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications: (a) it preserves the hitchhiker structure — whether a benefit is extended now depends on how the recipient is predicted to behave later — but the predictor is no better than a shrewd human reading trustworthiness from a conversation, with no perfect accuracy and no science-fictional technology; (b) the agent has no access to precommitment devices, binding contracts, or reputation systems, so being predicted trustworthy is achievable only by actually being trustworthy; (c) the setting is ordinary life — a loan among acquaintances, a rental, a stranded-motorist rescue — and the scenario itself is one paragraph. The twist I want engineered into the case: the predictor should be self-interested and should profit from the recipient's future irrationality in a way that only a genuinely irrational recipient would permit, so that being predictably rational costs the agent the benefit, being predictably irrational secures it, and yet the benefit is worth less than the exploitation it exposes the agent to. After the scenario, walk through what causal, evidential, and updateless agents each do and why; identify which structural feature is doing the work; then address the Gauthier-style move — whether an agent can rationally choose a disposition whose value it could only recognize by already having it, or whether that regress undermines the whole "rational irrationality" response to the hitchhiker. Finish by telling me what minimal change to the case would restore the standard lesson that being disposed to keep one's word dominates.
