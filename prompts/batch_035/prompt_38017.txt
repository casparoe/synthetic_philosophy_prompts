I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums since well before they had this name — long enough that expected-value reasoning feels less like a tool I apply than a habit I have. These days I do grant evaluation for a small longtermist fund, and we're stuck on a decision that I suspect is not really about the decision in front of us.

The proposal would fund a program of small-scale field trials for stratospheric aerosol injection. Our forecasters produced a stream of estimates of the program's net effect, and the estimates alternate in sign as the analysis is extended: modest near-term benefits from the knowledge gained, then a set of medium-term costs — political complacency about emissions, some regional disruption — that offset part of the benefit, then longer-term benefits that offset part of that, then further costs, and so on, with each successive layer smaller in magnitude than the one before, by roughly a constant factor. Read at any finite horizon the program looks clearly worth funding; it's the total that's the trouble.

When three of us tried to total it, we got three different verdicts. Summed in chronological order, the estimates come to a small positive number, and the program clears our funding bar. A colleague who modeled the identical estimates by causal depth instead — all direct effects first, then first-order side effects, then second-order ones — got a negative total from the very same numbers. A third excluded everything past a horizon she judged too speculative to count, and landed somewhere in between. Each of them insists their way of adding it up is the natural one, and I've noticed that each of them prefers, as it happens, the arrangement that yields the verdict they walked in wanting.

So my question is not whether we should fund the trials. It's whether, given a stream of estimates like this, there is any fact of the matter about the program's expected value at all — or whether we've landed in a case where the appeal to expected value doesn't determine an answer, and the order of summation is doing all the work while passing as a technicality. If the total genuinely depends on how the terms are arranged, what should a fund whose entire methodology is expected-value reasoning do here? Is there a principled way to select one arrangement, or a principled reason to say that no such selection can be made? And if the answer is that our method simply runs out on cases like this, I'd like to know how to recognize the boundary in advance — how to tell which of our decisions the numbers can actually settle, and which ones merely appear settled by them.
