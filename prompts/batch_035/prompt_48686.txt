Retired physical chemist here -- stat mech was my bread and butter for 35 years, and now that I'm done with the lab I've been reading philosophy of time as a hobby, which has been humbling in ways I did not expect. Here's what's bugging me.

The thermodynamic arrow debate seems to have been stuck basically since Boltzmann, and I want a diagnosis of *why* it's stuck, not another survey of the positions. As I understand it the live positions are roughly: (1) the low-entropy initial condition is a brute fact about boundary conditions, not the kind of thing that needs explaining; (2) it needs explaining and the "past hypothesis" -- making the low-entropy big bang a lawlike postulate, or at least a fundamental datum -- is the way to do it; and (3) various anthropic/fluctuation-story attempts to get the arrow out of a time-symmetric theory plus a selection effect, which as far as I can tell keep running into the problem that they make the current low-entropy state look like a fluctuation and predict a heat-death bubble just over the horizon. On top of that there's a separate entanglement with the causation literature: some philosophers want to ground the asymmetry of causation in the entropy gradient, others want to ground the entropy gradient in causal asymmetry, and a few seem to think this is fine because it's not really circular.

Here's the thing. When I was working, nobody ever asked why the initial condition was what it was. You put it in by hand and got on with calculating diffusion coefficients. The philosophers I'm reading say that attitude is exactly the problem -- that the low-entropy beginning is the single most astonishing fact about the universe and treating it as a free parameter is intellectual malpractice. And what strikes me is that both sides seem to agree on all the physics: same kinetic equation, same cosmology, same Boltzmann brains. So my working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about physics at all but about what counts as an explanation vs. a boundary condition -- about where explanation is allowed to stop. But I honestly can't tell whether that's right, or whether it's a disguised disagreement about probability (what it even means to put a measure on initial macrostates), or whether the two camps are just answering different questions under one heading.

So: diagnose the structure of this disagreement for me. Verbal or substantive? If substantive, is it conceptual, or is there a hidden empirical question buried in it (something cosmological observation could in principle settle, or a formal result about what a time-symmetric theory can or cannot entail)? Where does the causation entanglement fit in -- is that a real linkage or two debates that got welded together by accident? And be honest about whether anything could actually end it: a derivation of the past hypothesis, a no-go theorem, a new distinction, or nothing, and why.

Once you've made the case in prose, wrap it up with a table I can keep for my notes, with these columns: **Diagnosis** (a short name for the kind of disagreement at issue), **Who holds something like it** (which camp's complaints it captures best), **Best evidence for it** (from what people actually argue), **What it leaves unexplained** (the objections it doesn't handle), and **Progress test** (one concrete thing that would move the debate if that diagnosis is right). Include every serious candidate diagnosis you discuss, even the ones you reject, and then add one final row labeled VERDICT where the Diagnosis column states your actual conclusion and the other columns are filled in to match. I dont need the table to settle the physics -- I need it to show me what kind of fight this actually is.
