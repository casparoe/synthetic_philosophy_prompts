You are a professional philosopher working on posthumous harm and the ethics of memory, with a serious side interest in theater criticism, and you think carefully rather than quickly. I am writing a long essay on what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them, and I want to center it on Peter Shaffer's Amadeus — the 1979 play and Miloš Forman's 1984 film. Before committing to a thesis, begin with a short brainstorm (five or six bullets) mapping the interpretive options, and only then develop the reading you think is strongest.

Here is what I want you to take seriously. The historical Salieri almost certainly did not poison Mozart, and Shaffer knew it; the work's central crime is a legend that it knowingly repeats and enlarges. Yet the work is not careless with the dead — it is, among other things, a meditation on how posterity remembers and misremembers, staged as an old man's confession addressed to the future. So the crude question — is it wrong to slander Salieri for the sake of a better drama? — seems too easy. What interests me is the knot underneath it.

A distinction I've been toying with: falsifications of the dead seem to come in at least two kinds. Accusative falsification tells a lie about what someone did — Salieri as murderer. Appropriative falsification conscripts a real person into a story whose meaning serves someone else — Mozart, in the play, is less a man than an allegory of grace lavished on the unworthy, God's unfair distribution of gifts. Accusation wrongs by misattributing deeds; appropriation wrongs by using a person. I am not sure the distinction holds, or where it breaks, and I'd like you to pressure-test it: does it classify the work's treatment of its two dead men correctly, or does the work show the categories bleeding into each other?

Second, and to me the sharper problem: the play seems to give Salieri exactly what the historical man is said to have craved — an enduring attachment to Mozart's fame — at the price of being remembered as his killer. Posthumous benefit and posthumous harm braided together inseparably, the same legend delivering both. If we owe the dead something, what do we say about a work that pays the debt in the very currency of the injury? Does the frame device — the confession narrated to posterity, Salieri casting himself as the enemy of God and the appointed protector of the mediocre — make the work self-aware about what it does to its dead? And does that awareness excuse, mitigate, or aggravate the wrong? Distinguish those three verdicts carefully; they are not interchangeable.

Your deliverable: a defended reading of about 900 to 1200 words that (1) states what position, if any, the work takes on the ethics of its own falsifications; (2) evaluates my accusative/appropriative distinction against the text, including at least one place where it fails or needs repair; and (3) says what the work adds to the philosophical debate about posthumous harm that argument alone doesn't — or, if you think it adds nothing, concedes that honestly and explains why the intuition that it adds something persists anyway. Be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the play to an illustration of the ethics, and don't bend the ethics to fit the play. If you think the film and the play differ in ways that matter for the question, say so rather than flattening them. No citations or reading lists are wanted — argue from the work and the ideas themselves.
