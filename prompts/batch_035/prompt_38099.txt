For over a decade the argument about the scientific standing of the free energy principle has been running in place, and I want a diagnosis of the standoff, not another round of it. On one side, Friston and the defenders: self-organizing systems that persist must, on pain of dissolving, occupy a small set of attractor states, and variational free energy is the upper bound on surprisal that such systems must keep low — this is presented as a mathematical framework that constrains and unifies process theories like predictive coding and active inference, not as an ordinary falsifiable hypothesis. On the other side, the critics: read one way, the principle is true by a selection effect (the systems we observe are by definition the ones that didn't encounter unsurvivable surprises, so 'organisms avoid surprise' is like noting that gamblers at the table are ones who haven't yet gone bust); read another way, it makes predictions that seem obviously false — an agent that genuinely minimized sensory surprise should retreat to a dark, silent room — and when the defenders patch this by distinguishing surprise about sensations from surprise about the states causing them, the critics charge that each patch converts an empirical claim into a definitional one. The defenders reply that demanding falsifiability of the framework itself is a category error: the risk lives in the models, not the principle, the way ideal gas laws or the good regulator theorem carry content through what you build with them. Nobody has moved in years.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive empirical dispute hidden under equivocation on 'free energy' and 'surprise' — a statistical bound on the one hand, a biological achievement on the other? Is it a clash between two philosophies of science — falsificationism versus a model-building, framework-first picture — dressed up as a dispute about the brain? Or is one side asking what the principle claims and the other what it's good for, so that both are right about their own question? I'll offer my own hypothesis, to be tested rather than accepted: that the principle is claimed at several timescales at once — moment-to-moment perception, an organism's lifetime, an evolutionary lineage — and it is near-definitional at the lineage level but empirically substantive at the perception level, with both sides sliding between scales and so talking past each other while believing they are refuting or defending one thing. Diagnose the impasse with reference to what each side actually says (including their treatment of the dark room objection and of the selection-effect charge), assess whether my hypothesis captures it or misses what's really load-bearing, and tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a worked model that both sides would have to score the same way — or whether the disagreement is so far upstream, in what a scientific contribution must be, that it should be expected to persist indefinitely. I've read the main exchanges, so don't survey them back to me; I want the structural diagnosis.
