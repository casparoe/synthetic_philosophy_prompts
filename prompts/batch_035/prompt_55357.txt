I'm in a philosophy of science reading group working through the laws-of-nature literature — we've spent a few weeks on Lewis's best-system approach and on Armstrong's view of laws as necessitation relations among universals — and one member, a fairly committed Humean, has a refrain she deploys in nearly every meeting: "the laws don't govern anything; they just describe what happens." Most recently she used it to answer the question of what enforces the regularity that like charges repel everywhere — nothing enforces it, she said, there is only the pattern, and demanding an enforcer is demanding a mysterious extra ingredient in the world over and above the facts. But a couple of meetings earlier, when someone asked whether the laws could have been different, or in what sense the regularities are necessary rather than just widespread, she reached for exactly the same sentence, and it struck me that the two uses pull in different directions: the first seems to deny that there is any substantive fact of lawhood beyond our summarizing activity, while the second seems to be a claim about the modal force — or lack of it — of whatever facts there are.

I'd like to know precisely what I'd be agreeing or disagreeing with if I nodded along. Please give me exactly five distinct, precise theses that "the laws just describe, they don't govern" could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion. Each entry should be a sentence or two: state the thesis, say whether it is plausible or contentious, and say whether it would actually support her first use (deflating the demand for an enforcer), her second use (deflating necessity), both, or neither. If any of the five theses is one an anti-Humean like Armstrong could happily accept, flag that in the entry, because I suspect she'd be annoyed to learn her slogan doesn't exclude him.
