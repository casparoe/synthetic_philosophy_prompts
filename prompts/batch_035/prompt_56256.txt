I teach the philosophy of mind section of an intro course, and every year the unit on consciousness collapses into a name-recitation contest: students memorize "Global Workspace Theory says..." and "Higher-Order Theory says..." while never having to commit to anything themselves. This year I want to force the issue with a diagnostic decision tree they'll walk through before we ever attach labels to the positions. Please write that tree for me.

The tree should start from a neutral starting point—something like "you grant that there is a real distinction between states we are conscious of and states processed in the brain without any awareness, as in blindsight"—and then branch on questions a student can answer purely on the basis of how they react to concrete cases, never on the basis of which theory they've heard of. So each node should be a case or thought experiment with a forced choice, and the leaves should be named positions. For example (use these or better ones of your own): the Sperling partial-report paradigm, where subjects report seeing a whole array of letters but can only report a few, as a probe for whether phenomenal experience outruns what is cognitively accessible; masked priming, where a word influences behavior while subjects deny seeing anything; split-brain patients, where the question is whether there are two streams of experience or one with impaired reporting; the hypothetical "phenomenal zombie" duplicate who is physically identical but experiences nothing; and the question of whether a honeybee, with recurrent but tiny neural circuitry, has any form of experience. The leaf positions I'd like covered are: first-order representationalism, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, recurrent processing theory, integrated information theory, and illusionism. Include "unnamed" leaves for combinations of answers that no camp actually occupies, with a sentence on why nobody sits there (incoherent? merely unexplored?), and flag any pair of leaves whose holders would give the same answers at every node—i.e., pairs your tree cannot genuinely separate even in principle.

Two constraints on construction: first, order the branch nodes so that the deepest metaphysical commitments (e.g., whether there is any fact of the matter about experience beyond function) come after the more case-driven commitments, so a student can be led into deep water gradually; second, keep every node answerable in good faith—a student should never need to reply "it depends on what you mean" without the tree offering that fork explicitly as a branch. Assume the audience is bright undergraduates with no prior philosophy of mind, so define nothing in jargon at the nodes themselves, but do use the standard names at the leaves. Please end with a final line beginning "Summary:" giving a one-sentence statement of the single hardest cut the tree forces on a student.
