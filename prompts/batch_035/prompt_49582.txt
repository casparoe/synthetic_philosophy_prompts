I'm the student representative on my university's Committee on Academic Standards, and at next month's meeting we'll consider a proposal that has split the faculty. The dean of students wants to pilot a "precommitment contract": during the first week of term, students could voluntarily sign away specific future options — no extensions on any coursework, no make-up exams without hospital documentation, a locked course load so that withdrawing after week three is recorded as a failure. Once signed, the contract could not be revoked, no matter how the student's circumstances or mind change. The rationale is that students systematically overestimate their future self-control and would, if given the chance, bind themselves the way Ulysses bound himself to the mast. A minority on the committee objects that this is not self-binding but a trap: the September self is effectively contracting on behalf of a different person in November, contracts will be signed under social pressure and one-upmanship, and the university quietly profits from the forfeits. My political philosophy seminar has just finished a unit on constitutional precommitment — we read Elster's Ulysses and the Sirens — and as the student member I'm expected to circulate a short opinion memo before the meeting. Please draft it: recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and make the recommendation turn on the hardest question underneath the proposal — when binding one's future self is an exercise of autonomy rather than a surrender of it, and who should be entitled to judge, after the fact, that the binding has gone wrong. The committee wants something actionable, with any conditions spelled out concretely, not a survey of theories of personal identity.
