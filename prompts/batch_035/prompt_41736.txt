My philosophy of science reading group has spent several months on anthropic reasoning, and we've hit a wall that feels characteristic rather than accidental. The dispute is the familiar one between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption: SSA tells you to reason as if you were a random sample from the observers in your reference class, while SIA tells you to weight hypotheses by the number of observers they predict, since you were guaranteed to find yourself existing either way. On the SSA side, the chief embarrassment is the Doomsday argument: applying SSA with the reference class of all humans, a straightforward application of Bayes shifts probability mass toward hypotheses in which humanity ends relatively soon, since your comparatively early birth rank is more probable under a short history. On the SIA side, the embarrassment is the presumptuous philosopher scenario: told that either one or a trillion observers will exist, with no evidence favoring either theory beyond their prior probabilities, the SIA reasoner confidently bets on the trillion, because the larger world makes their own existence far less surprising. Each camp's position seems to be maintained mostly by pointing at the other camp's embarrassment: defenders of SIA say the Doomsday conclusion is a reductio of SSA, defenders of SSA say that settling trillion-to-one cosmological bets on nothing but observer counts is a reductio of SIA, and neither side appears moved.

What I find frustrating is that the empirical and formal setup is not in dispute at all. Everyone agrees on the physical facts, on the priors, on Bayes' theorem, and on how to model ordinary self-less evidence. Yet after two decades of literature the two sides haven't converged an inch, and attempts to broker peace — arguing over reference classes, reformulating the choice between SSA and SIA as a choice of reference class plus sampling principle, connecting the issue to the Sleeping Beauty problem or to the measurement problem in Everettian quantum mechanics — seem to multiply the positions rather than reduce them. My working hypothesis, offered as just that, is that the disagreement is really about what one's own existence is evidence *of*: perhaps the SSA camp is treating 'I exist' as an ordinary observation to be conditioned on in the usual way, while the SIA camp is treating it as a precondition of there being any observation at all, in which case the two camps are running the same formal machinery on different inputs and their disagreement is verbal or structural rather than substantive. But I can also see it going the other way: perhaps there is a hidden substantive question about the metaphysics of possible observers, of whether 'I could have been' one of the people who exist in a larger universe, and the stalemate reflects that nobody wants to pay the metaphysical price openly.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal, substantive, a case of two questions being run under one heading, or a clash of starting intuitions that no argument can touch? Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims, say where you think my hypothesis goes right or wrong, and be concrete about what would count as progress — a theorem, a decision-theoretic reframing, a physical case where the two assumptions measurably diverge, a conceptual distinction — or whether the impasse is better explained by the question being ill-posed than by it merely being hard.
