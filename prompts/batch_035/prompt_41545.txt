I teach an intro ethics elective at a community college, and we just wrapped a unit on theories of punishment — retribution, deterrence, rehabilitation, the usual greatest hits. Next up is the death penalty, and instead of running another debate I thought I'd screen Twelve Angry Men (the 1957 Lumet film, Henry Fonda as the holdout juror) and have them read the film through the punishment debate. My hook is that the film is basically a feature-length thought experiment about executing the innocent: a capital case where the verdict has to be unamimous, the penalty is death, and the entire drama is about how much epistmic confidence the state needs before it kills someone. There's that old line about it being better for ten guilty people to go free than for one innocent person to be punished — I think it's usually credited to Blackstone, and the number has bounced around for centuries, ten, a hundred, a thousand — and I want to argue that reasonable doubt, as the film dramatizes it, is just that ratio made into a legal rule. Or maybe it's something deontological instead, a flat prohibition on killing the innocent that no ratio can price.

But I want to be careful not to cheat with this reading, because the film does something sneaky. As far as I can remember it never tells us whether the kid actually did it — the acquittal is purely about the standard of proof, not about innocence, which I actually think is the most philosophically interesting thing about it. Also juror 8 kind of engineers the whole thing (he buys an identical switchblade to undermine the uniqueness claim, he goads the others into re-enacting the old man's walk to his door), and that cuts both ways: is he a hero of epistemic humility or a rogue juror running his own private investigation outside the trial? And then there's the retributivist side — the film's most retributive voice, the furious dad with the broken relationship with his own son, is clearly the villain of the room, which feels like the deck being stacked: the strongest version of retribution never really gets to speak in that jury room. I'd want a reading that's honest about that imbalance rather than one that just celebrates the verdict.

So: can you give me a reading of the film through the punishment debate that I can build a class session around? Please structure it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion — I'm imagining five moments from the film (the opening show-of-hands vote, the knife, the re-enactment of the witness's walk, the holdout's final breakdown — you pick the best five) each read through one of the concepts: reasonable doubt as a ratio, retribution, deterrence, irreversibility of error, whatever fits. At least one point should argue the side of the film's losers — what a retributivist or a deterrence hawk would say the jury actually got wrong. Mark which of the five points you'd most want students to argue against each other on, and say plainly if any of my framing above doesn't hold up. No citations or reading lists, just your reading of the film.
