I live in a 40-person cohousing community where basically everything gets decided in two-hour evening meetings, and there's a recurring fight I'm tired of having. Someone does something in their own unit or garden -- cooking smells, weird paint, hoarding stuff on their patio -- and half the community says "your business, it's your home," while the other half says "we all share air and sightlines and the vibe, it affects us, it goes to consensus." We have no principled way of deciding which is which, so every dispute gets relitigated from scratch and the loudest person usually wins. I'm on the 'stewardship circle' and I've been tasked with coming up with something better than vibes.

Here's why I'm asking you and not a management blog: a friend who did grad school in philosophy told me about Sen's liberal paradox, and it's been bugging me since. As I understand it, Sen showed that you can't simultaneously have (a) a rule that if every single person prefers one option over another, the group should never pick the one nobody wants, and (b) a rule that each person gets to be decisive over at least some pair of options in their own sphere -- if you insist on both, the group's preferences can cycle with no consistent outcome. So the naive "everyone affected gets a say, but people are sovereign over their own stuff" combo is apparently mathematically doomed, at least taken strictly. I half remember the example being about a book one person is prudish about. Anyway: build on that, but don't assume I remember the details, and feel free to tell me if I've garbled it.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure my tired, non-philosopher neighbors can actually run when a dispute comes up: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with "individual decides, period," and where it correctly refuses to settle the matter and hands it to the group because it's genuinely a values question. It has to cope with our real hard cases, which include: a resident whose composting is genuinely foul near a shared patio, a resident who wants to keep their blinds permanently down in a way that makes the courtyard look derelict, and someone who wants to display something political on their own front door that others find hateful. Then run it on those three, and include at least one case where the procedure gives the answer most of the community would hate -- and say what the procedure can't do, e.g. whether it can stop us from just gerrymandering the 'sphere' of each decision until we get the outcome we wanted anyway. Keep the Sen machinery under the hood as much as you can; my neighbors will not read a paragraph about social choice theory, they will read a checklist.
