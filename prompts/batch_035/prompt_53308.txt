I'm teaching an introductory political philosophy course, and we've just finished a unit on luck egalitarianism -- the brute/option luck distinction, the thought that inequalities are unjust when they trace to circumstances but not when they trace to choices, and the standard objections about whether gambles are really voluntary and whether talents are earned. For the last session before finals I want to screen a film and have the students write a short response, and I've settled on Trading Places (1983), because its premise is almost a laboratory experiment the luck egalitarians never ran. Two elderly commodity brokers make a one-dollar bet about nature versus nurture and arrange a complete life-swap: they frame their pampered managing director, Louis Winthorpe, for theft, strip him of his job, money, and fiancée, and install Billy Ray Valentine, a homeless street hustler, in his house and position. The Dukes, in effect, are running their own crude version of the experiment that egalitarians ask us to imagine when they say our social positions are morally arbitrary.

Here's what I want you to do: give me a reading of the film through luck egalitarianism, one that takes both the film and the theory seriously and is honest about the fit in each direction. It seems obvious that the middle of the film flatters the luck egalitarian: Winthorpe's swift collapse into desperation and crime suggests his success was scaffolding rather than desert, and Valentine's competence in the managing director's job suggests his poverty was circumstance rather than character. But I'm less sure about the ending, and I'd like your help thinking it through. Valentine and Winthorpe, tipped off by the Dukes' own crooked plan to steal a crop report, corner the frozen orange juice market and destroy the Dukes -- a fortune acquired in a single afternoon on the basis of inside information. Is that ending a triumph of egalitarian desert, a restoration of luck all the way down (their windfall is the purest brute luck imaginable), or does the film just not care about desert and instead punish the Dukes for their cruelty and dishonesty, which is a rather different and more relational thought? I'd also like to know whether the film has any implicit answer to the question of whether the Dukes *deserve* to lose everything on a one-dollar bet they made freely, which is the classic problem about whether gamblers really own their outcomes.

Since my students won't be able to rewatch specific scenes easily, flag the moments you'd want them to remember in class discussion -- for instance Valentine's first day inside the exchange, the scene where he overhears the Dukes planning to return him to the streets, Winthorpe's breakdown, and the final trading-floor sequence -- and say what each one shows or complicates. Please don't quote dialogue; work from plot and situation. And end by telling me whether you think the film adds anything to the luck egalitarianism debate that the arguments by themselves don't, or whether it only illustrates positions we already have on paper -- if the latter, I should probably pick a different film, and I'd rather know that now.
