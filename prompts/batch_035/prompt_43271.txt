In three months I am to be married, and a question about the ethics of belief that I had always been content to treat as academic has recently become anything but. My oldest friend, whom I have long consulted on what one may rightly believe, has over the years handed down verdicts on a series of cases, and I would like a prediction of what she will say about mine — along with the reasoning behind the prediction, since I suspect the answer is not obvious even to someone who knows her well.

Her verdicts so far: first, a neighbor told she has roughly a forty percent chance of surviving five years, who believes she will be among the survivors — permissible, even admirable. Second, an acquaintance founding a company who believes it will succeed against crushing base rates, when a market study he could commission for a few thousand dollars would largely settle the matter — not permissible. Third, a woman who believes her husband faithful despite an anonymous letter, where the only ways of investigating would involve corrosive spying on a man who may well be innocent — permissible. Fourth, a graduate student who believes her favored hypothesis true before running the experiments, on the ground that conviction makes her a bolder scientist — not permissible. Fifth, a colleague raised without religion who came in her thirties to believe in God, not on evidence but because the belief gave her life a meaning she could find nowhere else — permissible.

My case: during a brief separation two years ago, my partner spent a weekend about which I know little. Her account of it has never wavered. A recent remark from a mutual friend — ambiguous, but troubling — suggested that someone was with her then who was not me. I could, with perhaps three quiet conversations, establish the facts; those conversations would themselves be small betrayals, conducted behind her back. Or I could go on believing her account, not because I can honestly say the balance of evidence favors it over the remark, but because the believing and the marriage it sustains seem to me one thing rather than two.

Predict her verdict, and more importantly, show your work. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record — I take it some Jamesian test for live, forced, and momentous options is one possibility, some Cliffordian rule about avoidable ignorance another, and perhaps a third rule about a belief's consequences for action — say what each predicts about my case, and tell me which you would bet on and why. I am less interested in the answer than in whether her precedents actually determine it. Finally, since the rules that would permit my belief may not be a single rule, name the further case, in this same register of intimate life, that would best tell them apart.
