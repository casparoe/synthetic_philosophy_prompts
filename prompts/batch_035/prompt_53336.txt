For thirty-one years I was a gravitational physicist, the last fifteen of them doing numerical work on compact-object mergers, and since retiring I have been auditing a university seminar on the philosophy of space and time. The seminar has spent most of a term on spacetime substantivalism versus relationism, and I am struck by how much it resembles a chess opening everyone has memorized. Newton's bucket; then the two globes joined by a cord, where the tension in the cord is supposed to betray true rotation even in an otherwise empty universe; Leibniz's insistence that motion is only relative; Mach's retort that the water rotates with respect to the distant masses and that in a universe emptied of them the surface would stay flat; and finally general relativity, which appears to hand a trophy to both sides — diffeomorphism invariance and the hole argument seem to strip manifold points of any individuality, while the metric field, a dynamical entity that interacts with matter and refuses to be reduced to it, looks for all the world like the substance the relationist swore off. The students cite all of this fluently, and nobody has moved an inch in ten weeks.

I will add a wrinkle from my own career. In the simulations, the labels on manifold points were pure bookkeeping: a gauge transformation relabeled them with no physical consequence, exactly as the hole argument says. And yet when we posed the initial-value problem, the constraints never let us specify the physics in purely relational terms — we always had to fix something extra on an underlying manifold, and I could never decide whether that was a fact about the world or an artifact of our formalism. I have also read enough of the relational reconstruction programs (the Machian style in which time itself is supposed to drop out of a dynamics over pure relative configurations) to know these are serious attempts, not nostalgia for Leibniz, and that their empirical equivalence to standard general relativity is itself disputed.

Here is my question, and I want a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict on who is right. The combatants agree on every equation and every experiment, and have since at least 1915; nobody converts. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it substantive — a genuine dispute about what exists, hostage only to future physics — or has it quietly become a dispute about explanation, with the substantivalist allowing unobservable inertial structure to do explanatory work and the relationist refusing that allowance as a matter of principle? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work after all — whether a fully relational dynamics is empirically distinguishable from a spacetime-based one — or are the two camps answering different questions under one heading, an ontological question and a methodological one? Note, in fairness to the substantivalist, that Kretschmann's old complaint about general covariance suggests some of the allegedly relational content of general relativity may be an artifact of formulation, which cuts both ways.

Diagnose the structure of the impasse using what each side actually says, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an experiment — or argue honestly that none should be expected and why. Whatever you conclude, end your answer with a final line of the form \diagnosis{...}, containing a label of three to eight words naming what kind of disagreement you take this to be.
