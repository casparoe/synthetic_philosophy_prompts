I work as an underwriting analyst at a mid-sized reinsurer, and our pricing pipeline assumes that every submission can be reduced to a loss distribution: actuaries fit severity and frequency curves to historical data, apply loadings, and quote. This works for windstorm and motor liability, where we have decades of instrumented data. But over the past two years we've been getting submissions where there is no meaningful base rate — business-interruption cover for a novel pandemic pathogen, silent cyber exposure in a legacy industrial portfolio, and contingent liability for companies deploying AI systems that make consequential decisions about customers. Our underwriters disagree wildly about whether to treat these as ordinary risks (just use expert-judgment priors and price as usual) or as categorically different. I've been tasked with producing a written procedure the team can run on any submission, and I want it grounded in the actual theory of decision-making under ambiguity rather than in gut feel about "unknowable" risks.

Here is the conceptual material I'd like the procedure to preserve, and the simplifications I'd like you to make explicit. The classic reference point is Ellsberg's three-color urn: betting on the color whose composition is known versus the one that is not, and the fact that people's choices there violate the sure-thing principle in a systematic way (the "ambiguity aversion" pattern). I understand there are several established ways to model this — subjective expected utility with a single prior that just encodes whatever we know, maxmin expected utility with a set of priors in the Gilboa–Schmeidler tradition, and smooth models in the style of Klibanoff, Marinacci, and Mukerji that separate second-order belief about which prior is right from an ambiguity attitude applied to it. I don't need the math; I need the procedure to translate the distinctions that matter: when "just put a prior on it" is defensible versus when it hides disagreement we can't justify aggregating; when treating all priors as equally possible is conservative versus sneaky (it isn't always either); and how expert disagreement, nonstationarity, and missing data should feed into whether a submission is flagged as ambiguous at all. Where the theory is genuinely contested — for instance, whether ambiguity aversion is a rational response to missing information, a proxy for how the world punishes overconfidence, or just a bias our procedure shouldn't institutionalize — the procedure should say which reading it is assuming and why, rather than presenting the choice as settled.

The deliverable should be a table with these columns: Step number; Question asked about the submission; Evidence or input needed; What counts as a sufficient answer; Ambiguity trigger (what answer would flag the submission); and Action if triggered. The actions can be concrete underwriting options — price with a standard ambiguity loading, require co-insurance or policy exclusions, escalate to the risk committee, or decline — and I'd like the procedure to say which of those actions follows from which modeling tradition, so the team understands the reasoning, not just the verdict. Please make the procedure honest about where it will misfire and mark the steps where a judgment call cannot be eliminated.

Then run it on three test submissions, at least one of which should produce an answer I won't like — in particular, include a case where we have abundant historical data but the underlying process has plausibly changed (e.g., flood exposure in a region where the climate baseline has shifted), such that the procedure flags it as ambiguous and blocks the routine renewal pricing our client expects. Another test case should be one where our own underwriters' disagreement is the main evidence of ambiguity, and the procedure has to say whether that disagreement is information or noise. Finally, for each test case, please conclude with a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "submission" (a short label), "ambiguity_flagged" (boolean), "triggering_steps" (list of step numbers), "recommended_action" (one of the concrete actions above), "modeling_tradition_relied_on" (string), "residual_judgment_call" (boolean), and "rationale" (two or three sentences). Follow the JSON objects with a short prose assessment of where the procedure is weakest overall.
