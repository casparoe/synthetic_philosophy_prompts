I'm on the ethics committee of a regional hospital network (three hospitals, ~900 beds total) and I keep getting pulled into rationing decisions without anything resembling a framework. It's been small stuff so far — who gets the last ECMO machine during a bad flu surge, whether to spend a restricted donation on one gene therapy for a kid with a rare degenerative condition or spread it across the NICU, how to hand out the last 40 doses of a vaccine in short supply. But the calls are getting more frequent and our written justifications are a mess: one trustee keeps saying "greatest good for the greatest number," a family practitioner on the committee says we can't ever trade one person's life against other people's benefits like they're line items, and our lawyer mostly worries about discrimination claims, so nobody's actually arguing from the same premises. Last month we justified two nearly identical cases in opposite ways and a local journalist noticed.

Years ago I took a political philosophy course and the one thing that stuck with me was Scanlon's contractualism — the idea that a moral principle is justified when nobody could reasonably reject it, and that what matters is being able to justify the decision to each individual person affected, not just producing a good total outcome. What I remember liking about it is that it seemed to explain why we can refuse to take one patient's organs to save five others, while still leaving room to say numbers *sometimes* matter. But I also remember some professor bringing up a paper about a guy who asks whether you should flip a coin when you can save either one person or five, which if you take it seriously seems to imply numbers count for nothing, and that can't be right for us — if we can vaccinate 200 people against a mild but real risk of serious complications or 1 person against a slightly higher risk, telling the 200 "sorry, coin flip" would get us run out of town. So my question is: can contractualism actually be turned into a usable procedure, and where exactly does the counting question come out?

What I want is a written decision procedure the committee can run in an actual two-hour meeting: the steps in order, what counts as settling each step, and — this is important — clearly marked points where the procedure has to hand things over to the committee's judgment because it's a values question the theory genuinely doesn't answer. The people using it are clinicians, an administrator, a lawyer, and me; no philosophers, and nobody will read anything longer than a page or two. It has to produce a public-facing written justification each time, so the steps need to map onto sentences a hospital board can sign. Please run it on three cases: (1) the last ICU bed going to either a 34-year-old with a good prognosis after a long stay or a 68-year-old with a moderate one; (2) the choice between funding one very expensive therapy for a single child versus a screening program that would prevent smaller harms for hundreds of patients; and (3) the vaccine case above. I specifically want to know if the procedure gives an answer my committee will hate in one of these — I suspect it does on case 2 — and what the procedure flat-out cannot decide. Also, if you can, say something about whether chances (a 30% risk of death for one person) can be weighed against certain smaller harms across many people, because that comes up constantly in our allocation fights and I've never seen anyone handle it cleanly. Keep in mind we need to accomodate a lawyer who will ask whether every step is legally defensible, and be honest if there's a point where contractualism just breaks down and we'd be papering over the break with procedure.
