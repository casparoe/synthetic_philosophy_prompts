As part of my grade in an upper-level seminar on the evolution of communication (fourteen students, one fifty-minute session each of us has to lead), I've been assigned the week on costly signaling and the handicap principle. We've already read the standard material — Zahavi's proposal that signals like the peacock's tail are credible precisely because they are costly, the objection that this seems to make communication wasteful rather than efficient, and the general point that costless signals are "cheap talk" that low-quality senders can mimic. My classmates can recite all of this. What they haven't felt is the genuinely subtle part: that cost alone doesn't make a signal honest, and that a signal whose cost is identical for all types can destroy information rather than create it — what separates honest from dishonest signaling is the cost being differentially burdensome, so that it's cheap for high-quality senders and ruinous for low-quality ones. I want my session to make that distinction land experientially rather than through me explaining it.

Please design the session for me. The core should be a game with real choices and a payoff scheme, played over multiple rounds in three phases that differ only in the signaling rule: in phase one, signaling is free; in phase two, signaling carries a flat cost that is the same for every sender; in phase three, the cost is type-dependent — senders who are "high quality" pay the signal from a surplus while "low quality" senders must pay it out of a scarce endowment, so the same nominal signal has different marginal costs. Use a market-like frame (some participants are senders of two qualities assigned secretly, others are receivers who profit only from accepting high-quality senders), with token payoffs or lottery tickets toward a small prize rather than real money. Crucially, senders of both types should profit equally from being accepted, so that a flat cost can never separate the types and participants discover this themselves. I don't want the terms "handicap principle" or "costly signaling" spoken until the debrief; the theory should ambush them.

Please give me complete rules, materials, a round-by-round timeline that fits fifty minutes, the actual payoff numbers for senders, receivers, and each cost phase, and a prediction of what should happen in each phase. Then the debrief: how to map each phase onto cheap talk, the naive "cost equals credibility" intuition, and the differential-cost condition for honest signaling, without lecturing for more than ten minutes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — collusion between friends, receivers accepting everyone because the stakes feel fake, senders drifting rather than strategizing, the timeline blowing up — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about which features of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief: my classmates will reach the separating equilibrium by conscious calculation and trial-and-error within one afternoon, not by selection accumulating over generations; the type-dependence of costs is imposed by my rules rather than emerging from anything about the senders; and there is no room in fifty minutes for the frequency-dependent dynamics that matter when signalers and receivers co-evolve. I'd rather know exactly where the analogy is thin than have a session that quietly overclaims.
