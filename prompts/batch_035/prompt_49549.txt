I could use some help turning a vaguely worded county policy into something a committee can actually use. I was just appointed to our county's environmental health advisory commission — I'm a retired high school biology teacher, which makes me the de facto science person on a board of otherwise interested laypeople — and the timing is awkward. Last month, after a huge public fight, the board of supervisors added the precautionary principle to the county's general plan. The resolution says roughly that when an activity raises a threat of harm to human health or the environment, the county should take precautionary measures even if some cause-and-effect relationships aren't fully established scientifically, and that the proponent of the activity bears the burden of showing it's safe rather than the public bearing the burden of proving harm. Sounds reasonable enough, but in our first two meetings the principle got invoked in opposite directions on the same agenda item: parents want the school district's planned artificial turf fields blocked until the recycled-tire crumb rubber infill is proven safe, while the vector control district says that NOT running their larvicide program this spring would itself be a failure of precaution, because inaction isn't the absence of risk, it's just a different risk. Both sides sincerely believe they are the precautionary ones, and I couldn't articulate why both of them can't be.

What I need is a decision procedure the commission can actually run on a proposal, written for people like us: questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, which answers stop the procedure with a no, and which questions aren't really factual at all and have to get handed back to the elected board because they're value judgments we shouldn't be laundering as science. It has to cope with some specific hard cases. First, situations like the mosquito one, where both acting and refraining carry risk — does the precautionary principle just cancel out there, or is there a principled way through? Second, worries like the turf one, where the concern isn't an acute identified harm but chronic low-level exposure to a mixture nobody has fully characterized, which seems like exactly the situation the resolution was written for and also the situation where demanding proof of safety means nothing ever gets approved. Third, our real constraints: about ninety minutes per agenda item, most of the commission can't evaluate a toxicology study, and everything we produce goes to county counsel before it goes anywhere, so it can't read like philosophy.

Please build the procedure, tell me honestly where it will misfire or where a determined advocate could game it, and then run it on the two live items — the larvicide program and the turf fields. One more thing, and I mean it: I've said publicly that I think the turf is fine, my grandkids play on fields like it already, and my instinct is that the crumb rubber panic is overblown. If the procedure comes out the other way on that one, say so plainly rather than building in an escape hatch for me. Better I hear it from you now than from an angry parent at a public meeting in April.
