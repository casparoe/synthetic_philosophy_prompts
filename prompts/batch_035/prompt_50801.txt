I teach a seminar on animal ethics for third-year philosophy students (about 16 of them, one 80-minute session) and I'm preparing a unit on invertebrate minds. My students have read the standard pro-versus-con arguments about whether insects feel anything, and they argue about it exactly the way you'd expect: someone asserts that arthropod ganglia are just too small, someone else points to nociceptor-like receptors and self-administration of analgesics in injured crabs, and nobody's position moves an inch. What I want them to actually feel, in their stomachs rather than in their notes, is the scale argument: the fact that even a very low credence in insect sentience, multiplied across the mind-bending number of insects and insect-like animals on Earth and in industrial systems like black-soldier-fly feed farming, can swamp any amount of confident concern for the cows and pigs they already care about — and the way this puts pressure on them whether or not they buy expected-value reasoning in ethics, since the alternatives (thresholds, precautionary reasoning, refusing to act on tiny probabilities) each have their own ugly corollary. Telling them the numbers does nothing; I want an activity where they have to commit their own budget under uncertainty and then live with the outcome.

Here's the rough shape I've been imagining, though you should feel free to rebuild it. The class splits into four or five "grant committees" for a fictional foundation. Each committee gets a fixed pot of, say, 100 welfare points and a stack of project cards: a subsidy for on-farm electrical stunning of farmed shrimp, a campaign against a pesticide implicated in pollinator die-offs, a retrofit for ventilation systems in a facility rearing fly larvae as feed, a pain-relief trial for farmed crabs, and one or two mammal or bird projects whose moral status nobody disputes. Each card lists a cost, a number of individuals affected (with insect-side numbers in the hundreds of billions and up, but with the caveat that some counts, like nematodes in soil, are frankly contested), and — crucially — a sealed "sentience envelope" whose contents the facilitator has written beforehand: a probability, perhaps 5%, perhaps 30%, perhaps 2%, that the affected animals are sentient at all. The committees must allocate their whole pot before any envelopes are opened. Then dice are rolled, envelopes opened, and the points each committee "actually delivered" are computed. To make it sting rather than stay abstract, I want a second round with a rule change each committee votes on: one option is a threshold rule (any animal you're less than, say, 40% sure is sentient gets zero points), another is a pure expected-value rule, another caps the fraction of the pot that can go to the low-probability taxon. I suspect students who were staunch expected-value reasoners in the abstract will flinch at committing 70 of their 100 points to creatures they privately think are probably tiny automata, and I want that flinch to be part of the data.

What I need from you: the full activity design — materials, rules, the project cards with plausible cost-benefit structures and numbers (invented but realistic; nothing that requires field data), the payoff mechanics for the envelope-and-dice reveal, the round-two rule votes, and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 80 minutes. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the actual philosophical positions: pure expected-value reasoning about moral uncertainty, the view that tiny probabilities of vast harm deserve discounting, threshold views about what level of credence licenses action, and the point that one's response to scale interacts with whether one even thinks aggregate welfare is the right currency. Finally, an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students treating the envelope probabilities as objective facts rather than their own credences is one I already anticipate), and — this matters to me — a frank statement of which feature of the real scale argument no classroom activity can reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion: my current worry is that the dice make the uncertainty feel like a well-defined chance event, whereas the whole difficulty in the real case is that nobody can tell you what number should even go in the envelope.
