A book I'm reading introduces the handicap principle with the classic image of the tycoon who lights his cigar with a hundred-dollar bill: anyone can claim to be rich, but only the genuinely rich can afford to burn money, so the waste is itself the proof. The author then generalizes: honest signals must be costly, and it is cost that keeps signals honest. My manager has absorbed this and now argues, roughly, that conspicuous spending is automatically credible — a startup burning cash on generous free tiers is signaling strength to competitors and investors the way the peacock's tail signals fitness, and the more it hurts, the more it proves. I'm a software engineer with no biology background, and I'd like an audit of the money-burning picture. First the mapping: what plays the role of the signal, the cost, the signaller's hidden quality, the receiver, and the receiver's payoff. Then where the mapping breaks — in particular whether the model is really about cost as such, or about cost that falls differentially on would-be fakers, and whether the receiver in the cigar story (onlookers granting status) is doing the same job the receiver does in biological signaling, where the signaller's benefit flows through the receiver's response. Finally: does the book's inference — costly, therefore honest — actually follow from the model, and does my manager's extension to firms survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If there's a better analogy for separating a genuine handicap signal from ordinary waste, say what it is.
