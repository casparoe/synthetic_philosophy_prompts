I'd like your help predicting how my tutor will rule on two new cases. She has a settled pattern of verdicts on questions about the distribution of burdens and benefits, and the new cases sit exactly where her earlier ones don't reach. Her verdicts so far:

1. A young cellist who submits to a decade of joyless technical drill in exchange for a later life of performing is being perfectly rational; gains and losses within a single life can simply be netted, and there is nothing further to weigh.
2. A hospital may not conscript one patient into a painful procedure for the sake of several others, whatever the arithmetic.
3. She has no objection to a national vaccination programme in which everyone runs a small, anonymous risk of a bad reaction in order to avert a far larger expected toll.
4. She rejects a proposal to move one named, unwilling village in order to build a reservoir serving a million people, even with generous compensation.
5. Someone who smokes in full knowledge of the costs is foolish but wrongs no one.

The new cases are:

Case A. A patient with advancing dementia, now lacking the capacity to decide, faces a gruelling year of treatment from which only the patient of two years hence will benefit — a later self who, we may stipulate, will retain almost no memories or projects connecting her to the present one. The family asks whether the treatment should proceed.

Case B. A fishing community in which, at the start of each year, every household freely agrees to a lottery that will send one household — whoever it lands on — to endure a bleak, difficult posting that greatly improves the year for everyone else. This year's loser, now identifiable and miserable, is asking to be released from the arrangement.

Please do the following. First, reconstruct the rules her five verdicts are consistent with — I can see at least three candidates: a strict separateness-of-persons rule, on which trade-offs are legitimate within a life but not between lives, with some exception for anonymous risk; a consent-and-identifiability rule, on which what matters is whether a determinate burden falls on an identifiable person who has not agreed to it; and a mutual-advantage rule, on which inter-personal trade-offs are legitimate only when embedded in a scheme that all parties could reasonably have accepted. State clearly what each rule predicts for Case A and Case B, noting that Case B turns partly on whether any risk exception is read ex ante, when the burden is still anonymous, or ex post, once it has landed on someone; and consider whether Case A additionally exposes a split within the separateness rule itself, between a version that tracks numerical identity and a Parfitian version that tracks psychological connection — verdict (1), after all, involved someone whose later self would remember the drudgery. Second, give your best guess at her actual verdict on each case, with your reasons for preferring the rule you think she is really running. Third, design the further case that would best discriminate between the rules — one she has not yet ruled on, on which at least two of the candidate rules come apart — and say what her verdict on it would establish.

Please write in British English throughout. End your reply with a fixed, machine-readable line of the form \answer{Case A: <verdict>; Case B: <verdict>}, where each verdict is exactly one of "permissible", "impermissible", or "undetermined"; all of the reasoning should come before that line.
