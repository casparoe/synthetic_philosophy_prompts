There's a woman in my book club who has a parlour game: she rules on whether a historical shift was a genuine moral revolution or just stuff that changed. Her track record so far — duelling among the English gentry, dead by the 1850s: yes. Britain abolishing slavery in the 1830s: yes. Footbinding in China fading out in the early twentieth century: yes. American Prohibition: no. The British banning of widow-burning (sati) in India in the 1820s: no, and she was quite smug about that one.

Next month she's bringing verdicts on four seperate modern cases and I want to beat her to them: (1) smoking's fall from glamour — doctors used to advertise cigarettes, now smokers get huddled outside doorways; (2) smacking children — Scotland and Wales have banned it outright, England still allows it under some Victorian reasonable-chastisement defence; (3) factory farming, which hasn't ended yet, so the call is whether her rule treats it as a revolution waiting on the right trigger or not a candidate at all; (4) the shift since 2017 in what counts as acceptable conduct at work.

Predict her verdicts on all four, and reverse-engineer the rule she's implicitly running. I can see at least two that fit her past calls — something Appiah-ish where it's the practitioners' own honour that has to collapse, versus something where it's moral persuasion ending in settled consensus, maybe also one about norms that stick without needing enforcement. Commit to a best guess and actually defend it, please don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick. Then give me the one extra case, historical or made up, that would best split the leading rules. And write it all in British English — she is insufferable about it.
