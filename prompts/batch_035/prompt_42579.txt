Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of four hours about the Hart–Devlin debate, and I want an essay that tells me which of us — if either — was right.

Here is the common ground we started from. After the Wolfenden Committee recommended in 1957 that homosexuality be decriminalized, Patrick Devlin gave a lecture (later collected in The Enforcement of Morals) arguing that a society has the right to enforce its shared morality through the criminal law, on the ground that a common morality is what binds a society together, and that a society may preserve that bond as it would its government or its territory. Devlin proposed a standard for identifying the morality in question: whatever an ordinary, reasonable person — the jury, in effect — finds deeply immoral. H. L. A. Hart's reply, in Law, Liberty, and Morality, was that this conflates mere popular revulsion with genuine reasons, that moral consensus proves nothing about correctness (there being a distinction Hart pressed between positive and critical morality), and that Devlin's standard, taken seriously, would have licensed the persecution of heretics and witches, since the reasonable man of those eras was firmly convinced on both counts.

The debate is now half a century old and the Wolfenden question is legally settled almost everywhere it was contested, so my friend and I both found it stale when framed as a fight about homosexuality. What kept us up was that my friend made two moves I could not cleanly answer, and I want the essay to take them seriously rather than treating Devlin as a strawman.

First, the hard cases. My friend conceded that Devlin's disintegration thesis — that society unravels without a single, enforced shared morality — is empirically dubious as applied to private consensual conduct. But he argued that legal moralism does not stand or fall with it, and pointed to the cases where even committed liberals hesitate: consensual adult incest between consenting adults using contraception, bigamy among fully willing adults, dueling by mutual consent, the sale of one's vote. He called these integrity crimes: wrongs in which no identifiable person is harmed in Mill's sense, no one is seriously offended, yet something public seems degraded. His challenge: either say these should all be legal, which he thinks is a reductio of the harm principle, or admit the harm principle is not the whole story and say what the rest of it is.

Second, the self-portrait argument. My friend maintained that a legal code is not merely a system of incentives but also a public statement of what a society is and what it holds sacred — a society's self-portrait, as he put it — and that a society is entitled to decide what its own portrait looks like. When I replied that this licenses whatever the majority happens to feel, he answered that a portrait can be painted badly or falsely, and that the question is what makes a self-portrait accurate rather than merely flattering or prejudiced. That question — what could distinguish a legitimate expressive use of law from mob sentiment dressed up — seemed to me to be exactly where Hart's deepest worry lived, but I couldn't get it into an argument.

Against my friend, I had two points I'd like the essay to weigh. One is Hart's charge that Devlin's reasonable-man standard hands the criminal law over to unreasoned feeling — disgust has a poor track record as a moral faculty. The other is the elasticity problem: if harm is defined loosely enough, harm principles quietly do all the work, but then they have become a different principle, and if it is defined tightly, the integrity crimes go unexplained.

Please write an essay that does three things. It should reconstruct the actual Hart–Devlin exchange accurately and fairly, without reducing either side to a caricature. It should then engage my friend's two moves — the integrity crimes and the self-portrait argument — as serious extensions of Devlin rather than as relapses, and say whether they can be met from within a broadly Mill–Hart framework, perhaps by adding an offense principle or a principle about public institutions, or whether they force a genuine departure from it. And it must reach a verdict, defended rather than gestured at: was Hart right, was my friend's Devlin right, or is the correct position a hybrid — and, whichever it is, say explicitly how consensual adult incest and vote-selling come out on your view, since those were the cases we got stuck on. I don't want an essay that ends by observing that the tension is delicate and both sides have insights; I want a conclusion I could bring back to my friend.
