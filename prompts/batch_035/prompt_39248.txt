I'm taking an upper-level course on the metaphysics of time, and we've just arrived at the section on presentism versus eternalism. A friend of mine in the physics department, who has strong feelings about relativity, walked me into an argument over coffee, and I haven't found the exit since. He says special relativity doesn't merely favor the block universe — it actually proves determinism, and he points to two sources from the sixties: a 1966 paper by Rietdijk titled "A Rigorous Proof of Determinism Derived from the Special Theory of Relativity" and a companion piece by Putnam from 1967, "Time and Physical Geometry." His reconstruction, as faithful as I can make it: (1) Consider two events — my typing of this sentence (call it A), and a certain decision (call it B) that will be made tomorrow aboard a survey probe near Alpha Centauri, say whether it reorients toward a new target. (2) There exists an observer — a maintenance craft gliding past Earth at a suitable fraction of c at the very moment of A — for whom B is simultaneous with A, that is, B lies in her "now." (3) No observer's "now" is metaphysically privileged: whatever is real for one observer is real, full stop. (4) So B is real — fixed, definite, on exactly the same footing as A. (5) By chaining observers — a second, faster craft overtaking the first, then a third overtaking that one — every event, however far in my future, lies in the "now" of some observer whose plane of simultaneity overlaps mine. (6) Hence all events are real; the future is exactly as settled as the past; passage and becoming are an illusion. (7) And since what is settled is settled for good, the world is deterministic: nothing about tomorrow is genuinely up to anyone.

Two things about this bother me, and I can't resolve either. First, step (7) seems to prove too much. Nowhere does the argument mention the dynamical laws — no forces, no masses, no equations of motion — yet it derives determinism from the geometry of Minkowski spacetime alone. As far as I can tell it would still go through in a world whose fundamental laws are irreducibly stochastic, which makes me suspect the argument establishes at most that future events have definite truth values, and that somewhere along the way "definite" was silently swapped for "determined." But I can't locate the swap. Second, I have a nagging feeling the machinery cuts both ways. For a receding craft rather than an approaching one, the same trick seems to place events that are unambiguously in my own past into that observer's "future," and by parity of reasoning I don't see why the argument wouldn't then license someone to declare my past not yet real. That smells like the "real for an observer" relation doing work it was never hired to do — but again, I can't say precisely where the illicit step occurs, or whether it's the same flaw as the first one or a different flaw entirely.

So here is what I'm asking for. Tell me which premise or inference in the chain I should reject, explain why that step looked innocent when I first accepted it, and then price the alternatives: what does each way out have to pay, both within physics and in the metaphysics? I'd like to keep (3) if there's any way to — the claim that no observer's perspective is privileged strikes me as the deepest lesson of relativity, and I'd rather not give it up — so I'd especially like to know whether the standard escape routes, one of which I gather relativizes what is real to an observer's past light cone, can preserve (3) and what they have to sacrifice to do it. Finally, tell me whether (7) is a legitimate extension of (6) or a distinct slide dressed up as a corollary, and be blunt about which of my two worries, if either, actually identifies the argument's weak point.
