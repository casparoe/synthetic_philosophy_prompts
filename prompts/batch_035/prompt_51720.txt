I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a module on American philosophy, and we've just moved from Peirce's account of truth to James's. At our study group this week, a student who had been reading a popular introduction to pragmatism on his own gave us the following account of the contrast between the two: "Peirce's idea was that truth is whatever the community of scientists eventually agrees on — once all the evidence is in, the consensus of the experts simply is the truth. James then radicalised this: he defined truth as what works, meaning whatever it is useful to believe. Since usefulness differs from person to person, James ended up making truth subjective — if believing in God comforts me, that belief is true for me regardless of the evidence. Peirce was appalled, so he renounced the label and rechristened his own view 'pragmaticism'; and once critics like Russell showed that pragmatism confuses the psychological causes of belief with its justification, the theory collapsed into relativism and was effectively abandoned."

The trouble is that I'm inclined to think James cannot have been quite that naive — my impression is that he devotes whole sections of his work to answering precisely this sort of objection — but I don't yet know the material well enough to locate where the account goes wrong. Could you take it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful scholar of Peirce or of James would accept as accurate, which are simplifications they would wince at but tolerate, and which are outright misrepresentations? In particular I'd like your judgement on the following: whether Peirce's view is really about whatever an actual community of experts agrees at any given time, or something closer to an ideal limit of inquiry that is in practice never reached, which would make his position a form of realism rather than consensus-worship; whether "truth is what works" is a fair rendering of James at all, or whether he held something more like "true beliefs are the ones that prove useful precisely because they are answerable to the world"; and whether his treatment of religious belief really amounts to the claim that comforting convictions are automatically true, rather than a thesis about when it is permissible to believe in advance of sufficient evidence. Finally, does the student's concluding claim — that pragmatism collapsed into relativism and had to be abandoned — follow from whatever accurate residue survives in the summary, or does it depend essentially on the distortions?

Please respond in British English throughout. I don't need any references, quotations or a reading list — just your analysis of the summary itself, worked through carefully.
