I spent most of my career as a seismologist, estimating rupture probabilities from data that were never as plentiful as I should have liked, and since retiring I have joined a small reading group devoted to philosophy of science and rationality. At our last meeting we discussed how one ought to represent uncertainty when the evidence is thin, using as a running example the probability that a particular dormant fault slips within the next fifty years, given only fragmentary historical records and contested slip-rate measurements. Two members of the group — one a retired statistician, the other an economist by training — became quite engaged on the question, and I found myself unable to say who had the better of it. I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, proceeded as follows. (1) The statistician argued that the only intellectually honest course is to assign a single probability distribution: state your prior over the unknown parameters as widely as your ignorance demands, condition on the data, and report the posterior. Sets of probability distributions, he said, are an unnecessary formalism; a sufficiently wide prior already encodes the admission that one knows little. (2) The economist replied that widening a prior changes its shape but not its character: it still yields definite numbers to arbitrary precision, and a figure like 0.0234 per century conveys an accuracy the evidence simply does not license. He added that the Ellsberg paradox — people's reluctance to bet on urns of unknown composition at odds they happily accept for urns of known composition — shows that rational agents treat sharp and unsharp probabilities differently, and that a theory of credence ought to respect the distinction rather than flatten it. (3) The statistician conceded nothing on the descriptive point but pressed a structural objection: a set of probability distributions, considered as a representation of belief, does not by itself tell you what to do. At the moment of action you must choose one option, so the imprecise theorist must bolt on a decision rule — maximin over the set, or something similar — and the choice among such rules reintroduces precisely the arbitrariness the set was supposed to eliminate. Worse, he said, updating a set of probabilities can produce dilation: conditioning on new evidence can widen the range of probabilities for some event, so that learning apparently makes you less sure, which he found untenable. (4) The economist answered that dilation is not a defect but a form of honesty: if the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, the representation should say so, even at the price of a wider interval. As for the decision rule, he granted that it must be specified separately, but argued that this is a virtue rather than a flaw: the value judgments that enter a choice under ambiguity become explicit and contestable, rather than being smuggled in invisibly as the third decimal place of a spurious prior. (5) The statistician closed by insisting that a representation of uncertainty that cannot, on its own, guide action has failed at the basic function of probability, and that in actual scientific practice the conventional remedy — reporting sensitivity analyses across a family of priors, and seeing which conclusions survive — accomplishes everything the formal apparatus of sets of probabilities claims a monopoly on, without the metaphysical overhead.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered steps, say which party is right about what, and to what degree, with brief justification. Then tell me whether either participant is missing something important that would reframe the whole dispute — in particular, whether the disagreement ultimately concerns the psychology of belief, the semantics of probability statements, or the pragmatics of decision, since I suspect that they are talking past each other on at least one of these, and I lacked the philosophical vocabulary to say so at the time.
