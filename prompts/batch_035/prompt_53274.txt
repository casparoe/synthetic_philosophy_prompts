I'm presenting at a departmental seminar next week — the room is mostly Humean naturalists who want reductive explanations, plus one Scanlonian contractualist and a game theorist who works on commitment devices. My thesis: promises bind because the act of promising transfers a bit of normative authority over the promisor's future conduct to the promisee. It's not a convention sustained by expectation (that's for the Humeans), and the wrong isn't just letting someone down or violating the assurance principle (that's for the contractualist) — it's usurpation: if I break the promise without your release, I've decided for you about something you now get to decide about. The wrinkle I actually want to defend is that this authority is asymmetrically alienable: the promisee can release you, but you can't unilaterally resign it, and I want to use that to explain why promises extracted under duress may never transfer anything at all, while promises made under a third party's threat to someone else sometimes still bind. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections each subgroup will actually raise, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And include the question I'm dreading — my guess is it's the game theorist asking why the whole authority apparatus isn't just a flowery redescribing of reputation and incentive constraints, but tell me if there's a worse one lurking.
