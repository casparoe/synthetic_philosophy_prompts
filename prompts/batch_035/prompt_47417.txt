I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a feature about the emerging insect-welfare debate — the startups rearing billions of black soldier fly larvae, the researchers studying whether bees show signs of distress, and the philanthropists wondering whether this should be a cause area at all. In my reporting I keep encountering a piece of arithmetic that functions almost as a movement slogan. The reasoning runs: there are on the order of ten quintillion insects alive at any moment; even if each has only a vanishingly small chance of being sentient — one in a thousand, say, or one in a million — multiplying that tiny probability by the vast population yields an expected quantity of suffering that dwarfs factory farming, perhaps all human-caused animal suffering combined. When I press people on whether you can really do ethics by multiplication, they reach for an analogy: these probabilities are like pennies on the sidewalk. No single penny is worth bending down for, but if there were trillions of them scattered about, only a fool would walk past; expected value is expected value, and small chances times enormous numbers are just how rational betting works.

Skeptics fire back with an analogy of their own: this is nothing more than Pascal's mugging. A stranger stops you and claims that unless you hand over your wallet, he will use his simulated universe to torture an astronomically large number of people; the claimed harm is so vast that even an epsilon of credence seems to demand compliance, and yet nobody thinks you should pay — so it can't be that tiny probabilities times astronomical stakes automatically generate obligations. The skeptics say the insect arithmetic has exactly this shape, and that anyone who accepts the penny-picking analogy has already paid the mugger. What I'd like from you is a careful audit of both analogies, since my article needs to adjudicate between them. Set out the correspondence table for each: what plays the role of the payoff, the ticket, the mugger's claim, the wallet, and the credence. Then identify where each mapping breaks — I have my own suspicion here that I'd like you to assess, namely that the sidewalk-pennies picture quietly assumes the insects' sentience probabilities are independent bets, like so many separately flipped coins, when in fact they may all hang on a handful of shared facts about insect neurobiology, such that one discovery about nociception could settle all ten quintillion "tickets" at once. Finally, tell me what each analogy smuggles in about moral uncertainty versus ordinary empirical uncertainty, and whether the prioritization conclusion — fund insect welfare now, ahead of more certain causes — survives once the disanalogies are laid out, or whether the arithmetic was doing less work than the rhetoric all along. Since I'll be publishing claims based on your reasoning, please say how confident you are in each verdict you reach, and single out the step in your analysis that you consider least secure.
