You are a professional philosopher specialising in formal epistemology, and I'd like your careful judgement on a dispute that came up at a party.

Someone flipped a fair coin, caught it, and covered it with a cup. My friend Sasha offered me even odds on heads. Our other friend, Imran, who studies physics, insists I would be irrational to accept. His reasoning: the coin is already either heads or tails, so the real chance of heads is now either 0 or 1, and my credence ought to track the real chance. He adds that with a sufficiently fast camera and the relevant mechanics, the outcome could have been computed before the coin came to rest, and that if the barman, who glimpsed the coin as it was covered, were to tell me what it showed, I'd update straight to certainty — which, he says, proves the 50/50 figure was never a fact about the world at all, merely a confession of ignorance. Sasha replies that until the coin is revealed, any rational person must price the bet at even money, and that if Imran's argument worked, it would equally show that bookmakers and insurers can never be rational, which is absurd.

Please begin by briefly mapping the different things that might be meant by "the probability of heads" here, and then adjudicate. Who is right, and what precisely is the disagreement between them? In particular, explain what role the barman's glimpse plays — why is it acceptable for me to ignore it, and what does its availability tell us about the status of the 50/50 figure? Please write in terms an educated layperson can follow, and be explicit about whether either friend is making a subtle mistake rather than merely talking past each other.
