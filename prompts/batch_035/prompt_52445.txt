Each year in my graduate seminar on the philosophy of law I set a session on political legitimacy, and I have found that the material that most unsettles students is not any theorist's account but the constitutional architecture of the mature democracies that retain hereditary monarchs. Consider what these states actually do. In the United Kingdom, no bill becomes law without royal assent, and assent has not been withheld since 1708; the monarch delivers a speech, written entirely by the government of the day, that opens Parliament and announces its legislative program; the head of state holds a weekly private audience with the Prime Minister and receives boxes of state papers, while swearing personally to remain politically neutral; and members of the armed forces and of Parliament swear allegiance not to the state, the constitution, or the people, but to the Crown and its successors. In Norway, the monarch formally appoints the government after each election, and the Sami Parliament is opened by the King. In Sweden, by contrast, the constitutional reform of 1974 stripped the monarch of even the formal residue of political function, transferring duties such as the appointment of the Prime Minister to the Speaker of the Riksdag, so that the same institution survives there in a purely ceremonial register. And in postwar Japan, the constitution was rewritten to reduce an emperor once held to be sacred to a symbol of the state and the unity of its people. All of these polities affirm, somewhere in their constitutional self-understanding, that sovereignty rests with the people; all of them entrench an office that is hereditary, unelected, unremovable, and religiously or historically charged.

Here is the puzzle I would like help articulating for my students. What theory of political legitimacy does this arrangement presuppose, if any? On its face the institution seems to require that we can drive a wedge between the source of authority and its exercise — that a thoroughly democratic distribution of effective power is compatible with a thoroughly undemocratic location of formal and symbolic authority — and yet the oath of allegiance is not to the body that actually wields power. Does the practice presuppose a theory of the state as a corporate entity that must have a personal head, so that the monarch is the legal person in whom the state inheres while the people are merely its sovereign interpreter? Does it presuppose that legitimacy over time is different in kind from legitimacy at a moment, with the hereditary line standing for the continuity of the polity itself in a way no elected office could? Or does it presuppose nothing at all about legitimacy, being a kind of inherited legal fiction that has outlived whatever theory once grounded it?

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and to distinguish what constitutional monarchy strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably alongside — in particular, whether the Swedish and Norwegian variants, one stripped of all formal function and the other retaining real formal powers of appointment, are in fact licensed by the same underlying commitments or reveal that the institution is philosophically underdetermined and could be sustained by several incompatible theories of legitimacy. Then tell me what would actually have to change in these practices if we replaced whatever theory you reconstruct with a strict and thoroughgoing popular sovereignty: would a purely nominal elected presidency preserve everything that matters in the institution, or does something in the practice — the oath, the assent, the hereditary person as such — presuppose a claim that no elected office could carry, and if so, what exactly is that claim and can it survive the acknowledgment, common among defenders of these monarchies, that the crown's authority is now nothing but convention?
