At my philosophy-of-medicine reading group, a colleague argued that Boorse-style naturalism about disease refutes itself, and I can't find where it slips. His argument: (1) On the naturalist view, a condition is a disease only if it involves a part functioning below the level statistically typical for the relevant reference class — members of the same species, sex, and age. (2) What counts as typical is fixed by current frequencies in that class: if most members have the condition, then for them it is typical. (3) Frequencies vary sharply by population — myopia already affects the large majority of young adults in some East Asian cohorts, and presbyopia is close to universal past fifty. (4) So in those populations myopia and presbyopia are not diseases, and diagnosing them there is a misclassification. (5) Worse, a treatment program successful enough to push a disease's prevalence below the threshold would erase the diagnosis while the patients kept the condition. My colleague concludes that "typical" must really mean some level of functioning organisms are supposed to reach — a design notion that naturalism promised to do without. I came in sympathetic to naturalism and would rather keep (1) than accept (4), but I want a genuine diagnosis, not a rescue: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please answer in American English.
