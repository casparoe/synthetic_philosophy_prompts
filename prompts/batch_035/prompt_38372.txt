So I run a little movie night thing with some friends and coworkers, mostly guys who like arguing more than they like films. Last month we did 12 Angry Men (the 1957 version, the one thats basically all in one room) and afterwards it turned into a two-hour arguement that never got resolved, so now its my turn to bring it back up somewhere else. My brother-in-law's position was that the jury never "decides" anything -- he says there's just twelve men who each individually decide, and we only call it a group decision as a figure of speech, like how "the crowd stormed the field" is really just a bunch of individuals running. My position was that the jury is more like one big slow brain with twelve lobes that dont like each other. The thing that got me started on this is the scene where juror 8 votes not guilty and everyone acts personally insulted, like he broke something -- if it were really twelve independent deciders, why would one guy's vote feel like a betrayal of the group? And then later when juror 10 goes on his rant and the other men literally turn their backs on him one at a time, that felt like the group expelling a part of itself, which is a weird thing for "just twelve individuals" to be able to do.

After movie night I fell down a wikipedia rabbit hole and found out philosophers actually fight about this exact thing, though the articles were over my head pretty fast. There seems to be one guy (Searle?) who thinks there's a genuine "we intend" that can't be broken down into "I intend" times twelve, and other people who want to build the "we" out of individuals who each intend something and coordinate, plus some woman Gilbert whose bit about "joint commitment" sounded interesting -- the idea being that once a group is formed, backing out isn't just changing your mind, it's more like breaking a promise to everyone else, which would explain alot of the tension in that room. I honestly couldnt tell which camp the movie comes down on, and thats what I want you to figure out. When the votes flip one man at a time from guilty to not guilty, is that a collective mind changing its mind, or twelve individuals each privately updating? When juror 3 finally cracks because of his own son and changes his vote, is he doing it *as part of the jury* or as an escape from it? And the fact that we never learn most of their names -- they're literally just "juror 4", "juror 8" for the whole movie -- does the film think they're persons at all, or just parts of a decision-making machine? I want a reading that takes both sides seriously (mine and my brother-in-law's), says where the film dramatizes something these philosophical theories can actually explain, and where the film maybe shows something the theories miss -- like whether a group can be more unified than the people in it want it to be, since half those men clearly want to leave and cant.

Do a row for each of these: the first straw poll where it's 11 to 1; juror 8 standing alone against the room; the secret ballot where one more man flips; the votes changing one by one through the middle of the film; juror 10's rant while everyone turns away; and juror 3's final breakdown and vote change.
