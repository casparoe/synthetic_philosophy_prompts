I convene a graduate reading group in philosophy of physics, and our next term is devoted to the relationship between determinism and predictability: whether a deterministic universe must, even in principle, be a predictable one. Most members arrive holding the slogan that chaos theory shows determinism does not imply predictability, but almost nobody has a worked-out view of where, exactly, the implication is supposed to break — in the finite precision of any measurement, in the amount of computation required, in the fact that any real predictor is itself embedded in the world, or in the laws not being deterministic in the first place. I want a diagnostic quiz that members can take before the first session, so that they arrive sorted by what they actually believe rather than by which label they find congenial.

Please write the quiz to the following specification.

Eight questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a member can answer without knowing which position their answer feeds. The cases should include at least the following five, plus three of your own choosing: a coin flip whose entire physical situation is modelled by equations we completely understand; a double pendulum whose initial angle is measured to a hundred decimal places and whose trajectory nevertheless diverges from the simulation after a while; a seasonal weather forecast that, once published, changes what farmers plant and hence changes the weather it was predicting; a two-slit experiment run one electron at a time, with every element of the apparatus specified; and a hypothetical predicting machine that is asked to output a complete description of its own state ten minutes hence. No question may use the name of any position, theory, or famous thought experiment.

Then a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto positions. The leaves must include: full-blooded Laplaceanism (an ideal intelligence given the complete state and the laws could in principle predict everything, chaos bearing only on practical prediction); the view that finite measurement precision is not merely a practical inconvenience but a principled barrier to prediction, so that even determinism plus full knowledge of the laws leaves prediction open-ended; a computational-irreducibility leaf, associated with Wolfram, on which even an agent with complete data and no resource limits must in effect live through the calculation; the position Popper pressed against historicism, that no predictor embedded in the world can forecast the future growth of its own knowledge, so that any published prediction can be falsified by the response to it; Copenhagen-style indeterminism; at least one deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics, such as Bohmian or Everettian, together with an account of why agents inside it still cannot predict their own measurement records; and finally the epistemic-sceptical position that determinism, whether true or false, is something we could never establish from inside the world.

In the key, please first set out the underlying questions that carve up the logical space — whether the fundamental laws are deterministic, whether determinism would guarantee predictability for an ideal external agent, whether embedding the predictor in the world changes the answer, and whatever further questions you think the cases turn on — and only then locate the positions above in that space. Where a combination of answers has no standard occupant, say so and diagnose why: incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate, rather than pretending the scoring key discriminates more finely than it does.
