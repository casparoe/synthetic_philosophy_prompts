So I'm a novelist — three books out, comic literary stuff, small press — and the thing I'm working on now is told partly through documents: emails, diary entries, a group chat that goes catastrophically wrong, that kind of collage. The document I'm stuck on is the advice column run by one of my main characters, a guy who washed out of a philosophy PhD program and reinvented himself as a life coach. He's got this whole proprietary method he thinks he invented. I want you to actually write the column for me, because I keep writing it too affectionately and I need someone colder to do it. Call the column "Simply Vernon" (his name is Vernon) and make it maybe 800-900 words, four or five readers' letters with his replies, plus a little italicized editor's tagline at the end like real advice columns have.

Heres the part I need you to get right. Vernon's philosophy, which he genuinely believes he discovered himself, is that anxiety comes from the unbearable weight of being totally free — no fixed essence, no excuses that are actually load-bearing, just choice all the way down. And his method is teaching clients to extinguish that anguish by becoming their roles completely, flattening themselves into the role so thoroughly that there's no leftover person who could have chosen otherwise. So every single reply he gives has to be real, textbook bad faith in the technical sense, not just generic self-help excuses: he tells people to treat their past as if it fully determines them ("you grew up in a chaotic home, so chaos is simply what you do — stop fighting your nature"), to treat their emotions as objects that visit them rather than responses they're generating, to lose themselves in playing their role the way Sartre's waiter plays at being a waiter — the whole trick of being nothing but a waiter, gliding on rails. He should coach one letter-writer who's dating someone she's lukewarm about to make herself an object in the moment, let things happen to her rather than deciding, sort of going limp into the situation the way Sartre's famous example of the woman leaving her hand where it is does when she doesn't want to notice what's being decided. And he loves labels as anchors — "I'm just not a math person," "I'm a depressive type," "I'm the responsible sibling" — anything that converts a choice into a property of a thing.

The horror I want, and this is the satirical engine so please don't soften it: his advice should work. People write back in later letters saying the vertigo is gone, they've never been calmer, they've stopped lying awake cataloguing all the lives they could still be living. That quiet success is what makes it a proper reductio rather than a cheap dunk. But one letter, somewhere in the middle, should be from a long-time client whose bad faith has started to crack — she was reaching for the coffee tin as "a tired mom" and suddenly saw, for like four seconds, that being a tired mom was something she was doing rather than something she was — and now she can't unsee it, she's describing full-on anguish, the naked experience of her own freedom. Vernon's reply to that one needs to be visibly strained, his usual patter fraying, because he has to re-objectify her fast and part of him knows what he's doing. He never breaks deadpan, never winks at the reader, never uses the phrase "bad faith," and never mentions Sartre or names any philosopher — he thinks this method is his IP and gets vaguely litigious about it in the replies. Someone who teaches existentialism should read it and nod at every single move he makes; someone who's never heard of any of it should still laugh at it as a self-help parody. If you can end the editor's tagline with something that lands the irony without underlining it, bonus points.
