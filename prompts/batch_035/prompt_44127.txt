I'm a PhD student in the history of science, and for a historiography field seminar I assembled an argument that was supposed to land a modest conclusion — that the 'Renaissance' is a later invention — and has instead talked me into something I can't accept. I'd like someone to find the exit, or at least tell me which door to close.

Here is the argument as I presented it:

(1) A historical period is real only if its boundaries are found rather than drawn: a stretch of the past that owes its edges to a later act of drawing is not a feature of the past, any more than Wyoming's straight borders are features of the rock they cross.

(2) The boundaries of the 'Middle Ages' and the 'Renaissance' were drawn, not found. Nobody in 1400 experienced themselves as living between two periods; the labels and the cutoffs were fixed long afterward by people with agendas — humanists who needed a benighted middle age in order to flatter their own revival of antiquity, and later historians who wanted a secular dawn from which to date the modern world.

(3) The same holds for the 'Scientific Revolution', which nobody is supposed to have noticed they were having while it was happening. The phrase is a twentieth-century product, and so is the decision that Copernicus in 1543 and Newton in 1687 make a matched pair of bookends — a pairing neither man could have conceived of.

(4) A period whose boundaries are drawn rather than found is a projection onto the past, not a part of the past.

(5) Therefore the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are not real; they are historiographical artifacts.

(6) But nothing in (1) through (4) turns on which periods we started with. Premise (2) holds of every macro-period whatsoever: even a sixteenth-century Italian writer who used the word 'rinascita' was not thereby standing at a boundary, and every proposed cutoff — the fall of a city, the arrival of printing, a treaty, a death — was selected after the fact from a continuum of candidate events, none of which comes labeled as an edge.

(7) Nor does the reasoning stop at history. Geologists fix the boundaries of the intervals in the geologic column by committee: a reference section in one particular cliff face is chosen, a marker horizon is agreed on, and the whole thing is colloquially called a 'golden spike'. They have spent the past decade publicly fighting about whether to add a new interval for the recent past, and everyone concedes the fight is partly about politics and practicality rather than where nature's edges are.

(8) The reasoning even covers the numbered centuries: the boundary between the 1490s and the 1500s rests on a numbering system with an arbitrary zero point, in a calendar that has no year zero at all.

(9) Therefore no period of anything — geological, historical, or calendrical — is real.

(10) But (9) is absurd, and not merely because my dissertation on instrument-makers in the 'Scientific Revolution' would then be about a nonexistent object. Taken seriously, (9) seems to entail that there is no fact of the matter about any question of the form 'did things change more during this stretch of the past than during that one,' since the stretches themselves are not real; and I cannot bring myself to believe that comparative history is about nothing.

The suspicion I can't make precise is that the move from drawn boundaries to unreality — the move I make in getting to (5) and again in getting to (9) — is sound the first time and somehow not the second, or that it is unsound both times and I only notice at scale. So: where does this go wrong, if it does? If some step is invalid, I want to know which premise it requires on a reading the others don't actually supply. If the argument is valid, I want to know which of (1), (4), or my refusal of (9) is the cheapest to give up — because from where I stand, they look equally priced, and I would genuinely rather be told the price than be reassured that periodization is 'socially constructed,' which everyone in my seminar says as though it settled something.
