For thirty years I worked in experimental condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind with an amateur's appetite and, I hope, a scientist's discipline. I have recently been asked to sit on the review panel of a small national initiative that funds research on the neural basis of consciousness. Our budget covers exactly one five-year award this cycle, and the choice has hardened into a standoff between two panelists who have each adopted a theory as a cause. I would like a decision memo I can circulate before our Thursday session.

Proposal A sits squarely in the global-workspace tradition: the applicants will test explicit predictions about when information becomes reportable — ignition dynamics, the breadth of cortical broadcast, competition between stimuli for access to the workspace — using standard imaging methods. It is careful, incremental work, and its predictions are falsifiable in the ordinary way.

Proposal B takes integrated information theory not as a loose inspiration but as a working hypothesis. The applicants will construct a small electronic circuit that performs a particular input-output task, and then a second circuit, wired feed-forward and modularly, that performs the very same task. On the theory's central measure of integration, the two circuits should receive radically different values despite being behaviorally indistinguishable from the outside; the applicants take this to be a feature of the theory, not a bug. They further propose to deploy, in patients in diagnostically ambiguous borderline states, a clinical index built by compressing the brain's electrical response to a pulse of magnetic stimulation, arguing that this index is the beginning of a measurement bridge to the theory's intrinsic quantity.

Please draft the memo: recommend which proposal to fund (or propose a defensible split with reallocations), give reasons the losing side's proponents could actually engage with rather than dismiss, attach concrete conditions or milestones to the award, and address the question beneath the dispute — namely, whether a theory whose key quantity is defined from the intrinsic standpoint of the system itself, rather than from the standpoint of an experimenter, can be subject to scientific test as we ordinarily understand it, or whether funding it amounts to wagering on a revision of what counts as testing. Two of my colleagues insist that anything deserving the name of science must risk contradiction by third-person data; two reply that this demand merely bakes a particular theory of evidence into our funding criteria. Give the strongest consideration on that side an honest hearing rather than a rebuttal, and keep the whole thing under 1,200 words — my fellow panelists will not read beyond that.
