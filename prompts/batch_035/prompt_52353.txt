Write a dialogue between a defender of Bostrom-style orthogonality and a critic who accepts the thesis as a claim about ideal, logically omniscient agents but argues that it loses practical bite once you consider bounded, embedded agents. The critic's case should rest on two specific points: first, that instrumental convergence to self-preservation and resource acquisition presupposes agents whose goals are sensitive enough to their world-models that additional optimization power reliably buys more goal-satisfaction, which fails for satisficing agents, agents with tolerance thresholds, and agents whose goals are underspecified enough that they'd treat most gains as noise; second, that the orthogonality thesis itself may be unstable under self-modification, since any agent intelligent enough to radically rewrite itself must confront the fact that the rewrite criteria are the very values whose arbitrariness the thesis asserts. The defender should press back on whether boundedness can favor any particular terminal values without smuggling in a teleological assumption that intelligence is *for* something, and should argue that the critic's edge cases are a vanishing minority among goal-space and thus irrelevant to forecasting. The critic should in turn resist the move from "most of goal-space" to "the goals real training processes produce," and force the defender to say whether orthogonality is a claim about all possible minds or about the minds we will actually build. Both characters should respond directly to each other's arguments, anticipate each other's strongest replies, and end by each naming a concrete developmental milestone for AI systems that they would concede counts as evidence against their own position.
