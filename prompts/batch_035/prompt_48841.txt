I'm an associate editor at an interdisciplinary journal -- think complexity science, philosophy of biology, and philosophy of mind in roughly equal parts -- and our referee pool has fractured over cross-level explanations. We handle something like forty manuscripts a year where the crux is whether an explanation that lives at one level is allowed to do its work there: agent-based models of market panics, appeals to downward causation in neuroscience, evolutionary explanations of organismal design, papers that say the whole system has properties its parts simply lack. One faction of our referees dismisses anything that doesn't bottom out in mechanism as mysticism, and the other faction returns those same papers with complaints that demands for micro-foundations amount to insisting we re-derive the whole from the parts every single time. The reports contradict each other, authors write angry letters, and I end up overruling referees on vibes, which is exactly what I'm not supposed to do.

I've become convinced that Dennett's apparatus is the right shared vocabulary for this: his distinction between skyhooks, which lower organization into a system from on high, and cranes, which build it up from previously generated complexity at the level below; and his accompanying complaint that demanding the reduction all at once, rather than allowing it to proceed by steps, is greedy reductionism -- reduction that kills the phenomenon by refusing the scaffolding. So here's what I want from you: a procedure, in the form of ordered questions a referee can actually run in the two or three hours they will realistically give a manuscript, that operationalizes this picture. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation to reject, where it terminates in a recommendation to accept, and where it must give up and hand the question back to the editor because what's left is a judgment call about how much unexplained structure a field is entitled to start from. The hard cases the procedure has to survive are the recurring ones: a system-level regularity whose underlying mechanism is only partially known; an author who gestures at emergence precisely where the explanatory work needs doing; and a demand for micro-foundations that no historical or field science could ever meet, since the micro-details are simply gone.

Then run the procedure on three submissions from last season's queue, described schematically: (A) a paper explaining a market flash crash via a feedback loop among algorithmic traders, where the authors characterize the loop structure and its amplification but decline to fully specify the individual algorithms, arguing the loop is the explanation; (B) a paper on the neural correlates of consciousness that posits consciousness as strongly emergent from a complexity index and claims the residual explanatory gap is dissolved once strong emergence is accepted; and (C) a paper explaining termite mound architecture through stigmergy -- local rules plus environmental trace, no designer, no complete inventory of individual termite behavior, but empirically constrained predictions about mound form. Where the procedure misfires, say so: I specifically want to know which of its steps would wrongly convict respectable historical sciences, and which step is just my faction's prejudice dressed as a rule. I'll admit up front that I want (C) rejected and suspect a consistent procedure won't give me that; if that's where it lands, I'd rather know now. Please end your reply with a single machine-readable line of the form \verdicts{A: ..., B: ..., C: ...}, where each slot is filled with exactly one of: crane, skyhook, needs-more-crane, judgment-call.
