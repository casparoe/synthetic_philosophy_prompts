Having recently begun donating a fixed share of my income and choosing a career direction, I've been trying to rank causes using the importance–tractability–neglectedness (ITN) framework my effective-altruist friends swear by: score each cause on each of the three factors, multiply the scores, and commit to the top product. Between malaria prevention (enormous importance, heavily funded, extensively studied) and lead-exposure reduction in low-income countries (perhaps comparable health impact per dollar, but a fraction of the attention), the multiplication seems to favor the neglected one. Two worries keep nagging at me, though. First, multiplying qualitative ratings appears to treat the three factors as independent and fully compensable — as though greater tractability or neglectedness can genuinely offset lesser importance. Second, I cannot tell whether neglectedness is a genuinely distinct consideration or merely a proxy for the diminishing marginal returns that should already be built into tractability, in which case counting it separately double-penalizes crowded causes. Write an essay evaluating whether ITN reasoning, in its best form, really supports choosing the neglected cause over the crowded but important one. Weigh the strongest versions of the multiplication-style reasoning against the strongest objections, and reach a definite verdict — including, if you conclude that the arithmetic cannot bear that weight, an account of what a defensible version of the framework would look like and what it would recommend in my case.
