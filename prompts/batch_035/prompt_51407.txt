I'm a postdoc in cognitive ethology, and I need to write a coding manual that our team of eight undergraduate observers will use to annotate hundreds of hours of animal-behavior video each semester — scrub jays re-caching food when watched, apes gesturing at keepers, a corvid pulling a string, a dog alternating its gaze between a locked door and its owner. Right now our coders are all over the place: some annotate "the jay knows the rival saw where it hid the seed," others annotate the same clip as "re-caching response to visual stimulus," and our inter-rater reliability is bad enough that reviewers have started asking hard questions. The theoretical tension I want the manual to resolve is this: I was trained on Davidson's radical interpretation and his principle of charity, which says that attributing beliefs to a subject is only intelligible against a background of mostly-true beliefs and mostly-rational connections among them — but Davidson himself drew the conclusion that creatures without language can't properly be said to have beliefs at all, and my more behaviorist-leaning colleagues would prefer we never annotate mental states at all. I don't want to settle that war; I want a procedure that lets observers make the call clip by clip, in under two minutes, without needing to have read a word of philosophy.

Please turn the machinery of radical interpretation — charity, the rationality constraint, the idea that interpretation proceeds by asking what a creature with mostly true beliefs would do in this situation, and the requirement that interpretation be anchored to a shared world of observable causes — into a step-by-step coding procedure my observers can actually follow. It should say what questions to ask about a clip in what order, what counts as passing each question, and where the procedure stops with "code as stimulus-response, not as a belief attribution" — while being honest about which steps are disguised judgment calls rather than real tests. Then run it on three worked examples: a scrub jay that re-caches food only when it was observed caching it, a dog that fetches its leash and gazes at its owner after hearing the word "walk" in an ordinary sentence, and a language-trained ape that signs "food" in what its trainers describe as a request. Please include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I won't like — I confess I'd be sad if the ape case failed, since the trainers have built their careers on those reports — and explain what the procedure fundamentally cannot decide, so we know which clips have to be escalated to the senior researchers rather than resolved by the manual.
