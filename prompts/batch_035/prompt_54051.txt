I'm a retired evolutionary biologist -- mostly social insects, some population genetics late in my career -- who has spent the last three years getting into philosophy of religion and the cognitive science side of religion, and I've realised I judge theories about this subject by criteria I inherited from biology without ever checking whether they fit. I'm writing a long essay for my local skeptics' society arguing that religion is best understood as a natural phenomenon -- mostly a byproduct of ordinary cognition plus a layer of cultural selection, not a biological adaptation in the strict sense -- but before I write it I want to get my standards straight, because I suspect half the disputes in this field are actually people using different desiderata without noticing.

So here's what I want from you. Play the role of someone who is both a philosopher of science and steeped in the evolutionary and cognitive study of religion, and think hard before answering -- brainstorm a bit first, don't just dump the first list that comes into your head. Then write me the desiderata: what any adequate naturalistic theory of religion has to deliver, covering origin, spread, and persistence as one package. Off the top of my head it has to include things like: explaining why religion is near-universal cross-culturally but also wierdly variable in content; explaining why it's so expensive -- time, energy, painful initiation rituals, celibate specialists, food taboos -- if it's supposed to be a mere byproduct; explaining why it persists stubbornly in some populations while secularisation hollows it out in others; explaining why the gods ordinary believers actually deal with (petty, local, morally mediocre) look nothing like the gods theologians describe; and not contradicting anything we actually know about agency detection, memory, and social learning in humans. For each requirement, say where it comes from -- which case or consideration motivates it -- and don't pad the list with things that are just preferences of one camp dressed up as demands.

Then the two parts I care about most:

1. Tell me which requirements are load-bearing and which are negotiable, and specifically whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. For instance, can you have full byproduct minimalism AND an adequate explanation of all the costly ritual stuff, without quietly smuggling adaptation back in or turning religion into a cultural parasite on the side? If my list itself forces a choice between two requirements, I want that named as a result, not hidden.

2. Be scrupulously honest about what is settled versus what is genuinely contested among people who actually know this topic. I've been burned by pop-science books that present one side -- the adaptationist group-selection stories, or the pure byproduct line, or the costly-signalling framework -- as if it were consensus when it plainly isn't. Where something is contested, say so and say roughly what the disagreement is over. Don't smooth it out to make my essay tidier.

You can skip scoring the main contending theories against the list for now, though if one requirement stands out as knocking out the most popular theory, mention which. One more thing: my own money is on byproduct-plus-cultural-selection, so if you notice I've smuggled that bias into how I've framed the requirements themselves -- made things requirements because my favored view satisfies them and rivals don't -- call me on it. That's exactly the self-criticism I can't get from newsletter readers who already agree with me.
