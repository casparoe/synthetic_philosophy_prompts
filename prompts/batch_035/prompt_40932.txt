I teach a seminar that pairs one Shakespeare play with a unit on theories of statutory interpretation, and this year I want to build a session around the trial scene in The Merchant of Venice, because it seems to stage the textualism-versus-purposivism debate almost didactically. Shylock demands enforcement of his bond exactly as written — a pound of flesh, no more, no less — and refuses every appeal to the transaction's presumed commercial purpose (he is repeatedly offered multiples of the principal). Portia in disguise first answers him as a purposivist or equitable judge would: she appeals to mercy, to the spirit of the law, to considerations that live outside the four corners of the document. When that fails, she doesn't simply rule for purposivism; she out-textualizes the textualist, granting the bond in all its literalness and then observing that it licenses flesh but not one drop of blood, and not a scruple more or less than a pound. Having destroyed him with literalism, she pivots to a previously unmentioned statute about aliens who seek the life of a citizen, reads it broadly, and the court strips Shylock of his estate and imposes a forced conversion.

I want a reading of the scene that takes both the play and the interpretive theories seriously, and I have three questions I'd like your answer to engage. First, does the play vindicate either method, or does it show something the theories themselves have trouble acknowledging: that the choice between interpretive methods may be downstream of the outcome the interpreter wants, so that methodology functions less as a theory of meaning and more as a weapon one picks up when it points the right way? Portia's method-switching is exactly this, but Shylock is not innocent of it either — his insistence on the letter of the bond is, after all, a post-hoc literalism, since the bond was never meant to be collected. Second, I'm curious whether the scene dramatizes what interpretive theorists call the level-of-generality problem: purposivists tell us to read a text in light of its purpose, but any transaction or statute has purposes at many levels of abstraction, and the level at which you characterize the purpose often determines the outcome. Shylock can describe the bond's purpose as securing repayment, which triple damages would fully satisfy; Portia and the Duke describe Venice's commercial law as protecting the very possibility of credit and trade among strangers, a purpose Shylock's enforcement would destroy. The scene never adjudicates which characterization is correct, and I suspect the play knows this. Third, be honest about where the play resists this whole reading: it is formally a comedy about mercy, and the forced conversion at the end is presented, appallingly, as an act of clemency — which suggests the play's own purposive appeals to mercy may be doing ideological work, exactly what textualists warn happens when interpretation is unmoored from text. If a modern judge reasoned like Portia, we would call it results-driven; if one reasoned like Shylock, we would call it monstrous. Does the play show us that the debate between the two camps is shallower than either camp admits, or does it show that each method is only as good as the institution and the interpreter wielding it?
