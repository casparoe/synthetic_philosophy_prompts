I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology and the two-envelope paradox has been bugging me for years, not because I don't know the resolutions but because the literature is completely stalled: everyone agrees there's no reason to switch, and yet people have been publishing "the definitive resolution" since the 80s and the proposals don't converge at all. One camp says it's a plain algebra error — treating the other envelope as equally likely to contain half or double regardless of the amount you see, which secretly assumes a uniform distribution over an unbounded range. Another camp says that diagnosis dodges the real issue, because you can cook up distributions where the expected gain from switching is positive no matter which amount you observe, and the naive calculation isn't just wrong but almost right, so something deeper about infinite expectations is doing the work. A third group shrugs and says the single switch is harmless and only the "switch forever" loop is incoherent, so there's no paradox, just a cute trick. Are these camps disagreeing about the math, or are they answering different questions under one name — where the fallacy is located vs. what decision theory should say when expectations are infinite vs. why the switching argument keeps feeling compelling? My working hypothesis is that it's mostly the second, that the "paradox" is really three puzzles (an algebra slip, an infinity problem, and a psychological one) that people keep trading as a single thing, which is why every resolution satisfies its author and convinces nobody else — but I can't quite make that stick, since the algebra people will say they've addressed everything worth addressing. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side actually claims, and tell me what would count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a theorem, or everyone just admitting the word "paradox" is doing the damage?
