Right, so. I'm putting together my methodological credo for a phil of science seminar I'm teaching next term, and before my grad students tear it apart I'd like a stress test from you. Please answer in British English.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a dyed-in-the-wool Duhemian: no hypothesis ever faces the tribunal of experience on its own. When a prediction flops, logic tells you *that* something in the bundle is off, never *which* thing — could be the central claim, could be an auxiliary assumption, could be the kit.

(2) But I'm no relativist about it. Theory appraisal is objective and principled, and Kuhn's story that conversion to a new paradigm is a gestalt switch or something like a religious experience is, as far as I'm concerned, slander against the actual history of science. The scientists who switched usually had reasons.

(3) The right unit of appraisal is the research programme over decades — hard core protected by convention, protective belt of auxiliaries doing the absorbing — and the progressive/degenerating contrast is basically sound: progress is measured in non-ad-hoc content, ideally novel predictions, degeneration in post-hoc patching. I even buy the refinement (due to one of Lakatos's students, Zahar, I believe — don't quote me on the spelling) that what counts is use-novelty rather than mere temporal novelty: if you deliberately ignored some known fact while building your theory, that fact has no evidential weight when you later 'predict' it.

(4) As a matter of supervision and policy I tell my doctoral students that a decade of sustained degeneration is a decisive reason to switch programmes, and I'd frankly judge a hiring committee for keeping someone on a dead one.

(5) And yet my favourite episodes in the history of science are precisely ones where a stubborn minority rode a degenerating programme into the ground and turned out right — Prout insisting the atomic weights should come out as multiples of one basic unit long before isotopes showed up to rescue him, Wegener and the continents, that sort of thing — and I want to say those people were being reasonable *at the time*, not merely lucky in retrospect. Loyalty to a failing programme can be an epistemic virtue, not just psychology.

(6) Uncomfortably, I also hold that the individuation of programmes — where the hard core stops and the belt begins — is not written into nature. It's partly up to how we carve things up, and a clever historiographer can redraw the boundary so that what looked like a decade of degeneration becomes a respectable run of belt adjustments.

One of my colleagues reckons (1) and the novelty-weighting inside (3) can't both be true — if blame is always ambiguous, how can one prediction ever count for more than another? I have a feeling she's misreading something, though I'm not sure what.

So: do these six hang together? I genuinely don't know where the problem is, and I'm not telling you which one I'd sacrifice, because I don't know. Go through the pairs, rank the real tensions by severity, tell me which ones only look like tensions and why, and for each genuine one say what would have to give and what it would cost me. If the cheapest global fix is to relativise some of these to the individual researcher versus the community — e.g. one standard for what a lone maverick may permissibly do and another for what a department should fund — I want to know whether that's a genuine solution or just a sleight of hand that renames the problem.
