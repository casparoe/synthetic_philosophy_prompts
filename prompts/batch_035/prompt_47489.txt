Before I take sides between finitism and Cantorian actualism about the infinite, I want the specification both sides are being judged against, and I want it fixed in advance rather than assembled in the course of arguing for one of them. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of actual infinity, where adequacy means: fit to adjudicate whether infinite totalities can exist, and fit to be consulted when mathematics or physics is tempted to posit one.

The theory must, at minimum: settle what it means for two infinite collections to be the same size, and say whether the observation, going back to Galileo, that the perfect squares can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the naturals despite being a proper part of them is a discovery about size or a reductio of comparing by pairing; keep cardinality from collapsing into magnitude, since a finite line segment and the entire line are equinumerous though not equal in length, and the Banach–Tarski result shows what happens to measure once equinumerosity is allowed to license rearrangement; deliver a verdict on Hilbert's hotel, in which every room is occupied and every room is free, saying what, if anything, is created or destroyed by moving each guest exactly once; do the same for supertasks — Thomson's lamp, whose state after infinitely many switchings follows from neither the fact that it was switched on infinitely many times nor the fact that it was switched off infinitely many times, and the collision supertask in which a chamber packed with an infinite sequence of progressively smaller particles empties itself in finite time — which requires the theory to say which physical magnitudes inherit a well-defined limit value and which are simply underdetermined by an infinite process with no last stage; say something principled about infinite lotteries, where the demand that every ticket be equally likely cannot be met alongside countable additivity when there are countably many tickets; adjudicate whether a beginningless infinite past differs in kind from an endless infinite future, given that only on the actualist reading is the past a completed totality to be traversed; and draw the actual/potential distinction precisely enough that the finitist's complaint can be stated as a substantive thesis rather than dismissed as verbal.

For each requirement, give the case or result that motivates it; mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are demands of one camp dressed up as constraints — I suspect unrestricted part-whole comparison and the uniformity of infinite lotteries belong to the second kind; flag any pair of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and where the conflict can be defused only by splitting one notion into two, say which notion is being split; and tell me which single requirement excludes the most otherwise-plausible contenders. Then, briefly, score strict finitism, straightforward Cantorian actualism, and a potentialist reading of set theory against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases — the scorecard is for me to fill in later if I want it.
