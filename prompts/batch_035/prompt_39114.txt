I sit on my city's heritage committee -- we decide what happens to monuments, plaques, street names, etc., maybe 15-20 items a year, one week to make a recommendation, and council has the final say. Someone told me Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation argues history is only justified by whether it serves life, and splits it into monumental, antiquarian and critical kinds, each healthy in its way and each with a disease it decays into when it dominates. Can you turn that into an actual decision procedure our committee could run on each item -- questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a firm recommendation (remove / keep / keep-with-recontextualizing), and where it has to hand the thing over to council because it's genuinely a values question no procedure can settle? Then run it on three cases: a Confederate-era monument, a crumbling 1920s grain elevator nobody petitioned about but the local antiquarian society wants preserved, and a statue of a founder who did genuinely great civic things and also owned enslaved people. I especially want the procedure to handle that last one honestly, even if the answer it gives is one most of our committee would hate. And flag where a Nietzsche-derived procedure is going to misfire -- I suspect he'd say a committee like ours is itself a symptom of the problem he was diagnosing, and I want to know if that sinks the whole exercise.
