I'm trying to understand how semantic externalism is supposed to be compatible with Frege's puzzle about identity, and I keep going in circles. Let me lay out the problem as I see it, and I'd appreciate a clear explanation of where my reasoning goes wrong or right.

Putnam's Twin Earth scenario is supposed to show that what my words mean depends not just on what's in my head, but on my environment. I'm molecule-for-molecule identical to my twin on Twin Earth, yet when I say "water" I refer to H2O, while my twin refers to the superficially identical but chemically distinct liquid that fills their oceans. Same internal state, different meaning — so meaning must be partly in the world.

The trouble starts when I connect this to Frege's puzzle. The statements "water is water" and "water is H2O" have the same subject and predicate as far as extension goes: both say of one stuff that it is identical to itself. Yet "water is water" strikes me as trivially uninformative, while "water is H2O" was a genuine empirical discovery. Frege's original explanation was that the two terms differ in "sense" — a mode of presentation, something like a way of thinking about the referent that lives at the level of thought. But if externalism is right, and my twin and I differ in what we mean despite sharing all our internal states, then the difference in meaning between his word and mine can't be located in any difference in how we think. So the externalist seems to have trouble even stating Frege's puzzle in its usual form: it looks like meaning outruns thought, while cognitive significance was supposed to be a fact about thinkers.

Please help me get straight on the following:

1. Does the externalist have to deny that sameness of meaning is required for sameness of cognitive significance, or is there a more subtle reconciliation available?
2. One tempting response is that the informativeness of "water is H2O" comes from the descriptions or theories each term is associated with in the speaker's head — water as the clear drinkable stuff in lakes, H2O as what chemists write on the blackboard. But doesn't this response quietly surrender externalism? If differences in cognitive value are always explained by internal differences, it's unclear what work the external contribution to meaning is doing.
3. I've seen hints that externalists can individuate "senses" two-dimensionally: by how a term is used across possible ways the actual world might turn out to be, versus how it picks out referents once the world is fixed. Can you explain, at an intuitive level, how this is supposed to recover the idea that "water" and "H2O" differ in cognitive significance while still referring to the same thing?

I'd like an explanation pitched at someone who has taken a first course in philosophy of language: technically careful, but with the core intuitions spelled out rather than assumed.
