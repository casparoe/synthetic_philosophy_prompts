My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the Libet experiments, and at some point he offered this summary, which I have tried to reproduce faithfully: "Libet hooked people up to an EEG and had them flick their wrist whenever they felt the urge, while watching a fast-moving clock to report when they first felt the intention. He found that the readiness potential — a buildup of electrical activity in the motor cortex — started about half a second before the movement, while people reported becoming aware of their intention only about a fifth of a second before. So the brain had already made the decision well before 'you' were aware of it, which means the feeling of freely choosing is an illusion that arrives after the fact. Libet himself concluded that free will, in any traditional sense, does not exist." I'm inclined to think there's something to the experiment but that his rendition glosses over a lot. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what a careful neuroscientist or philosopher of action would accept, what is a subtle misrepresentation — for instance, anything that turns preparatory brain activity into an already-made decision, or overstates what the experiment could actually predict — and whether his conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate. I'd also like to know what the standard rejoinders are, including ones Libet himself endorsed, and how far they go.
