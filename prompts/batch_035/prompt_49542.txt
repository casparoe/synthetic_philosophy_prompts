I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I'm working on a piece about why smart people disagree. My editor keeps pushing me to include the "Aumann thing" because she heard about it on a podcast, and when I asked her to say more she gave me this: "There's a famous theorem by a Nobel economist, Robert Aumann, from the 1970s that proves two rational people can never agree to disagree. If you and I have the same information and we're both honest reasoners, we have to end up with the same beliefs, and if we find out we disagree about something -- say the minimum wage, or who'll win the election -- then at least one of us is being irrational or lying. The proof works because we can just keep telling each other our opinions back and forth until the disagreement evaporates. Aumann's point was that disagreement is basically a psychiatric condition, not a fact about the world."

I have my doubts. She's usually pretty sharp on this stuff, but the "psychiatric condition" line sounds like the podcast host editorializing, and I have a vague memory that the theorem has some pretty heavy fine print about what counts as the same information. Could you go through her summary bit by bit and tell me which parts an economist or philosopher who actually works on this would nod along to, which parts they'd want to flag as sloppy popularization, and which parts are just wrong? In particular I'd want to know whether the claim that disagreement evaporates after we trade opinions back and forth is really part of the theorem or something people added later, and whether her conclusion -- that ordinary political disagreement must involve someone being irrational or dishonest -- actually follows from whatever the true version says. If the honest answer is "the theorem is technically true but much more fragile than the podcast suggests," I want to be able to write that sentence without getting letters from angry decision theorists, so please also tell me where the fragility lives, in plain language I can put in a piece for general readers. (Please no reading lists or citations -- I just need the reasoning.)
