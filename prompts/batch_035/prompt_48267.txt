I'm taking a seminar on Hume and the ethics of life-and-death decisions, and I've gotten myself into an argument I can't get out of. We just read Hume's short essay "Of Suicide," where he tries to dissolve the charge that suicide disturbs the order of nature (his reply, roughly: we constantly interfere with the course of nature -- diverting rivers, taking medicine -- so mere interference with the natural course can't be what makes an act criminal; and a life is of no greater consequence to the universe than that of an oyster, so there is no scale-of-disturbance story either). He then argues that suicide can only be criminal, if at all, as a breach of duty -- to society, to ourselves, or to God -- and he tries to show that these duties are conditional: they bind only while we can be of use, and they are discharged by prior service. The step that fascinates me is his treatment of duties to society: he compares ending one's life, when one can no longer be of use and one's conduct has been blameless, to the innocent death of a citizen; he suggests that once one has rendered some memorable service to mankind, one has acquired a right to retire from life, and he says, roughly, that no one is more culpable for preventing such a death than for meddling in the affairs of strangers. My professor then remarked in passing that, if Hume is right, a physician who resuscitates a patient who has competently and settledly attempted to end a blameless, service-rendered life does something morally on a par with a busybody who obstructs that person's chosen retirement from society -- that the doctor is not preventing a harm but canceling a liberty. That remark started me on the following chain, which now strikes me as proving far too much:

(1) On Hume's account, a settled decision to end one's life, made by someone whose conduct has been blameless and who has rendered great service to others, breaches no duty -- to society, self, or God.
(2) Where there is no breach of duty, there is no wrongdoing to prevent; so preventing such a death is not the prevention of a wrong but the obstruction of a liberty, and it is morally on a par with meddling in the affairs of strangers, as Hume says.
(3) So a physician who resuscitates such a patient, against the patient's settled wishes, acts wrongly -- no less so than a stranger who physically obstructs a peaceful, blameless person's chosen course of conduct.
(4) By parity of reasoning, this holds for anyone who prevents the death: friends who confiscate the means, an ambulance crew, a bystander. Each of them, in the moment of intervention, is a meddler in someone else's affairs.
(5) Therefore, if Hume's essay is right, we ought to say that a doctor who revives such a patient, or a friend who quietly removes the means, has done something wrong and owes something like an apology -- and, more troubling, that ordinary medical ethics, which instructs physicians to preserve life in exactly these cases, instructs them to do wrong.
(6) That conclusion seems to me clearly too much: I don't believe the friend who confiscates the means, hoping for a better season of life for someone she loves, is a meddler in the wrong, and I don't want to say medical ethics is systematically mistaken here.

I'd rather keep (1) -- I find Hume's conditional-duty picture persuasive, at least for the blameless, service-rendered case -- and I can live with giving up (6) if I must. My suspicion is that something slips between (3) and (4), or between (4) and (5): perhaps the blamelessness of preventing a death and the wrongness of preventing it come apart somewhere, or perhaps the parity step generalizes a restricted liberty into something wider than Hume's comparison supports. But I can't name the slippage precisely. Which step should I reject, why did that step look so natural to me, and what do I give up by rejecting it? Please lead with your answer in the first sentence -- name the step you'd reject -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
