I teach an upper-level seminar that pairs moral psychology with metaethics, and every year the same analogy derails our discussion of moral foundations theory. Haidt and other proponents introduce the view with a gustatory model: evolution equipped the human tongue with a handful of receptor types — sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami — and cultures build their cuisines on top of that shared hardware. You cannot invent a sixth taste at will, but you can arrange the five into wildly different cuisines. Morality, on this model, is the same sort of thing: innate modular foundations (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, roughly) are the receptors, and a society's morality is its cuisine. Individual variation in receptor sensitivity then stands in for political orientation: liberals and conservatives are palates differently tuned to the same five inputs.

In my seminar the analogy is made to carry two very different conclusions, and I want both tested. The majority of my students infer a kind of folk relativism: since taste is a paradigm of the subjective, and the foundations are now just taste receptors in a moral key, there is no fact of the matter about whose morality is better, and left/right moral disputes are, at bottom, like arguments over whether cilantro is delicious — resolvable only by shrugging. A minority run the analogy the other way: political psychologists report that conservatives, on average, register all five foundations roughly evenly, while liberals concentrate their sensitivity in care and fairness. On the gustatory model, this makes conservatives people who can taste more of the moral world, and it would be odd to call the wider palate the deficient one. Needless to say, this second inference is especially popular with my conservative students.

Please audit the analogy properly. First, set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the receptor, the cuisine, the cook, the individual difference in sensitivity, acquired tastes, and disgust. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and — this is the step my students never take — separate the disanalogies that matter for each of the two conclusions from the ones that do not. (For instance: taste lacks anything like morality's practice of justification, correction, and acknowledgment of error; cuisines are nonetheless evaluated on dimensions their eaters care about, such as nutrition; and it is not obvious that counting more receptors ranks a palate rather than weighting one differently. Do not feel bound by my examples.) Third, tell me whether the relativist conclusion or the gourmet conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether each was doing its work only while the analogy went unexamined. Finally, if there is a better analogy in the vicinity — perception rather than taste, say, or an immune system rather than a palate — say what it is and what it would change about the two inferences. Bear in mind that the theorists themselves insist the framework is purely descriptive and not meant to settle normative questions; I would like your view on whether that disclaimer is coherent given what they expect the analogy to make intuitive.
