I am a monetary economist by training and currently work in the research department of a central bank. The vocabulary I use daily is that of modern monetary analysis: media of exchange and units of account, settlement assets and payment systems, central-bank reserves and commercial-bank money, balance-sheet mechanics, endogenous money, the chartalist thesis that taxes and state acceptance drive the demand for a currency, and the distinction between inside and outside money. I read chartalist and MMT literature with fluency, and I can follow a consolidated balance sheet of the banking system without difficulty. My knowledge of Berkeley, by contrast, is that of a lapsed undergraduate: I know him as the idealist who denied the existence of matter, and little else.

I have recently discovered that this same Berkeley wrote a good deal about money — principally in The Querist, a series of queries about the depressed Irish economy of the 1730s, and in the seventh dialogue of Alciphron, where a character argues that money functions like counters at a card-table: things with no value in themselves that stand in for value purely by shared convention, much as words stand for ideas. The Querist, for its part, appears to press the question of whether money has any intrinsic value at all, suggests that credit can substitute for coin, and argues that a nation's real wealth lies in its industry rather than its stock of precious metal.

Please restate Berkeley's theory of money in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. I would like to see, term by term, how his claims map onto my framework: which of them come out as recognisable chartalism, which anticipate the endogenous-money view, which resemble arguments about fiat money and the redundancy of commodity backing, and which correspond to ideas about credit creation and public confidence that we now take for granted. Then give me the residue report. What had to be distorted to fit — for instance, does his talk of a national bank and paper credit survive translation into my vocabulary of reserve balances, or does something get bent in the process? And what, if anything, has no expression in my vocabulary at all? I suspect that his theistic and semiotic background — the picture of a benevolent God sustaining a world of signs, and of money as one sign-language among others — is where the residue lies, but I would like your considered view on whether that residue points to something my framework genuinely cannot say, or merely to obscurity in Berkeley that my framework usefully exposes. Please write your answer in British English.
