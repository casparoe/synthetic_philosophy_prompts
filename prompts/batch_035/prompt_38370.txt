I direct the individual-engagement program at a mid-sized environmental nonprofit with roughly 40,000 supporters. The program consists of voluntary personal pledges: each quarter we decide which new pledges to add to the campaign — skip one discretionary flight per year, drop beef, vote in the off-year local election. Two recurring problems. First, every proposal gets challenged with the inefficacy objection: your one flight doesn't cause fewer flights to be scheduled, one burger doesn't change herd sizes, one vote almost never turns an election — so why ask anyone to do it? Second, my program officers answer these challenges ad hoc and inconsistently: one week they say "every little bit helps," the next that "it's about sending a signal," and neither answer survives contact with a skeptical supporter.

Write a decision procedure my officers can actually run on a proposed pledge, with the following requirements.

The form: numbered steps, in order. For each step, say what question it asks, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it explicitly hands the decision back to a human because the remaining question is one of values — how much we may ask of volunteers, whether a pledge is required or merely admirable — that the procedure cannot settle. Officers with no philosophy training will run this in under thirty minutes per proposal on about a dozen proposals a quarter, so it must be usable without the theory behind it; be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

The causal core: the procedure needs a committed position on the inefficacy objection. Please don't give me a menu of options (threshold or conjunctive-act views, straightforward expected value, expected value with thresholds, integrity-based reasons) and leave the choice to me. Pick one as the procedure's official test, defend the choice in a short preamble, and then apply it consistently throughout — even where it bites. If the test you pick sometimes makes the procedure pass a pledge for the "wrong" reason, or reject one my officers wanted, that is a feature, not a bug.

The procedure must handle three hard kinds of case without collapsing: (1) threshold cases, where the effect materializes only once some fixed number of people change, so the marginal individual act seems to change nothing; (2) pivotality cases, such as elections, where a single act has a very small probability of a very large effect; and (3) coordination cases, where the point of the pledge is partly to make other people act. For the worked cases, stipulate whatever numbers you need — recount thresholds, batch sizes, uptake rates — rather than reaching for real-world statistics; the logic of the procedure is what I need, not empirical grounding we don't have.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) skip one discretionary flight per year; (b) drop beef entirely; (c) vote in your next off-year local election. For (b) I'll be candid about my priors: I want it to pass, and I do not want the procedure telling supporters that no single purchase matters. If the test you committed to yields that result anyway, say so plainly rather than rigging the case to please me.

Close with a section on what the procedure cannot do: the questions about individual pledges it leaves open, and the situations in which it will predictably misfire.
