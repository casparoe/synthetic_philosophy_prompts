so I'm a science-and-ideas writer, and I'm working on a piece about what happens in the brain when someone acts. I spent some time around a theater company, and I came away confused about something that is maybe more philosophy than neuroscience, and I'm hoping you can untangle it.

the acting profession seems to want it two different ways about emotion, and I can't tell if it's holding one coherent theory or two incompatible ones.

on the one hand: the gold standard of praise for an actor is that they really felt it. we build legends around the method people who stay in character for months, and the implicit idea seems to be that a performance is only truly great when the emotion on stage is a genuine state occuring in the actor, not just a convincing portrayal of one. the awards, the profiles, the mythology all run on that.

on the other hand: the audience is crying over a death that everyone in the room knows never happened, in a person everyone knows doesn't exist. and the profession is completely fine with that. the whole art form is built on the audience knowing its fiction and being moved anyway.

and then the part that really gets me. when the actor claims the emotion got too real — actually fell in love, actually couldn't shake it off after the scene — the profession treats that as a failure, sometimes even as something requiring intervention, while still demanding that the emotion look real. meanwhile child actors often get shielded from the darkest parts of the story, as if knowing it's fiction is supposed to matter to what it does to you. and acting teachers in the same breath tell students to stop playing the emotion and play the objective, as though the emotion is a byproduct nobody should aim at.

so heres my question, as someone who has to explain this in print. taken as a bundle, what do these practices presuppose about how fiction works on us? is the implicit theory something like: the actor has to supply real emotion while the audience only sort of imagines feeling it — and is that even a stable line, or does the shield-the-child-actor instinct quietly contradict it? or is the profession just philosophically underdetermined, where almost any account of why fiction moves us could license what they do, and the really-felt-it praise is basically marketing? I'd genuinely like to know which parts of the practice actually pin down a theory and which parts could go along with nearly any view.
