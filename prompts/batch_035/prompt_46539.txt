hey, i've got a draft essay i need torn apart before i invest more time in it. the topic: whether the simulation argument should make us less fanatical about the far future. i've gotten to the stage where i can't tell if the core move actually works or if it's just a cute reframing. be brutal about the logic in particular, that's where i'm least sure of myself.

the rough thesis: fanatical longtermist reasoning (tiny probability, astronomical payoff — pascal-style bets on securing a vast future civilization) is undermined by taking the simulation argument seriously, because once you condition on being simulated, the expected-value machinery that fanaticism runs on stops tracking anything real.

argument sketch:

1. fanaticism needs a relatively clean expected-value calculation over the far future: the probability of extinction/catastrophe is small-ish, and the payoff — trillions of valuable future lives — dwarfs everything near-term. that's the whole sales pitch.

2. but if we're in a simulation, the far future is compute that belongs to someone else. the duration of our future is capped by a shutdown probability we can't estimate even in principle, since it's set by the simulators' purposes, not by anything we could learn about physics or our own institutions. multiply your astronomical payoff by an unknown-in-principle truncation probability and the fanatical bet becomes a bet on a variable you've conceded you cannot model.

3. worse, if simulated civilizations tend to run their own simulations (which is what makes us probably simulated in the first place), we should expect a deep nesting hierarchy with rungs getting more resource-poor as you go down. simple anthropic reasoning — most observer-moments are on the deep, cheap rungs — suggests our compute budget is modest by construction. so the astronomical-payoff scenarios get systematically less likely the more you condition on simulation, which pushes in exactly the direction the fanatic was already nervous about but never lets into the calculation.

4. sharpest version, i think: if simulators are running ancestor simulations for historical fidelity, then our descendants aren't really our descendants, they're our audience, and the far future we're "securing" is a script. trying to change it is either futile (they terminate the run at the historically faithful endpoint anyway) or actively risky (deviation from the target trajectory flags us for shutdown). you get something structurally like classical fatalism, except it arrives via lab equipment instead of theology.

concessions i want to make explicitly: simulated suffering still counts as suffering, so the essay can't collapse into "nothing matters if we're bits"; and the simulation argument's own premises — that simulators would be numerous and interested in running people like us — are shaky in ways i should flag rather than hide behind.
