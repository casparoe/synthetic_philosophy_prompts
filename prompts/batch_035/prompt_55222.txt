I have a set of gut verdicts about what I would pay to play variants of the St. Petersburg game, and I want to know whether any single, precisely stateable decision principle captures them, or whether my gut is simply inconsistent and some of the verdicts have to go. For reference, the plain game is: a fair coin is flipped until the first heads; if that comes on flip n, the payout is 2^n dollars, so each flip-number contributes exactly 1 to the expectation and the expected value is infinite.

My verdicts:

1. The plain game: I would pay about $25 to play, and I feel no temptation at all to pay $1,000, even though the mathematics says any finite price is a bargain.

2. The game truncated after 40 flips (payout zero if no heads appears): the expectation is just under $40, and I would pay maybe $30. The gap between $30 and $40 already feels natural to me.

3. Here is where I start to embarrass myself. Truncated after a million flips, the expectation is on the order of a million dollars. I would pay... perhaps $31. My gut says the entire tail beyond the fortieth flip is worth about a dollar, regardless of how much expectation it carries.

4. The shifted game: the payout is 2^n − 2,000 dollars. Every outcome with n ≤ 10 is a loss of roughly two thousand dollars (probability about 99.9%), while the large outcomes remain astronomical and the expectation is still infinite. I would not play this game even for free.

5. The alternating game: the payout is +2^n dollars if the first heads comes on an odd flip, −2^n dollars if it comes on an even flip. The expectation is the series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ..., which has no determinate value. My gut says I would accept the game for free but would not pay anything to play it.

6. Two independent tickets to the plain game, played back to back: I would pay about $35 for the pair. So the second, identical ticket is worth about $10 to me, not another $25.

7. The bulk offer: a thousand independent plays of the plain game at $25 each. Here my gut reverses course entirely — I feel that across a thousand plays the swings must average out and $25 per play is a clear bargain. I believe this appeal to the law of large numbers is actually illicit for a heavy-tailed game, but the feeling persists.

8. The slow-growth game: the payout is 1.5^n instead of 2^n, so the expectation is a finite $3 or so. I would cheerfully pay $2.50, close to the fair price.

What I would like from you: a principle, or at most a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to compute with — something in the neighborhood of bounded utility for money, or pricing by a quantile or typical outcome, or risk-weighted valuation, or a portfolio-level rather than ticket-level rule, or something I have not thought of. For the principle you favor, please give its price for each of my eight cases, so I can see which verdicts it saves. Then apply it to three cases I have not yet considered and say what it dictates: (a) a coin that lands heads with probability one-third on each flip (so the expectation diverges even faster than the original — does your principle make this game worth more than the plain one, and should that bother me?); (b) a version of the alternating game where the payout is +2^n/n for odd n and −2^n/n for even n, so the expectation is the alternating harmonic series and converges to about 69 cents; and (c) a version where the entire payout goes to a charity I endorse rather than to me. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and tell me which one you would tell me to give up. In your answer, please indicate how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and single out the point at which you are least sure of yourself — for instance, whether the principle is genuinely correct or merely the best available rationalization of verdicts like mine.
