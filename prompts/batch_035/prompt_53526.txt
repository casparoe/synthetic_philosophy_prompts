After a career in experimental neuroscience — three decades of recording from visual cortex, where I insisted that a detector readout counts as a measurement of a quantity only if that quantity causally affects the readout — I have retired into philosophy of mind and been unsettled by a problem I keep circling back to. If phenomenal experience is epiphenomenal, then my verbal reports about my experience are physical events with physical causes; the experience itself seemingly does nothing to make those reports accurate. Yet I am reluctant to conclude that everything I have ever said about my own consciousness is lucky guesswork, and equally reluctant to abandon physical causal closure to rescue introspection.

Before I try to adjudicate any particular theory, I want the specification. Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the relation between phenomenal consciousness and physical processes must meet. At minimum it should cover: whether causal closure of the physical is negotiable or not; what would count as a non-accidental explanation of the reliability (or documented unreliability) of phenomenal judgment; whether such an explanation is compatible with the evolutionary account of the neural machinery that produces reports about experience; whether a theory may collapse the distinction between having an experience and judging that one has it; and how it should handle the epistemology of beings — infants, patients with certain aphasias, possibly non-human animals — whose reports are partial or absent. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are, on inspection, merely preferences of one camp in the debate. The part I most want to see is whether some subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, such that no theory could meet them all; if so, say which ones and why, since that would itself settle something important. Do not spend time scoring the familiar contenders against the list — the criteria are what I need.
