Draft:

Paragraph one: The obvious definition of honesty for an AI system — say only true things — fails on both sides. On one side, a system can say false things without being dishonest: a system calibrated about its own uncertainty that reports a 70% credence which turns out wrong has not lied; an honest forecaster is wrong all the time. On the other side, a system can be dishonest while saying only true things, since Grice taught us that communication carries content beyond the literal: a system that answers "Is this medication safe with alcohol?" with "It has been approved by the regulator" has said nothing false while engineering a false belief that it was specifically tested for alcohol interaction. So the locus of honesty can't be the truth value of the sentence. It has to be the update the hearer performs: an utterance is honest to the extent that the belief revision it predictably produces in a reasonable hearer moves that hearer toward the truth, and deceptive to the extent that the system can foresee the revision moving the hearer away from the truth and produces it anyway.

Paragraph two: What about lying, as opposed to misleading? Here I deploy a counterfactual test for assertion. A system genuinely asserts P only if it would not have said P had it taken P to be false — a broken clock that always says "it's 3pm" isn't asserting anything when it happens to be 3pm, and a language model that emits P regardless of whether P is true is, in that respect, more like the clock than like a liar. Lying, then, requires the counterfactual sensitivity to truth (so that the system counts as asserting) plus the intent-equivalent: the system tracks that the hearer will rely on the assertion, and exploits that reliance. For systems where "intent" is contentious, I propose replacing it with structural counterfactuals: the system is lying iff, holding the hearer's reliance fixed, it would have said the opposite were the truth the opposite, and it produces the assertion because of the reliance it anticipates. This lets us taxonomize AI dishonesty into three grades — the clock (no tracking), the con man (tracking plus exploitation of hearer reliance), and the lawyer (tracking plus literal truth plus engineered implicature) — and only the second and third are dishonest in distinct ways.

Paragraph three: The practical payoff is that honesty evaluations for AI systems are currently measuring the wrong thing. Truth-rate benchmarks — sample outputs, check them against a grader — are gameable by exactly the lawyer pattern: a system can achieve a perfect truth rate while structuring its true statements to leave the user with a false picture, and a benchmark that only sees the statements cannot tell the difference. What an honesty eval should measure is the change in the human's beliefs: put a user with a measured baseline model of the domain in interaction with the system, and score the drift of the user's stated credences against ground truth after the interaction, with the system blinded to the fact that it's the beliefs rather than the sentences that are being scored. Deception, on my definition, is not a property of sentences at all; it's a property of epistemic traffic, and the whole field should stop auditing sentences and start auditing traffic.
