The exam question was:

"Q4. The epiphenomenalist holds that conscious experiences are caused by physical processes in the brain but do not themselves cause anything — not behavior, not speech, not even our own beliefs and reports about those experiences. (a) Explain why this appears to generate a distinctive problem about the status of our judgments and reports concerning our own conscious experiences. (b) Explain why this problem does not arise, or arises differently, for the physicalist and for the dualist who allows conscious states to have physical effects. (c) Must the epiphenomenalist concede that our beliefs about our own experiences are unjustified? Defend your answer."

And here is the student's answer, typed out exactly as they wrote it:

"(a) On epiphenomenalism, physical processes cause conscious experiences, but the experiences themselves have no causal powers: they don't affect the brain, they don't affect behavior, and they don't affect anything we say or think. So when I sincerely report 'the afterimage looks orange to me,' that utterance is caused entirely by physical states of my brain — the orange phenomenal character itself contributes nothing to it. The report gets produced, as it were, with the experience playing no role in bringing it about. That is the problem: our reports about experience seem to be made blind to the very thing they are reports of.

(b) The physicalist doesn't face this, because for her experiences just are physical states, so they cause reports the ordinary way any brain state causes speech. A dualist who lets conscious states have physical effects also escapes: if qualia can push neurons around, then my saying 'I'm in pain' can be partly caused by the pain itself. Of course that sort of dualist then has to answer to physical causal closure, which is a different and probably worse problem.

In fact the epiphenomenalist's difficulty here is really just the traditional problem of other minds in modern dress: the epiphenomenalist cannot verify that anyone's reports — including her own — track their experiences, which is what the skeptic about other minds has claimed all along.

(c) Yes. Worse, if my reports are not caused by my qualia, they are not about my qualia at all, and a report that is not about what it seems to be about is a false report. So whenever the epiphenomenalist sincerely says 'I am in pain,' she is saying something false. A theory that makes every sincere avowal of experience false thereby refutes itself. Epiphenomenalism is self-refuting, and can be set aside without weighing its other merits."
