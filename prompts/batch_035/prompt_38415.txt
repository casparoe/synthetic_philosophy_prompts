My roommate has been on a tear about the procreation asymmetry and I can't find the hole in his argument, though I'm sure there is one. The asymmetry, as he frames it: it's wrong to create a person whose life will be worse than nothing for them, but it's not wrong — not even a missed opportunity — to decline to create a person whose life would be well worth living. He says if you accept the first half and reject the second, you're being sentimental, and here's why:

(1) Ask why it's wrong to create the miserable person. The badness has to be grounded in one of two things: either it's impersonal (the world ends up containing more suffering than it otherwise would), or it's person-affecting (somebody ends up badly off, or worse off than they otherwise would have been).

(2) It can't be impersonal. If adding suffering to the world is impersonally bad, then, by the same token, leaving happiness out of the world is impersonally bad too, and then we're all obligated to have as many children as we feasibly can — which is exactly the conclusion the asymmetry was supposed to spare us from.

(3) So the wrong must be person-affecting: the miserable person ends up worse off than they otherwise would have been.

(4) But before you create anyone, there is no "them" to be worse off. A miserable life compared to nonexistence is something compared to nothing, and nothing isn't a condition anyone can be better or worse off than.

(5) So the wrong can't be person-affecting either.

(6) Therefore the half of the asymmetry everyone agrees on — don't create miserable people — has no ground left. Either it's false outright, or we crawl back to (2) and procreative maximalism, and either way the asymmetry collapses.

He's clearly too pleased with this. My instinct is that something fishy happens between (3) and (4), or maybe that (2) is doing more work than it lets on, but every time I try to say exactly what, I end up conceding something I don't want to concede. For what it's worth, I want to keep both the wrongness of creating miserable lives and the permission not to create happy ones — if I can't keep both, I'd rather know which one I'm sacrificing and why it's the cheaper one. Please answer in American English, walk me through the steps, and then give me a final answer in this exact format on the very last line: \answer{Reject step N} (or \answer{the argument is valid}, if you think it really is).
