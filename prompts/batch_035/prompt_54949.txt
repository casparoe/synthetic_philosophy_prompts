I never went to college -- I coordinate freight shipments for a living -- so please forgive me if I use my own words for things that have proper names in philosophy. For the past six years I've worked closely with a colleague from Hamburg, and I've stayed with her family twice in Germany, and somewhere along the way I started keeping notes on how differently we judge little everyday situations. In America, at least where I grew up, a rule feels like something you follow until your conscience gives you a good enough reason not to. With her it seems closer to the reverse: the rule itself is the reason, and conscience has to make an extraordinary case to override it. I've been wondering whether these are two coherent folk moralities rather than one right and one sloppy version, and which one has the better of it. But mostly I want to test whether I actually understand her.

You are a professional moral philosopher with expertise in comparative ethics and moral psychology. Please think very carefully before answering, and please start by brainstorming several candidate principles before committing to any of them.

Here is her track record, as fairly as I can state it:

1. Crossing against a red light on foot at 3 a.m. on a completely empty street, which is illegal in Germany: wrong.
2. Driving well above the speed limit to get a badly injured person to a hospital: not wrong.
3. Telling a friend you love the handmade gift she gave you when you actually dislike it: wrong, even though it spares feelings and hurts no one.
4. Downloading a movie from a piracy site when you would never have paid to see it anyway: wrong.
5. Cutting in line when you're in a genuine hurry: wrong.
6. Under-reporting a small side income on taxes out of real financial hardship, where the odds of detection are nil: wrong.
7. Breaking a promise to attend a friend's party because a close relative fell seriously ill that week: not wrong.

Last month I finally got to press her on something. Her rowing club has an old rule that members may not take boats out on one particular weekday afternoon. Everyone I spoke to privately agrees the rule is pointless; she herself has called it absurd. Her nephew took a boat out anyway.

So here are my questions. First, what do you predict she said about her nephew's trip -- wrong or not wrong? Second, I have two more pending cases I expect to find out her verdicts on, and I'd like your predictions there too: (a) a different nephew openly and non-violently blocks a deportation he believes is unjust, announces what he's doing beforehand, and accepts arrest; (b) she once promised a friend to keep her new address secret from everyone, and now the friend's abusive ex-partner is at the door asking politely where the friend lives. Third -- and this is what I really care about -- lay out for me the different principles that could be driving her verdicts. I can see at least two that fit everything above: one where rules and order matter in themselves, with a narrow exception for emergencies, and one where rules matter only because of what they do for everyone, such that even a pointless rule must be followed until it is formally changed. There may be others I haven't thought of. For each principle, tell me which of the three cases -- the nephew's boat, the protest, the promise -- it comes down on differently, and give me your best guess with reasons. Finally, tell me what further case I could put to her that would most cleanly tell the principles apart, since the ones I've listed may not do the job. Take your time and show your reasoning; a bare list of predictions won't help me.
