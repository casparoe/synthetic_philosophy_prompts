I help run the cause-selection workshop for a university effective-altruism student group, and we always use the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework: rate each candidate cause on how big the problem is, how solvable it is, and how little attention it's already getting, then combine the scores (multiplicatively, in the versions we use) to rank causes. Over several semesters of running this exercise I've noticed a recurring pattern that I don't have a word for, and it's starting to make me distrust the whole exercise. When we score a shared list of causes, the final ranking almost always comes out determined almost entirely by the neglectedness column: importance is effectively constant because everyone agrees the causes on the list are all "big" by the time they've made it onto the list, tractability gets quietly nudged to whatever value keeps the causes people already care about near the top, and neglectedness does all of the discriminating work. That's suspicious on its own, but there's a second layer: the three ratings are supposed to be independent of each other, and they plainly aren't. The fact that a cause is uncrowded is often itself a signal that capable people have looked at it and walked away — so the framework hands its highest scores to causes that have already been silently examined and rejected, while a cause can look "crowded" only because it is genuinely promising and has drawn attention precisely for that reason. The rubric treats the columns as separate measurements of separate facts about the world; a lot of the time they seem to be three readouts of one underlying fact, namely how much attention the cause has already survived.

So my question is whether there's an established concept for this failure mode: a multi-criterion scoring scheme whose factors are advertised as independent but are actually correlated through the history of who has already worked on the options being scored, such that combining them either collapses into a single factor plus noise or actively rewards options that prior scrutiny has vetted negatively. The nearest things I know of are the efficient-markets idea (but that's about prices and returns, not rubrics, and it doesn't capture the collapse-into-one-column part) and Goodhart's law (but Goodhart is about a proxy being gamed or optimized against; here nobody is gaming anything — the problem is that the "independent" factors share a common upstream cause, even when everyone scores in perfect good faith). Is there a standard name for this in decision theory, psychometrics, economics, or elsewhere — something in the measurement-error or composite-indicator literature, perhaps? If nothing established fits cleanly, I'd like you to coin a term and give a definition precise enough to distinguish it from both of the two nearest neighbors I mentioned, say where its edges are (for instance, what should we say about a case where two of the three factors really are independent and only one pair is entangled, or where the correlation runs in the opposite direction — neglectedness as evidence of a blind spot everyone else genuinely missed?), and say what, if anything, a group like mine could do to patch the exercise without abandoning it. Please write your answer in American English.
