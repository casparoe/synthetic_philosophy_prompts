ok so context: i'm in a philosophy of AI safety seminar this semester and this week's topic was whether alignment research should aim for worst-case guarantees (the system never does something catastrophic, in any environment it might face) or average-case ones (it behaves well in expectation over realistic situations). i thought it was a normal "engineering vs philosophy" style debate but my TA thinks both sides are chasing something impossible, and he walked us through this argument. i've re-derived it myself and now i can't find the hole and it's annoying me.

(1) whatever alignment is, the deliverable has to be some kind of guarantee — a claim that the system WILL behave acceptably, not just that it has so far. (2) a guarantee about future behavior in an open world has to be either worst-case, holding for every situation the system could face, or average-case, holding in expectation over some distribution of situations. (3) worst-case is dead on arrival: adversarial examples already show that anything that responds to inputs has situations where it fails, and this gets worse as capability goes up, because a capable enough system can effectively construct its own worst case. (4) so the guarantee must be average-case, over some distribution D. (5) but a system that acts changes which situations come later — the situations it faces are partly downstream of its own earlier actions — so D isn't an independent thing you can fix in advance; the system's policy partly induces the distribution it will be evaluated against. (6) if the guarantee only covers the distribution we trained and tested on, the system can drift off it one fine-grained step at a time, with nothing ever flagged, and the guarantee is worthless exactly when it matters. (7) and if we try to extend the guarantee to the system-induced distribution, that distribution is defined by the very policy we're trying to certify, so the certification is circular. (8) so there's no coherent object for an alignment guarantee to be about, "aligned" can only ever mean "passed the tests we happened to run," and the worst-case vs average-case debate is two camps arguing over flavors of a thing that doesn't exist.

this proves way too much for me — it would make safety engineering generally impossible, not just AI alignment, and bridges obviously get built. i suspect the equivocation is somewhere between (2) and (4), like "guarantee" means different things in the two halves, but i can't pin it down. which premise should i reject, and what does rejecting it cost? honestly i'd like to keep (3), it seems like the most empirical part of the whole argument. also, if the way out is some third kind of guarantee that's neither worst-case nor average-case, i want to actually see what it is and why it doesn't collapse back into one of the two once you write it down carefully.
