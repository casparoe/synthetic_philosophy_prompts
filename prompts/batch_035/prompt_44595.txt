I'm a novelist at work on a book set in the Warring States period, and one scene has stalled me. Two of my characters — an itinerant Mencian and a harder-headed scholar in the mold of Xunzi — argue beside a village well, and I need to know which of them should get the better of the exchange. My consultant, a retired sinologist who now mostly edits my prose, drafted the Mencian's case for me, and I built what I took to be a devastating reply for the Xunzian. The trouble is that the reply, if it works, seems to prove too much, and I cannot tell whether I have given my Xunzian a real argument or a cheap one. Since I refuse to let either character win by authorial fiat, I want to know where, if anywhere, the exchange actually breaks.

The Mencian's case, as drafted:

(1) Anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and distress — not to win the parents' gratitude, nor the neighbors' praise, nor to avoid a reputation for callousness; the response arrives before any calculation of advantage. This is Mencius's own example, from the famous passage on the sprouts: the heart of compassion as the sprout of benevolence, and so on for righteousness, ritual propriety, and wisdom.

(2) Whatever arises in us spontaneously, prior to learning and reflection, belongs to human nature (xing).

(3) So human nature contains the beginnings of the virtues, which cultivation grows to full size, as a sprout becomes a tree.

(4) Therefore human nature is good — Mencius's claim that xing is shan.

My Xunzian's reply, built to mirror it:

(5) Anyone who watches a rival carry off a prize they wanted feels a flash of spite and envy, equally before any calculation.

(6) By (2), that flash belongs to human nature too.

(7) So human nature equally contains the beginnings of the vices.

(8) Therefore, by the same license under which the Mencian concluded (4), human nature is bad — which is Xunzi's own thesis.

(9) The Mencian's method thus proves both that nature is good and that nature is bad; a method yielding two incompatible conclusions proves nothing; so (4) is unwarranted.

My consultant says the reply misses and my Xunzian should lose the scene; my editor says it lands and the Mencian should lose. I can't adjudicate. It seems to me the pressure point is either (4) — the slide from "nature contains sprouts of virtue" to "nature is good," which (8) then exploits by parity — or (2), which both characters rely on and which perhaps smuggles in something neither could accept. But I notice that if I read (4) as a claim about what human nature grows into when tended, rather than an inventory of what it contains, the envy parallel seems to lose its bite — and I don't know whether that reading is genuinely Mencius's or a modern kindness done to him.

So: which step, if any, should I have a character reject, and what does rejecting it cost the character who rejects it? If you think the Xunzian's parody is answerable within Mencius's own resources, tell me how the Mencian should respond to the flash of spite at (5) — whether Mencius has somewhere to put an unruly spontaneous feeling without conceding the point. And if you think the parody is fatal, tell me whether Xunzi would even want to be vindicated this way, given that his own case for the badness of nature seems to run through desire, scarcity, and the necessity of ritual (li) rather than through any catalogue of spontaneous feelings.

One further request, since the book will carry an author's note: please keep separate what is settled among scholars of classical Chinese philosophy from what is genuinely contested — for instance, how xing and shan are best rendered, whether the sprout metaphor commits Mencius to a developmental claim about what nature becomes rather than a claim about what it contains, and whether Xunzi's thesis is best read as a direct denial of Mencius or as a different claim about a different subject — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Where the literature has simply not settled a question, say so plainly; I would rather have my characters disagree honestly than have my note pretend there is one right answer.
