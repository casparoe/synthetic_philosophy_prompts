I am organizing an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of practical reason who have been talking past each other for years, and I need the joint statement they should be able to sign at the end.

The first holds that what is good for a person, and what that person has most reason to do, is determined by what she would endorse under idealized conditions: full and vividly appreciated information, correct reasoning, freedom from compulsion and distorting influences. Call this the ideal-advisor view. The second holds that this strategy is caught in a dilemma: either the idealization is kept modest enough that the idealized endorser is still recognizably the same person, in which case it cannot correct her actual, deeply flawed desires and so fails to do any normative work; or the idealization is strong enough to do that work, in which case the resulting preferences belong to a stranger, and the theory is just external-standard theory wearing a procedural mask. Call this the alienation objection.

Draft the document such a collaboration should produce: a numbered section of claims both can sign, stated neutrally enough that neither would balk at any item; a table of the exact residual disagreements, each phrased in wording the side holding it would accept as fair; and the cruxes separating them. I suspect, but want you to verify rather than assume, that at least three genuine cruxes exist: whether the circularity worry about the "correct reasoning" clause is vicious or benign; whether cases of adaptive preference formation, where informed endorsement and actual contentment come apart, are evidence for the ideal-advisor view or against it; and how the theory should handle the fact that different idealization procedures, or even different orderings of the same disclosures, appear to issue in different verdicts for one and the same person. For each crux, specify a case, argument, or thought experiment both parties already agree is probative, and record in advance what each side predicts it would show. If any portion of their dispute strikes you as merely verbal — a fight over who gets the word "welfare" rather than over substance — identify that portion and explain the deflationary reading both could accept, while flagging anything in their disagreement that survives it.
