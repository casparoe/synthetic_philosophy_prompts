I've spent fifteen years in management consulting, first as an analyst and now as an engagement manager, and there's a pattern in my industry I've never seen named. Everyone at my firm is expected to be visibly responsive at all hours: midnight emails answered within the hour, weekends given over to "quick turns," juniors competing to copy the widest distribution list on work nobody will read. This is expensive for every one of us, and — this is the part that puzzles me — it can't be demonstrating anything, because everybody does it. A client can't learn anything about my team from behavior that is uniform across the entire industry, and internally a partner can't infer that one associate is more devoted than another, since all of them perform the same display at the same intensity. Yet the display is compulsory: the handful of people who opt out are marked, and not favorably.

I see the same shape elsewhere. My partner is a postdoc, and in her field nearly everyone referees papers for journals, unpaid and under time pressure; the work is expected of all, performed by most, and, as far as I can tell, read by almost no one as evidence about anyone's quality, since the people who do the most of it are not the people who get the jobs. And at a wedding this summer I watched two families spend sums neither could comfortably afford, each knowing the other was doing the same, and neither able to stop first. What unites these cases, I think, is that a costly display has become universal: the cost is still real and paid by all, but whatever it was once supposed to reveal, it now reveals to nobody — and yet no one can abandon it unilaterally.

I dimly remember that evolutionary biologists have a story about absurdly expensive displays — peacock tails, stag antlers — in which the very wastefulness of the display is what makes it believable: only a genuinely healthy animal can afford to squander resources that way. But in that story the display still varies, and the choosing party still discriminates on the basis of the variation. In my cases the display no longer varies, so there is nothing left to discriminate on; the expense has become a tax everyone pays and no one benefits from. Three questions, then. Does this configuration — a costly display that has saturated, uniform across a population, informative for none, yet still compulsory — have an established name in economics, biology, or sociology? If so, I want to know exactly how the established concept differs from my cases, since the peacock story, and the half-remembered economics claim that university degrees function to display something to employers rather than to teach, both seem to require that the display still separate people, and mine don't. If there is no established name, I'd like you to coin one, define it precisely, and mark out the boundary cases — most importantly the one I suspect holds the key to the whole pattern: when someone opts out, the opting out is itself read as a statement, usually an unflattering one about the person's commitment or taste, even though everyone privately agrees the display is meaningless. Please don't just hand me a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
