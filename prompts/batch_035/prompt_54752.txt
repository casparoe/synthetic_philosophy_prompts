So I'm writing a thesis chapter on the knowledge argument (Frank Jackson's Mary thing) and it's slowly dawning on me that the literature is basically a pile of rival responses -- ability view, old facts under new modes of presentation, phenomenal concept strategy, illusionists just shrugging -- with no agreement anywhere on what a response even has to accomplish. Before I engage any of them I want the spec.

Write the desiderata for an adequate account of what Mary gains, or doesn't gain, when she steps out of the black-and-white room. The standard of adequacy is: good enough to settle whether physicalism is actually in trouble, not just to say something interesting about concepts.

Things I suspect belong on the list but don't know how to weigh:

- It has to give verdicts on the variants, not just textbook Mary. Like the congenital achromatope who ALSO mastered all the color science -- if your story is that Mary gains abilities, did the achromatope have them all along, and if so why does the thought experiment lose its grip on that person? Or amnesiac Mary, who saw red once as a toddler before entering the room and forgot. Or the superblindsighter who can name the red ball every single time but insists there's nothing it's like for them -- whatever residue you say Mary gains, your account has to explain why that patient still seems to lack it.
- It can't be ad hoc. Whatever mechanism it invokes has to be independently motivated and not invented on the spot to save physicalism. This is where I keep feeling the phenomenal concept strategy is on thin ice, but tell me if that's fair.
- It has to explain the pull of the intuition, not just deny it. If the response is that Mary learns nothing, there better be an error theory of why it seems so obvious she does learn something -- and the error theory better not itself presuppose the thing in question.
- It has to keep apart gaining a new concept, gaining acquaintance, and gaining a new fact, and say which one Mary gets and why the other two can't do the work the dualist needs done.
- It must generalize beyond color, since the argument was never really about color. Whatever it says should carry over to every other phenomenal domain, and it should say why the color version convinces people.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Then flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable that's the part I most want to see, since the list would then itself be a result -- and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most responses. Don't do a full scorecard against the rival views, just say which one dies fastest against the list.
