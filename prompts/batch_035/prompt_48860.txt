I am a novelist, currently drafting a book in which two characters — a retired classics professor with Epicurean sympathies and a hospice physician — spend a long night arguing about whether death is bad for the person who dies. As it happens, I have just overheard an argument on almost exactly this question between two friends of mine, and I would like to understand who was right about what before I decide how the fictional version should go. My friends are a philosophy teacher (call him Marcus) and a palliative-care nurse (call her Elena).

Marcus opened with the Epicurean line: death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because a harm requires someone who is worse off, and when death is present, the person is not — there is no time at which the deceased is around to suffer any misfortune. Elena replied that this is a lawyer's trick with tenses: being deprived of forty years of life is a genuine loss, and losses do not require the loser to be present at the time of loss any more than a burglary requires the victim to stand in the empty vault. Marcus then invoked Lucretius's symmetry argument: no one lies awake grieving over the eternity that preceded their birth, and posthumous nonexistence is no different, so the deprivation Elena describes should be as harmless as prenatal nonexistence. Elena answered that time runs one way: our plans, projects, and attachments are aimed at the future, so a future that is taken from us is unlike a past we never had; the asymmetry lies in what we care about, not in the geometry of the two eternities. Marcus countered that if the badness of death rests on what we merely happen to care about, then it dissolves for the person who has made peace with dying — which is, he said, what the Epicureans were after all along: not the bare claim that death is nothing, but that the fear of it can be dismantled. Elena, who has watched hundreds of people die, replied that plenty of her patients are entirely composed about their own deaths and are still being robbed — that being deprived of what one has stopped counting on is still being deprived — and she pointed to slanders published after a person's death, which everyone agrees wrong the dead even though there is no subject around at the time. Marcus's final move concerned the comparison class: to call death bad for someone, we apparently need a counterfactual in which they live on — but which life counts? The one with the extra decades? The one in which they never fell ill at all? If the choice of comparison is that arbitrary, he argued, the alleged badness may be an artifact of the bookkeeping. Elena replied that the same arbitrariness infects every harm we assess counterfactually, including ones no Epicurean denies, such as losing a leg.

They ended amicably but unresolved. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each step, say who has the better of it and why, note where either of them is relying on a premise the other never concedes, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — perhaps something a novelist drafting this scene ought to know that neither raised. Since I will be putting words in characters' mouths on the strength of your judgment, please say how confident you are in each of your rulings and single out the point in their dispute about which you feel least sure.
