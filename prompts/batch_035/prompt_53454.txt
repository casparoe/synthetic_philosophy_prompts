A little background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent most of my career as an epidemiologist, building models of how disease moves through populations, and I retired a couple of years ago at 68. Somewhere in the retirement I caught the philosophy bug, and lately I've been down a rabbit hole on cliodynamics — Peter Turchin's project of treating history the way we treated epidemics, with structural-demographic variables like elite overproduction, wage trends, immiseration of commoners, and his secular cycles of a couple centuries. What really hooked me was his old forecast, published I believe around 2010, that the US and western Europe were heading for a spike of instability in the 2020s — and then, well, the 2020s happened. Now, I spent forty years around forecasts, so I know perfectly well that one hit doesn't validate a model any more than one miss kills it (flu forecasts have made fools of better modelers than me), but I can't stop chewing on whether this is the real thing or an elaborate curve-fit that got lucky. My historian friends mostly roll their eyes and say it's physics envy, which is exactly what the old-timers said about epidemiology before it started working.

Here's the actual ask. I've got, if I'm honest, maybe one good decade of clear-headed work left in me, and I'd like to spend a chunk of it contributing to this rather than just reading about it — I can still do math, I can't read seventeenth-century French archives, so factor that in. Rank the open questions in cliodynamics for someone like me and defend the ordering. Some candidates to get you started: is the theory genuinely predictive or just retrodictive with enough free parameters to fit anything (the "physics envy" worry); is elite overproduction causally upstream of instability or just a correlate that travels along with it; how much contingency at the level of individual events and leaders can coexist with lawlike behavior at the century scale before the laws stop meaning anything; and does any of this transfer from the agrarian states where the cycles were mostly documented to a modern service economy with the opposite demography? Rank those, add ones I haven't thought of, and tell me your criteria — importance, tractability for a math guy who isn't a historian, neglectedness — and where the criteria pull against each other. I care way less about the list than about the reasoning for the ordering, and what evidence in the next few years would move a question up or down.

Two housekeeping requests. First, no reading list and no citations — I've got a library card, what I want is your actual thinking. Second, and this matters to me: flag your confidence as you go, distinguishing what's solid in the literature from what you're extrapolating from a general picture of the field, and name the single claim in your answer you're least sure of. I'd rather have a map with the soft spots marked than a confident one that isn't. Take an extra minute if you need it — the last thing I want to do is spend my retirement decade on a question that was a dead end all along.
