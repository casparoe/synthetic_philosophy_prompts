I'm on the content review board at a mid-sized studio — I'm an engine programmer by trade, not a philosopher, so bear with me. We're deciding whether to approve a system our lead designer prototyped for our next immersive sim. The NPCs in it are persistent: they remember how you've treated them, they flee and beg when cornered, they mourn dead companions and avoid the place where one of them died. The design doc calls it the cruelty system, because the point is that if the player chooses to be cruel the world reacts in a viscerally realistic way, and the designer argues this makes the players' choices actually mean something — the game punishes cruelty through consequences instead of just blocking it. Another board member wants the whole thing cut, saying what we're really building is a machine for selling simulated suffering to people who enjoy inflicting it, and that we'd never greenlight a system whose entire purpose was making a child character scream convincingly. We're split 3–3 and I'm writing the recommendation this cycle.

What keeps tripping me up is that everyone's arguments slide between two different questions. One is whether the NPCs themselves are harmed — our AI guy says obviously not, there's no one home, it's scripts and weights all the way down. But the NPCs pass basically every behavioral test we'd run on a dog, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a reason to take their distress seriously or whether it's just theater that exploits our reflexes, in which case maybe the thing that matters is what it does to the player instead. The other question is whether something bad happens when players treat fake creatures as harmable — the "it trains cruelty" side keeps mixing up an empirical claim about desensitization with a philosophical claim about what kind of person you become by doing it, and I don't know which one is doing the work. And there's a smaller decision attached: our platform currently allows mods, and people have used the mod tools to build what are basically torture sandboxes populated by these NPCs. We can remove mods but we've never articulated a principle for when, so whatever we decide about the main system kind of forces a call on that too.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday: a clear recommendation (approve as designed, approve with conditions, or reject), with the conditions spelled out concretely enough that the design team could actually implement them — things like which behaviors stay, whether there's a distinction between cruelty being possible and cruelty being the marketed point, anything about the mod policy. It should take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than steamrolling it: if your recommendation is that the NPCs can't be harmed, I want to see why that doesn't just collapse into "anything goes as long as its fake," both for the shipped game and for the torture mods. And if your recommendation goes the other way, it should say what work the NPCs' behavior is actually doing, since "it looks sad" can't be the whole story. No need for name-dropping or citations unless it's genuinely load-bearing — argue it out from the situation itself. Take whatever position you actually think is right; the board just needs something rigorous to react to.
