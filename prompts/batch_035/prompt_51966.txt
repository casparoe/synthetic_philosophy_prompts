I run a small film club at the community center where I live, and next month we're screening Inception, which I've seen maybe five times. Years ago, in my twenties, I read Douglas Hofstadter's book Gödel, Escher, Bach more or less for fun, and one image from it has never left me: Escher's 1948 lithograph Drawing Hands, where two hands rise out of a sketchbook page, each one drawing the other's cuff, each apparently creating the other. Hofstadter calls this a tangled hierarchy, and his point (as I remember it) is that the loop isn't actually closed: the two hands may draw each other, but they don't draw the paper they stand on, and neither drew Escher's real hand, which drew them both. There's always an outside level that stays inviolate, untouched by the tangle, and the strange-loop feeling comes from the way the picture tricks you into ignoring it. I've been wondering for a while whether Inception is playing the same game with its nested dreams, and I'd like your help thinking it through before I present it to the group.

On the surface the film looks like the Escher picture: a dream within a dream within a dream, each level's sleepers descending to produce the next, the kicks chaining back upward. But the more I think about it, the less sure I am that this is a genuine tangled hierarchy rather than just a neat stack of boxes, with each box dreaming the box below it and no level reaching back up to draw the one above. In Escher's picture the hierarchy is tangled because each level acts on its neighbor; in the film, the levels seem to interact mostly through sound bleeding down (the Edith Piaf song, the van crashing into the water) — which feels more like one-way plumbing than mutual drawing. And then there's the inviolate level, which is what really keeps me up at night. Hofstadter says the paper and Escher's real hand stand outside the loop, and that no loop can draw its own floor. Does Inception have such a floor — a level that is nobody's dream — and does the film ever let us be certain we've touched it? Cobb's spinning top at the end is presumably the film taunting us with exactly that question, but I also wonder about Mal, who is at once a memory inside Cobb and a projection inside every level he dreams, and about limbo, which is supposedly "just" the deepest shared dream but seems to function like the paper itself — the level with no distinct dreamer beneath it. Is limbo the inviolate level in disguise, or is that a category mistake on my part?

I should say upfront that I'm a retired engineer, not a philosopher, and I've never taken a course on any of this — Hofstadter's book is basically my entire education in the topic, and I may be misremembering or half-inventing parts of what I've attributed to him above. So please be candid with me: if the tangled-hierarchy lens simply doesn't fit the film, or if I've got Hofstadter's point about the inviolate level wrong, or if the whole comparison rests on me being charmed by two superficially similar images, tell me that plainly instead of finding clever ways to make the reading work. What I'd like, if the reading does have legs, is a walkthrough of the specific scenes that support or resist it — the opening Saito interrogation, the dream-collapse in Mombasa, the snow fortress level, the final top — plus an honest note on where I'd be forcing the film into the picture, and a suggestion for the one question I should put to my film club that would get them arguing about it afterward.
