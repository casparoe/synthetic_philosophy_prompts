I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal models of conflict, and we recently covered safe Pareto improvements — the idea that when parties to a bargaining problem are uncertain about their relative strength, they can sometimes commit in advance to an outside option (an arbitration rule, a fixed split, a standing procedure) that makes everyone better off in expectation than the default of fighting it out, precisely because conflict burns surplus no matter who would have prevailed. The canonical illustrations are stylized — delegating to an arbiter in advance of a dispute over an indivisible good, and so on — and I've been trying to test my understanding on a real case. The one I've settled on is the Moscow–Washington hotline, and I'd like you to help me work it through.

Here are the salient facts as I understand them. During the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962, communication between Kennedy and Khrushchev moved through slow diplomatic channels — translating, encrypting, and delivering a message reportedly took hours — so that each side spent agonizing intervals unsure whether the other's latest message reflected current intentions or positions already abandoned. Partly in response, the two governments signed a memorandum of understanding in June 1963 establishing a direct teletype link between the Pentagon and the Kremlin, operational by August of that year. (Yes, teletype, not the red telephone of popular imagination.) The link did not resolve any underlying dispute: the status of Berlin, Cuba, and the arms race were all as unresolved in August 1963 as they had been in October 1962. It is standard to describe the hotline as a crisis-management tool that reduced the risk of accidental or unintended escalation.

So here is what I'd like you to do. First, reconstruct the crisis bargaining game that the hotline was meant to modify: identify the players, the stakes, the default path to war, and the specific role of uncertainty — and be careful here, because I suspect the relevant uncertainty is not the kind the SPI literature usually assumes. SPI theory typically posits uncertainty about relative bargaining power: neither side knows who would win the fight, so both prefer committing to an option that is better than the fight regardless of who is stronger. But in the hotline case, the war the mechanism is meant to avert is not the war of the underlying dispute — it is a war nobody wants, triggered by delay, misread signals, or false alarms. Whether that is a genuine extension of the SPI framework or a different animal entirely (a Pareto improvement over accident risk, not over the cost of fighting) seems to me the crux, and I'd like a careful verdict on it. Second, assess the fit: does the hotline satisfy the safety condition — that is, is it Pareto improving in every state of the world the parties cared about, including states where the link is used for bluster, deception, or last-minute threats? Consider also the possibility of moral hazard: if leaders believe accidental escalation has been made nearly impossible, does that license riskier brinkmanship in the underlying game, eroding or reversing the improvement? Third, say where the framework is silent. Neither government trusted the other, yet both accepted the mechanism without resolving any substantive disagreement; SPI theory, as I understand it, has something to say about why such deals are possible between self-interested parties, but I want to know whether it actually predicts that agreements like this come first and substance later, or whether that ordering is an accident of history the theory doesn't explain. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory — whether it is a clean confirmation, an awkward fit, or a case the framework never anticipated — and, if you think another framework (cheap talk, crisis signaling, organizational routines) does better, say so plainly rather than forcing the SPI reading.
