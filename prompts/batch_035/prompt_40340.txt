I'm a fourth-year PhD student in machine learning, and I'd like your advice on a dispute inside my lab. Three research groups share a compute cluster, with time allocated quarterly by an internal leaderboard that scores models on public benchmarks and demo-day results. Nothing on the leaderboard measures robustness, calibration, or behavior on off-distribution inputs, but everything measures headline performance. So each group spends its allocation doing the cheap thing — training more, evaluating less — because whichever group unilaterally spends a quarter on careful red-teaming and ablations drops in the rankings, loses compute share to the other two, and the more careful systems it would have produced never get the resources to exist. Everyone privately agrees the leaderboard is steering us toward shipping systems none of us would want to be responsible for. Our lab director is sympathetic but says he can't justify changing the allocation rule unless someone hands him a concrete proposal that the groups can't quietly defect from. A few of us have drafted one: a shared evaluation harness through which all results must pass before leaderboard submission, with each batch audited by a rotating pair of members drawn from different groups.

Act as a philosopher trained in decision theory and ethics, and think carefully before answering. My questions: Is our pact likely to hold, or does the structure of the situation all but guarantee it gets undermined — and if the latter, is it still worth trying? Is unilateral restraint here a principled stand, or just unilateral disarmament that changes nothing except which group absorbs the cost? And is there a way to change what the leaderboard itself rewards, so that the individually greedy strategy and the collectively good one converge, rather than anyone needing to be policed? Give me a committed answer: take a definite position on whether to push our proposal, what to push for instead if not, and why — don't lay out the options and leave the choosing to me. Bear in mind I present at our all-hands in two weeks and expect pushback along the lines that the leaderboard is just a measurement, not a mandate, and no one is forced to cut corners. Keep the entire answer to at most 400 words, and if you have to drop something to fit, drop the survey of possible positions rather than the verdict and its reasons.
