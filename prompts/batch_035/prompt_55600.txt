At work we've been arguing about rate limits for our API, and one teammate keeps ending the argument with what he calls the sorites move: "one more request can't make the difference between a healthy system and an overloaded one." Add a single request and nothing changes, so any hard cutoff is arbitrary, and to him arbitrary means unjustifiable — better to have soft signals and human judgment than a hard number. Another guy says that's exactly the heap-of-sand puzzle: it's true that no single grain turns a non-heap into a heap, and yet somewhere between 5 grains and 5 million you definately have a heap, so "no single step matters" and "there is no line" are getting smuggled togther as if they were the same claim.

I know enough from browsing around that this is the sorites paradox and that philosophers have positions like epistemicism (there IS a sharp cutoff, we just can't know where) and supervaluationism (no single cutoff, but it's still determinately true that 3 grains isn't a heap). But I don't want a survey of those. What I want is a menu of the distinct precise things "one more request can't make the difference" could actually mean — a claim about the physics of load, about the meaning of the word "overloaded", about what we can ever know, about what's defensible in a design review — and for each: is it trivial, substantive and plausible, or just false? And crucially, does that reading actually support my teammate's conclusion that we shouldn't set hard limits at all, or does it only support something weaker, like "don't pretend your exact number is principled"?

Add a top-level field "summary" with the bottom line I can bring to the design review.
