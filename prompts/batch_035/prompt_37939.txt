As a PhD student in epistemology, I have spent the last two years convening a departmental working group on the ethics of belief, in which two senior members — one a strict evidentialist working in the tradition of Clifford's 1877 essay The Ethics of Belief, the other a pragmatist in the tradition of James's 1896 lecture The Will to Believe — have argued past each other with steadily diminishing returns. We have now agreed to run a structured adversarial collaboration, and the drafting of the joint statement has fallen to me. I would like you to write that document.

For orientation, and stated as each camp would state itself: the Cliffordian holds that it is wrong, always and for anyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence — that even a privately held, unacted-upon belief without adequate evidence is a wrong, both because belief inevitably leaks into action and because every such belief corrodes our collective habits of inquiry. His touchstones are the shipowner who stifles his doubts about an unseaworthy vessel and drowns a family of emigrants, and the agitator who raises a false accusation against a religious minority on the strength of his own sincerity. The Jamesian holds that where a genuine option between hypotheses is live, forced, and momentous, and where evidence and argument are in principle unable to settle the question in advance, we are entitled — sometimes obliged — to let our passional nature decide, and that in such cases declining to decide is itself a way of deciding. The two have never even managed to agree on a neutral statement of what divides them, which is precisely the problem the joint statement is meant to solve.

Please structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both sides can sign without wincing. This should include what each takes Clifford's examples to show (both should be able to concede the shipowner is a genuine wrong, but the drafter must find wording on why he is a wrong that does not quietly commit the Jamesian to the thesis that all unevidenced belief is wrong). It should also cover the points of overlap neither side usually advertises: that evidence matters, that wishful thinking is a real and common failure, that the burden of proof lies somewhere, and whatever else you can extract that is neither vacuous nor slanted. A claim only counts as agreed if it is one the other side could not have used as a premise against its signer.

Second, a disagreements table, with one row per residual disagreement, each phrased twice: once in wording the Cliffordian would accept as a fair statement of the evidentialist position, and once in wording the Jamesian would accept as a fair statement of the pragmatist position. The two phrasings should be recognizably about the same question, but not so laundered that the disagreement dissolves. Candidate rows include: whether unevidenced belief can ever be permissible (not merely excusable) when the belief is partly self-fulfilling; whether there is a genuine category of options that are live, forced, and momentous in James's sense, or whether that category, once made precise, turns out to be nearly empty; and whether the wrongness of the shipowner's belief attaches to the belief itself or only to the foreseeable downstream action.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux, identify a concrete case both sides would accept as genuinely bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. To keep this from collapsing into the standard textbook exchange, the cases should be drawn from the following set, which I have constructed for the collaboration:

1. The climber, hanging at a stance she cannot leave before dark, needs to commit to a move she can execute only if confident; the evidence on her ability at this exact move is a coin flip, and her belief about herself causally feeds the execution. Does she wrong herself or anyone by deciding to believe she can do the move?
2. The referee, who has a persistent hunch that a celebrated published result is wrong, with no evidence yet; the hunch is what motivates the months of checking that would either vindicate or embarrass him. Both sides presumably praise the checking — but was the hunch itself permissible?
3. The friend, who continues to believe in an old friend's innocence of a serious charge after the public balance of evidence has shifted against him, and whose ongoing belief is what sustains the only relationship keeping the accused from isolation.
4. The physician, who judges that a placebo will help a patient only if the patient genuinely believes in it, and who therefore has an interest in bringing that belief about — with the further wrinkle that the belief may causally produce the improvement that then confirms it.
5. The doctoral student, deciding, on no independent evidence, that she is the kind of person who can finish this dissertation, in circumstances where the belief is what keeps her at the desk and its absence is what would take her away.
6. The agitator, Clifford's own case, included as a boundary marker both sides should be able to locate.

The cruxes should be formulated so that settling one of them would move at least one side, and at least one crux must be one on which the two sides' written predictions genuinely diverge — if the predictions come out identical for every crux, the collaboration has failed, and you should say so rather than manufacture a divergence.

Finally, a section on which parts of the dispute are verbal. My working hypothesis, which you may overturn, is that at least one disagreement dissolves once we distinguish belief proper from something like confident hope or self-trust, and that the Jamesian wins some of the six cases only if what gets formed in them is not belief but a neighboring attitude. If you find that a residual dispute turns on the question of doxastic voluntarism — whether and in what sense we can decide what to believe at all — say how the joint statement should register that fact, given that both participants accept that direct voluntary control is absent while disagreeing about indirect control.

Tone: formal, unhurried, and scrupulously even. The document should be one both parties could put their names to at a departmental seminar, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than blurred.
