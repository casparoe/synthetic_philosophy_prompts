I teach an intro ethics course, and every year we hit the Kant unit and students discover that Kant apparently thought you do no wrong *to* a dog when you kick it — you only wrong humanity by damaging your own character. Half the class finds that monstrous and the other half finds it weirdly fascinating, and I want to stop arguing about labels with them and instead sort them by what they actually think. So: please write a "which view on Kant and animals do you actually hold?" quiz for my students.

Some ground rules. Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, and no question may mention Kant, duties, rights, or any philosopher by name — the students are mostly pre-vet and pre-med and will tune out instantly if it reads like a reading quiz. The cases have to be ones where the positions genuinely come apart, and I want at least these four in there somewhere: (1) the old faithful dog who can't work anymore — shooting him promptly, is anything wronged besides our own character, and by whom; (2) the hermit who is vicious to animals but demonstrably kind and just to every human he ever meets, has done wrong or not; (3) a case where the object has no mind at all, like desecrating a corpse or smashing up beautiful rock formations, since that's meant to be the purest test of whether the wrongness is really all about vice-formation with no victim needed; and (4) a forced choice between a being that resembles humans a lot but feels less (say a chimp with muted affect) and one that resembles us barely at all but seems to feel more — that one should separate people whose intuitions track resemblance/analogy from people whose intuitions track sentience.

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves must include: the classic indirect-duty reading (all wrongness is damage to our own moral character, full stop); the stronger "animals as analogues of humanity" reading that some Kant scholars defend, where the duties go beyond just not-being-cruel; the textual revisionist view that Kant's actual remarks already leave room for something like direct duties to animals; Korsgaard's Fellow Creatures reconstruction where the reasons come from our own standpoint but attach to animals directly; a contractualist leaf where animals fall outside justice entirely; and at least two non-Kantian escape hatches (Singer-style, Regan-style) so the students who just want to abandon the Kantian framework have somewhere to land rather than getting mislabeled.

Two more asks. First, build the tree from the logical space — figure out what the diagnostic questions are first, locate the actual positions in it, and then flag any answer-combination your key can't distinguish (I'd bet the Korsgaard leaf and the analogues leaf overlap badly on my case list — if so, say what question would fix it, and add it). And note any combination that's logically available but nobody holds, with your best guess at why. Second, keep the whole thing in American English please, including any answer option text — a couple of my students are exchange students and I don't want to grade against their spellings.
