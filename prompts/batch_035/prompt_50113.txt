I am grading an exam for an advanced course on formal epistemology, and I would like a careful evaluation of one of the answers. Below I reproduce the exam question, followed by the student's answer. Please assign a numerical grade out of 100 and justify it with a point-by-point assessment of the answer: credit what is correct, and identify and explain each mistake, including any subtle ones that a hasty reading might let slide (steps that do not follow, conflations, misapplied calculations). Please be precise about which claims in which parts of the answer are at issue.

Exam question:

"Sleeping Beauty volunteers for an experiment whose details she is told in advance. On Sunday night she is put to sleep, and a fair coin is tossed. If it lands Heads, she is woken and interviewed on Monday only. If it lands Tails, she is woken and interviewed on Monday and again on Tuesday. After each interview she is given a drug that erases her memory of it, so on any waking she cannot tell which day it is or whether she has been woken before. During each interview she is asked for her credence that the coin landed Heads.

(a) State the halfer and thirder answers, and briefly reconstruct one standard argument for each.

(b) Some thirders argue as follows: on any waking, Beauty's total evidence is compatible with exactly three centered possibilities — (Heads, Monday), (Tails, Monday), and (Tails, Tuesday) — and her evidence does not favor any one of these over the others, so each should receive credence 1/3. Assess the force of (i) this indifference-style thirder premise and (ii) the standard halfer reply that waking gives Beauty no new information.

(c) Now modify the experiment as follows: on each day of the experiment, independently of the main coin and independently of the day, the experimenters toss a second fair coin to determine the color of the walls of the room in which Beauty wakes that day (red or blue, each with probability 1/2). Beauty is told about this arrangement in advance. She wakes, sees that the walls are red, and is asked for her credence in Heads. What credence should a halfer assign upon seeing red walls, and what credence should a thirder assign? Show how you arrive at your answers."

Student's answer:

"(a) The halfer says Beauty's credence in Heads on any waking should be 1/2; the thirder says 1/3. The thirder's argument: if Beauty were told the coin landed Tails, she could not tell Monday from Tuesday, so P(Monday | Tails) = P(Tuesday | Tails) = 1/2; and if she were told it is Monday, the fairness of the coin gives P(Heads | Monday) = P(Tails | Monday). These two steps together make the three centered possibilities (Heads, Monday), (Tails, Monday), and (Tails, Tuesday) equally likely, so each gets probability 1/3, and hence P(Heads) = 1/3. The halfer's argument: Beauty's credence on Sunday is 1/2, and waking gives her no information she did not already have, since she knew all along that she would be woken at least once; so her credence should remain 1/2. One corollary of halving is that P(Tuesday) = 1/4, and some halfers — 'double-halfers' — even hold that Beauty's credence in Heads remains 1/2 when she is told it is Monday.

(b) The halfer is right that there is no new information in the ordinary sense: nothing about the coin's outcome or about which day it is becomes known to Beauty when she wakes, since she knew in advance every detail of what would happen. And the thirder's indifference premise just is the assumption that the three centered possibilities are equally likely, which is simply thirding itself stated as a premise, so it begs the question. On the other hand, the Dutch book argument settles the dispute: if Beauty is offered the same bet on Heads each time she is woken, and pays or collects on each occasion, then a halfer who prices the bet at 1/2 is exploitable, whereas the 1/3 price breaks even. So a rational agent should be a thirder.

(c) The red walls change the halfer's position. The halfer's whole case was that there is no new information; but now Beauty plainly does receive new information — the color of the walls — so the halfer's central premise fails, and the halfer must move toward the thirder answer. Computing: P(Heads and red walls) = 1/4 and P(red walls) = 1/2, so by Bayes' theorem P(Heads | red walls) = 1/3. The thirder, meanwhile, already assigns 1/3 and stays there, since the walls are independent of the coin. So after seeing the red walls, the two positions converge on 1/3."
