I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, working on a paper about moral compromise. The specific idea I'm defending is that when two parties hold systematically different value systems, they can sometimes achieve something structurally analogous to Pareto improvements: each concedes on an issue it weights less, in exchange for concessions on an issue it weights more, so both end up with more of what they care about by their own lights — gains from trade, but between moral systems rather than within one. I want to stress-test my defense of this idea before I send the paper to my advisor, so I need a serious opponent, not a strawman.

Please role-play a committed critic of moral compromise: a sharp, fair professor of moral philosophy who will concede small points but never the main thesis. Her core position is that the gains-from-trade framing smuggles in a false picture of value. Preferences, she'll argue, can be traded because they attach to outcomes and can be aggregated within a person; values are not like that. A deontologist who accepts a slightly smaller rights violation in exchange for a somewhat larger welfare gain has not gotten *more* of anything she cares about, because what she cares about is not a quantity of rights-protection that can be increased or decreased, but the standing of a constraint that either binds or does not. On her view, when two value systems apparently trade, what actually happens is that one or both parties abandon part of their view for non-moral reasons — stability, peace, fatigue — and the rhetoric of rational exchange launders capitulation as consistency. Give her real resources: the distinction between compromising interests (which she accepts as ordinary and often admirable) and compromising principles; the claim that one system's moral outputs cannot be priced from outside that system without already adopting further moral commitments — the currency problem; and the observation that a settlement can be all-things-considered good while the reasoning that produced it remains a bad way to reason. She is not a fanatic: she accepts that politics would be impossible without compromise and that it is often the right thing to do given the world as it is. Her claim is the precise one that compromise is justified as a tragic necessity and never as a source of moral improvement — the metaphor of trade is a conceptual error even when the resulting settlement is good.

Stay in character until I write stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph — conversational and precise, not a lecture. If one of my arguments is ambiguous, ask me which version I mean rather than answering a stronger one I didn't give.

Here is my opening move. Your currency problem cuts both ways. You say there is no common measure across value systems, but your own explanation of why compromise is sometimes the sad necessity presupposes exactly such a measure: to conclude that I ought to compromise for the sake of peace and stability, you have to weigh the value of peace and stability against the value of keeping my principles intact — and if you can make that comparison well enough to conclude that compromise is sometimes obligatory, you have already granted that the deliverances of different value systems can be weighed against one another. So the disagreement between us is not about whether cross-system comparison is possible; it is only about who does the weighing and when. And once that much is on the table, the gains-from-trade view is simply asking the natural next question: if the weighing is happening anyway, why refuse the improvements it reveals?
