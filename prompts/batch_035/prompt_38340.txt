I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so forgive me if I use the wrong words for things. At the office a colleague and I run a small giving circle, and last week we were choosing between two donation targets. I argued for a direct cash-transfer organization: give poor households money and let them decide what improves their lives, whether that's a tin roof, school fees, or starting a small business. My colleague argued for an organization funding treatment for severe chronic pain in places with almost no palliative care. His clincher, delivered like a function he'd called a hundred times, was that 'suffering matters more than happiness' — so relief of suffering beats the creation of well-being, and the cash-transfer cause is out.

The slogan has stuck with me because I think he was using one string to call several different functions. My instinct as an engineer is to treat 'suffering matters more than happiness' like an overloaded API: it compiles everywhere, but each call site may be binding different semantics. As far as I can tell it could be:

(1) a claim about magnitudes — that an episode of suffering counts against an episode of happiness with unequal weight, so the two are aggregated on different scales;
(2) a claim about lexical priority — that no amount of happiness can compensate for any amount of suffering, so even mild suffering trumps enormous joy;
(3) a claim about creation versus relief — that we have a duty to prevent suffering but no corresponding duty to create happiness, which seems to be about our obligations rather than about how much each thing is worth;
(4) a claim about the actual world — that given how much suffering there is and how cheap some of it is to relieve, relieving it happens to do more good per dollar, which is empirical and could change if the world did;
(5) a claim about worst cases — that we should rank options by their worst outcome rather than their average, which is about decision-making under uncertainty rather than about the value of the outcomes themselves.

Please treat my list as a first pass: sharpen the readings where I've muddled them, add ones I've missed, and for each reading say whether it's plausible, whether it's trivially true, or whether it has implications almost no one would accept — my colleague, for instance, would not endorse painlessly switching off all life to eliminate suffering forever, and I understand some strong versions of this family of views run into exactly that problem. Then, crucially, tell me which of the readings, if any, actually support his conclusion that the pain-treatment charity beats the cash-transfer charity. My suspicion is that on the weaker readings the slogan is true but doesn't settle our donation, and on the stronger readings it settles the donation but is far from obviously true — but I'd like that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.

Two further requests. First, flag explicitly where any of the readings depends on contested assumptions about how to compare different people's experiences, since my colleague keeps sliding between 'her headache' and 'headaches in aggregate' as though the sum were well-defined; if the aggregation itself is the shaky part, I want to know which readings inherit the shakiness. Second, please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts and single out the point in your answer you're least sure about — I'd rather have calibrated uncertainty than a clean line.
