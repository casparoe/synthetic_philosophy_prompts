Last year I did a school exchange in Lyon, and I became close friends with a classmate there named Camille. Our civics class argued about laïcité almost weekly, so over the year I built up a fairly detailed record of her verdicts on particular cases rather than just her slogans. She supports the rule that stops teachers in French public schools from wearing conspicuous religious symbols — headscarves, large crosses, kippahs — and once told me that the point of it is that a teacher is supposed to be on every student's side, not on God's. But she opposes extending that rule to students themselves. She thinks the nationwide ban on full-face veils worn in the street goes too far. She was indignant when she read about a supermarket that fired a cashier for wearing a headscarf. She defends the Christmas nativity scene in her town hall on the grounds that it is heritage rather than religion. And she sides with a Sikh man who wanted to ride his motorcycle wearing a turban instead of a helmet. She insists her position is not simply whatever French law says — she disagrees with French law at least twice in that list — but whenever I press her for the underlying principle, what comes back is a slogan about the Republic staying neutral, not about people.

Now that I am back home, I keep encountering new cases and wondering what Camille would say about them. Three in particular: first, our school wants to bar a mother who volunteers to chaperone class trips from wearing her headscarf, on the ground that a chaperone in some sense represents the school; second, our town hall uses a private cleaning contractor, and one of its cleaners wears a headscarf while working in the building, and some councillors want her reassigned elsewhere; third, a private driving school's examiner wears a kippah while testing students who have paid for the exam and cannot simply walk out. What would Camille say about each of these? More importantly, what rule or rules could actually generate all of her earlier verdicts, which of those rules fits her best and why, and what further case would most cleanly distinguish between the candidate rules?

I would like you to be honest if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer rather than forcing a single reading onto her, and to flag where each candidate rule needs something ad hoc to survive one of her verdicts. Also, since I may present this in class: please keep separate what is basically common ground among people who debate secularism and multiculturalism — say, the bare distinction between the state being neutral and private citizens being free — from what is genuinely contested even among people who know the topic well, so that I don't present a contested interpretation as the settled one, or present Camille's view as simply "the French view" when the French quite plainly disagree among themselves.
