You are a philosopher of economics and an experienced university lecturer, and I'd like your help planning a teaching session rather than a summary of its content. Think carefully and, before committing to a plan, brainstorm several possible orderings of the material and explain why you settle on the one you do.

Context: I teach a second-year seminar for economics and philosophy joint-honours students. They have had one term of probability theory, so they can handle expected values and priors, but they have never read Keynes on probability and know him only as the macroeconomist from a first-year textbook paragraph. Next week I have one fifty-minute session to introduce his views on probability and uncertainty, and the session must leave them with the threefold distinction between (a) a proposition's probability relative to some evidence, (b) the weight of that evidence — how much of the relevant material it captures, whether or not the probability number moves — and (c) genuine uncertainty, where the idea of a probability relative to the evidence we actually have barely gets a grip at all. The students' default frame, drilled in by their stats course, is: everything is either a known probability or an unknown probability you should estimate and then act on anyway.

Please plan the session's explanatory arc, not deliver the explanation itself:

1. **Ordering and dependencies.** What has to come first, and what depends on what? In particular, decide whether the probability/weight distinction should precede or follow the weight/uncertainty distinction, and justify the sequence — my worry is that if I start with uncertainty proper, they file it under "we can't know the future," and if I start with probability, they think we've never left the stats course.

2. **The carrying example.** Design the single concrete scenario the whole session should be built around. It must let probability and weight come apart (evidence can grow while the probability stays the same), and it must also let weight and uncertainty come apart (there must be a point at which no amount of ordinary evidence-gathering produces anything that behaves like a probability). Then assess the candidates I've already considered and rejected or hesitated over: repeated coin flips (too clean — probability is all there is), tomorrow's weather (they'll insist meteorology has a number), and long-run election forecasts (invites a slide into current politics I don't want). Explain what your chosen example has and lacks, and why it beats these.

3. **The misconception map.** List the misconceptions they will predictably form, in the order they will form them, and place each at the point in the sequence where I should head it off rather than after it has hardened. I can already predict some: that "uncertainty" just means a low probability; that Keynes is claiming we can never quantify anything, so it's a license for vibes; that the fix is simply to assign a diffuse prior over the unknowns (I want them to see that this is a substantive epistemic commitment, not a neutral default); and, from the sharper students, the slide into "so expected utility maximization is refuted and irrationality is fine." Add whatever else you'd expect, including ones specific to the weight-of-evidence notion, which I suspect they will silently convert into "confidence in your estimate."

4. **The boundary of the session.** What should I openly decline to explain at this level and say so: the formal machinery, the differences between Keynes's position and Knight's, whatever later debates this feeds into. Flag where the temptation to overreach will arise.

5. **The check.** Give me one question — a genuinely diagnostic one, not a quiz-style recall item — I can pose in the last five minutes that would distinguish a student who has absorbed the three-part distinction from one who has merely learned to say the words. Ideally it should be a question where the uncomprehending student's answer sounds sophisticated but conflates two of the three notions.

Where you make a judgment call, show the reasoning behind it; where the secondary literature would disagree with my framing above, note the disagreement briefly so I'm not blindsided, but do not send me to readings — I want your reconstruction and your plan, not a bibliography.
