I'm a science journalist putting together a feature on decision theory for a general audience, and I've gotten myself tangled up on what one of my sources calls the "weird pair": counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. As I understand it, in counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin before showing up, and it only visits you if the coin lands tails, asking for $100 — but had it landed heads, you'd have been handed $10,000, conditional on Omega having predicted you're the kind of person who pays in the tails world. The coin comes up tails, and the fashionable answer among the updateless crowd is that you hand over the $100 even though the coin is already flipped and the money is just... gone. Meanwhile in XOR blackmail, someone has arranged things so you only receive their threatening letter if the embarrassing photos DON'T exist (something about the letter and the photos being exclusive), and the same crowd says you refuse to pay, even though paying would be good news about your situation, and the letter is sitting right there in front of you looking very threatening.

Here's where I've gotten stuck, and it's basically going to be the framing of my article unless you tell me it's wrong. Both cases seem to run on the same machinery: someone who modeled you in advance is demanding money NOW for a state of the world that's already fixed, and the recommended action is determined by some spooky correlation between your decision and that fixed state. If that's really all there is to it, then it looks like anyone who can predict me reasonably well — my editor, a blackmailer with a good psych profile, whoever — can just reach into my wallet by making their demand depend on some fact about me. So the question I was planning to build the piece around is: why isn't a decision theory that pays Omega just a theory that's permanently exploitable by anyone with a decent model of you?

Is that actually the right question to ask, or have I misdescribed what's going on somewhere? Specifically I'd want to know: what exactly is doing the work that makes these two cases get opposite verdicts (pay in one, refuse in the other), and does that thing rule out the "anyone who can predict me can rob me" conclusion, or does it just... not? I've got a draft intro that leans pretty hard on the "you're hostage to anyone who knows you well enough" angle, and I'd rather find out now than from the fact-checker. Also happy to be told my descriptions of the two scenarios above are subtly off — definately better to know before print.
