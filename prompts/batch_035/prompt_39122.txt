I teach a third-year seminar on corruption and governance — 18 students, one 90-minute session. Design a role-play where they feel for themselves why "what counts as corruption" is actually contested, not just sloppily defined. Here's roughly the shape I want, but you fill in everything:

The class is split between (a) a village committee in a fictional region where nothing official happens without accompanying gestures — a gift when you arrive, food when you negotiate, a "sitting fee" for people who gave up a day to attend, favors owed among families that go back decades; and (b) staff of a foreign NGO that has a grant for repairing the village school, who carry a compliance checklist requiring itemized receipts and a blanket no-gifts clause. Maybe a district official in the middle. Then run it in two rounds: round one under normal rules, where they discover they literally cannot schedule the first meeting without either violating local norms or writing something on the form that reads like a bribe; then round two, after HQ sends an auditor with a zero-tolerance policy, so the choice becomes: a formally clean failed project, or a successful school with fudged paperwork.

Give it a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money but makes the conflict real — project points vs. compliance points, designed so you can't max both. Private role sheets with actual objectives (the committee chair loses standing if she hosts a meeting with no food; the auditor gets fired if a violation slips through and gets flagged later). Maybe a third round where a rival NGO shows up that quietly tolerates local norms and tries to poach the village — I want them to feel the enforcement problem, not just the definitional one.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the real distinctions — whether the chair even holds "public office" in the relevant sense, diffuse reciprocity vs. a specific quid pro quo, clientelism as particularistic-but-legal distribution, the fact that gifts create open-ended obligation while bribes close a transaction, and whether the whole framework is a Western export or genuinely universal. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume half of them will just play caricatures), and be honest about which part of the real issue the role-play can't reproduce no matter how I set it up.
