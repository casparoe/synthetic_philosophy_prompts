I've basically talked myself into strong longtermism -- the view that positively shaping the very long run of the future is a top moral priority, because the fraction of people who could ever exist who exist now is tiny, the stakes are astronomically asymmetric, and there's no good reason to discount lives just because they're later. I mostly got there via the "time of perils" idea: we might be in a short window where existential risk is high but where we could also lock in good stuff permanently.

Lay out what would actually count against this. Not the lazy version -- I mean the real load-bearing stuff:

1. The person-affecting constraint. If morality is only about making people better off, not about creating happy people, then an empty future isn't a tragedy and the astronomical stakes argument collapses. How bad is this for my view really? Can longtermists just bite the bullet, or is there something the view needs to say about, like, someone whose life is prevented by extinction who would've been glad to exist?

2. Cluelessness. The claim that our actions' ripple effects wash out or become unpredictable over centuries, so "shaping the long term" isn't actually something we can do, just something we can tell stories about. Is this fatal or just humbling? What would it take for the longtermist to have genuine rather than imagined leverage?

3. The sign of the future. If you think the far future is as likely net negative as net positive (s-risks, permanent dystopia, whatever), then reducing existential risk stops being obviously good. How much uncertainty here can my view tolerate before it flips into "x-risk reduction might be actively bad"?

4. The empirical crux I'm least sure about: how plastic is the future? If values/technology/institutions drift and correct continuously, there's no lock-in and "shaping" is mostly fantasy. If there are one-way doors (unaligned AI, stable totalitarianism, value lock-in), longtermism gets its grip. What observations would tell me which world I'm in?

Rank these defeaters from would-actually-make-me-drop-the-view to merely-uncomfortable. And tell me honestly: which of these would a serious longtermist concede as counting against them, versus wave off with a redefinition? I get the feeling the view survives a lot by quietly weakening from "the future dominates the moral calculus" to "the future matters somewhat" -- and I want to know if I'm holding the strong version or have already talked myself into the weak one without noticing.
