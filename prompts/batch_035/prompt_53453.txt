My mother is recovering from a stroke, and last week a capacity assessor was brought in to determine whether she may still decide for herself where she lives. My brother, a second-year law student, has since produced an argument that I find hard to fault but impossible to accept:

(1) Her abilities to understand and weigh information are returning by imperceptible degrees, so that no single day's improvement ever takes her from unable to able.
(2) Whether she has capacity is settled entirely by whether she has those abilities: the statutory test simply paraphrases them — can she understand, retain, use or weigh the relevant information — it does not sharpen them.
(3) So either no day's improvement ever crosses the threshold, in which case she never regains capacity, or some particular day's improvement does cross it, and that one imperceptible increment makes all the difference between two imperceptibly different states.
(4) Since no imperceptible difference can ground so momentous a difference, there is no fact of the matter whether she has capacity; the assessment was an exercise in fiction.
(5) And what goes for capacity goes for every threshold laid across a gradual property — adulthood, criminal responsibility, even death — so none of these categories ever states a fact.

I can accept that she might be a borderline case. I cannot accept (5). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I will say, in fairness to my brother, that for some of his examples the law simply stipulates an age, and stipulation seems to create a fact where none was before — but I do not see how that rescue is available for the capacity test itself, which is couched in the very gradual words that caused the trouble. For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (1). Please answer in British English.
