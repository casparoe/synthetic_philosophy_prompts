I'm a second-year PhD student preparing a dissertation prospectus, with roughly eighteen months of research time, and I'd like help turning a half-formed idea into a proper proposal.

The idea concerns the debate over whether the world has become less violent. The standard affirmative argument leans on per-capita death rates from war and homicide, but that conclusion is hostage to two choices that are usually made implicitly: what counts as a death from violence at all (versus, say, deaths from neglect or structural deprivation), and what the denominator should be. Per-capita figures normalize by population, but population is not the only natural denominator. One could normalize by person-hours of life, since longevity has risen so much that falling per-capita death rates coexist with more total violent deaths per person born. One could normalize by potentially violent contact: urbanization packs more strangers within one another's reach than at any earlier point, so even a constant per-capita rate would mean violence per encounter has fallen. One could even normalize by capability: a modern state with surveillance and precision weapons can abstain from violence more cheaply than a medieval one could, so perhaps it deserves less credit for abstaining. My suspicion is that some of these normalizations preserve the decline verdict and others reverse it, and that much of the residual disagreement between declinists and their critics is a disguised disagreement about which denominator is normatively relevant, not an empirical dispute at all.

So my question is twofold: (a) is there a principled way to choose among denominators, or is the choice irreducibly evaluative; and (b) if it is irreducibly evaluative, does the question of whether the world has become less violent retain a fact of the matter, or does it become merely verbal in the way that some philosophers claim certain disputes are?

Please write the prospectus with a precise statement of the research question; an account of why the existing positions leave it open (describe the positions rather than citing them, since I will fill in references myself); a month-by-month method section explaining what I would actually do, including which historical comparisons I would need and how I would handle the fact that some normalizations require data that barely exists before 1800; the possible conclusions and the considerations that would decide among them; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing or needing to be scaled down to fit the time available.
