I teach a philosophy of science seminar to 18 second-year undergraduates; we have one 90-minute session on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. Design an in-class simulation in which students experience the protective belt from the inside rather than hearing about it. The idea is that they run rival "programmes" against a stream of anomalous data cards that I deal out, and they must choose each round between adjusting auxiliary assumptions to absorb an anomaly, standing firm, or attempting a novel prediction that earns points only if it comes up before the facilitator reveals the next data card. Please specify rules, materials, a scoring system, and a round structure that fits the time.

One requirement I care about most: the scoring should make it possible — perhaps even likely — for a group whose programme has become clearly degenerating (post-hoc adjustment after post-hoc adjustment) to win the game anyway, because that will set up the debrief question of whether Lakatos's progressive/degenerating distinction is meant to guide scientists in the moment or only to let later historians rationally reconstruct what happened. The debrief should map specific moments in the simulation onto the hard core, the protective belt, and novel prediction, and should also ask the students to consider the comeback case — whether a degenerating programme that later stages a recovery (say, after its rivals founder on an anomaly none of them can absorb) is a counterexample to treating degeneration as a reason to defect, or exactly what Lakatos's "rationally reconstructed" history of science was supposed to accommodate.

Also include: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with fixes, and an honest note on which feature of real scientific practice the simulation cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by fiat. Keep the whole plan — rules, materials, scoring, timeline, debrief, pitfalls — under 900 words.
