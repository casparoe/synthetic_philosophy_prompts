I'm a journalist — I mostly write long-form pieces on science and ideas — and a few of us have been running a monthly workshop for our junior reporters on how we actually know the things we print. The theme this year is basically epistemology smuggled in as newsroom craft, and next session I want to tackle the regress of justification: the fact that when you give a reason for a claim, that reason is itself a claim that can be asked for a reason, and so on — until at some point you either loop back on yourself, keep digging forever, or just stop somewhere and declare it bedrock. Reporters run into this constantly (every source is itself a claim about the world), but we never name it, so I want them to feel it in there bones before I put any philosophy labels on it.

Logistics: about 12 participants, 90 minutes, a room with a few tables, a whiteboard, and nothing else — no laptops, no phones. My half-formed idea is some kind of sourcing game: each reporter gets a claim they're trying to "publish," and the standing rule is you can't publish anything unsourced — but then a rival desk or an editor character gets to challenge the source itself as just another claim in need of sourcing. There should be a hard filing deadline so that the keep-digging-forever strategy visibly fails on its own, and maybe some way for the loop move (two sources that just cite each other) to become available and then get caught. But I genuinely haven't worked out payoffs, rounds, what materials to print, or how to stop it feeling like a hazing ritual instead of a game.

So design the whole thing for me: rules, roles, a timeline, a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, and what "winning" even means here. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened in the room onto the three standard escape routes — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism — including which players ended up playing which one without knowing. And two honest notes at the end: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and which features of the real regress problem a 90-minute game with a clock structurally cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself in the debrief.
