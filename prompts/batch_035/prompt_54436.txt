I serve on the grants committee of a mid-sized family foundation, and at our December meeting we must decide how to split an unexpected bequest of roughly $200 million between two buckets: near-term global health and development programs whose outcomes would be measurable within ten to fifteen years, and long-horizon grants aimed at reducing catastrophic and existential risks, whose benefits, if any, would accrue mostly after 2100. The sticking point turns out to be philosophical. One trustee, a retired cosmologist, argues that the doomsday argument should tilt the entire allocation toward the near term. As he presents it: treat your own birth rank among all the humans who will ever live as a randomly drawn sample (what philosophers call the self-sampling assumption), note that something over one hundred billion humans have already lived, and conclude that unless our birth rank is wildly untypical, the total number of humans who will ever live is unlikely to be vastly larger than the number who have lived already — in which case the long future on which our second bucket's case rests is probably just not there to spend on. A second trustee counters that this reasoning is self-undermining: if you instead adopt the self-indication assumption, which holds that you should also take into account the fact that you exist at all (making worlds with more observers more likely to contain someone like you), the doomsday conclusion largely dissolves. A third trustee keeps returning to the reference class: by "humans who will ever live" do we mean anatomically modern Homo sapiens, everyone capable of experience, or eventually even digital minds — and doesn't that choice determine the whole calculation, with no principled way to settle it? She adds that Richard Gott's cruder version, on which a phenomenon's future duration should be expected to be on the same order as its past duration, seems independently to support discounting very long horizons.

Please draft the committee's decision memo for circulation before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete allocation, with actual numbers rather than a call for balance, and the reasoning should be laid out so that a trustee who is not a philosopher could follow it and, if she disagreed, know exactly where to push back. It must give an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses: if you recommend mostly long-horizon grants, the self-indication response and the reference-class indeterminacy have to do real argumentative work rather than serve as a fog the cosmologist can't answer; if you side with him, the dissolving arguments need to be met rather than waved off. You should also address a suggestion from one of our program staff — that even if the argument is sound, it cannot give the foundation a special reason to shift its portfolio, since it would bear equally on every long-term plan anyone holds, from treaty commitments to our own pension obligations — and close with a short section on what developments over the next decade, if any, should make the committee revisit the allocation.
