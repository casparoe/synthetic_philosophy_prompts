I'm writing to ask for your help diagnosing an argument that has been disturbing me for a week, and I'd like you to take on the role of a senior philosopher working in ethics and moral psychology — someone comfortable with Susan Wolf's essay "Moral Saints" and with the literature on the demandingness of morality — and to work through it with the patience and precision of a careful examiner.

The argument was given to me by my housemate, who has been reading Wolf. He took from her essay a lesson that I believe proves far too much, and I cannot locate the flaw. Here is his argument as he delivered it:

(1) The morally better a person is, the more of her life she must give over to moral ends: becoming a better person just is redirecting time, attention, and emotional energy away from her own nonmoral projects and toward helping others and doing what is right.
(2) The more of a life is given over to moral ends, the less of it is available for nonmoral goods — wit, style, craft, play, and the love of particular people and places for their own sakes.
(3) Nonmoral goods of that kind are genuine goods of a human life; a life with no room for them is worse, other things equal, than a life with room for them.
(4) So moral goodness and nonmoral goodness trade off against each other, unit for unit, within a fixed budget of time and attention.
(5) Whenever two genuine goods trade off monotonically against each other, the best life contains strictly less than the maximum of each; a life that maxes out one is worse than it could have been.
(6) Therefore the best life contains less moral goodness than its bearer could have achieved — all things considered, it is better to be somewhat morally worse than you could be.
(7) And, he added, if (6) holds, then raising your child to be as morally good as she can be is doing her a harm, so a decent parent should deliberately hold her back a notch.

He insists that (6) and (7) are simply Wolf's view made consistent with itself. But (7) strikes me as monstrous, and I don't believe even (6), though when I trace the steps, each looks fine on its own. My sense is that something is being smuggled in somewhere early in the chain, but I cannot name it.

Please begin with a brief brainstorm of candidate diagnoses before committing to one — think about possible equivocations between steps, premises that are plausible only under a reading that later steps quietly drop, and any shifts in the sortal under which goods are being counted. Then:

(a) Name the step or steps you would reject, and explain precisely why each fails — including why it looked acceptable at first glance.
(b) Show how rejecting it blocks the route to (6) and to (7).
(c) Price the alternatives: if I insisted on keeping the step you reject, say what else I would have to give up instead — whether elsewhere in the argument, or in some independently plausible conviction — and which of those costs is highest.
(d) Tell me whether my housemate is right that the conclusion is just Wolf's position made consistent. In particular, check whether his reading distorts her: as I recall her, she argues about a person for whom moral perfection is the organizing principle of the whole life, and about whether such a person is an admirable ideal — not about whether we are better off, or our children better off, at a notch below our moral ceiling. If that recollection is fair, explain exactly where his argument needs her to be saying something stronger.

Please think carefully and be rigorous about the distinction between what each premise asserts and what the inference to the next premise requires of it; that is where I suspect the trouble lies, and I want to understand it well enough to reconstruct the argument's failure myself.
