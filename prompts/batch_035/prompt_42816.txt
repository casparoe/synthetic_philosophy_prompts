So I run a little reading group on history of economic thought, and two of the members have been going at the socialist calculation debate for what feels like a year now. One of them is basically a Langean market socialist — thinks public ownership plus a simulated price mechanism gets you everything capitalism gets you calculation-wise, maybe better. The other is a hard-line Austrian type who thinks Mises and Hayek settled this a century ago and gets genuinely annoyed at any mention of trial-and-error procedures. The two of them have agreed to try writing something together instead of just re-enacting the same fight at every meeting, and I've been tasked with finding someone to draft the thing. That's you. Act as a philosopher of economics who knows the Mises/Hayek side and the Lange side well enough to state each one in terms its own partisans would actually sign off on — no strawmen, and no letting either camp quietly redefine a term to win.

The document I want is the joint statement a proper adversarial collaboration would produce. Start with a numbered section of claims both sides can sign: things like (and feel free to refine these) — that for a given set of preferences, resources, and technology, the formal conditions for efficient allocation are the same under any system that achieves them, and that a planning bureau could in principle solve the equations for a static economy; that real economies aren't static, so the interesting question is about adjustment and discovery, not about solving a fixed system; and that the practical case for or against planning doesn't rest on the bare logical possibility of calculation. Then a disagreements table: for each remaining point of divergence, two columns with the wording each side would accept for itself, phrased neutrally enough that neither feels sandbagged — e.g., whether the debate is really about information or whether it quietly collapses into an incentives problem once the informational issues are conceded; whether the kind of knowledge that matters is articulable at all, so that it could in principle be transmitted to a planner, or whether it's tacit and only shows up in market behavior; and whether the dynamic/innovation point is a distinct argument or just the knowledge point wearing a costume. For each disagreement, flag whether it's substantive or partly verbal, and say which part and why — my own suspicion is at least one of them is a definitional fight over "calculation," but I want your honest read, not confirmation of mine.

Finally, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side, and for each, a concrete case, historical episode, or argument both would agree bears on it — with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. The predictions should be ones they'd be embarrassed to fudge afterwards, so keep them specific (they could use things like the behavior of actually-existing firms that do internal planning at large scale, or how markets performed under particular informational conditions, but pick whatever works — you know this literature better than I do). Think carefully before drafting, maybe brainstorm what each camp would refuse to sign and why, and make sure the agreed-claims section doesn't quietly include anything either side would want to move to the disagreements table. Also, keep it honest about where the two of them simply stop sharing assumptions, rather than papering over it with vague "further research is needed" language — I've read enough bad joint statements to know what that looks like, tbh.
