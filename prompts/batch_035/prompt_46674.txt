I'm a physical chemist by training and temperament — thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, and solution chemistry are my daily working language, and my knowledge of philosophy of mind is entirely secondhand. Last month a philosopher friend at a dinner party walked me through panpsychism, the view that consciousness is a fundamental feature of matter all the way down, so that even fundamental particles carry some extraordinarily primitive form of experience, and the rich consciousness of a person is somehow assembled from them. She then confessed that the view lives under a standing threat called the combination problem, and she mentioned that it isn't one problem but several: a version about qualities — how micro-experiences with whatever proto-qualities they have could mix or sum into the specific qualities of my experience, which I gather philosophers sometimes call the palette problem — and a stranger version about subjects, about how many points of view could ever compose into a single point of view at all. I followed the first one but genuinely lost the thread on the second, and it's been nagging at me since.

Here's what I'd like you to do, and please think it through carefully rather than giving me the standard encyclopedic treatment: act as a professional philosopher of mind who is fluent enough in physical chemistry to be held accountable by one. Restate the combination problem — both versions, kept clearly separate — in the vocabulary I actually use: additivity and non-additivity of properties, extensive versus intensive variables, mixtures versus compounds, phase transitions, degrees of freedom, coarse-graining, and the statistical-mechanical sense in which macroscopic behavior "emerges" from microdynamics. Be honest about where the mapping is exact, where it only works with distortion, and where it fails outright. Then give me the residue report. My working instinct is that the whole dispute reduces to non-additivity: chemistry is entirely at peace with wholes having properties that no part has and that no simple sum predicts — volumes of mixing, boiling points of alloys, critical points — while remaining fully computable and fully third-personal. So either the panpsychists are just behind the curve on non-additive properties, or there's something in their problem that my vocabulary literally cannot state.

I want to know which, and I want you to be candid about the trap in my framing: when I ask whether the whole has properties the parts lack, my examples are all structural properties of things with no inside, and I suspect the second, subject-related version of the problem is precisely the part that doesn't survive being translated into a language whose every quantity is a quantity of structure. Tell me whether that's right, and if it is, whether the residue shows a defect in my framework or a defect in the way the panpsychist originally stated their problem — or whether it's the genuinely interesting case: something real that both of our languages can gesture at but neither can say. Take your time and don't soften the verdict to be polite to my discipline.
