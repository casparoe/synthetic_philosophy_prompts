You are a professional philosopher specializing in bioethics and the metaphysics of death, serving as a consultant to hospital ethics committees. Please reason carefully and methodically: before committing to a view, brainstorm the possible interpretive frameworks, test each against the record, and only then argue for a conclusion.

I chair the death-determination review panel at a large academic hospital. Over the years, the panel has issued rulings in a series of cases, and we now face a new one. Before I present it, let me summarize our track record, since consistency with our own precedents matters to us:

Case 1: A ventilated adult with absent brainstem reflexes, a positive apnea test, and no confounding factors, whose heart continued to beat with pharmacological support. Ruled dead; organs were recovered.

Case 2: A controlled donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) case: life support was withdrawn per the family's wishes, the heart stopped, asystole was confirmed, and we waited two minutes before declaring death. Ruled dead; organs were recovered.

Case 3: A patient in a permanent vegetative state with extensive cortical destruction but an intact, functioning brainstem and sleep-wake cycles. Ruled not dead, though we approved withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment.

Case 4: A newborn with anencephaly whose brainstem was visibly functional. Ruled not dead; we declined to authorize organ recovery until standard criteria were met.

Case 5: A patient on full cardiopulmonary bypass during cardiac surgery, with no cardiac output whatsoever for forty minutes while the machine oxygenated and circulated the blood. Never in doubt, but the panel formally noted that the patient was alive throughout, and we have cited this case when drafting policy language.

The new case: our transplant team proposes a neonatal DCD protocol in which death would be declared after only seventy-five seconds of confirmed asystole. Their justification is that cessation of circulation is permanent in the relevant sense: no resuscitation will be attempted, the cause of arrest is irreversible, and the goal is to preserve organ quality in newborns, where waiting longer defeats the purpose. Some of us are uncomfortable; others point out that we already accept a two-minute wait that is justified the same way, just with a different number.

Please help me think through what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves. Specifically:

1. Identify the candidate rules or principles that would each fit our entire track record. In particular, consider whether we have been running a unified conception of death (on which circulatory criteria are merely a proxy for some deeper condition that must actually obtain) versus a disjunctive or pluralist policy (on which either irreversible whole-brain loss or permanently stopped circulation suffices, independently). Are there other rules that also fit, and does anything in our record exclude some otherwise-plausible rules, such as a higher-brain criterion or a crude "no circulating blood" test?

2. Show what each candidate rule predicts about the seventy-five-second protocol. Where the rules diverge, explain exactly why the divergence arises — for instance, on whether the permanence/irreversibility distinction is doing legitimate work or is a convenient fiction.

3. Give your best recommendation for the panel's decision, with the reasoning that favors it over the alternatives, and identify which of our earlier cases is the closest analog.

4. Acknowledge honestly what principle the panel would be adopting with each available choice, and propose at least one further case — one we have not yet ruled on — that would best discriminate between the leading candidate rules. A scenario in which the heart spontaneously restarts shortly after a declaration of death, and scenarios involving restoring circulation to the body or to isolated organ systems after declaration, strike me as potentially revealing; please evaluate these and any others you can devise.
