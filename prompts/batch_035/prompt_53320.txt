My friend and I spent basically all of saturday arguing about responsibility gaps — the idea that when an autonomous system (self-driving car, a triage algorithm, a drone swarm that re-plans mid-mission) causes harm, there can end up being nobody who satisfies the usual conditions for being responsible, since no human could foresee or control what the system did, and the system itself isn't a candidate for blame. She thinks the whole framing is hype: she says responsibility for harm has always been smeared across corporations, bureaucracies, armies of engineers, that we never had tidy one-responsible-agent cases anyway, and nobody serious ever demanded that every harm have an owner. I kept pushing back that she's missing what's genuinely new — a system that learns can do something nobody chose, even indirectly, not just something nobody *specifically* chose. We got nowhere, and it dawned on me on the walk home that half the problem is we never fixed what a theory here is even supposed to be for. So before round two:

Write the desiderata. What an adequate account of responsibility for harms involving autonomous, learning systems has to deliver — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, what it has to be consistent with. Cases I'd want on the list at minimum (add others if I'm missing obvious ones):

- the classic one: system behaves in a way its designers couldn't reasonably have predicted
- distributed design: hundreds of engineers, each fully on top of their own bit, nobody with the whole picture
- a human-in-the-loop who just rubberstamps the system's recommendation
- system does exactly what it was designed to do, and the harm traces back to a tradeoff somebody did choose (say, prioritizing the car's occupants)
- user operates it outside its intended domain and it fails badly

Distinctions it has to respect — blame vs liability vs forward-looking duties (like who has to clean up or recall the thing), causal contribution vs responsibility, responsibility for the outcome vs for the risk taken. And flag for me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's wish list. In particular: is "every harm must have a responsible party" a genuine requirement or does basically nobody accept it once you say it out loud? Relatedly, is the purpose relative — does the list look different if the point is assigning blame vs deciding who compensates vs designing institutions going forward? Tell me if you think it does. Most importantly, tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — my hunch is the no-orphaned-harms requirement and the control-or-foreseeability requirement are the pair that can't both hold, but I want to know if thats actually the real tension in the literature or something I made up.

Two asks: seperate what's genuinely settled from what's actually contested among people who work on this stuff, and don't present a contested view as the consensus — my friend does enough of that for both of us.
