I have taught our upper-level seminar on personal identity for some years now, and each cycle I grow more dissatisfied with how quickly students deploy the standard vocabulary — psychological continuity, the animalist's objection, the "further fact" — as if labelling a position were the same as having a view. This term I want to test my own commitments instead, and I have come to realize that my considered verdicts about the survival cases we discuss are not obviously captured by any of the theories I lecture on. So I am asking you to do for me what I ask of them: take my verdicts as data and tell me what principle, if any, generates them.

Here are the cases and my gut verdicts. (1) The ordinary teletransporter: a machine scans you on Earth, destroys you, and constructs on Mars an exact replica with all your memories and dispositions — I think you survive that; it is not death. (2) Gradual replacement: over ten years of routine surgeries, each of your neurons is substituted, one at a time, by a functionally identical silicon prosthesis, with no interruption in your waking life — survival, though I confess to some unease. (3) Fission: the teletransporter malfunctions, destroys the original, and produces two simultaneous replicas on Mars and Europa — I find myself thinking neither survived, but also that nothing died, and I do not know what to call that. (4) Deferred reconstruction: you are scanned, live on for another week, die naturally, and the scan sits unused on a server for fifty years before the replica is built — I am fairly firm that the replica is not you; the fifty-year gap matters to me. (5) Cryonic suspension: your heart stops, your brain is vitrified for thirty years, and you are then revived with most memories intact but a decade of therapy needed to rebuild your dispositions — survival, clearly. (6) Lossy teleport: the scanner must compress your psychology into a lossy sketch, and a generative model on Mars fills in the gaps plausibly, so the replica resembles you roughly as much as you will resemble yourself after a decade of ordinary change — here I wobble, but I lean toward saying this is not survival, which is the verdict I least understand holding alongside my verdict on case (1).

What I want from you is a principle — or a small set of candidates, if no single one suffices — stated precisely enough to deliver each verdict, with the case that motivates each clause. I suspect the real pressure is on the pair (4) and (5): both involve decades of interruption and the death of the original body, yet my verdicts are opposite, and I cannot tell whether some causal-chain condition (say, requiring that the later person depend counterfactually on the earlier one in a way the dormant scan does not provide) can mark the difference, or whether I am secretly tracking something other than survival, such as what deserves my prudential concern now. I would also like you to be blunt about which of my verdicts no defensible principle can accommodate — I am prepared to give one up, though I would rather not give up (1). Finally, tell me what your preferred principle predicts for three cases I have deliberately not taken a stance on: a deferred reconstruction in which the scan sits unused for only ten minutes; a deferred reconstruction in which the original, still alive, lives alongside the replica built from the fifty-year-old scan; and a version of case (2) in which the prosthetic replacements are carried out by a process that occasionally errs and fills the gaps the way the lossy teleporter does. If my verdicts cannot be saved by one principle, say exactly where the rupture is and which verdict you would overturn, and why that one rather than its rival.
