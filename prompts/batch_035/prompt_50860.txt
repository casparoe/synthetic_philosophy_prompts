My book club has been reading Sapiens, and one member has adopted Harari's line "We did not domesticate wheat. It domesticated us." as a conversation-stopper for anything that sounds like a progress narrative. Deployed last week, it was meant to establish that the Agricultural Revolution was a disaster for the people who made it — worse diet, harder work, more disease — and that everything since is a long rationalization of that mistake rather than an improvement on foraging. I suspect this single sentence is smuggling in several claims of very different strength, but I can't sort them out. What precisely could "wheat domesticated us" be asserting? Lay out a menu of readings, from the near-tautological (farming reorganized human life around wheat's needs as much as around ours) through substantive causal and welfare claims (early farmers were worse off, in body and in hours, than the foragers they replaced — claims that in principle face skeletal and dietary evidence) to the exotic (plants as agents pursuing their own evolutionary interests). For each reading, say whether it is true, defensible but contested, or false; whether it is doing anything more than flipping the point of view for rhetorical effect; and, most importantly, whether it actually supports the conclusion he wants from it. If some readings are compatible with agriculture having been good for the farmers' descendants, or with the "revolution" being a slow, patchy, partly reversible process rather than a single fateful event, flag that too, since he treats those concessions as defeats.
