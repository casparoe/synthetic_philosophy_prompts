I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I keep a running list of phrases that do far more work in arguments than the people deploying them seem to notice. Near the top sits *vox populi, vox dei* — the voice of the people is the voice of God. It resurfaced in our newsroom last week while we were planning coverage of a citizens' assembly that had just concluded several weeks of deliberation. A colleague used it to wave off a panel of specialists who had come out against the assembly's recommendation: if a couple of hundred randomly selected ordinary citizens, after hearing evidence and arguing it through, land on a conclusion, that deserves more weight than the verdict of a handful of experts. The room nodded along, and I found I lacked the vocabulary to ask what had actually been claimed. The slogan clearly lives somewhere in the neighbourhood of the Condorcet jury theorem, the story I've retold myself about Francis Galton and the county-fair ox, and the whole wisdom-of-crowds genre — but the neighbourhood is not the house, and I'd like to know exactly which house my colleague thought he was standing in.

What I'd like is a disambiguation I can actually think with: a menu of the distinct, precise claims that the one Latin phrase could be expressing, and for each one a verdict — trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or merely pious — plus, crucially, a note on whether that reading would actually vindicate the use the slogan was put to, of collective deliberation outranking expert opinion. I can see some of the pieces but not how they fit together: the jury theorem's twin premises that each voter is better than a coin flip and that votes are independent; the fact that Galton-style crowd averaging pools estimates rather than counting votes; the wrinkle that a deliberating assembly is precisely *not* a set of independent voters, since deliberation is meant to correlate everyone's errors and updates; and the suspicion that the theorem assumes a common state of the world that everyone is trying to hit, which may not describe the political questions where the slogan gets brandished. I'd also like to know whether the slogan is even best read as an epistemic claim in the first place — perhaps it's about legitimacy rather than truth, in which case all the Condorcet machinery is answering a different question from the one my colleague was asking, and the nodding was unearned for a reason nobody in the room could name.

Please answer in British English, and assume I know what a conditional probability is but am a working journalist rather than a mathematician: rigour welcome, formalism only where it earns its keep, and no theorem invoked without a prose gloss on what it assumes.
