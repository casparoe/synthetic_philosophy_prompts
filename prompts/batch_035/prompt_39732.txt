I've spent over a decade reading the rationalist corner of the internet — LessWrong and its descendants, the effective altruism Forum, the comment sections of the psychiatrists-turned-bloggers — and I keep noticing a pattern that puzzles me: in those spaces, the egalitarian question "equality of what?" was effectively settled by fiat in favor of welfare. When people there quantify the good, they reach for QALYs or DALYs, they argue about whether to fund malaria nets or mental health interventions on the basis of subjective well-being scores, and a recurring debate about whether to trust stated preferences versus hedonic self-reports never seems to end. Meanwhile, in academic political philosophy, the same question — famously posed by Amartya Sen in his Tanner Lecture — has been going in circles for over forty years: welfare egalitarianism was largely abandoned after the expensive-tastes and adaptive-preferences objections, Dworkin's equality of resources introduced the envy test and the brute-luck/option-luck distinction, Sen and Nussbaum's capability approach occupied a middle ground, and Cohen tried to split the difference with a more inclusive currency. Everyone has fired their best arguments, the counterarguments are canonical, and my impression is that nobody has been moved in decades.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? I have a hypothesis I'd like you to evaluate, not just catalog: the impasse persists because each side is implicitly answering a different question. The resource theorists are designing an institutional metric — something a state can allocate and audit — and their real criterion is operationalizability under an envy test. The capability theorists are answering a question about what makes a life go well, and their tolerance for indexical vagueness reflects that. The welfare theorists, meanwhile, may be answering yet a third question, something closer to what an idealized altruist with a common currency of compassion should allocate. If the disagreement is really about which question deserves the name "egalitarian justice," then it is partly verbal — but there might also be a hidden substantive issue underneath that nobody foregrounds: whether there is anything stable enough to be equalized in the first place. The behavioral economics literature on preference construction and preference reversals suggests that people's preferences are partly generated by the elicitation procedure; the behavioral welfare economists' "preference purification" program is an explicit attempt to reconstruct a coherent agent out of a noisy one. If both welfare and preference-based currencies are measurement-dependent in this way, then perhaps the whole debate presupposes a fixed quantity that psychometrics and revealed-preference theory jointly suggest does not exist — a problem of metric invariance masquerading as a problem of moral theory.

Please take on the role of a professional political philosopher who is also fluent in welfare economics and measurement theory, and think carefully and systematically before you write. Begin by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses — verbal dispute, different questions under one name, divergent standards of operationalizability, bedrock moral intuitions about responsibility for one's own tastes, or a genuine hidden empirical question about the stability of welfare and preference — and then argue for the one you find most plausible, using what the main positions actually claim (the envy test, adaptive preferences, the indexing objection to capabilities, the QALY-style operationalizations common in cost-effectiveness analysis). Finally, tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a better measurement technology, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be dissolved. I care about the diagnosis, not a survey of positions I already know, and I don't need citations — just the reasoning.
