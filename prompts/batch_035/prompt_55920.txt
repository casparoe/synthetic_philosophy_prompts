I run a weekly discussion group on decision theory and AI strategy for a mix of grad students and early-career researchers — 12 people, one 90-minute session, tables that can be rearranged for pairs. Next session is on commitment races: the worry that in multipolar strategic settings, agents gain bargaining power by making binding commitments early, before they know how things will turn out, and that if everyone reasons this way, they can end up committed to mutually disastrous courses of action. The participants will have done some reading on commitment and bargaining in principle (Schelling-style ideas, the value of credibility), but not on the race dynamic itself.

Design a multi-round in-class game in which the race dynamic is something participants run into rather than something I tell them about. The base situation each round should be a two-player distribution problem over a fixed surplus with a costly failure mode — something in the neighborhood of chicken or divide-the-dollar with an explosion option — so that a credible commitment to "I will not accept less than X" is genuinely powerful, and so that two clashing commitments produce a disaster rather than a compromise. On top of that, build a commitment mechanism with a cost schedule tied to timing: a commitment made in an early phase (before players know who their opponent is, or before some uncertainty about the round's parameters resolves) is cheaper and more credible than one made later, so that the strategic logic pushes players toward committing blind. Include a rule for how commitments are verified and enforced within the game, whether and at what price a commitment can be revoked, and how information about other players' commitments leaks across rounds — the group should be able to discover the escalation pattern over three or four rounds without anyone lecturing.

Then give me the debrief: a mapping of specific moments in the game (who committed first, who was burned by committing blind, who profited by staying flexible, any round where two commitments collided) onto the components of the commitment races problem — first-mover advantage, the option value of remaining uncommitted, the cost of committing under uncertainty about the other side, and the way mutual knowledge of everyone's incentive to commit early can produce a worse outcome for all. I want the debrief to end by opening outward toward the AI case, where the unsettling features are stronger: agents that can model each other well, act under logical uncertainty, and may be able to commit to policies across possible worlds before knowing which one obtains.

Finally, two honest sections: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (players not understanding the commitment rules, excessive caution producing boring rounds, a dominant strategy trivializing the whole thing), and which feature of the actual commitment races problem no classroom game can reproduce and I will have to supply myself at the end.
