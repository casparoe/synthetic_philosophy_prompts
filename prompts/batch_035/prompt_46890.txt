My oldest friend and I ended a long dinner argument last night about the fourteen-day rule, and neither of us left satisfied, so I want to put the question to someone who can reconstruct the philosophy underneath it.

The practice itself, in case it needs describing: in Britain, and in several other jurisdictions, the law permits research on human embryos outside the body only up to the fourteenth day after fertilisation, and requires that they be destroyed at that point. The limit is commonly justified by appeal to the appearance of the primitive streak, the first visible sign of the body's axis, and to the fact that before roughly that stage an embryo can still split into identical twins, so that there is not yet a distinct individual to be harmed or protected.

The argument with my friend turned on what this practice presupposes. My friend said the rule presupposes a particular answer to the question of what the early embryo is: that moral status attaches at individuation, when one embryo becomes one candidate person in the counting sense, rather than at conception, sentience, or birth. I resisted: surely the rule is philosophically underdetermined, and a gradualist could accept it as a compromise line rather than a metaphysical one. But then my friend raised what still bothers me. The same legal system permits abortion, on certain grounds, well past fourteen days — up to twenty-four weeks in most cases — which looks like a flat contradiction unless the two practices presuppose different theories of the same thing. Destroying a two-week embryo in a dish for research is a criminal offence; destroying a ten-week embryo in a clinic, under the statutory grounds, is lawful medical care. Can a single theory of what life is, or of when a life begins, license both, or is the law quietly operating with two incompatible views?

So, please: reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguishing what the fourteen-day rule strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with; say whether the apparent inconsistency with abortion law is genuine, or whether it dissolves once one notices that the two practices answer different questions — what may be created for research and then discarded, versus what may be ended within a pregnant woman's body, where the interests of the woman are also in play; and tell me what would have to change in the rule if we replaced the presupposed view with, on the one hand, a sentience-based view, and on the other, the view that the embryo is one of us from conception. I would also like your honest opinion on whether the line can fairly be described as a convention — a line drawn where the argument is thinnest — and whether that is a scandal, given that any line whatever will look arbitrary from close up.

Please answer in continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write a considered letter.
