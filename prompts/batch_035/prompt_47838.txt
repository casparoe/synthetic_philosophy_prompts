I spent thirty-five years as a condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and I have lately been working through the decision-theory literature, where I keep tripping over the same roadblock and wondering whether my training has made me insensitive to it. The roadblock is the Pasadena game and its relatives: a St. Petersburg-style gamble whose expectation series converges only conditionally, so that the sum has no well-defined value, and by the rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series, reordering the outcomes can be made to yield any value one likes. Philosophers I have read treat this as showing that expected utility simply runs out for such gambles—that they are, in a strict sense, beyond valuation.

My physicist's reflex is that this situation is not scandalous but familiar, and that the philosophical reaction is exactly backwards. We routinely extract confirmable numbers from formally divergent quantities: the Casimir force between plates is computed by subtracting one divergent mode sum from another, and the result matches experiment to high precision; dimensional and zeta-function regularization assign finite values to divergent integrals and sums that would earn an undergraduate a failing grade in a pure mathematics course. The lesson I was taught is that "undefined" is often a fact about the representation, not about the world, and that the productive move is to find a principled regimentation scheme whose outputs are constrained by something outside the formalism itself—symmetries, boundary conditions, or, ultimately, measurement.

Please argue that decision theory should take precisely this lesson from the Pasadena paradox: that the correct conclusion is not that conditionally convergent gambles are beyond rational evaluation, but that their values are scheme-relative, and that the genuine theoretical task is to identify which regularization scheme a rational agent should use—for instance, summing outcomes in the canonical order in which they are presented, or damping the contributions by some principled weighting and taking the limit. On this view, the analogue of the physicist's experimental arbiter is the requirement that the scheme deliver sensible verdicts in the convergent limit and reduce to ordinary expected value where ordinary expected value exists, and perhaps that it be invariant under mere redescription of the gamble.

In arguing for this position, I would like you to confront the most serious disanalogy head-on rather than hoping I will not notice it: in physics, regularization is disciplined by experiment, and there is no obvious analogue of experiment for a normative theory of choice. A skeptic will say that any "canonical" ordering of a game's outcomes is exactly as arbitrary as any other, that the rearrangement theorem therefore reinstates the indeterminacy one level up, and that my analogy is a case of smuggling in an external arbiter precisely where none can exist. Address that objection, say what in the structure of the choice situation could play the role the symmetries and boundary conditions play in physics, and be explicit about whether your answer makes the resulting scheme objective or merely conventional. If you think the conventionality is ultimately ineradicable, say so and argue that scheme-relative value is still enough for rational decision-making.

Please write formally and with care about the mathematics—I know what conditional convergence and damping functions are, and loose talk will irritate me—but you need not derive anything. I do not want citations; I am interested in the argument, not the bibliography.
