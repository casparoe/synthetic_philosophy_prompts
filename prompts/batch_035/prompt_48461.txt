I'm a graduate student working on a paper about the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction, and I'd like comments on a draft argument before I commit to writing the full thing up. The idea, roughly, is that the distinction should be individuated by loss conditions rather than by the category of the state involved. We standardly lose knowledge-that through epistemic defeat: if I acquire decisive evidence that my belief is false, or that my source was unreliable, I no longer know, even if I keep believing. But knowing-how is not lost through anything like that. It is lost through disuse, injury, or the loss of a supporting artifact — the pianist who hasn't played in fifteen years, the swimmer after a stroke, the driver whose route-knowledge atrophies once she relies entirely on GPS. And, more suggestively, knowing-how can survive the exact defeaters that would destroy corresponding knowledge-that: if I learned a certain technique for escaping a rip current, and later receive overwhelming expert testimony that the technique is dangerous and based on a falsehood, I may be irrational to rely on it — and yet, if I can still execute it when panicking in the water, there seems to be an important sense in which I still know how, even while I no longer know that it works. My tentative thesis is that Ryle was right that there is a deep distinction here, but that his real insight concerns norms of persistence and criticism, not the fact that knowing-how is an ability or disposition rather than a state; and that Stanley and Williamson, who analyze knowing how to F as knowing a true proposition about a way of F-ing, get something right about the ascriptions while smuggling in a false picture of what kind of knowledge is ascribed — because the knowledge-that that epistemologists actually study is the kind individuated by defeaters, and on that criterion knowing-how simply isn't a species of it. The draft is deliberately concessive: I don't want to fight the semantics, only to relocate the debate from grammar to epistemology.

I'd like comments on three things in particular. First, the core move: is "kinds of knowledge are individuated by their loss conditions" a respectable principle, or am I dressing up a restatement of my conclusion? It would help to hear whether there are counterexamples on either side — propositional knowledge that is lost through competence-style conditions, or practical knowledge lost purely through epistemic ones. Second, the coherence of the concession: if I allow that knowing-how ascriptions are, grammatically, ascriptions of propositional knowledge, does my claim that they ascribe a *different kind* of knowledge do any real work, or does it collapse into the familiar intellectualist view with a footnote? Third, accuracy and interest: I'm reconstructing Ryle and Stanley and Williamson from memory and secondary discussions, so please flag anything in my characterization that sounds off, but don't try to supply quotations or references — I'll verify the texts myself. Above all, please tell me plainly if you think the whole framing is confused — for instance, if persistence conditions are simply the wrong level at which to draw a distinction between kinds of knowledge, or if the rip current case shows nothing more than that reliance and knowledge come apart. I'd much rather hear that now than after I've written thirty pages.
