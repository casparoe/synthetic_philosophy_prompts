I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. Everything I know about philosophy of language comes from encyclopedias, blog posts, and arguments at work — I've never read Putnam or Burge in the original — so please correct my framing wherever it is loose. I'm drafting an essay for my engineering blog on what Nineteen Eighty-Four says about meaning, and I would like a serious critical reading of the novel through the lens of semantic externalism, the Twin Earth family of ideas. The reading needs to be faithful in both directions: the novel shouldn't be reduced to an illustration of the theory, and the theory shouldn't be bent to fit the plot.

The lens, as I understand it: Putnam's Twin Earth, a place just like Earth except that the clear liquid in the rivers isn't H2O but some other stuff, so that the word "water" there means that stuff, not this one, regardless of what is in the speakers' heads. And Burge's case: whether a patient's word "arthritis" covers a disease in the thigh depends on how the linguistic community uses the term, not on the patient's private understanding. Meaning is fixed partly by the world and the community; it was never wholly in the head. As a programmer I find this congenial — a symbol's meaning isn't in the token, it's in what the whole running system binds it to, and an identifier in a dead repository means nothing.

The thesis I want tested: the Party is an externalist state. It rarely needs to reach into anyone's skull; it rewrites the world in which skulls mean. It burns the books, owns every press, watches every conversation so that no private community of usage can form, and gives Syme the job of destroying words rather than adding them. If Burge is right, Winston's political vocabulary was fixed by his community all along, and once that community is replaced, the words really do change: his diary sense of "freedom" survives the way an archaic sense of a word survives — remembered, no longer live. On this reading Room 101 is less persuasion than mopping up.

The parts I cannot work out, which the reading should confront: (1) Winston's stubborn private attachment to the old "freedom" — is that a residue of meaning in the head that externalism cannot account for, or only a memory of how a community once spoke? (2) Doublethink: Winston remains perfectly competent in both vocabularies at once — does that display the split between competence in the head and meaning fixed socially, or does it cut against my thesis? (3) Whether the Party can make it the case that two and two make five, or only make everyone say it — does externalism draw that line, and does the novel respect it?

Here is my own variant, and I suspect it is where the essay lives or dies: run the Twin Earth experiment with the environments identical and only the communities differing. Both planets have the same physics and the same water; on one of them, a Ministry of Truth has been operating for three generations. On externalism, the two populations' word "free" now genuinely means different things, though their brains are the same. Fine — but I cannot find any resource inside the theory for saying that the engineered community deformed a meaning rather than simply having a new one. Does the novel supply what the theory lacks here — and if so, what exactly, and where does it resist my reading? Please also tell me whether to make anything of the appendix on Newspeak, which reads as though composed from a time after Newspeak's era has ended.

I don't need quotations from the text; I need the reading, and an honest account of where the novel refuses to cooperate with it.
