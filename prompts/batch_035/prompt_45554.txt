I've been keeping a notebook of cases in which I have firm views about when it is proper to assert outright, as opposed to hedging or qualifying, and I would like to know whether a single norm of assertion captures my verdicts — and at what cost. Below are the cases with my verdicts. Assume every assertion happens to be true, so the question is never whether the speaker got it wrong, only whether it was proper to say it flatly.

1. A meteorologist, whose models assign rain an 80% probability, tells a friend planning a picnic: "It will rain tomorrow." Improper. She should have said "probably."
2. The same meteorologist, with the same credence, tells the friend: "There's a high chance of rain tomorrow." Proper — even though I doubt she could pass any demanding test for *knowing* that probabilistic fact.
3. A lottery with ten million tickets. The seller tells a buyer, "Your ticket will lose." Improper, despite a credence of 0.9999999.
4. A woman asserts "The bakery is on Fifth Street" on the strength of having walked past it daily years ago, never having heard of its closing, and with an intact memory. Proper; I'd say she knows it.
5. A man with a progressive neurological condition, which he knows is eroding his recall, asserts the same thing about his own former neighborhood bakery with about the same confidence as the meteorologist in case 1, and it is true. I genuinely waver: on bad days I find it proper, on good days improper, and I cannot articulate what separates it from case 1, where I am confident.
6. A juror who has just voted to convict — thereby asserting, in effect, "he is guilty" — on a felt certainty produced by ambiguous evidence, happens to be right. Improper, and far more so than case 3. Yet when the juror's spouse, after watching a video, says with the same felt certainty and no argument, "What that person did was cruel," I find that proper.

My tentative principle, which I cannot get to cover everything: outright assertion requires knowledge, except that low-stakes practical assertions are permissible on justified true belief alone. This handles 1 versus 4, perhaps; it stumbles on 2 (does she know the chance-fact?), on 3 (the lottery case seems to be knowledge denied, yet my exception invites it in), on 5, and on 6 (whose stakes count — the speaker's, the hearer's, or the subject's?).

Please test this principle against the six cases, repair it or replace it, and state the result precisely — a norm, or a small set of candidates, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me what the repaired principle predicts for cases I have not listed; I have three in mind. First, an expert who asserts a hedged probabilistic claim in which she has low credence ("there is a modest chance of complications"). Second, a mathematician who asserts a result she has verified only by computer, with no human-readable proof. Third, a navigation system's flat declarative outputs — "You have arrived at your destination" — which we apparently do not hold to any norm of assertion at all, or do we? Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and whether the right response is to complicate the principle or to overturn the verdict. If it helps, you may situate the principle relative to the familiar positions in this debate — that outright assertion is governed by a knowledge norm, a truth norm, or a justified-belief norm — but do not cite literature; I will track down references myself.
