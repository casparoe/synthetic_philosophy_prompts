I have no academic training — I read history and old travel writing for pleasure, and I think about what I read on my own — and there is a move I keep meeting in talk about national character that I would like a name for. I can describe it exactly, and my question is whether the configuration itself has been identified in the literature, and if not, I want you to commit to coining a term for it rather than leaving me with a list of candidates.

The move works like this. A claim is made about a people as a whole — that they are proud by nature, or orderly, or passionate. When a member of that people behaves in accordance with the trait, this confirms it; that part is fair enough. But when a member behaves against it, the trait is not refuted — the behavior is instead read as a reaction against the trait. The famously orderly German whose private life is chaos is described as rebelling against his own orderliness; the famously passionate nation that submits quietly for a generation under a dictatorship is said to have turned its passion inward, and then when it finally rises up, the same passion is cited again, now bursting outward. Compliance and deviation, obedience and revolt — each is assigned one of two roles, expression or reaction, and both roles count as evidence for the claim. The claim about the people ends up explaining everything any person from that people does, which, as far as I can tell, means it predicts nothing.

What seems specific to this, and why I don't think it is just a vague or untestable claim, is the structure: the trait belongs to the collective, and the individual is only ever allowed to be evidence about the collective, never a counterexample to it. It is as though the individual's conduct is never permitted to be about the individual — it is always about the people. I have also noticed the same double direction spread across time in arguments rather than books: a people called naturally servile under one regime is called naturally ungovernable a generation later, and both descriptions are presented as the same national character showing a different face.

So, my questions. Does this pattern have an established name? The nearest things I can formulate myself are, first, a claim that simply cannot be checked, and second, the habit of patching a claim with an after-the-fact excuse — but neither seems quite right, because in this pattern no new patch is improvised at the last minute; the two interpretations, expression and reaction, appear to be available in advance, waiting. If there is no established name, please do not simply say so and stop: commit to coining one, define it, and mark its edges. In particular, I want a ruling on two boundary cases, with reasons. First, is the defender who narrows the claim instead of widening the interpretation — retreating to orderly at work, though not at parties — making the same move, or a different and sometimes legitimate one? Second, does the pattern require a collective subject at all, or could a claim about a single person's character qualify, as when someone's humility is explained away as a form of pride? I would rather you take a definite position on each of these and defend it, even at the risk of being wrong, than set out the possibilities and leave the choosing to me.
