i run a weekly rationality reading group and next session is on imprecise probabilities. i want to open it with a quiz that sorts people by which decision rule they actually use, not which one they say they like. so the questions all have to be concrete cases — nothing like "do you endorse maximin expected utility?", dont ask anything answerable straight from a label.

give me 8-10 questions, each a scenario with 2-3 answer options, drawn from cases along these lines (adjust as needed but keep the spirit):

1. an Ellsberg-style urn described plainly — 30 red balls, 60 that are black or yellow in unknown proportions, the usual suspects — with the classic sequence of four bets, so we can see who switches and who doesnt.

2. this one exactly: you can take $50 for sure, or a bet that pays $100 if a red ball is drawn from an urn where all you know is that between 45% and 60% of the balls are red. options: (a) take the $50 — when i only know a range, i go by the worst end of it; (b) take the bet — the top of the range is real information too, and 60% of the time it's the better call; (c) there's no fact of the matter about which is better, either choice is fine and nobody can call me irrational for either.

3. a question about whether there's always a number: some one-off event with no frequencies and no experts to consult (will the new head of HR pick the open-plan office, say). is "somewhere between 40% and 60%" ever the more honest answer than any single number, or is vagueness always just ignorance about your own precise credence?

4. a paying-for-unambiguity case: two bets with identical value ranges, one on a fair coin and one on the ambiguous urn. can you be rationally required to pay a premium for the coin, or is that always a bias?

5. a sequential one: you can bind yourself today to a plan you'd endorse now, or leave the choice open and decide later, knowing later-you will face a smaller set of candidate probabilities. does commitment ever beat keeping your options open?

then write a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the actual positions: strict bayesianism (precise prior, ambiguity is just risk like any other), Γ-maximin, E-admissibility, interval dominance, and minimax regret if it turns up anywhere. flag any combination of answers that no real position occupies, and say whether that's because the combo is incoherent or just because nobody's bothered to defend it. also be upfront about which pairs of leaves the quiz genuinely can't seperate — if E-admissibility and interval dominance only come apart on cases too exotic to put in a quiz, say so instead of pretending some question splits them.

the audience has done the readings, so the key can use the real names, but every question should be answerable by someone who's never heard any of the jargon.
