In our metaethics reading group my supervisor has been issuing quick verdicts on which of our moral intuitions evolutionary debunking arguments really undermine, and I have kept a list. So far she has ruled: (i) the aversion to sibling incest is undermined, even in the modified case where the couple are adults using reliable contraception; (ii) disgust-based judgments about harmless but unnatural or gruesome conduct are undermined; (iii) the intuition that pushing the large man off the footbridge is impermissible is undermined; (iv) the judgment that one ought to redirect the trolley to save five at the cost of one is not undermined; (v) the judgment that one ought to keep a promise whose breach would never be discovered by anyone is not undermined. Next week's case is this: the calm, considered judgment that a parent owes more to her own child than to any number of strangers' children — the sort of judgment people defend reflectively, with arguments about the value of intimate relationships, though it is also just what gene-centred explanations of attachment would predict. Predict her verdict. I would like you to set out the different rules that fit her track record so far (I can see at least two, and they disagree about next week's case), say what each one predicts, give your best guess together with your reasons, and propose one further case of your own invention that would best tell the candidate rules apart. Please write in British English, and end your reply with a final line of the form \answer{undermined} or \answer{not undermined} giving your prediction for next week's case.
