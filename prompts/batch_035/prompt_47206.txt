I'm the newly appointed chair of the exceptionality committee at a mid-sized regional hospital, which decides, roughly every week or two, how to allocate a fixed pool of money or medication when demand exceeds supply. I spent fifteen years as a bedside nurse before this, and the appointment has landed on me rather than being something I trained for. Our decisions are usually versions of the same dilemma: one identifiable patient with a large need versus many patients with smaller ones. A single very expensive one-off therapy for one child with a rare disease versus funding our vaccination outreach for a season; a full course of a scarce antiviral for the three sickest patients on the ward versus partial prophylactic doses for everyone; a budget cut that forces us to choose between withdrawing one chronic patient's ongoing therapy and degrading wound-care supplies for the whole floor. I understand the utilitarian answer is typically to maximize the aggregate, and I also understand there is a deep objection to it, going back to Rawls and Nozick: that persons are separate, that there is no single vessel whose welfare is being topped up, and that treating losses to one person as offset by gains to another is precisely what morality forbids, as if we were trading prospects within a single life rather than across distinct ones. What I do not have is any way to turn that objection into practice, and my committee — clinicians, not philosophers, rotating every six months, with the medical director signing off on everything — meets for thirty minutes and has to leave with a decision.

What I want from you is a decision procedure we can actually run in that half hour. It should be an ordered sequence of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, and it should say explicitly where the procedure terminates in a firm "no, we will not aggregate across persons here" and where instead it must hand the question back to a named human being because what remains is a matter of values rather than facts. The procedure cannot presuppose any theory, since my colleagues will not read one, but it should carry over the distinctions that genuinely matter — the difference between using a person as a means and merely giving them lower priority, the difference between an identifiable claimant standing before us and a statistical beneficiary who may never learn of our choice, the difference between aggregation within one patient's treatment plan and aggregation across patients — and it should be honest about which distinctions had to be dropped for the sake of usability and what could go wrong as a result. Then I want you to run the procedure on the three cases I described above, showing the reasoning at each step. At least one of these should come out the way I privately hope it will not, and you should tell me which one and why the procedure forces that result. Finally, describe the procedure's failure modes plainly: the situations where aggregation will slip back in through the wording of a question, where an advocate could steer the outcome by framing, and where the procedure simply has nothing to say.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, the way a long letter would read, since I intend to paste it directly into our committee minutes and circulate it by email.
