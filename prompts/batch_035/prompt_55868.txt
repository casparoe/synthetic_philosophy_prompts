I'm in an online forecasting community where, whenever two members' probability estimates fail to converge after a long exchange, someone reliably trots out the line that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. Last week it was deployed to conclude that a member who kept her estimate at 80% after learning that three peers each held 60% was, in virtue of that alone, irrational. On other occasions the same line has been used to mean that further argument is pointless, that the parties should split the difference, and that stubborn disagreement between reasonable people is strong evidence that one of them is hiding information. I suspect these uses are trading on several different readings packed into one ambiguous sentence. Please lay out, as a list I can post back to the community, the distinct precise claims the line could be making: as a theorem about idealized agents who share a prior and whose posteriors are common knowledge; as a claim about what follows between real people who merely tell each other their numbers; as a claim about whether iterated exchange of estimates must converge; and as an inference from persistent disagreement to irrationality or dishonesty. For each reading, say whether it is true, whether it is a platitude or a substantive claim, and—most important—whether it actually supports the use the line was put to last week, namely that the 80%-holder was irrational. Please write your answer in American English.
