I'm putting together a one-page statement of my historical views for a historiography seminar, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency check. Here is where I stand:

(1) The serious explanatory work in history is done at the level of what Braudel called the longue durée: climate, geography, soils, disease environments, and slow-moving demographic and agrarian structures. Histories organized around kings, battles, and treaties are closer to journalism with footnotes than to explanation.

(2) That said, I think the timing and location of the Industrial Revolution were substantially contingent. Shift the accessibility of coal seams a few hundred kilometers, delay the steam engine's refinement by a couple of generations, and the map of global wealth today looks unrecognizably different. Deep structures set the stage, but they did not decree that northwestern Europe in the late eighteenth century was the place and moment.

(3) I reject environmental determinism as classically formulated, from Montesquieu's climate typologies through the mid-twentieth-century versions, as both empirically careless and morally corrosive — too often a charter for assuming that the peoples of the tropics were simply built for subordination.

(4) I nevertheless think individual agents are explanatorily real. There are named people whose absence would have altered events in ways no structural variable absorbs — particular unifiers, particular public-health campaigners, particular constitution-writers — and a historiography that cannot say this has lost something.

(5) I'm skeptical of counterfactual history. If the deep structures dominate, then rerunning the tape with a different cast should produce roughly the same world at the scale of centuries, so "what if" exercises are parlor games, not arguments.

(6) I'm genuinely excited by the return of climate to historiography — Little Ice Age volatility, the volcanic darkness of 1816, climate-driven accounts of the seventeenth-century general crisis — and I think explanations running through harvest failure and mortality crises are, on the whole, more promising than explanations running through ideas and doctrines.

Please tell me which of these commitments are actually in tension with each other, which only appear to be, and what the least costly revision would be for each genuine conflict. Order the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most. I genuinely don't know where the pressure is, so don't assume I have a favorite commitment to protect.
