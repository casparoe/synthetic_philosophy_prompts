I've joined the board of the housing cooperative where I've lived for the past nine years, and our first real fight concerns a proposal to hire a private security firm to patrol the complex after a string of break-ins. The firm's guards would be armed and licensed, and would hold people for the police under our state's citizen's-arrest rules. At the last meeting, one board member shut the proposal down by insisting that the state has a monopoly on legitimate violence, so that armed private guards exercising coercive authority over residents and strangers alike are illegitimate by definition. Two hours later, a second member used the very same phrase to argue the opposite: that our city has already lost that monopoly — hence the break-ins, hence the slow police response — which is precisely why the cooperative needs patrols of its own. Neither of them, as far as I could tell, was using the phrase to make the same claim, and when I pressed each of them, neither could say what exactly it commits them to. I understand the line traces back to Max Weber, but what I need is not the history; it is the analysis.

Please lay out, as a list I can bring to the next board meeting, the distinct precise claims that the phrase could be expressing. I can think of at least the following candidates, and I want each taken seriously rather than collapsed into the others: an empirical or sociological claim about what successful states in fact manage to do; a definitional claim about what even counts as violence, or as force of the relevant kind, when non-state actors do it; a normative claim that only the state may be permitted to use force, however things actually stand; a claim not about who may use force but about who may authorize its use, under which a state could legitimately license private guards while never losing its monopoly; and a claim about ultimate backstop authority — that everyone with force must ultimately answer to the state, whether or not the state wields the force itself. Include any other readings the phrase can bear, including any that render it circular or unfalsifiable.

For each reading, tell me three things: whether it is plausible; whether it is actually true of modern states as they exist, given that private security, bouncers, bounty hunters, and armed homeowners already operate more or less openly within them; and, crucially, whether it would actually support dismissing our security proposal on the one hand or mandating it on the other. Finally, add a note identifying which reading each of my two board colleagues was most likely deploying, so that I can tell them apart at the next meeting — and tell me honestly whether either of them is entitled to conclude anything from the phrase at all.
