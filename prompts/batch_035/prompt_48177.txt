I write a column on science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm at work on a piece about what philosophers call anthropic reasoning — the question of how our evidence should be adjusted for the plain fact that we exist, and exist at this particular point in history rather than another. I want to build the column around a famous test case and the dilemma it creates, and because the piece has to take a side rather than merely survey the terrain, I need your actual position, with its pressure points exposed, not a neutral overview.

The test case, which I'll present to readers roughly as follows, comes from Nick Bostrom's book Anthropic Bias. Cosmologists have narrowed the field to two theories, T1 and T2, that are empirically equivalent — no experiment anyone can run distinguishes them — except that T1 says the universe contains vastly more observers than T2 does (say, a trillion trillion versus a few billion). Before any experiment is conducted, a philosopher announces that T1 is already overwhelmingly more likely: if T1 were true, there would be vastly more minds in exactly her epistemic situation, and she should treat herself as likelier to have arisen in the bigger pool. Bostrom dubs this character the Presumptuous Philosopher, and nearly everyone's first reaction is that something has gone badly wrong — a theory should not be confirmed a trillionfold merely by positing more observers. The trouble is that the reasoning behind her declaration is precisely the Self-Indication Assumption, the principle that a hypothesis implying more observers gains evidential support from one's own existence. That same principle is also what defuses the Doomsday argument, the gloomy inference that our relatively early birth rank among humans suggests we shouldn't expect humanity to have a long future. The rival principle, the Self-Sampling Assumption — reason as though you were a random sample drawn from the observers in some reference class — blocks the Presumptuous Philosopher but walks straight into the Doomsday argument and into the notoriously unresolved question of how the reference class is supposed to be drawn, since different choices yield different conclusions. Each assumption, in short, buys its way out of one embarrassment by purchasing another. The same fork reappears in other cases, including the halfer/thirder dispute over Sleeping Beauty, where the two principles line up on opposite sides.

So here is my question, and I'd like a number with its derivation. What is your credence that the Self-Indication Assumption, or something that behaves like it, is the correct principle of anthropic reasoning — right enough that you would stake a print verdict on it over the Self-Sampling Assumption? Please decompose the credence into the sub-questions it rests on: for instance, the probability that anthropic reasoning poses a genuine epistemic problem at all, rather than dissolving into ordinary Bayesian conditioning once indexical information is handled properly; conditional on the problem being real, the probability that the Self-Indication style of reasoning wins — for example, that thirding is correct in Sleeping Beauty; the probability that the presumptuous conclusion about T1 and T2 is a genuine cost rather than a bullet worth biting, perhaps because theory pairs of that shape never actually arise in science; the probability that the Self-Sampling Assumption's dependence on reference classes is fatal to it; and any further terms you think belong, such as whether fine-tuning and multiverse arguments in cosmology count as evidence one way or the other. Give each term a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into your overall number, and identify the term you are least confident in and why.

Finally — my editor rightly insists that any verdict we print must confess its exposure — tell me what would change your mind. Which single argument or consideration, if it succeeded, would move your number most, and where in your own decomposition do you think the reasoning is most likely to fail? If your credence would differ between the technical claim and a cautious, print-friendly formulation of it, say so and explain the gap.
