I've come to accept a higher-order theory of consciousness: a mental state is conscious only because some further mental state represents it — the awareness that makes a state conscious is itself a mental state directed at the first one, not some intrinsic glow the first state has on its own. Tell me what would count against this view. Beyond the familiar regress worry, I want to know which observations or findings would genuinely damage it: for example, evidence that subjects are in states they sincerely deny being in, or report awareness of states that careful first-order measures suggest they lack. Include the misrepresentation problem — on this view a higher-order state could in principle make me "conscious" of a pain I'm not actually in, and I want to know whether that consequence is a real liability or an innocuous idealization. Also assess the theory's available retreats: from actual higher-order thought to merely dispositional representation, or toward same-order views on which the first state represents itself. Do these amount to qualifying the view into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect in a theory of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and indicate which of them a committed higher-order theorist would concede as counting against the view and which they would wave off.
