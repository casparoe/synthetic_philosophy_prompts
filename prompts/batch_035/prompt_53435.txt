I sit on the advisory board of a program that prepares newly appointed civil servants for office — not cabinet ministers facing ticking-bomb emergencies, but mid-level officials who will routinely face pressure to shade the truth, bend rules, and make trade-offs their personal morality condemns. We're deciding what to build into the ethics component, so rather than a survey of positions, I want a prioritization of the open questions in the dirty-hands debate. Candidates I'm aware of, roughly: whether genuine dirty-hands cases are truly paradoxical (that it is both right and wrong to do the deed) or merely tragic; whether the moral residue involved is a distinctive kind or just agent-regret under another name, such that the standard treatment of tragic choice absorbs the whole phenomenon; whether dirty hands is essentially exceptional or can be routine — which matters most for our audience; whether institutions can distribute or dilute the dirt, as on the thought that a well-designed division of labor lets one set of hands decide while another executes; and whether the agent is ever restored by acknowledgment, confession, or punishment, or whether that whole washing question is confused from the start. For each question you prioritize, explain why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable by philosophy rather than by institutional design or psychology, and what covering it in the curriculum would actually change in an official's conduct. Then tell me which questions are overrated for our purposes: in particular, does the classic framing that leans on Machiavelli and on Weber's contrast between an ethic of responsibility and an ethic of conviction earn its place for people who will never wield sovereign power, or is it a distraction from the routine cases? I want a defended ranking, not a bibliography.
