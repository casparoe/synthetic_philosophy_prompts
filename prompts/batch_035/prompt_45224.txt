I should say up front that I spent forty years in radio astronomy — pulsar timing, mostly — before retiring, and that I have spent the years since reading philosophy of science with a small group. A puzzle from that group is what I want your help with. Please take the role of an expert philosopher of science, think carefully, and begin with some brainstorming: lay out the candidate rules you can see before you apply any of them to the new cases.

One member of our group, a retired virologist and by far our most committed Popperian, has been issuing rulings on whether a given theory counts as scientific. Her track record so far:

- Einstein's general relativity as it stood in 1915, before any of the classic tests had been performed: scientific.
- Freudian psychoanalysis: not scientific.
- Hoyle's steady-state cosmology as it was still being defended in 1960: scientific — even though she knows perfectly well it eventually lost to the Big Bang.
- Lysenko's account of the inheritance of acquired characteristics: not scientific.
- The popular evolutionary-psychology habit of explaining whatever mating pattern turns up, always after the fact: not scientific.
- Newspaper horoscope astrology: not scientific.

She has promised rulings next month on three further cases, and I would like to predict them in advance:

1. A grand-unification-style model that predicts proton decay, but with a mean lifetime around 10^39 years — so that the predicted decays could not plausibly be seen by any detector anyone could build.
2. A modification of Einstein's equations that departs from general relativity only in gravitational waves below about 10^-5 Hz, frequencies at which detection would require an interferometer with a baseline wider than the Earth–Sun distance.
3. A model from my own field: a few years ago it predicted a characteristic irregularity in the arrival times of millisecond pulsars; when the decade-long datasets came in and showed nothing, its proponents introduced what they call a "dark wind" of particles whose density they are free to set as they please, so that the model can now be made to fit any data at all — though on paper the original version remains falsifiable.

What I would like from you: first, the rules that fit her track record. I can make a rough guess at two of them — something like falsifiability in principle, and something like issuing risky predictions that can be checked with instruments available in the near term — and I suspect her astrology ruling points to a third that has to do with how proponents behave once refuted. For each rule you consider, tell me what it predicts for each of the three cases; then give your best guess for each case, with your reasons; and finally, tell me which further case, if she were to rule on it, would best tell the rival rules apart. One observation to weigh as you go: her steady-state ruling suggests that for her, "scientific" and "correct" have come apart entirely, so rules that turn on whether a theory is currently believed or currently confirmed are probably not what she is running. For my part, the three predictions matter less than the reasoning that connects her track record to them.
