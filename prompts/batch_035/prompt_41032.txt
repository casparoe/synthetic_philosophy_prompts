You are a philosopher of law with a strong background in game theory and the economics of cooperation. I want you to examine a practice in antitrust law and reconstruct the theory of joint action it implicitly rests on. Please think carefully, and begin your response with a short brainstorming section listing candidate views before committing to any of them.

Here is the practice. Under Section 1 of the Sherman Act, American antitrust law makes agreement the threshold concept: competing firms that explicitly agree to fix prices commit a serious criminal offense, while competitors who arrive at the same high prices without any communication commit no offense at all. If each of three airlines independently watches the others' posted fares, matches any cut, and restores the supracompetitive price when rivals do, the result for consumers can be identical to — or worse than — a formal cartel's, yet there is nothing to prosecute. Courts have long held that this kind of parallel behavior, often called conscious parallelism, cannot by itself establish an unlawful conspiracy; prosecutors need some additional evidence of a meeting of minds — a phone call, a signal exchanged at a trade association, prices announced in a way that only makes sense as an invitation. Meanwhile, the very economic theory that antitrust agencies and courts use everywhere else — oligopoly theory, the iterated prisoner's dilemma — says that durable collusion among a few repeated players is exactly what you should expect in the absence of communication: tit-for-tat, grim-trigger strategies, and price-leadership conventions can sustain cooperative prices among firms that never exchange a word and never form any joint intention, each simply maximizing its own discounted payoff given what the others have done. The line the law draws thus appears to put all the wrong things on either side of it: a cartel that agrees and then collapses in cheating gets prosecution; tacit colluders who actually hold prices up for decades get a pass.

Write a careful essay that does three things. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of joint action and responsibility the doctrine presupposes: does the law's distinction only make sense on a view on which blameworthy cooperation requires a communicated meeting of minds, or are there other candidate views — an evidentiary rather than metaphysical rationale, a fairness-based entitlement of firms to set prices unilaterally, a concern about deterring ordinary competitive monitoring — that would license the same line? Second, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: is the agreement requirement doing genuine philosophical work, or is it an administrable proxy for something structural that the law could not articulate when the doctrine was formed? Third, say what antitrust enforcement would have to become if we replaced the agreement-based line with the game-theoretic one — if we took seriously the idea that market structure (few sellers, repeated interaction, transparent prices) rather than communication is what makes cooperation emerge. Confront the hardest objection to the structural view honestly: on that view, is merely operating as one of three firms in a concentrated market a kind of ongoing collusion, and can any legal system punish a firm for participating in an equilibrium it cannot unilaterally escape? Finish by taking a clear position on whether the existing doctrine is philosophically coherent, merely pragmatic, or incoherent, and defend that verdict against the most natural objection to it.
