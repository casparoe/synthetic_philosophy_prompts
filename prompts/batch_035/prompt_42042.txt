The exam question read as follows:

The doomsday argument was first formulated by the astrophysicist Brandon Carter in the early 1980s and was later elaborated and defended by the philosopher John Leslie. It reasons from the number of humans who have lived so far — on the order of tens of billions — to a conclusion about how many humans will ever live. (a) Reconstruct the argument, making explicit the sampling assumption it makes about how you should regard your own birth rank, and show how the conclusion is supposed to follow. (b) Explain what the self-indication assumption (SIA) says, how it is supposed to counter the doomsday argument, and give one reason why a rational person might nonetheless be reluctant to endorse SIA. (c) Does the choice of reference class — all human beings, all observers whatsoever, only those humans who are aware of the doomsday argument — undermine the argument? Defend your answer.

One student wrote:

(a) Carter's argument, later taken up and elaborated by Leslie, rests on the self-sampling assumption (SSA): each observer should reason as though they were a random sample drawn from the set of all observers in their reference class. Carter and Leslie were building on Richard Gott's Copernican argument, published in 1993, which relies on the same sampling assumption. Roughly 60 billion humans have been born before me, so my birth rank is around 60 billion. If the total number of humans who will ever live were in the trillions, the fraction of observers at my rank or earlier would be minuscule, and finding myself there would be surprising; on hypotheses where the total is only a few times the number already born, my rank is unremarkable. Bayes's theorem therefore instructs us to shift probability mass away from hypotheses with vast totals and toward hypotheses with modest ones, so the future of humanity is likely to be much shorter than we might otherwise have thought.

(b) The self-indication assumption says that, other things equal, one should assign higher prior probability to hypotheses on which there are more observers, since a hypothesis with more observers gives you more chances of existing at all. Under SSA, a large future population makes your early birth rank surprising and so counts as evidence against large totals; under SIA, those same hypotheses receive a compensating boost in the prior, because more observers means more chances for you to be one of them. The two effects cancel each other exactly, so the evidence of your birth rank neither raises nor lowers the probability of a large future. In fact the cancellation is so clean that SIA does not merely defuse the doomsday argument — it reverses it, leaving us more confident than we were before that the future contains an enormous number of observers. Still, one might hesitate to endorse SIA, since it seems to let us settle empirical questions a priori: in Bostrom's story of the presumptuous philosopher, SIA lets you favor the theory with more observers without making any observations at all, and that cost may be too high to pay.

(c) The reference class problem is often called the doomsday argument's Achilles' heel, but I think it does less damage than advertised. Since the argument concerns the future of the human species, the correct reference class is simply all human beings, past and future. In any case the choice hardly matters: whether you widen the class to include all observers — extraterrestrials, future machine minds, and so on — or shrink it to only those humans who have heard of the doomsday argument, your birth rank within the class stays fixed, and so does the ratio of observers before you to observers after you. The posterior distribution over the total comes out the same on every choice of reference class, so there is no independent problem for the argument here.
