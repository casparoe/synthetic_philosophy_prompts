I'm TA-ing an intermediate decision theory course for econ and philosophy students (about 18 people, one 75-minute section, no budget for real payouts). Next week we cover subjective probability and Savage-style foundations, and I want to open with an activity where they discover ambiguity aversion in themselves before I name it or show them Ellsberg's urns on a slide. My rough idea is some kind of betting game with two urns — one where the composition is known (say, exactly 50 red / 50 black) and one where it's genuinely unknown, maybe filled in advance by someone they have reason to think isn't out to get them, or maybe by a classmate, I'm torn on that — and they have to actually commit to bets, in a way where their choices get scored and tabulated so the preference reversal shows up in their own aggregated data rather than just in hypothetical polling.

A few things I'd like you to handle. First, the mechanics: how to run the bets without real money (I can use course tokens, extra-credit raffle tickets, or candy), how to elicit choices cleanly enough that I can reconstruct each person's implied probabilities for events involving the unknown urn, and how to set it up so at least one participant visibly realizes mid-game that they're treating "unknown composition" as worse than a deliberately bad known composition, not just different from a fair one. Second, I want a twist round that separates ambiguity aversion from plain risk aversion — maybe comparing bets on the unknown urn against bets on a known urn with an unattractive but certain mix — so they can see that it's not just variance avoidance in a costume. Third, I've been toying with letting one group design the unknown urn in a later round, so the class has to confront whether their aversion depends on believing an adversarial opponent chose the composition versus it being random, which I gather was Ellsberg's own hunch about what drives the effect.

For the debrief, I want to map specific moments in the game onto the formal machinery — where exactly the choices violate the sure-thing principle or Dutch book coherence, what it does to the idea of a unique subjective prior, and how this connects to the maxmin / Gil-Schmeidler style response where people hold sets of priors instead of one. And please be honest about the limits: which parts of the theoretical debate (e.g., whether this is just a failure to randomize, or the status of the "Ellsberg choices are irrational" claim) the activity can't possibly settle and I'll have to just assert or discuss afterwards. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing things like people trying to game the token system, or the sample being small enough that the numbers look noisy — and what to do about each. If it matters, the room has a whiteboard and a projector but no clickers, so any elicitation scheme has to work with raised hands or slips of paper.
