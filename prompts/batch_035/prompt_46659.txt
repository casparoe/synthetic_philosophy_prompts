I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar on the welfare state, and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on benefit conditionality and the 'undeserving poor' that cannot be answered by reciting the standard positions — Rawlsian redistribution, Nozick's objections to redistributive taxation, the welfare-dependency debate, or a survey of the labels 'new paternalism' and 'workfare'. Students will have 90 minutes and roughly 1,200 words. Do not require them to cite specific texts or name specific authors beyond the major figures; the question should be answerable on the strength of the reasoning alone.

The question must be built around a concrete fictional policy so that students have to do applied reasoning rather than survey writing. Use this scenario: a government proposes a two-tier benefit. Tier One is an unconditional basic payment available to all low-income residents. Tier Two is a substantial supplement conditioned on demonstrated job-search compliance, verified by a partially automated system. The system's known error profile is asymmetric: it very rarely pays the non-compliant, but fairly often sanctions the compliant — missed appointments caused by shift work, childcare, or administrative delay are recorded as non-compliance, and appeals take months. The government's white paper defends the supplement with two distinct rationales, used interchangeably: (a) a fairness rationale, under which conditionality protects taxpayers from paying people who could work and won't; and (b) a paternalistic rationale, under which the behavioral requirements benefit recipients themselves, by preserving work habits and preventing the long-term scarring of unemployment.

The question should present this scheme and ask students whether it can be justified, and if so, under which rationale and with what modifications. But the real crux — which the question should not give away, though it should be fully extractable from the scenario — is that the two rationales come apart on the error asymmetry. Under the fairness rationale, false negatives (sanctioning the compliant) are a minor cost and arguably a feature: the system errs in the direction of not paying the undeserving. Under the paternalistic rationale, false negatives are a serious wrong, since they harm precisely the people the rationale claims the policy serves. A top answer must notice that the government cannot consistently invoke both rationales while retaining the asymmetric system, and must say what follows: which rationale should be dropped, how the error costs would have to be recalibrated under the survivor, and whether any recalibration is stable once the other rationale is abandoned.

The question should also require students to confront the deserving/undeserving distinction itself, not just its administration: whether that distinction is doing classificatory work that survives even a perfectly accurate verification system — i.e., whether the distinction, rather than its misapplication, is the moral problem — or whether it marks a genuine moral difference between, say, inability and refusal that an egalitarian can and should accept. This gives students who want to attack the whole framework a route that is more than "stigma is bad," and it gives defenders of conditionality a real burden: to say what the distinction tracks that isn't just a judgment about the claimant's character.

Deliverables: the exam question as students would see it; a model answer of about 600 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks with five or six named must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note for each on how a B answer typically misses it. The marking scheme must be neutral between answers that defend conditionality and answers that reject it: the A/B separation should turn on whether the answer (i) spots the divergence between the two rationales and works out the opposing directions of acceptable error cost, (ii) treats the asymmetry as a structural feature of the choice between rationales rather than a technical bug to be fixed, and (iii) gives a substantive account of what, if anything, the deserving/undeserving distinction tracks — not on how many positions from the syllabus are mentioned. Answers that merely survey the literature on workfare, or that assume without argument that conditionality is either obviously justified or obviously cruel, should cap out at a B regardless of polish.
