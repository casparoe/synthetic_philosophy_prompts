I am a novelist, presently at work on my fifth book, which is narrated by a fictional amateur lepidopterist cataloguing a single genus of moths in the western Highlands in the years between the wars. The novel's emotional burden is her dawning suspicion that the entities to which she has given her life — species — may not be there for anything to be given to. In connection with the book I have written a long essay, part research and part confession, which a literary review has agreed to publish. Before I sign the proofs I would like scrutiny from someone with philosophical training rather than the usual literary courtesy. I am asking for detailed comments of three kinds: on the accuracy of the historical and biological claims, which I assembled from reading and may well have got wrong in places; on whether the argument of the middle sections actually supports the conclusion I draw from it; and on whether the final turn — from the philosophy to my character's fate — is earned. Please be unsparing; I would rather be corrected before publication than after.

The relevant sections of the essay follow.

When I began the novel, I assumed the species in my character's cabinets were as fixed as the pins that held them. The philosophers of biology have relieved me of that comfort. For most of the twentieth century, biology ran on Ernst Mayr's biological species concept: a species is a group of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations, reproductively isolated from all other such groups. It is a beautiful definition — it makes a species a happening rather than a thing — but it has an awkward limit, for it applies only to organisms that reproduce sexually. Since bacteria have no sex, and have never had it, the definition that governed the century of biology cannot govern the greater part of life.

Mayr himself drew the boldest conclusion from this. Species, he came to argue, are not classes of things at all but individuals — historical particulars, like the Roman Empire or a family, with a birth, a career, and a death, extended in space and time rather than defined by a shared essence. If that is right, my moths are not members of their species, as a citizen is a member of a nation; they are parts of it, as my thumb is part of me. And nothing composes a class in that way: a class is merely a set of things that share a property.

The clincher, for me, is the ring species. Around the northern hemisphere, the herring gulls form a chain of populations, each interbreeding freely with its neighbours, until the chain closes in Europe — where the two ends meet and refuse each other. On the definition above, the gulls are one species and not one species in the same breath. Nor is this a gull's eccentricity: the Ensatina salamanders of the California hills are said to tell the same story. What the rings reveal, I believe, is not an oddity of nature but its ordinary condition. The living world is not an archipelago of island populations; it is a delta of gene flow, and the species we name are simply the channels we happen to be able to see from where we stand.

Darwin saw it first and saw it whole. He held that the varieties cluttering a naturalist's notebooks are incipient species, differing from what the books call species only in degree, and concluded that specieshood carries no essence — that the term is applied for convenience, as the term genus is applied for convenience. My lepidopterist, accordingly, has no way of knowing, from the moths themselves, which of her species are incipient and which are senescent; the world does not wear its divisions on its sleeve.

It follows, I think, that species do not exist. Biologists have proposed more than twenty definitions of species, and each carves the living world at different joints — joints which cannot all be real. A world that tolerated twenty incompatible but equally serviceable anatomies of itself would be a world in which the anatomy was ours, not the world's. My character's moths are real; her species are a filing system. The tragedy of the novel is that this does not stop her, or me, from loving the files.

Three specific worries. First, in the second section I attribute the species-as-individuals argument to Mayr, and I have a nagging feeling I first encountered it somewhere else; is the attribution as it stands defensible, or have I misassigned a view to its author? Second, does the ring species section overstate what the phenomena show, either in the particulars or in the generalisation drawn from them? And third, does the final section's inference — from the disagreement among species concepts to the non-existence of species — actually follow from anything I have established, or does it quietly trade on an ambiguity? If there are further errors I have not asked about, I would be glad to hear of those too.
