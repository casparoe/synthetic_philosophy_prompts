Design a thought experiment for a philosophy of biology unit on the dispute between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. The case should revolve around a single realistic trait with two distinct physiological effects, engineered so that the two accounts deliver partly overlapping and partly divergent verdicts about what the trait is *for* — and the divergence must fall out of the theories themselves, not be stipulated in the setup.

Specification for the main case: the trait must be ancient (present in the lineage for many generations, not newly arisen by mutation), physiologically ordinary (a protein, an organ, a structure, or a behavior — no engineered organisms, no accidental duplicates, no science-fictional biology). One of its effects, call it A, must have a genuine selection history: it was selected for in the lineage. The other effect, call it B, must currently contribute to some organized capacity of the organism and be fitness-enhancing now, but must never have been selected for — not directly, and not via any story an etiological theorist could plausibly reconstruct. The case must not be a vestigial organ, a spandrel-like byproduct that merely happens to be useful, or a trait that has become newly beneficial because the environment changed. This rules out the textbook examples; I want something where the temptation is to say B is just obviously one of the trait's functions, and only the etiological account's historical requirement says otherwise.

After presenting the scenario, do the following: (1) Walk through the verdict each account delivers — which functions it assigns, which it withholds, and the reasoning on each side. (2) Identify the structural feature of the case that does the work, and tell me what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed — specifically, if we extend the selection history so that B, like A, was selected for. (3) Attempt the reverse case: a trait that the etiological account counts as functional for some effect E while the causal-role account denies it has any function at all. Either produce a realistic scenario meeting this specification, or argue that no such case can be constructed without effectively stipulating the verdict, and explain what this asymmetry — if you find one — reveals about the respective commitments of the two accounts (for instance, about the direction in which each can drift from our intuitive function attributions). (4) Close with the strongest countermove available to the losing theory in each direction: how would a defender of the etiological account resist your main case, and how would a defender of the causal-role account resist your reverse case or your claim that no such case exists? Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph; the analysis can take as much space as it needs.
