Write an essay on whether cognition penetrates perception — that is, whether what we believe, desire, or expect can literally change how things look, sound, or taste, rather than merely changing where we direct attention or what we judge after seeing. Anchor the essay in three kinds of case, drawn from real strands of the empirical literature: (i) the finding that identical wine is reported as tasting better when labeled with a higher price; (ii) reports that faces displaying threatening expressions are perceived as physically closer than they really are, or that hills look steeper to the fatigued and encumbered; and (iii) expert perceptual learning, as with the radiologist who genuinely seems to see a lesion where a novice sees only noise. For each case, press the question of whether it is best explained by genuine penetration, by pre-perceptual shifts in attention, or by post-perceptual judgment contaminating the report — and note which alternatives the experimental designs actually rule out. Your essay must also engage what Firestone and Scholl call the El Greco fallacy: the point, borrowed from the old art-historical error of explaining El Greco's elongated figures by astigmatism, that if a psychological state distorted the perceived world, it would typically distort the standard of comparison along with the thing compared, so the net distortion should cancel out — and, applied to experiments, that top-down effects should not survive when the experimenters ensure both stimulus and standard are equally affected. Assess how damaging this challenge really is: does it show that the studies it targets fail, or only that they were run on a wrong implicit model of how penetration would work?

Reach a definite conclusion — either that at least one of the three cases amounts to genuine cognitive penetration, or that none do — and defend it rather than reporting a standoff. A purely definitional resolution ("it depends what you mean by perception") is acceptable only if you argue that fixing the definition is itself the substantive question at issue. Close the essay with a brief coda stating how confident you are in your verdict, expressed in your own terms, and identifying the single step in your argument that you regard as least secure, together with what evidence would most change your mind about it.
