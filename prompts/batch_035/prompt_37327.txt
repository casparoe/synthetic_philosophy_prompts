I'm a philosophy graduate student who has just started working on objective probability. In our seminar, a classmate presented a putative counterexample to the Principal Principle. The case: an agent knows the objective chance of some event — say, that a particular coin lands heads with chance 0.5 — but she also holds higher-order evidence that the chance assignment itself is untrustworthy: the physicist whose calibration fixed the 0.5 has just been exposed as having fudged every calibration of her career, and the agent has independent, though defeasible, indications that this case is no exception. The classmate argued that rationality does not require this agent to set her credence in heads to 0.5. Our professor waved the case away with the proverbial line that chance is the guide of life: once you know the chance, conforming your credence to it is what rationality demands, and anything else is just confusing your own degrees of belief with the world's propensities. The seminar moved on; I wasn't satisfied.

I don't think that one-liner can bear the weight the professor put on it, but I can't say exactly where it gives way. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making in this setting. At a minimum I'd want the menu to separate readings along these lines — but go beyond them if you see more: (a) a platitude to the effect that if you know the chance and have no other relevant information, matching your credence to it cannot be a mistake; (b) the Principal Principle itself as a substantive norm, leaving a genuine question of whether higher-order evidence about the chance's pedigree counts as inadmissible information; (c) a definitional reading on which chance just is whatever rational agents defer to, so that the counterexample is ruled out by fiat; (d) a decision-theoretic reading about which bets it is prudent to accept; (e) anything else the phrase might be smuggling in. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or unstable when applied to the seminar case.

Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually license the professor's dismissal — in particular, whether any reading can distinguish between deferring to a chance you know and deferring when you also hold evidence about the reliability of the process that fixed the chance. And flag explicitly if the only reading that gets him his conclusion is the definitional one, since then the slogan isn't guiding anything; it's changing the subject.
