I'm taking a philosophy of science course this term and we just did a couple of weeks on inference to the best explanation, but I still feel shaky on what the view actually commits you to. Then, as luck would have it, a friend sent me a podcast episode on "how scientists actually reason," and their bit on IBE is now competing with the lectures in my head. The host put it roughly like this: "Inference to the best explanation was invented by Charles Sanders Peirce, who called it abduction and defined it as the permission to jump from any puzzling fact to whatever hypothesis strikes you first. In its modern form it's simple: you gather your surprising facts, line up the candidate explanations, and pick the simplest one, on the principle that nature prefers simplicity -- essentially Occam's razor wearing a lab coat. Darwin is the textbook case: he noticed species were well adapted to their environments and chose the least complex explanation available to a Victorian gentleman. But the fatal flaw, which critics spotted early, is that explaining something beautifully and being true are two different things, so at best IBE hands you the most satisfying story, never a reason to actually believe it. That's why philosophers of science have by and large abandoned it in favour of Bayesianism."

I suspect the host is being a bit loose, partly because in class we spent a lot of time on why Darwin is supposed to be a *strong* case for IBE rather than an illustration of picking the easiest option, and partly because my lecturer treats IBE and Bayesian confirmation as living side by side rather than one replacing the other. But some of it sounds right to me -- the simplicity point especially, since in my stats course we were told that simpler models tend to generalize better, which seems like it could vindicate the "nature prefers simplicity" bit, or maybe not, I honestly can't tell anymore.

Could you go through the host's characterization piece by piece and tell me which parts a defender of IBE would accept, which are sloppy but forgivable popularizations, and which are just wrong? I'd especially like to know (a) whether the Peirce attribution is doing any work or whether abduction and IBE are actually different things that got merged together somewhere along the way, (b) whether "best" in inference to the best explanation really just means "simplest," or whether it's some broader notion of explanatory virtue, and (c) whether the "explaining well isn't evidence of truth" objection is a knockdown refutation or a live debate with rebuttals on the table. And finally, does the host's conclusion -- that IBE gives you stories but never reasons for belief, and that this is why everyone moved to Bayesianism -- follow from whatever true bits of his summary survive your corrections?
