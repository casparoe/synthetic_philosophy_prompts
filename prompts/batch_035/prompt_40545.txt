I co-teach an upper-level seminar on environmental ethics, cross-listed with conservation biology — 14 students, a single 3-hour block, which I can split however the design needs. The seminar unit is on the ethics of intervening in wild animal suffering, and my worry is that students treat it as either a curiosity ("of course nature is cruel, that's just how it is") or a slogans contest between "compassionate conservation" and "nature knows best." What I want is an activity in which they run into the real structure of the problem themselves, before we name any positions: the sheer arithmetic of r-selected species (a moth laying hundreds of eggs, nearly all offspring dying before maturity), the epistemic opacity of interventions (a trophic cascade can make a well-meaning act backfire in ways you cannot see beforehand), and the genuine conflict between aggregate welfare and ecosystem-level values that don't reduce to anyone's welfare.

Please act as an experienced facilitator and instructional designer with a background in both philosophy and ecology, and design the full session for me. My suggestion — which you should feel free to improve or replace — is a role-played grant committee: a fictional regional conservation fund has money to allocate on a managed wetland reserve, and students, playing distinct roles (a welfare biologist sympathetic to intervention, a conservation scientist worried about unintended ecological consequences, a holist who values the integrity of the ecosystem itself, an animal rights theorist uneasy about humans deciding which animals live and die, and a funder's representative who answers to a board), must choose among three concrete, fully specified proposals. I'd like at least one proposal to involve an intervention whose welfare benefits and ecological costs pull in opposite directions — for instance, releasing a parasitoid wasp against an invasive moth, where the wasp's larvae consume caterpillars alive — so that no option is cleanly dominant. Include a numerical component: simple life-table or population figures that let students compute, however roughly, the scale of death involved under each proposal, since I've found the numbers do argumentative work that words can't. I'd prefer no real money as payoffs; use points, tokens, or allocation voting instead. Rules, materials, a phase-by-phase timeline, and a decision mechanism are all needed.

The debrief matters most: it should map specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual terrain — the asymmetry between a charismatic deer and ten thousand anonymous moth larvae, the difference between valuing suffering reduction and valuing ecosystems as wholes, the way uncertainty about consequences ratchets up the moral stakes rather than excusing inaction — without requiring that anyone name any author or school of thought. Please also tell me honestly and concretely what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students collapsing into obvious coalitions, gaming the numbers, treating the holist role as comic relief), and how I should handle each. Finally, be frank about the central feature of wild animal suffering that no classroom simulation can reproduce — the many-orders-of-magnitude difference between the deaths in the scenario and the ones actually occurring in the world — and tell me how, in the last ten minutes, I should supply that missing scale myself rather than pretend the activity generated it.
