I teach an upper-level seminar on rational choice for a mixed group of philosophy and economics students — about 18 of them, one 90-minute session — and we're about to reach the sunk cost material. By this point they've seen the standard treatment enough times that they can recite it: past unrecoverable expenditures shouldn't figure in forward-looking choice, the theater-ticket and Concorde-style examples, escalation of commitment as a corporate pathology. What I want is a game that unsettles that recitation rather than confirming it. Specifically, I want participants to run into a situation where continuing to pour resources into a project they've already sunk money into *feels* like the classic fallacy from the inside, while the game's hidden structure makes it the right call — and, symmetrically, a situation where cutting losses cleanly feels virtuous but is actually the mistake.

Here's the design problem I'd like you to solve. Build a multi-round investment game in which teams allocate a fixed budget of tokens across projects whose quality is only partially revealed over time. The key feature: the game should have two phases or conditions that are experientially identical but structurally different. In one, past spending carries no information and no external consequence — so continuing is the textbook fallacy. In the other, sunk costs are informative (only projects that survived an earlier vetting round are eligible for further funding, or the team's own earlier choice screens the options, so their past selves' decisions are evidence about the current ones) or strategic (whether you quit is observed by partner teams who then decide whether to invest alongside you, so abandonment carries a reputational price). Participants should discover, in the debrief, that they generated the *same behavioral profile* in both conditions and that only the information structure differs — which is exactly where the simple "honoring sunk costs is always irrational" slogan breaks down. Payoffs in course tokens or grade-independent credit rather than money; keep materials to printed project cards, a log sheet per team, and a facilitator spreadsheet at most.

Beyond the rules and payoff scheme, I need three more things. First, a log-sheet design that lets me reconstruct, team by team, which continuation decisions were warranted given the hidden structure and which weren't — I want to be able to point at an individual decision in the debrief and say what the right call was there, and how many teams got it. Second, a frank section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: teams colluding, the information condition being too subtle to notice, participants behaving well because they know they're being observed in a game about irrationality, and whatever else you anticipate. Third, an honest note on which feature of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce — my own guess is the blame-avoidance and career-stakes structure that drives much escalation in organizations, where the person who cancels the project absorbs the loss and the person who rode it out doesn't — but I'd like your diagnosis, plus how the debrief should supply it. If you can also build in a way for some teams to over-correct — abandoning too readily, since the game's own incentive to look rational might push them past the optimum — that would round it out nicely.
