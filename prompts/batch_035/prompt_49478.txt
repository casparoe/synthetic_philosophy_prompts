Write a parody of the "Gifts and Hospitality" section of a multinational bank's anti-corruption compliance manual. It should consist of exactly five numbered clauses, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion; aim for roughly 300–400 words in total, and write it in British English.

The humour must come from getting the definitional problem right rather than from mere absurdity: each clause should quietly reveal how the line between a gift and a bribe is actually drawn in such manuals. Some ideas you might build on (choose your own rather than using all of them): the $800 client dinner at a conference is "relationship-building" while a home-cooked meal offered by a foreign official's family is a "potential inducement"; gifts the company gives are hospitality, gifts it receives are "exposure to risk"; hiring the minister's cousin is "local talent development"; a donation to a university whose dean sits on the procurement committee is "corporate social responsibility"; lobbying is "stakeholder engagement."

The piece should never openly acknowledge the anthropology it is suppressing — that virtually every culture treats gifts as things which create obligations, that reciprocity and clientelism shade into each other, and that the compliance regime quietly assumes a world of arm's-length strangers striking deals is the only legitimate world, exporting one culture's ethics as a universal standard. Let the final clause be the one where the exceptions swallow the rule (perhaps an exemption granted by a review board whose own meetings are expensed as client entertainment).

The satire should stay deadpan throughout, as though the compliance department genuinely believes it has solved the problem of corruption once and for all; a reader versed in the debates about clientelism and universalist anti-corruption norms should wince in recognition rather than roll their eyes, and a reader who isn't should still find it funny and legible.
