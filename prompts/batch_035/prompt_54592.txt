I've recently been appointed to a committee at my university that must decide, within the term, whether to rename a hall that honours a nineteenth-century benefactor whose fortune owed much to industries built on indentured labour, and who publicly defended those arrangements against the critics of his day. At our first meeting a colleague circulated a passage from a well-reviewed popular book on the ethics of judging the past, which she takes to settle the matter. It reads as follows:

"Presentism, properly understood, is not a vice but the historian's condition. The word names the practice of judging past actors by the standards of the present, and its critics have never come to terms with the fact that this is unavoidable. Historians write from inside their own conceptual world; they have no access to the past except through present categories; so every history is a presentist history, and the much-invoked distinction between merely using the present to frame one's questions and using it to deliver verdicts cannot bear the weight placed upon it. Nor is there anything to apologise for. The standards of the present are the only standards we have any reason to endorse; insisting that we judge a slaveholder only by standards he himself accepted would be letting the dead vote on our moral questions. What is called historicism is therefore not neutrality but the conservatism of an earlier present. In the end, the charge of presentism is simply what one generation of historians calls the moral advance of the next."

I confess I find this half-persuasive, particularly the inescapability argument, and the book has a reputation for seriousness. Another colleague who opposes the renaming says the passage is "glib", but I cannot tell whether that complaint is substantive or merely an expression of discomfort. Since the decision is partly mine to make, I would like a proper audit rather than a reaction. How much of this would a working historiographer accept, and where does it conflate things that careful historians keep apart — for instance, the sense in which all history is written from a present standpoint and the quite different sense in which it judges by present standards? Is the framing-versus-verdicts distinction really as fragile as the passage claims, and does the author leave out any middle positions? Which of the claims are modest truths stated persuasively, which are overstated, and which would a defender of the anti-presentist side regard as misdescribing their position? Finally, and most importantly for me: does the passage, once corrected, actually license the conclusion my colleague draws from it — that the benefactor should simply be judged by our standards, that any contextualisation amounts to special pleading, and that the renaming requires no further methodological defence? If some parts do not survive correction, I would like to know what distinction the committee actually needs in order to decide this well.
