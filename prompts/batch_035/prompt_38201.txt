After six years of watching from the sidelines, I was elected chair of my department's graduate admissions committee this autumn, and in the first full meeting I chaired I watched something I still have trouble believing happened. We had agreed on a procedure: score each shortlisted applicant against three criteria — writing sample, fit, preparation — vote yes or no on each, and advance only applicants who pass all three. On our first serious case, a majority of the committee judged the writing sample strong, a majority judged the fit good, and a majority judged the preparation adequate, and yet the applicant was rejected, with nobody remarking on the oddity, because the criterion-by-criterion results gave us no way to see that the three majorities did not overlap in any single person. When I drew the table on the whiteboard afterwards, one colleague said it was a trick of how I had presented it, and another insisted it was an artifact of our particular procedure and that no procedure would have done better. I want the next committee — and, more to the point, the graduate seminar on collective choice that I am teaching in the spring — to feel what happened rather than see it argued on a slide.

Design the session. It is a seminar of about fourteen students, one ninety-minute block, no lecture beforehand, and the standard names (discursive dilemma, doctrinal paradox, and anything in that family) withheld until the debrief. The students should not be told the point in advance; I want the reversal to ambush them. Specifically:

1. The game itself: a staged committee making real decisions. I would like at least one round in which students hold fixed, assigned judgment profiles — please construct the actual profiles, with three or four binary premises, a conclusion that is a conjunction of them, and between three and five assignments that yield majority-true premises together with a majority-false conclusion, with no individual holding an outright contradiction. I would also like a second round in which something other than the aggregation rule does the work: for instance an agenda effect, where the outcome depends on which question is put to the group first, or on whether the committee votes on the conclusion directly or is forced through the premises one at a time. Tell me exactly what materials I need, how votes should be recorded so the pattern is visible without becoming legible too early, and how to keep students from coordinating around the trap.

2. Stakes that are not money but are felt. Perhaps each student's standing in the game rises when the committee's final decision matches their own sincere judgment on the conclusion, so that premise-oriented and conclusion-oriented participants in the same round have structurally different interests, and the tension between thinking the applicant should be admitted and thinking the criteria were or were not met becomes the material of the activity rather than commentary on it. I am open to a better scheme if you have one, but I do not want it to collapse into a trivial majority-wins exercise.

3. The debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the formal distinctions — premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation, the gap between the group's verdict and every member's verdict, and, if you use the agenda round, how a sequence of votes can determine what the group believes without anyone changing their mind. I want the debrief to close by returning to my admissions meeting, so please give me a way to reconstruct what happened there from what the students have just experienced.

4. Failure modes: what will predictably go wrong on the first run. Students seeing through the design; abstentions; and, most important, discussion — real committees usually escape this problem by talking, and I suspect that if the students deliberate freely they will dissolve the dilemma by exchanging reasons before it ever bites. Tell me whether to forbid deliberation, and what is lost either way.

5. Honesty about limits: which feature of the problem no classroom game can reproduce. My suspicion is that in a real committee the judgment profiles are endogenous — they are shaped partly by the very procedure that then aggregates them, something a game with assigned profiles cannot fake. Tell me whether that is right, and what else the activity necessarily misses and I will have to supply in the debrief.
