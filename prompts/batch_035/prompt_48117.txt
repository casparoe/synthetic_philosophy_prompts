Our reading group has been going through big-picture history stuff -- we did Polybius, now Toynbee, and one guy keeps pushing Spengler on everybody. At the last meetup that friend used "history repeats itself" to shut down an entire discussion. His version: the West is in the late "civilization" phase, the pattern has played out for every culture thats ever existed, so arguing about whether particular policy choices matter is basically pointless -- winter comes whether or not you rake the leaves. I couldn't put my finger on what's wrong with that on the spot.

Here's my problem though: "history repeats itself" seems to be doing a bunch of really different jobs depending on who says it. When Polybius describes the anacyclosis (constitutions cycling through kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mob rule, and back around), is that even the same claim as when Ibn Khaldun says a dynasty rarely outlives three or four generations because group solidarity (asabiyyah) decays? Neither of those looks like Spengler's thing, where each culture lives out a roughly thousand-year life course through its own seasons. And none of them look like the boring sense in which people just mean "there are wars, then later more wars."

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims the proverb could be making -- running from the trivial reading up to the strong fatalist one my friend needs for his conclusion? For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and actually defensible, and whether it would genuinely support "decline is baked in and policy can't change it." I'd especialy like you to figure out where Toynbee fits, because from what I remember of A Study of History he's not really a straight cyclicalist -- challenge and response, creative minorities, that sort of thing -- and I want to know whether his version even belongs to the same family as the proverb, or whether it's quietly a different claim that the proverb flattens into the rest.

Give it to me as a list I can bring to the next meetup, with a short verdict on each reading.
