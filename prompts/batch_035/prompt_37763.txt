Next month I give a departmental colloquium defending the thesis that akrasia, as a philosophical problem, dissolves under the weight of hyperbolic discounting — and I need a Q&A prep sheet built for the specific audience that will be in the room.

My argument, in outline, since the objections should target it:

1. Behavioral premise: human delay discounting is roughly hyperbolic rather than exponential, which produces systematic preference reversals — I prefer finishing the chapter over an evening of wine when both are a week away, and prefer the wine when it is on the table — and these reversals are a stable feature of valuation, not noise or ignorance.
2. Diagnosis: Davidson's puzzle in "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" gets its grip only from the assumption that a single, temporally persisting agent bears a single evaluative judgment that intentional action can contravene. Drop the assumption that the person, rather than the time-slice, is the bearer of the preference ranking, and the supposed contradiction between judgment and action evaporates: at the moment of choice, the akrates is doing exactly what she most prefers.
3. Redescription: what remains after the dissolution is not a failure of practical rationality within an agent but a coordination problem among successive selves — an intimate contest for self-command. Self-control is therefore not a faculty of will but a matter of institutional design: precommitment, temptation-proofing one's environment, buying illiquidity. The akratic is not irrational; she is badly governed.
4. Residual claim I will concede needs defense: whether any standpoint privileges earlier selves' preferences as "the person's" own. My answer is a parity claim — no time-slice is privileged, "the person's good" is a construct over successive selves, like a social welfare function, and any weighting of those selves is a normative choice rather than a discovery.

The audience: a philosophy department with three philosophers of action trained on Davidson, for whom incontinence is a real and distinctive phenomenon of judgment rather than preference; a decision theorist who will be watching what happens to the word "rational"; and, because our colloquium is joint with the business school, two behavioral economists who work with the quasi-hyperbolic beta-delta discounting model and will have opinions about whether I have earned the right to use their machinery.

Please prepare the sheet as follows. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually face from this composition of the room — not the generic ones — ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumble it. Phrase each in the vocabulary of the sub-audience that would raise it: the philosophers will talk about all-things-considered judgment, acting intentionally, freely, and in the light of knowledge, and about whether I have merely redescribed compulsive behavior rather than akrasia; the economists will talk about time inconsistency, sophistication versus naivete about one's own future reversals, and commitment devices, and may well complain that what I am selling as a dissolution is something the literature settled into a modeling convention decades ago. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply I can deliver from the podium, plus a candid note on where the reply is soft and what I should concede rather than contest. Include at least one objection on which you think I should simply take the hit in front of this audience. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that aims at the weakest joint in my four-step sketch rather than at its surface — and say what my best available answer is even if it costs me something.
