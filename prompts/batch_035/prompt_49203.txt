I'm the registrar at a mid-sized natural history museum, and my team keeps getting stuck on a deceptively simple question: is this one thing or several things? We catalog objects, we insure them, we loan them out, and all three run on object counts. A fossil skeleton partly reconstructed in plaster and partly borrowed from a second individual of the same species -- one specimen or a mixture? A pair of earrings recovered from an archaeological dig, stored together for eighty years -- one record or two? A taxidermy mount whose original skull was swapped for a replica during a 1970s restoration -- still the same object the old catalog entry refers to, or a new one? Right now everyone just vibes it, and two of us will give different answers on the same item.

A friend of mine who did philosophy mentioned that there's a whole literature on this -- she called it the special composition question, which as far as I understand it is just: when do some things add up to one further thing, rather than being a pile? Apparently philosophers have fought about whether it's about the things being stuck together, or arranged so that they do something together, or whether there's no fact of the matter at all, or even the view that nothing ever composes anything bigger. I don't need the metaphysics, but I suspect the philosophy has real lessons for us about which questions actually have answers and which are just us choosing a convention for convenience.

What I want is a procedure an intern can run in twenty minutes per item: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with "one object" or "several objects," and crucially where it should stop and escalate to me or a curator because the question is a policy or values call rather than something with a fact of the matter hiding in it. It has to cover composition (many things, one thing) and identity over time (the thing survives change, but how much change), since our catalog entries conflate them constantly. Then run it on three actual cases from our current backlog: the composite fossil, the earring pair, and the taxidermy mount with the replica skull. At least one of these should come out the way I don't want it to -- I'm guessing the fossil, since I've been telling the insurer it's a single specimen for six years -- and I need the procedure to be honest about that rather than bending to my convenience.

Please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or anything, just like a long email I can forward to my team. And flag clearly which steps of the procedure are real judgments dressed up as checkboxes, because if we pretend it's mechanical the curators will never trust it.
