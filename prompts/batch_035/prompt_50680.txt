I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and my department houses a research institute that runs a small internal grant scheme. Twice a year, the institute awards "sustained-line" grants of £8,000–£15,000 to junior researchers pursuing a line of work over several years; applicants may reapply in successive rounds, so the scheme effectively decides which local research programmes to keep feeding and which to starve. The committee is six people, mostly empirical scientists, with me as the resident methodologist; we have one afternoon per round for eight to twelve proposals, every decision needs a one-paragraph justification sent to the applicant, and the institute director signs off on the final list. At present the decisions are made on vibes, and after a seminar I gave, the director has asked me to write a decision procedure grounded in Lakatos's methodology of research programmes — his idea being that we should ask whether a proposal's parent programme is progressing or degenerating rather than trading impressions about "impact." So the rubric should turn the standard apparatus to work: the hard core and protective belt, the positive and negative heuristic, theoretical and empirical progress, degenerating problem shifts, and the question of when a belt repair counts as ad hoc.

The translation is harder than it sounds, and I want the procedure to be honest about that rather than launder our intuitions through vocabulary. First, the obvious asymmetry: as I understand it, Lakatos himself granted that his methodology mostly adjudicates with hindsight, whereas we have to render verdicts prospectively on a decade of an applicant's future. Second, the ad hocness judgment: whether a new auxiliary postulate is a legitimate belt repair or a fudge is precisely the thing scientists dispute for years, so the procedure has to say who counts as a competent judge and what evidence they should point to. Third, the drought problem: a programme can sit in a long degenerating phase and then turn around — Lakatos made much of Prout's programme, whose central claim seemed refuted by the best atomic-weight measurements for roughly a century before coming right — so a rule that defunds anything degenerating for five years will periodically kill future successes. The procedure should state explicitly where it degrades back into committee judgment, and where the question in front of us is really one of values (say, whether the institute should hedge across several programmes or concentrate) that no methodology can settle.

Please run the finished procedure on three cases, using plausible invented details where needed: (1) an established programme with a strong past record of novel predictions, now ten years into a run of anomalies and unexplained residuals, asking for a further round; (2) a young programme with no novel predictions to its name, whose applicants argue its heuristic is unusually powerful and that its hard core will only become testable after several more years of work; and (3) a Prout-style case, where the programme's central claim is currently contradicted by the best measurements but keeps generating a steady stream of new sub-problems and refinements. I should confess my own priors: I am inclined to fund case (2) and defund case (1), and I half expect to want case (3) killed on instinct. I would like you to tell me plainly, for each, what the procedure actually yields — including whether it turns out that my priors, not the methodology, were doing the work all along — and to close by saying what the procedure cannot do.
