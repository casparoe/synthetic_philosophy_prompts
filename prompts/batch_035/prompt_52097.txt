I'm taking a philosophy of biology course, and we've just finished the unit on defining life. I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts on borderline cases, expecting them to add up to something, and I'd like your help finding the principle behind them, if there is one.

My tentative thought is that something is alive when it is a bounded, self-maintaining system carrying out its own metabolism and belonging to a lineage that evolves by natural selection. I'm not confident this survives my own cases, though:

- A healthy cat: alive, obviously.
- A virus drifting between hosts: not alive.
- The same virus once inside a cell and issuing instructions: the case I keep going back and forth on. Part of me wants to say the virus is merely the cell being sick; part of me wants to say it is, for that stretch, alive.
- A mule, sterile: alive.
- A worker honeybee, also sterile: alive; and, separately, I don't think the colony as a whole is a living thing — it's a lot of living things, the way a forest is.
- A plasmodial slime mold, one enormous cell crawling over a log and solving mazes: alive, and somehow more of an organism than the bee colony, which I can't justify.
- A seed that sat dried in a collection for a century and then germinated: alive throughout the century, I'd say.
- A bacterium recovered from tens-of-thousands-year-old permafrost and revived in a lab: alive the whole time too, but I notice I assented to this far more easily for the seed, and I can't say what changed.
- Prions: not alive, even though they replicate.
- A computer worm that copies itself across machines, occasionally with copying errors: not alive, and I don't feel the pull I feel with the virus.
- Red blood cells: here I genuinely waver — they're cells and they burn glucose, but they can't divide or repair, and I'm inclined to say a stored blood sample isn't quite alive.
- HeLa cells in culture: alive, and this is the verdict I find strangest, since the person they came from is dead.
- A bacterium living inside insect cells that has lost so many genes it cannot survive outside, with a genome smaller than some viruses: alive, and more confidently than the virus.
- A virophage — a virus that can only replicate inside another virus, itself only able to replicate inside an amoeba: not alive, but it feels closer than an ordinary virus, whatever that means.

Is there a single principle that captures all of this, or something near enough? If some of my verdicts have to go, I'd rather know which than receive a principle contorted to save them, so please be explicit about the tradeoff: the complex principle that preserves everything versus the simpler one that overturns a verdict. Once you've settled on a principle, I'd like its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: a brain-dead patient on a ventilator; a frozen embryo; a self-replicating protocell that copies itself faithfully, with no heritable variation; and a mitochondrion — whether its free-living ancestor was alive, and whether the mitochondrion in one of my cells is alive now.
