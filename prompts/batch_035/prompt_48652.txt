In a seminar on population ethics, a visiting speaker closed off discussion of whether choosing to have a child can make the world a better place by invoking the slogan 'making people happy, not making happy people' — the one Johann Frick used to title his defense of the procreation asymmetry, though the underlying contrast goes back to Parfit's work on the distinction between benefiting people and creating people who will be well off. The speaker treated the line as if it settled the question on the spot: since morality concerns making people happy, not making happy people, the decision to create a flourishing child adds no value to the outcome, and no one has a moral reason to have a child for the child's sake. I have the feeling the slogan is doing several different jobs at once, and that some of them are near-tautologies while others are the most contested claims in population ethics. Before I raise this at the next seminar, I want a disambiguation I can put on one page.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, as a list I can bring to class. For each reading, say whether it is a triviality (something no one who understood the words could deny), a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is false or incoherent, and say what the grounds for that verdict are. I would expect the readings to separate at least the following dimensions, but add or correct as you see fit: whether the slogan is a claim about what we have moral reason to do, about which actions wrong someone, or about which outcomes are better than which; whether 'people' means actual people, people who will exist regardless, or people whose existence depends on the choice; whether it applies only to choices that create new people or generalizes to all beneficence; and whether it is meant to be agent-relative or a claim about the impersonal value of outcomes. The readings I most need distinguished are the modest person-affecting one — that you cannot benefit someone by bringing them into existence, because there is no one to benefit before the choice — and the stronger evaluative one that a world containing additional flourishing lives is not, in virtue of that, a better world. The speaker's conclusion, as I understood it, needed the stronger one, but I want to be sure before pressing the point.

For each reading on your list, state explicitly whether it would actually support the speaker's use of the slogan: ending the question of whether creating a happy child improves the outcome. And close by telling me which reading the slogan's popularity most plausibly rests on — the trivial one that makes it feel obvious, or the contentious one that would make it do real work. If some readings have well-known counterexamples or place the slogan in tension with other commitments people sympathetic to it typically hold, such as concern for future generations, note that briefly. No bibliography; this is for my own use in seminar discussion.
