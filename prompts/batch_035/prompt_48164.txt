At my reading group last week, two members argued about a claim in a book one of them had been reading: that religion should be studied as a natural phenomenon, the way we study weather or disease. Maya and Dev went back and forth for several rounds and I honestly couldn't follow the last round at all, so I'd like a verdict. Maya began by insisting that studying the natural causes of religion is neutral: a causal explanation of why people believe in gods — say, as a byproduct of cognitive mechanisms that over-detect agency — says nothing about whether gods exist; explaining how a belief arose is not explaining it away, and treating a belief's origins as evidence against its truth is the genetic fallacy. Dev answered that explanations of this kind are never neutral because they are selectively deployed: we reach for this style of explanation only for beliefs we already take to be false (astrology, witchcraft) and never for those we take to be true, so a causal story about religion gets told only once someone has quietly decided religion is false — the debunking is smuggled in by the framing, not earned by the findings. Maya replied that her point concerned a different question: if belief in gods would have arisen whether or not gods exist — if the belief is insensitive to its own truth — then its evidential value is undermined no matter who deploys the explanation or when, just as a witness's identification counts for nothing once we learn he would have made the same identification regardless of what he saw. Dev countered that Maya is just assuming the mechanisms underlying religious belief are unreliable in a way perception isn't, and that this is an empirical question the causal explanation itself does not settle: on the hypothesis that something real is being detected, over-active agency detection looks like a cheap but usable detector with false positives, like a smoke alarm that also goes off when toast burns — annoying, not discredited. Maya's last word was that a detector that fires almost regardless of whether its object is present can't be "usable" in any sense that helps — if the alarm rings for toast, steam, candles, and smoke alike, its ringing tells you nothing — though she looked unsure whether that really settled things. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right at each stage, assess whether Maya's analogy to the witness or Dev's analogy to the smoke alarm holds up, and consider whether either of them is missing a way in which treating religion as a natural phenomenon could be neutral in one sense and hostile in another.
