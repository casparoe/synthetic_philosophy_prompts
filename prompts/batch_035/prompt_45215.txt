I run the mathematics club at my high school — about fourteen students, one 45-minute meeting — and next month I want to do a session on Simpson's paradox. My problem is that every presentation I've seen just shows people a table in which one treatment wins in every subgroup yet loses overall, and the reaction is a shrug: the arithmetic is trivial once you see it. I want them to feel the reversal before it's named. So I'd like you to design an activity in which the club members themselves generate the data — some competitive game with dice or cards, with a real but trivial prize (candy works) and a strategic choice at stake, where the winning strategy is genuinely better in both of two conditions yet the aggregate scores favor the losing one. The hard design constraint, which is where I keep failing, is that the reversal has to happen reliably and the members must not see the confounder while they're playing: they'd need to be split, without knowing it, into an easy round and a hard round, and the better strategy has to be disproportionately chosen in the hard round. Please give me the exact mechanics, the number of trials needed, a payoff scheme that works without real money, and a debrief that maps what they experienced onto the algebra — including the moment where I reveal the confounder and they realize what they were doing. Two more things: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular I suspect the deepest part of the paradox is that in real cases no one tells you which partition of the data is the legitimate one — whether the subgroups are real structure or cherry-picking — and I assume a club activity has to hand my members that knowledge for free. If there's a way to stage even a taste of that second problem, I'd like to hear it.
