A friend of mine who works on chess engines has an argument against satisficing, and it bothers me because it seems to prove too much: if it works, it takes down maximizing as well, and I cannot see which rung to kick out without falling off the ladder entirely.

Some background first. She points out that chess engines never search to the end of the game tree; they cut off at a fixed depth, prune most moves without evaluating them, and stop when they have found a move that is good enough by their evaluation. Search programmers even have a standard technique they literally call an aspiration window: rather than searching with fully open bounds, you search only around the score you expect and widen the window only if the answer falls outside it. So the strongest players we know of are bounded searchers who stop at "good enough," and she takes this as evidence that Herbert Simon was right to think satisficing is not a concession to weakness but the only rationality there is — a full maximizer, human or machine, is not an ideal but a fiction.

Her sparring partner, a committed maximizer, replied with the following argument, and this is what I want examined:

(1) To choose rationally is to choose for reasons that suffice — to be able to answer the demand "why this option rather than another?" with something adequate, and not merely adequate-by-my-lights.
(2) The satisficer's answer to that demand is that the option cleared her aspiration level: it was, by her own standard, good enough.
(3) Nothing in rationality dictates the height of the level; aspiration levels are set by the agent, and two agents with very different levels can both be fully rational. The satisficer insists this is the whole point of her view.
(4) A maximizer, then, is just a satisficer whose aspiration level happens to be set at the best available option; so if we are entitled to fault the low-level satisficer, we must be assessing her level from outside the theory — but the same external assessment applies to the maximizer's placement of her level at the top, which is equally unset by rationality.
(5) Therefore both satisficing and maximizing always supply sufficient reasons for choice, whatever the level — including the trivial level, so low that every option on the table clears it, in which case flipping a coin between the best and the worst option is exactly as rational as taking the best.
(6) But a theory of rational choice on which coin-flipping between the best and the worst option is no less rational than selecting the best draws no distinction that matters, and so is no theory of rational choice at all.
(7) Therefore satisficing fails as an account of rational choice — and, by (4), maximizing fails with it.

That conclusion is far too strong for me to accept: it says there is no substantive dispute between the satisficer and the maximizer and no theory of rational choice anywhere in the neighborhood. My suspicion is that something slips between (3) and (5), that "sufficient" slides from sufficient-relative-to-a-standard-the-agent-endorses to sufficient, period — but I cannot make that precise, and I notice that the same slide would indict every theory that lets agents set their own standards of adequacy, which is a lot of theories, so I suspect my suspicion is too easy.

Tell me which premise to reject and exactly what rejecting it costs. For what it is worth, I would like to keep (3): it really does seem to me that rationality does not dictate any particular aspiration level. And I would like to keep (6) as well. If keeping both requires rejecting (2) or (5), I want to see precisely what does the work there — in particular, whether the satisficer can have reasons that outrun her own level, and if so what those reasons are reasons for. Finally, tell me whether the chess-engine material is load-bearing or merely decorative: engines stop at "good enough" only relative to a horizon they cannot help but have, and I want to know whether that unavoidable boundedness is being smuggled into a claim about what suffices.
