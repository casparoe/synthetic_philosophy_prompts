quick background: I'm on the science & ideas desk at a magazine, and last month I got sent to cover a conference on the future of wellbeing -- futurists, some neuroscientists, a couple of philosophers, the usual mix. the closing panel had this suffering-focused ethics guy (negative utilitarian, he called himself, "or something in that neighborhood") who has been making the rounds arguing that we're massively misallocating our civilizational attention, and his whole case rested on one analogy that the audience really latched onto. he said the world is basically a giant hospital, not a festival. in a hospital, sure, the staff might enjoy the coffee and the banter, but nobody thinks the coffee is the point -- the point is the patients, the ones in pain, and everything else is at best recovery and at worst distraction. so, he said, any moral accounting that treats adding another happy person like it's on par with preventing one case of torture is like a hospital administrator who logs the staff parties as output. from there he drew two conclusions: (1) that reducing suffering isn't just the first priority but lexically first -- you don't trade any amount of it off against any amount of fun, the same way a hospital doesn't trade off surgeries against nicer vending machines, and (2) the spicier one, that in the limit the best achievable world might be one with no one in it, since a hospital that's empty has no patients and that's not a tragedy, that's everyones discharge day.

I wrote up the talk without endorsing anything, but my editor wants a follow-up piece interrogating the analogy itself, since it's now showing up in op-eds by people who heard about it secondhand. before I do that I want to actually stress-test it instead of just quoting people arguing past eachother. can you walk me through it: what's supposed to map onto what (hospital -> world, patients -> sufferers, what plays the role of the doctors, the treatment, the discharge?), where the mapping starts to creak -- I have a hunch that the fact a hospital is instrumental to something outside itself (the life you go back to after) is doing more work than he'd admit, since there's no "outside" for the world to discharge you to -- and then the key question for my piece: of the two conclusions above, does either one actually fall out of the analogy, or did he import it in with the framing? also curious whether there's a tweaked version of the same picture that a suffering-focused person could still use without smuggling in the lexical priority or the empty-hospital bit, since that'd be the charitable version of the panel I'd want to represent fairly. table of correspondences is fine, just keep it at a level I can steal for print.
