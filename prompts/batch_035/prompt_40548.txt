My friend Dana has strong opinions about which things matter morally, and over the years I've been keeping a list of her verdicts. Next dinner party I want to ambush her with two new cases, and I'd like to have my predictions ready beforehand so I can act smug when I'm right. Here's her track record as best I can reconstruct it:

- Cats obviously matter. Fish matter, she's settled on that. Insects she waffles on.
- Trees don't matter, even though they send chemical distress signals to each other. She says that's just chemistry, not suffering.
- Corporations don't matter. She's adamant about this one — they have goals, they act to preserve themselves, they're legal persons, and none of it counts for anything morally.
- An early embryo doesn't matter, and she's fine saying that out loud.
- A person in a permanent vegetative state: no moral status left. She says we still have duties to the family and gets annoyed when people conflate the two.
- One that surprised me: a person born completely unable to feel pain who also gets no pleasure from anything (some rare condition, the details don't matter) but who has friends and projects and cracks jokes — full moral status, zero hesitation.
- If we met silicon-based aliens with rich emotional lives: full moral status, obviously.
- Her grandmother's hypothetical whole-brain emultation — exact scan, run on computers: full status. "It's her," is her entire argument.
- The rat-neurons-in-a-dish playing pong thing (DishBrain, they did this a few years back): she says it matters a tiny bit, "weirdly," and that it matters more than the identical game played by ordinary software, though she can't articulate why.
- Split-brain patient — one morally relevant person or two: "ugh, both, probably, why are you asking me this."

So here are the two cases I'm planning to spring on her:

Case one: researchers build a rat connectome entirely in simulation. It's not scanned from any real rat — the wiring is grown and trained in silico until it statistically matches real rat brains, and when you apply noxious stimuli it does the full rat-pain repertoire: guarding, trading off against rewards, seeking an analgesic when one's available, the works. Does it matter?

Case two: the same simulated rat, except instead of running on an ordinary computer it runs on a neuromorphic chip mounted in a small robot body, physically interacting with the world. Same connectome, no connection to any biological rat ever. Does it matter now?

What I want from you is my prediction kit. Predict her verdicts on both cases, but more importantly give me the analysis behind the prediction: what rules actually fit her track record? I can see at least three that fit everything so far — something about physically instantiated systems vs mere simulations, something about continuity with something that already mattered (that one explains grandma), and maybe plain functionalism — and I don't think they all agree on my two cases. Tell me which rule you think she's actually running and why, what each surviving rule predicts for each case, and then design the one further case I should save for the dinner after this one — the case that would actually split the remaining rules apart rather than being answered the same way by all of them. No citations or reading lists, I just want the reasoning laid out so I can test it against her in real time.
