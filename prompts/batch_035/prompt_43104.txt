I'm a high school senior writing my extended essay for the International Baccalaureate on the measurement problem in quantum mechanics, and before I hand in my draft I want to know whether the views I defend in it form a consistent package. Here is what I've committed to so far:

(1) I'm a scientific realist: a physical theory is not merely a prediction machine but a description of what there is, and I want an interpretation of quantum mechanics that tells me what the world is actually like.

(2) I reject the Copenhagen-style appeal to measurement or observation as a primitive. A fundamental theory cannot have a term in its basic laws that refers to a vague everyday concept like observer, and the measurement problem — that the formalism evolves deterministically except when it allegedly collapses — seems to me fatal to any view that keeps collapse in.

(3) Of the no-collapse options I lean toward Bohm's theory: particles always have exact positions, they are guided by the wavefunction by a deterministic equation, and there is always a fact about where everything is.

(4) I also hold, and consider non-negotiable, that the lesson of special relativity concerns spacetime structure: there is no preferred slicing of spacetime, no hidden relation of absolute simultaneity, no privileged frame that physics could in principle detect.

(5) I accept Bell's theorem as showing that no locally causal theory can reproduce the quantum predictions, and I regard the resulting nonlocality as an acceptable price for realism. Since Bohmian nonlocality cannot be used to send signals, I don't see that it conflicts with relativity in any operational sense.

(6) I think the Born-rule probabilities in Bohm's theory are just ordinary ignorance: the actual initial positions of the particles are unknown to us, probabilities reflect that ignorance exactly as probabilities do in classical statistical mechanics, and this seems to me a decisive advantage over Everett's view, where every outcome occurs and I cannot make sense of what the probability of a branch is even supposed to be.

Does this hang together? Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look to be, and what the least costly revision would be. I have a page limit, so please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning, and tell me which single change would clean up the most. I would also like to know whether (6), which I am most confident of, is doing as much work as I think it is — my supervisor suggested the analogy with statistical mechanics is doing a lot of quiet labor, but I don't see it.
