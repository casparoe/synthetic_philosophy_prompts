Every few months my rationality meetup re-litigates the paradox of fiction, and this time someone attached a second half to it that I can't shake. The chain:

(1) Genuine fear, pity, or grief requires believing that its object exists — you cannot be afraid of a bear you are certain is not there. (2) When I weep over a novel, I believe no such thing about its characters. (3) So what I have is not grief but a quasi-emotion: I make believe that I grieve, the way a child playing monster makes believe to be chased. (4) Whatever holds of fiction holds of any content one imagines while withholding belief: dreams known to be dreams, hypotheticals, thought experiments. (5) Our responses to the trolley case, the experience machine, dust specks versus torture, are responses to content we withhold belief from. (6) So those responses are likewise quasi-emotional: we make believe that we judge. (7) A state one merely makes believe to be in cannot carry evidential weight about its object. (8) Therefore intuitions about hypothetical cases are evidentially worthless, and armchair philosophy by intuition pump — a nontrivial fraction of what gets argued on the blogs — is theater all the way down.

The premises all still look fine to me individually and I refuse the conclusion, which tells me something slipped and I can't say what. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me — including what I have to say about the person who is terrified of the thing under the bed while firmly believing there is nothing there? If forced, I'd sooner give up (1) than the evidential force of thought experiments, but I want the price list before I sign. End with a line of the form \answer{N}, where N is the number of the single weakest step.
