My study partner, who reads a lot of behavioral genetics, ran the following argument at me, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) In a given population, the heritability of a trait is the share of the variation in that trait that tracks genetic differences among its members, given the environments those members actually have.
(2) In affluent countries today, the heritability of height is roughly 0.8 to 0.9; differences in adult height within such populations are therefore mostly differences in genes.
(3) A difference with a genetic cause is not a difference an environmental change can remove — that is what having a genetic cause amounts to. Environmental programs can shrink a population's differences only insofar as those differences have environmental causes.
(4) So within such populations, no nutritional, medical, or educational program can do much to reduce differences in a highly heritable trait: with some 85 percent of the variation genetic, environmental levers can reach at most the remaining 15 percent. It is arithmetic.
(5) Therefore, anyone claiming that such programs could substantially narrow inequality in height — or, by the same reasoning, in any comparably heritable trait, such as measured intelligence — is running against the genetics, not merely against tough odds.

When I objected that average Dutch male height rose by something on the order of 18 cm between the mid-nineteenth century and today — far too fast for genetic change, so it must have been environmental — he said the objection only confirms the argument: since, by (3) and (4), environmental change cannot move a highly heritable trait much, the Dutch gain must after all have been genetic, a few generations of taller-than-average men having disproportionately many surviving children. I do not believe that story for a minute, but I also cannot point to the step where he cheats.

For what it is worth, I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can: the definition is the standard one, and as far as I know the estimates are solid. My suspicion is that something happens between (1) and (4) — some clause in the definition that quietly stops doing work once the argument gets going — but I cannot name it.
