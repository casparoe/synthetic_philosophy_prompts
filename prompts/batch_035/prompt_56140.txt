Every year my department has two grad students whove been fighting forever co-write a "state of the dispute" doc — basically an adversarial collaboration — that gets posted on the department website so other students can see what a productive disagreement looks like. This year it's us. I'm the consequentialist (roughly maximizing act consequentialist, I think the demandingness objection is mostly special pleading for our own comfort), the other guy thinks any acceptable moral theory has to leave agents room for their own projects, something like a hybrid view with an agent-centered prerogative. We've tried drafting this thing twice and both times it collapsed into re-litigating the shallow pond for the nth time. Draft the actual document for us.

A few things we've noticed while arguing past each other that we want worked in:

1. The objection isn't one thing and we want that sorted out early. We've found we can distinguish at least (a) the "can't be a guide to action" version — the demands exceed what humans can actually motivate themselves to do, (b) the "can't be a fitting object of blame" version — it would be wrong to blame someone for keeping their Saturday instead of working at the shelter, even if Saturday-shelter-work is what the theory says is best, and (c) the "alienation" version — the theory leaves no room for the agent's own perspective and projects. My hunch is we only genuinely disagree about (c), his is that we disagree about (b) too. The doc should say which is which.

2. The compliance question. He says the objection is at its strongest in non-ideal worlds: when others don't do their part, consequentialism says *you* must pick up the slack, and that's supposed to be what's unacceptable about it. I actually think there's something here but I want it pinned down: does his prerogative track "what would have been fair if others complied," or does it hand you the same slack no matter what anyone else does? Relatedly there's something we started calling the many-hands dividend: as the number of agents grows, each one's marginal share of any fixed pool of need shrinks. Does that make demandingness complaints weaker in big populations (everyone's slice is tinier) or stronger (more noncompliance, so more slack that needs picking up)? We each have a prediction and want both written down in advance.

3. A crux we suspect we have but have never managed to state cleanly: if the objection works at all, does it work against consequentialism as a criterion of rightness or only as a decision procedure? I say only the latter, and that once you grant that, the objection loses most of its bite. He thinks at least some versions bite at the criterion level. Each formulation needs to be phrased so its own side would actually sign it — that's the whole point of the exercise, and last time he vetoed three of my phrasings as smug, which, fair.

4. The guilt thing. He keeps insisting that even if I'm right that you must give away nearly everything, there should be some residue when you don't — something more than "that act was suboptimal," something closer to regret or an owed apology. I keep saying that from inside the theory there's just a fact about which act is best and everything after that is psychology. Is this a real disagreement or a verbal one about what we're each using "blameworthy" to mean? If part of our dispute turns out to be verbal, the doc should say so plainly and say which part.

Format: numbered section of agreed claims (we suspect we can agree on more than either of us admits in public — e.g. that empirical facts about burnout and motivational capacity are real but don't settle anything normative by themselves), then a disagreements table with a row per disagreement, and columns for the phrasing I'd accept, the phrasing he'd accept, the crux it hangs on, what argument or case we'd both agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction about it. Nothing vacuous like "we agree that morality is important," and nothing slanted toward either of us — it has to be something we could both actually put our names on.

Also, to be clear, we're not asking for a lit review or bibliography, we both know the pond and the violinist and all the rest, we want the document itself.
