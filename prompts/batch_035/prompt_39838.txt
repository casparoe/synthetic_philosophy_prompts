Alan Turing famously argued, in his 1950 Mind paper and in a late-1940s lecture to the London Mathematical Society, that instead of trying to program an adult mind wholesale, we should build a "child machine" and educate it — through a regime of rewards, punishments, and training, much as one teaches a child, with the process expected to be slow, laborious, and largely opaque to the machine's teachers, who would not be able to inspect or fully understand what was happening inside the learner. He even mused about the sensory channels through which rewards and punishments would be delivered, and in a 1951 talk he speculated that intelligent machines might eventually outstrip us and take control. Now suppose we could put a well-briefed Turing — briefed on everything since 1954, up to and including modern large language models — in front of a contemporary school-reform panel. What would he actually say about the current debates at the intersection of machine learning and education? In particular, I want predictions on the following live issues, each keyed as far as possible to his documented views rather than to generic speculation:

First, the ongoing controversy over students using LLM-based chatbots and AI tutoring assistants for homework, with some school systems banning them outright while others embrace "a patient personal tutor for every child" — the marketing slogan of the adaptive-learning platforms. Would Turing see these tools as the fulfillment of his own analogy between machine learning and child education, or would he object to something specific about them — for instance, that the training signal is imitation of human text rather than reward and punishment by an environment, or that the software is optimized for engagement metrics rather than for the long, slow course of education he described? Second, the recurring "calculator moment" argument: that offloading arithmetic, spelling, and now essay-writing to machines will atrophy children's developing minds. Given that Turing's whole program involved treating the untrained mind and the untrained machine as parallel starting points, would he think there is a meaningful difference between a child using a machine as a tool and a child being educated alongside machines? Third, standardized testing and rigid curricula: does anything in his remarks about the arbitrariness of what counts as education, and about teachers not understanding their pupils' internal processes, suggest he would be sympathetic to critiques of one-size-fits-all assessment?

Please distinguish clearly between extrapolations that are strongly anchored in his actual writings (say which ones and why), extrapolations that are merely consistent with his views, and points where his pre-1954 framework simply lacks the resources to have an opinion — for example, whether his child-machine analogy even survives contact with systems that learn from oceans of text rather than from an educator's corrective feedback. And since he speculated that machines would eventually take control, say something about whether he would treat classroom AI as a benign early chapter of that story or as something schools should resist.
