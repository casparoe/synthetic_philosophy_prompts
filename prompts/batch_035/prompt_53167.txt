I've followed the debates about causation by omission on and off ever since arguments over Singer's drowning child made the rounds on the rationalist blogs, and I've never managed to pin down where my uncertainty actually lives. I want a credence on a precisely stated proposition, with the reasoning decomposed into its parts.

The case: a gardener is paid to water a client's flowers while the client travels; the gardener never comes, and the flowers die. Contrast the poison case: a vandal poisons the flowers, and they die. The proposition I care about is:

(P) The gardener's not watering the flowers is a cause of the flowers' death in literally the same sense of "cause" as the vandal's poisoning is a cause of the flowers' death — not in some merely derivative, loose, or "causally relevant but not causal" sense.

Decompose your credence in P along lines like the following, restructured or supplemented if you think a term is missing:

1. Whether absences are ontologically respectable causal relata at all — whether "the gardener's not watering" names an event that exists, as opposed to a deflationary picture on which such talk abbreviates claims about the gardener's positive states and doings (his other appointments, his sitting at home) plus a covering generalization.
2. Suppose some deflationary ontology is right: can P still be literally true, or does the ordinary word "cause" carry the ontological commitment that P demands?
3. The profligacy problem: the neighbor who never agreed to help, and the Queen of England, also failed to water the flowers, and a simple counterfactual analysis makes each of their omissions a cause of the death. Whether picking out the gardener's omission as *the* cause can be done on principled grounds (defaults, contracts, norms) rather than pragmatic ones — and, if the selection turns out to be norm-relative, whether that relativity is compatible with P's being true in a single, literal sense.
4. Whether the standard rescue moves — recasting the case as double prevention, or locating the causation in the gardener's positive dispositions rather than in the absence itself — preserve P's literal truth or quietly concede it.

Give me a number for P, a probability and a one-line justification for each term, and the combination rule you used. Then tell me which term carries the most weight in your final number, and what argument or kind of evidence would move that term most. For context, my own credence in P is about 0.4: I oscillate between thinking the norm-relativity of omission talk is fatal to P, and thinking that ordinary causal talk was never ontologically austere in the first place, so demanding austerity specifically for omissions is unmotivated. Don't give me a survey of the literature with the verdict left open — commit to a position on whether P is true, defend the decomposition that gets you there, and say plainly which of my two oscillating thoughts you take to be closer to the mark.
