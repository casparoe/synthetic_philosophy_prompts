I've been assembling my political-philosophical commitments on inheritance and equal opportunity for a seminar paper, and I'd like a consistency audit before I commit to the position in writing. Here is the package:

1. I am broadly luck egalitarian about opportunity: advantages that are unearned — traceable neither to effort nor to choice — are the paradigm case of injustice, and the state is entitled to correct them.
2. I take self-ownership and the entitlement to the fruits of one's labor very seriously: taxing wages to fund opportunity-equalizing programs is at best a regrettable necessity, and the state's claims should fall on unearned windfalls rather than on earnings.
3. It follows, I believe, that steeply progressive taxation of inheritance and bequests is the natural — perhaps the only fully just — revenue source for an equal-opportunity regime.
4. At the same time, I regard the family as a legitimate and valuable sphere of partiality: parents not only may but should advantage their children — through time, attention, encouragement, cultural transmission, private schooling, and savings.
5. I accept that genuine equality of opportunity is compatible with substantial inequality of final outcomes, provided the inequality traces to choice and effort.
6. Finally, two case verdicts I feel strongly about: I think a founder who spent forty years building a business expressly to hand it to her children, who worked in it, has a weighty claim the tax code should respect; and I think a billionaire is perfectly entitled to blow her fortune on consumption — yachts, parties, whatever — rather than being nudged by tax law toward leaving it to anyone.

Are these six jointly tenable? Which pairs are in real tension, which only look like it, and for each genuine tension, what is the cheapest revision and what would that revision cost me elsewhere? Please order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and identify any single change that would clean up the most.
