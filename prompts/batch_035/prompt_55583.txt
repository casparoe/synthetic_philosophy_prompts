I've been staring at a gene's-eye-view argument about the units of selection, and I can't decide where it breaks. It runs: (1) For an entity to be a unit of selection, it must be a replicator — an entity that leaves copies of itself, whose differences make a difference to how many copies exist in later generations. (2) Organisms fail this test: except in freak cases, an organism is never duplicated; meiosis and recombination break each genotype apart every generation, so what persists down the generations is the gene, not the individual carrying it. (3) Therefore any talk of selection favoring, say, faster cheetahs can be restated without loss as selection among alleles that build faster cheetahs. (4) A level of description that can be systematically restated in the terms of another level is mere bookkeeping, not a distinct unit of selection. (5) So the gene is the only genuine unit of selection, and the whole individuals-versus-groups debate rests on a confusion between what gets selected and what merely gets used and discarded.

Here is my problem. Run the same reasoning one level down. Take an arbitrary stretch of chromosome — the first half of one gene fused to the last half of another, say. It is copied as faithfully as any gene is; its copies vary in number across generations depending on what they happen to sit inside. By (1)–(3), selection favoring fast cheetahs can be restated, without loss, as selection among these arbitrary chunks. Then (4) implies that the chunks, not the genes, are the units — or rather, that any replicating stretch is as good a unit as any other, and nothing is privileged. So the argument that demotes organisms from the status of units seems to demote genes too, and I can't accept that nothing is a unit of selection. Which step should I reject, why did it look acceptable in the first place, and what do the main positions in the units-of-selection debate each have to pay to reject it? For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (1) if I can, since it seems close to a definition; it's (4) I've come to suspect, but I want to know exactly what giving it up costs the gene's-eye view.
