I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been drafting an essay on what computational complexity theory implies beyond mathematics. Before I write it out in full prose, I'd like comments from someone who can check my technical claims and, more importantly, tell me whether the philosophical moves are sound. Below are my current draft notes.

Thesis: complexity theory, not computability theory, carries the real philosophical weight of the "universe as computation" idea, and it does so in three specific ways.

1. The verification asymmetry. If P ≠ NP, then for a large class of problems, verifying a solution is fundamentally cheaper than finding one. This is often glossed as meaning that creativity can't be automated or that discovery is essentially hard. I want to argue something narrower: it gives an objective grounding for the felt difference between understanding a proof and producing one — a structural difference, not just a psychological one — at least for problems in NP that lack known polynomial algorithms. I'm aware that whether this extends to everything humans find hard is exactly what the popular glosses skip over.

2. Worst-case versus the world. Complexity claims are asymptotic and worst-case: they concern instances as n grows, and the hardest instances at each size. But almost everything interesting in the actual world happens at small n with heavy structure. SAT is NP-complete, yet industrial SAT solvers clear instances with millions of variables. So I want to claim: complexity theory describes the shape of the space of problems in the limit, not the difficulty of any actual task. Any argument that moves directly from NP-hardness to a conclusion about human practice — say, about the limits of scientific discovery — needs an extra premise about how actual instances are distributed. My tentative line is that this premise is almost never available, which is why most talk about the philosophical implications of P ≠ NP is unwarranted — with one exception. In cryptography the designer chooses the distribution: worst-case hardness is deliberately engineered into the instances. Cryptography is the one place where complexity theory touches the world directly, and notably it's the one place where we design the world to fit the theory rather than reading the theory off the world.

3. Representation-independence. Polynomial-time reductions are invariant under polynomial changes of encoding, which is why the theory feels lawlike rather than notational. But the invariance class is chosen by us. A physical system doesn't come tagged with a problem size; someone decides what counts as an instance and what counts as its size. My claim: complexity classes are properties of our descriptions of systems, together with a fact about which transformations between descriptions we're willing to call insignificant. This doesn't make them unreal — temperature also involves a conventional choice of scale — but it does mean that claims of the form that the universe is solving an NP-hard problem whenever it does X (protein folding, in some popular accounts) smuggle in a choice of encoding and can be made true or false by redescribing the system. I'd like to defend this while conceding that under any reasonable encoding the class of what's efficiently computable is stable — that stability is why the theory works at all.

The conclusion I'm aiming at is negative and modest: complexity theory doesn't tell us that the universe computes, or that creativity is special. It tells us where to look for a gap between checking and creating, and it warns that difficulty is a fact about a problem class paired with a distribution, not about tasks as such. I'd rather end there than with grand claims.

(b) Is point 3 philosophically respectable, or is it just the observation that all mathematical facts are facts about our descriptions, applied to a new case?
