Under the American doctrine known as the third-party rule, information you hand over to a company generally loses its Fourth Amendment protection: the government can obtain your bank records, the phone numbers you dial, and similar data without a warrant, on the reasoning that you exposed those facts to a third party and assumed the risk of its disclosing them. Yet the same body of law protects the contents of a sealed letter in transit even though a postal carrier physically holds it for days, protects the contents of a phone call even though they pass through the phone company's equipment, and, since the Supreme Court's 2018 decision on cell-site location records, protects a record of everywhere you carried your phone even though that information, too, sat in a carrier's database. The pattern is not simply that the law protects communication content and not records about communications, because a postcard's content is unprotected while a bank's record of your deposit slip is protected against some other intrusions, and location data crossed the line only in 2018. Reconstruct the theory of privacy this scheme presupposes: is it secrecy, in the sense that whatever another party can see is no longer private; is it control over information; is it some notion of contextual appropriateness of information flows; or is it instead a rough proxy for the intrusiveness of surveillance, with no coherent theory of privacy beneath it at all?
