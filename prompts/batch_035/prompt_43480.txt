At a memorial-studies workshop last month someone on the panel shut down a Rieff-style argument about a state eventually letting the memory of a civil war fade — letting the annual commemorations lapse, teaching it in one textbook paragraph instead of a semester — with the Santayana line: "Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it." I'm a political theory PhD student and it struck me that I genuinely don't know what precise claim that slogan makes, and neither, I suspect, did anyone in the room. Can you lay out the distinct theses it could be standing for — from something trivially true about learning requiring retained information, to some causal claim about collective memory preventing recurrence, to something much stronger like "uncommemorated trauma gets re-enacted" — and say for each whether it's a platitude, defensible, or just false? The part I care about most is which readings would actually license the use it was put to there: overriding the case for forgetting. My hunch is the causal reading is the weakest of the bunch — the Balkans feel like a natural experiment in obsessive remembering fueling repetition rather than preventing it — but I want the verdicts to be honest rather than flattering to the position I came in with. Fold in Margalit's thick vs thin relations stuff if it's relevant (does a duty of remembrance even bind at the level of a state rather than a community?). And if you think my framing is confused — say the slogan was never doing causal work and is really about piety or identity, or the whole remember/forget dichotomy is a bad carve-up of the options — just say so instead of running with my question. I'd rather get corrected at this stage than two chapters in.
