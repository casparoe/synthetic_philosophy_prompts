I'm taking an upper-level political philosophy course, and we've just finished a unit on epistocracy versus democracy that ended in a genuinely unsettled seminar debate. A classmate defending epistocracy leaned heavily on the airline analogy: no one thinks passengers should vote on the flight path, and no one experiences the pilot's exclusive authority over the cockpit as an insult; we let demonstrated competence decide in every domain where mistakes are catastrophic, and government, where mistakes are arguably most catastrophic, should be no different. She then extended the analogy on her own: passengers still choose which airline to fly, patients still choose their hospitals, so even a competence-filtered electorate would leave ordinary people with meaningful exit and choice, and the familiar worries about domination and second-class citizenship are overblown. The other side of the seminar room countered with the jury analogy: twelve ordinary citizens, each only somewhat reliable, can collectively outperform any single expert witness, and that is supposed to be what democratic voting does with dispersed social knowledge — a Condorcet-style picture of majority rule as an aggregation machine rather than acompetence contest.

Both sides were clearly doing philosophy by analogy rather than by argument, and I'd like help auditing the two analogies rather than adjudicating the debate directly. For each, set out explicitly what is supposed to correspond to what — for the pilot picture, the roles of the pilot, the flight plan, the passenger, the licensing authority, and the commercial market my classmate smuggled in with her "choose your airline" extension; for the jury picture, the roles of the juror, the evidence, the verdict, and the defendant. Then say where each mapping breaks. The disanalogies I'd want you to weigh are things like: aviation has an uncontested, verifiable common end (arriving alive) whereas politics is partly a fight over ends; pilots are certified by a process that already presupposes legitimate expertise in a way political competence-testing cannot; the jury picture seems to treat political questions as having a fact of the matter the way guilt does, and it quietly assumes independence and sincerity among voters that partisanship, polls, and demagoguery may destroy. Crucially, distinguish the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusions these analogies are being used to license from the ones that are merely incidental, and tell me whether either conclusion survives once the load-bearing mismatches are on the table — or whether each analogy was carrying its conclusion the entire time. If either debate position is better served by some third, less popular analogy, I'd like to see it.

First, since I can't check sources right now, separate what you take to be firmly established in the literature (say, in the region of Brennan's and Estlund's debate, which you're welcome to discuss by name) from your own reconstructions of how the analogies work. Second, be explicit about where your audit is most likely to go wrong: which single consideration, if it came apart, would flip your verdict on whether epistocracy's or democracy's favored analogy survives, and what a determined defender of the losing analogy would say you've missed.
