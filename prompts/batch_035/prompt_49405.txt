I am a novelist at work on a book narrated in the first person by a woman now in her sixties who, in her early twenties, underwent a corpus callosotomy — the severing of the band of fibers joining the brain's hemispheres — as a last resort for severe epilepsy. She is a professional translator by trade, has lived alone for decades, and appears to everyone who meets her as an ordinary, coherent person: witty, opinionated, settled in her habits. My problem is that I do not know what her inward narration should sound like, and I have come to suspect this is not merely a craft question but a philosophical one about the unity of consciousness. I would therefore be grateful for the most important implications of the split-brain literature — the experiments in which one hemisphere is shown something the other cannot report, the disowned left-hand actions known as alien hand behavior, the left hemisphere's tendency to invent explanations for actions it did not initiate, and the subtle cross-cuing strategies by which some long-term patients reintroduced information between the hemispheres without realizing that was what they were doing — for the following concrete questions.

First, does the evidence support the popular picture of a split-brain patient as two persons in one skull, or the opposite picture of one person whose internal access routes have merely been rerouted? This determines whether my later chapters, set long after she has adapted, are honest in presenting her as a single narrating self, or whether that smoothness is a fiction I am imposing on her. Second, in the early chapters, when her left hand does things she disavows, should the narration present her disavowal as a puzzle she gradually pieces together, or would she more likely confabulate — offering fluent, sincere explanations for the hand's behavior that have no basis in anything she is aware of? I want the early chapters to convey what her life is like from the inside, and for that I need to know whether there is, at that stage, one subject of her experience or two partial subjects that never meet. Third, given that she is a translator — someone whose profession consists of holding two versions of a text in mind at once and judging their equivalence — are there respects in which that comparative working life would change after the surgery, for instance in which hemisphere handles which language or in how she resolves a choice between competing renderings? And fourth, what light does her case cast on the ordinary, unsplit reader's own sense of being a single mind: is the unity of consciousness something the intact brain produces, or something a settled story like the one she tells about herself produces on the brain's behalf?

Please give your answer in American English.
