I am a novelist, and I am stuck on a scene I suspect I cannot write honestly until I understand something about a debate that, as far as I can tell, has been going on among physicists and philosophers for decades without anyone moving. My current novel turns on the relationship between two characters: an aging cosmologist who holds that the thermodynamic arrow of time — the reason coffee cools and never uncools, why we can remember yesterday but not tomorrow — is explained by the universe having begun in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, what people in this literature call the Past Hypothesis; and his former student, now a philosopher of physics, who has spent her career arguing in the spirit of Huw Price that this whole style of explanation cheats. Her complaint, as I currently write her, is twofold: first, that calling a boundary condition an explanation merely renames the thing to be explained, since a brute fact about 13.8 billion years ago is at least as strange as the regularity it is meant to account for; and second, subtler, that any explanation which treats the past as intrinsically privileged — as the place where the special condition sits — has already smuggled in the temporal perspective it was supposed to earn, because "past" is not a direction available to physics until the asymmetry is somehow accounted for. The argument between them unfolds, in the novel, over a week of walks along a tidal estuary, and I have noticed that my drafts stall in exactly the way I gather the actual literature stalls: each character makes a move, the other concedes the local point, and the disagreement survives every concession intact. A third colleague in the book wants to dissolve the whole thing with a story about our universe being a baby universe budded off from a larger entropy-generating structure, but I have a feeling that is a change of subject dressed as a resolution, and I would like to know whether my feeling is right.

What I need from you is not a survey of these positions — I know them well enough for my purposes — but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, in the sense I mean above, so that I can decide how the book has to end. Is this a substantive dispute about facts, in which case something (a discovery about the early universe, a theorem) could in principle settle it and one of my characters is simply going to lose? Is it verbal, a disagreement about what "explanation" requires, in which case the argument scenes should be written as two people failing to notice they are using the word differently? Or are the two of them answering different questions under one heading — one doing cosmology, one policing the concept of explanation — in which case the honest ending is not victory but the two of them seeing that, and I should write the final walk accordingly? Please give me your diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each position actually commits itself to, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis, and tell me plainly whether you think no progress should be expected — since if the impasse is structural, that fact belongs in the book, and if it is not, I need to know which of my characters is holding out beyond what the arguments warrant.

One request about form: after your full discussion, give me a final line beginning "Verdict:" and containing only a short phrase naming the kind of disagreement you have diagnosed — e.g. substantive, verbal, different-questions, or some compact label of your own — so that I can copy it straight into my notes at the top of the scene.
