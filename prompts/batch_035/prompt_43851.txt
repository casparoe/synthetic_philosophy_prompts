My film club is watching Kurosawa's Rashomon next month, and I want to bring in some Jain philosophy instead of the usual "is truth subjective" discussion. Read the film through anekāntavāda, the Jain doctrine that reality is many-sided and any single statement captures only part of it, and syādvāda, the sevenfold predication where every claim gets qualified -- in some sense it is, in some sense it is not, in some sense it is indescribable, and so on. The film gives us the bandit, the wife, the dead man speaking through a medium, and the woodcutter, each telling a version that implicates themselves differently. Is this anekāntavāda dramatized, or is it something else entirely? The standard reading of the film is basically skeptical -- the truth of what happened in the grove is unknowable -- which is nearly the opposite of the Jain view, since the Jains think the partial truths can all be held together in one qualified synthesis rather than leaving us with four incompatible stories. Does the film give us any resources for combining the accounts, or only for despairing of them? Related: the Jains also distinguish standpoints (the naya doctrine -- you can describe the same thing as one entity or as many parts depending on your purpose), and its tempting to say each witness narrates from a standpoint shaped by what they need the story to do for them -- honor for the bandit, victimhood for the wife, dignity for the dead man. Does that reading work, or does it flatten them into psychology? Then the ending: the woodcutter takes the abandoned baby after the commoner steals the kimono, and the priest's despair lifts. Is that meant as a move from epistemic failure to moral commitment that doesn't depend on solving the epistemic problem, and is there anything in the Jain framework that would make sense of it, given the Jains tie right conduct to grasping reality's many-sidedness rather than giving up on it? One more wrinkle worth addressing: the woodcutter's final account of the fight (the one he tells the commoner at the gate, after we learn he lied to the court) -- is it a fifth standpoint among the others, a slightly more plausible one, or does the film quietly privilege it, and would privileging any single account betray the doctrine? I want a reading that takes both the film and the doctrine seriously and says where they resist each other, not just a neat mapping of one onto the other.
