I'm a graduate student in a seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and this term's running battle has been over Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. The class has settled into two camps that, in my view, have been arguing past each other for weeks, and our instructor has asked each pair of opposing students to produce the document an adversarial collaboration would yield: not a verdict on who's right, but the joint statement both camps could actually put their names to. I've been assigned to draft it for the two sides in our seminar, and I want your help writing it properly.

Here are the two camps, described so that each would accept the description of its own view. Camp A are the nonreductive physicalists: they hold that mental properties are irreducible to physical properties but that mental causation is real, because a mental cause produces its effect by being realized by a physical cause — the mental event does its causal work through its realizer, and this "causal inheritance" is supposed to be compatible with, even explanatory of, the irreducibility of the mental level. Camp B are the exclusionists: they hold that Kim's argument goes through — given the causal closure of the physical and the supervenience of the mental, an irreducible mental cause would either overdetermine its physical effect (in the way two independent simultaneous sufficient causes would) or be excluded by it, so mental causation is genuine only if mental properties are reduced to, or identified with, physical ones; what Camp A calls inheritance, Camp B calls capitulation. The document should give the claims both camps can sign about the argument's premises (closure, supervenience, no systematic overdetermination), about what the two-bullets contrast case does and does not show, and about the fact that holding the physical fixed while varying the mental is impossible by physicalism's own lights; then the precise disagreements, each phrased so its own camp would accept the wording; then the cruxes. I suspect one crux is whether overdetermination reasoning applies to pairs of causes that are metaphysically dependent rather than independent, and another is whether causal relevance is relative to a level of description — whether a generalization at the mental level that holds across radically different physical realizers (the same belief-type produced by quite different neural machinery in two people, with similar behavior ensuing) is causal and projectible in its own right or merely a handy summary of the physical story.

Please format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table (disagreement, Camp A's wording of its own side, Camp B's wording of its own side), followed by the cruxes — and for each crux, name a concrete case or argument both camps would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction about the case written down in advance so it could in principle come out against them. I'd also like a closing section on which residual disagreements are substantive and which have turned out, on inspection, to be verbal — in particular whether the fight over "causal inheritance" is a real dispute about reduction or a dispute about who gets to keep the word "genuine." Our seminar values precision over diplomacy, so resist any formulation that is so watered down that either camp could sign it without conceding anything; every agreed claim should be one that, a month ago, at least one camp would have disputed.
