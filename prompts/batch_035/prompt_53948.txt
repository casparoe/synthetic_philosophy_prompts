I help run a graduate reading group on the foundations of probability, and we've reached the point where the group can recite the standard three interpretations and their stock objections in their sleep. Before we go back to primary literature, I want to fix the standard of evaluation itself: write the list of desiderata that any adequate interpretation of probability should meet, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. For our purposes, adequacy should be relative to a specific job description: the interpretation is supposed to (a) underwrite statistical practice — what a confidence interval or a p-value is a statement *about*; (b) make sense of physical chance in genuinely indeterministic setups like radioactive decay; (c) handle ordinary single-case ascriptions, e.g., an oncologist telling one patient there is a roughly 30% chance her biopsy comes back positive; and (d) guide rational credence and action under uncertainty. Not every camp will accept every item you'd naturally put on the list, so part of the task is separating genuine desiderata from camp prejudices: for instance, whether the requirement that probabilities be recoverable from empirical frequencies is a theorem-shaped demand (via convergence results) or just frequentism smuggling itself into the spec.

Seed the list with some cases our group keeps circling. There is the coin already flipped but hidden under a cup: the physical facts seem settled, yet the probability of heads seems neither zero nor one — which desideratum handles that, and does it pull toward epistemic or dispositional readings? There is the reference-class problem for a single patient: 43-year-old smoker with a family history — is the requirement that a single-case probability be well-defined a real desideratum, and if so, is it jointly satisfiable with the demand that probabilities be empirically grounded? There is the forecaster who assigns a 0.3 probability to exactly one geopolitical event and is only ever calibratable at the portfolio level, never event-by-event: should the spec demand per-event correctness conditions (like the calibration of a known chance), and what does that demand do to subjectivist interpretations? There is also the conditioning problem: when new evidence arrives, credences are supposed to update by conditionalization, while propensities famously resist reading inverse probabilities off them (the Humphreys' paradox difficulty) — flag which pairs of your desiderata look jointly unsatisfiable given results like that, since an inconsistent list is itself a finding, not a failure.

After the requirements, give a compact scorecard: how limiting-frequency views, subjectivist/Bayesian views, and single-case propensity views do against the list, and — the part I most want to see — which single requirement excludes the most contenders, with your reasoning. A table is fine for the scorecard, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. Assume the reader has had a first course in probability and statistics but no philosophy of probability.
