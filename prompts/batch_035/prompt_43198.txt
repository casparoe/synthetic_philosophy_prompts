In my political philosophy seminar we've reached the topic of inheritance taxation and dynastic wealth, and the discussion keeps running on a single analogy: equal opportunity means a fair race, and leaving heirs large fortunes is like moving some runners' starting blocks a hundred meters toward the finish line. The course readings deploy it to argue that steep inheritance taxes merely restore the conditions of fair competition rather than redistributing prizes after the fact. A classmate objects that the analogy is doing all the work: families, she says, are less like individual runners than like relay teams across generations, so passing wealth on is just the baton pass, and a state that breaks it up is not leveling the track but dissolving the team. I'd like a proper audit of the analogy. Please give me the correspondence table — what plays the finish line, the prize, the runner's own legs and training, the starting block — then identify where the mapping breaks down (for instance: whether natural talent is any more earned than inherited wealth, whether life has a single finish line or dimension of value at all, whether the "race" is even zero-sum), separate the disanalogies that matter for the tax question from the ones that don't, and say whether the pro-tax conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether it needs independent support. If there is a better analogy available — one that captures what defenders of equal opportunity are actually pointing at — I'd like to know what it is. Please end with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating whether the analogy's conclusion survives.
