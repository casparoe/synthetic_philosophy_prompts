I'm on the grantmaking committee of a mid-sized foundation that funds work on global catastrophic risks — asteroid detection, engineered-pandemic prep, that sort of thing — and we have an application in front of us that's turned into a genuine philosophical mess. $2.5M over three years for a program targeting very-low-probability, very-high-stakes scenarios. Their case rests on an expected-value model that enumerates scenarios of ever-smaller probability and ever-larger stakes, and here's the problem: the sign of the payoff flips across branches. Some branches the intervention averts catastrophe, other branches it backfires — raising the profile of a bio-risk and inspiring copycats, hardening the exact infrastructure it meant to make resilient, etc. Summed in the order they present it, the EV comes out as a big positive number. One of our analysts re-sorted the scenario table by decreasing probability and the sum flipped negative; another lumped all the backfire scenarios first and got something near zero. So the model is basically a Pasadena game — if you know that one, it's St. Petersburg except the payoffs alternate in sign, and by the rearrangement theorem for conditionally convergent series you can make the "expected value" come out to literally anything you like just by reordering the terms. Credit where due, the applicant's appendix is quite candid that the ordering choice is doing all the work.

The committee has split into two camps. One wants a standing rule: any application whose EV calculation isn't absolutely convergent — i.e., where reordering the scenarios can change the answer — is automatically ineligible. Clean, principled, and it kills this one. The other camp thinks that rule is just an excuse to defund all tail-risk work, since nearly everything we fund looks like this to some degree (the backfire scenarios are harder to see in most applications, not absent), and besides, refusing to decide is itself a decision, and arguably the one with the worst expected value of all. Our bylaws say grants must be justified by "a defensible assessment of expected impact," which is doing a truly heroic amount of load-bearing work here.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before thursday's meeting — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete bottom line and criteria the committee could actually apply to similar applications in future funding cycles, not just a guided tour of the paradox. I want the strongest point of whichever side loses engaged seriously rather than dismissed, and if your honest view is that expected value just doesn't settle cases shaped like this and we need a different decision procedure for them entirely (e.g. something about thresholds, dominance, or how to handle decision-relevant but order-dependent sums), then say that and spell out what the alternative rule would be.
