I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've gotten fixated on a problem that apparently has a name. My team owns alerting for a large distributed system, and every threshold we set is arbitrary in the same way: one slow request isn't an incident, so how many slow requests does it take? A colleague who minored in philosophy told me this is the sorites paradox, and that there's a whole literature of responses to it. As best I can tell, the main ones are roughly: epistemicism (there is a sharp cutoff, we just can't ever know where it is); supervaluationism (there are many ways of making the vague term precise, and a statement is only true if it comes out true on every precisification); degree-theoretic approaches (truth about borderline cases comes in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing); and contextualism (where the cutoff sits shifts with the context of use). I think I've got the gist of each right, but correct me if I've garbled any of them before proceeding.

Here's the task. Imagine each of these responses is a proposed policy for when to page the on-call engineer at 3 a.m. — a rule for converting fuzzy telemetry into a binary page/don't-page decision that then has to be defended in a postmortem in front of executives and lawyers. Rank the four approaches into tiers by how much you'd trust them under those conditions, with a one-sentence justification for each placement and a trust score between 0 and 1. At least one approach should land in a lower tier than its standing in academic philosophy would predict, and I'd like you to say explicitly where engineering practice and the philosophical debate genuinely part ways, and why. Finally, my teammate insists epistemicism is obviously crazy as a view — tell me whether, nonetheless, it's the approach we would actually end up running in production, even if no one would ever admit to it in the design doc.
