I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science writing a paper on the epistemic status of what people call the physical Church-Turing thesis — roughly, the claim that any function computable by any physical machine is Turing-computable. My department splits into two camps. My advisor treats the thesis as close to a definitional truth: once you fix any notion of "machine" precise enough to evaluate, she says, either it collapses into Turing computability or it quietly smuggles in idealizations (infinite precision, zero noise, unbounded time) that no physically instantiable system enjoys. A younger faculty member in philosophy of physics thinks it's a substantive, open empirical hypothesis: there are real questions about whether spacetime is continuous, whether differential-equation dynamics can encode non-Turing-computable functions, and whether "in principle" physical possibility is enough to falsify the thesis even when no finite, noisy, bounded-energy device could exploit it. They disagree loudly and mostly past each other, and I'd like to know where the actual disagreement lives.

Give me your credence that the physical Church-Turing thesis, stated as above, is true — a real number, not a hedged "it depends on how you formulate it." Then decompose it into the sub-questions that seem to you to carry the weight, such as (a) whether the thesis is empirical, definitional, or too vague to have a truth value at all — including whether a proposed hypercomputer could ever count as a counterexample rather than as a reason to revise the term "computation"; (b) whether the fundamental laws are discrete, or continuous in a way that permits encoding uncomputable behavior in the evolution of real-valued quantities; (c) whether idealized physical possibility (infinite precision available, noise absent, unbounded time) is enough to refute the thesis, or whether only buildable devices count; and (d) whatever other terms you think belong, like whether chaos, thermal noise, or quantum limits make the idealizations in principle unreachable rather than merely in practice unaffordable. Assign a rough probability to each, give a sentence of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and say which term you'd least bet on. Finally, my advisor sits around 0.9 and the younger faculty member around 0.4 — tell me which sub-question is most likely the locus of their disagreement, and what argument or (idealized) experimental result would move your own number the most.
