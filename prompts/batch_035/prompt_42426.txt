I am a retired isotope geochemist who has spent the last two years reading political philosophy with more enthusiasm than rigor, so I would appreciate a careful adjudication of a dispute I recently witnessed and could not resolve.

The occasion was the annual meeting of my regional natural history society, roughly 700 members, governed by a council of twelve, with officers re-eligible for re-election without limit. A council member I will call Haas proposed a bylaw amendment limiting officers to two consecutive two-year terms, with re-eligibility restored after one year away. Another member, Lenz, opposed it. Their exchange, as I recorded it, ran as follows.

(1) Haas observed that for eleven years the presidency and the treasuries have rotated among the same three people; that the nominating committee has never once put forward a candidate not already on council; and that in that time not one member under fifty has stood for office. The posts, he said, are effectively closed, and term limits reopen the funnel.

(2) Lenz replied that no one is barred from running: the society wields no power over anyone, entry onto the ballot is free, and the reason the same people hold office is simply that nobody else wants the work. Forcing out the willing does not create new leaders; it creates vacancies, which will then be filled by whoever least wanted the job.

(3) Haas answered that "nobody else wants the work" is not an independent fact of nature but a product of the arrangement: members do not run because challenging an ensconced incumbent is futile and mildly humiliating, whereas an office with a known exit date is a different object altogether — one applies for a slot rather than unseating a person. Willingness to stand, he argued, is endogenous to the rules.

(4) Lenz granted the endogeneity point arguendo and raised what he called the expertise problem: a treasurer must learn the insurance, the audit, and the grant reporting; a president holds the working relationships with the museum and the county; the learning curve is steep and the pay is zero. A hard term limit means the society is perpetually administered by novices — like a laboratory in which no one is permitted to master the equipment before rotating off it.

(5) Haas then offered what he took for a compromise: leave officer terms alone but draw the nominating committee by lot from the membership roll. Random selection is neutral, no one is forced out, and a closed circle cannot reproduce itself through a lottery.

(6) Lenz said the lottery conceded his case: it hands the society's agenda to conscripts with no demonstrated interest and, worse, no accountability, since a drawn member faces no electorate to answer to. And he noted that repeated re-election, whatever its flaws, selects for precisely the member for whom holding office is itself the reward — the careerist, if one likes — and that this selection is what keeps institutions running between crises. Better a willing careerist than a conscripted amateur.

(7) Haas closed by charging Lenz with modeling power on the state: nobody coerces anybody, true, but whoever holds office sets the agenda — which rooms we rent, which speakers we invite, whose proposals reach a vote — and eleven years of unchallenged agenda-setting changes what the society is without anyone ever deciding it. That, he said, is entrenchment without coercion, and a term limit is the only check that does not require some member to mount a challenge first. Lenz closed by asking what disease the surgery is for: term limits are a mechanical cure for patronage and self-dealing, and a volunteer society with no rents to distribute has neither.

I ask you to adjudicate this move by move: for each of the seven numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere between, with a brief justification grounded in the actual content of the exchange rather than in generalities about term limits. Then tell me who is more right overall. Finally — and here I most want your help — identify what both of them missed: I have a suspicion there are positions available to them (about intermediate rules, about the difference between limiting re-eligibility and limiting service, or about what an unaccountable nominating body actually does) that neither so much as considered, but I cannot see the terrain clearly enough to say.
