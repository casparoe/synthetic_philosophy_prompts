I run a graduate reading group in my philosophy department — mostly ethicists, ancient philosophers, and one political theorist, with two mathematically inclined undergraduates who tag along — and the next unit is Gödel's modal ontological argument. We have two ninety-minute sessions and a whiteboard. What I want from the members is that they leave understanding the thing as a piece of modal logic with a genuinely contested load-bearing premise, not as a curiosity or a scandal, and I want your help planning rather than your own exposition of the argument. Specifically: first, the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what — I take it the S5 bridge from possible necessity to plain necessity has to be secured early, before anyone sees it do real work, but I am genuinely unsure whether the definition of positive properties should come before or after a first pass at the proof sketch, and where the essence and necessary-existence axioms belong relative to both. Second, the one example that should carry the notion of a positive property across both sessions, since that definition is where all the work happens and where readers of the argument talk past one another — and I would like you to say why the obvious candidates, morally admirable properties and Anselmian great-making properties, will each misfire in a different direction. Third, the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence to head each off: my guesses are that they will read positive evaluatively, that they will experience the possibly-necessary-to-necessary step as a conjuring trick that could prove anything whatsoever, that several will reach for Kant's observation that existence is not a predicate and assume it dispatches this version too, and that the mathematically inclined members will regard the whole question as settled the moment the derivation goes through. Fourth, how to stage the standard objection that the axioms as Gödel left them entail modal collapse — everything true being necessarily true — and whether to bring in Anderson-style emendations or leave that as an open thread between sessions; I would also like your view on whether collapse is best taught as a reductio of the axioms or as a diagnosis of which axiom does the collapsing. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I am inclined to skip the second-order logical machinery and the question of which accessibility relation is metaphysically the right one, and to tell the group plainly that I am skipping them. Finally, one question I can pose at the end of the second session whose answer would show me whether they have located where the argument's weight actually rests, and what an answer that counts as success would look like.
