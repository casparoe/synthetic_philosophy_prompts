so in my philosophy of law class we just read david lewis's paper "do we believe in penal substitution" (yes, the possible-worlds guy, apparently he had opinions about criminal justice too). the setup: an offender is justly sentenced, and a devoted, innocent friend volunteers to be punished in his place — everyone consents, nobody is deceived. lewis's point was that most of us are "of two minds" about this: we say no for prison and death penalties, yet we kind of accept it in other contexts (some christians even think the atonement works exactly like this).

anyway at my study group yesterday two people got into it. priya said substitution is obviously impossible, and her reasoning was: a debt can transfer because all that matters is that the creditor gets paid, it doesn't matter by whom. but punishment just IS the guilty person suffering for their guilt — if someone else takes it, you no longer have a punishment, you just have an innocent person being harmed, which is like the difference between paying a fine and being mugged for the fine's amount.

jonah said we already accept substitution all over the place: parents pay their kids' parking tickets, in hockey a teammate serves another player's penalty, and in lots of legal systems a family used to be able to pay blood-money for a relative's crime. so priya's "can't transfer" is just squeamishness dressed up as metaphysics.

priya replied that in the ticket and blood-money cases what transfers is money, not punishment, and the hockey case is really the team being punished as a unit (a new punishment, not a transferred one). and she pushed the point further: notice lewis's case is fully consensual — the offender wants it, the victim doesn't object, the volunteer freely chose it. so her claim isn't that someone's being cheated; it's that with nobody cheated and no suffering by the guilty, the thing simply stops being *punishment* and becomes, at best, some weird ritual between friends.

jonah's last word was that this proves too much: if punishing someone who insists they were justified, or doesn't accept guilt, still counts as punishment (and it does — we punish people who don't feel guilty all the time), then the guilty person's inner state can't be what makes it punishment. and he added that a volunteer suffering in your place arguably expresses the community's condemnation even more vividly than jailing a resentful offender does, so maybe it's priya's view that mangles the concept.

they asked me to referee and honestly i couldn't. can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what? and tell me if there's something both of them are missing about what lewis was actually pointing at with the "of two minds" thing?
