I'm a novelist who has spent the last six years writing historical fiction set in the 1840s, and I've found that drafting these books has forced me to commit, in practice, to a set of claims about how we may morally engage with the past — claims I've never properly examined together, and which I now suspect pull against each other in ways I can't locate. Let me lay them out. First, a craft conviction: the historical novel should render its agents from within their own normative universe, using their moral vocabulary rather than ours, and I regard anachronism — a planter's daughter in 1843 suddenly speaking as though briefed by a modern ethics seminar — as a failure of imaginative fidelity, not merely of accuracy. Second, the novelist qua narrator must not editorialize retrospectively; the book should show and never scold. Third, and despite those first two, I believe the historical novel is fundamentally a moral instrument: a novel about slavery that leaves its present-day reader comfortable with slavery has failed, whatever its fidelity, and the tradition I work in exists largely to indict past evils for living readers. Fourth, I think present readers are entitled — obliged, even — to condemn past actors and practices by our own standards; I endorse that presentist criticism as fully legitimate and don't accept that it's some vulgar anachronism. Fifth, a fair-opportunity principle I find myself applying in characterization: an individual agent is not blameworthy for failing to see what their entire world rendered invisible, so my slaveholding planter is a man acting within the only moral horizon he has, and I write him accordingly. Sixth, I hold that present moral standards are not merely different from past ones but in some respects genuinely better — that there has been real moral progress. And seventh, I accept that readers a century from now will find my own novels complicit in something I cannot see, and I accept their judgment as just.

The problem is that when I try to state these together, I can feel friction in several places, but I can't tell which frictions are real. For instance, it seems like the show-don't-scold rule and the indictment claim must conflict — how can a book sealed inside the period's categories condemn the period? — yet I also have the vague sense that there's some device or structure by which the reader condemns from outside while the narrator never does, and I can't tell whether that resolves the tension or merely hides it. And the fair-opportunity principle sits oddly with my endorsement of presentist condemnation: I want my reader to condemn the planter, while my own principle about blameworthiness seems to take him off the hook. Then there is what worries me most, though I may be wrong about it: if I accept the future reader's judgment of me as just, in advance and whatever its content, it's not clear what grounds my confidence that my standards are the better ones now — or whether condemning by one's own lights is the same enterprise when one is the one being condemned.

What I'd like is a proper audit rather than a survey of positions: go through my seven commitments, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me at the level of craft — since some of these are, for a novelist, working principles rather than abstract theses, and a revision that is cheap in theory may be ruinous in the writing chair. Where a tension dissolves, show me exactly how; where it doesn't, be specific about which formulation of mine is doing the damage. Finally, if you had to recommend one single revision that would clean up the most, tell me which, and say honestly whether the resulting package would still leave me with a historical novel worth writing. I have no settled view about which commitment I'd surrender, so don't infer one from my ordering.
