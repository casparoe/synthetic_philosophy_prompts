I run a small game theory reading group (grad students plus a couple of stubborn undergrads) and our next session is on Nash's bargaining solution vs the Kalai-Smorodinsky alternative. Everyone can recite the axioms fine, but I'm convinced nobody's actual intuitions match the solution they claim to like — half of them say they prefer KS on grounds that would actually commit them to the egalitarian solution, and one guy keeps insisting the whole axiom game is pointless and it's all about bargaining power anyway. So I want to actually catch them out.

Write me a "which bargaining solution do you *actually* accept?" quiz. The setup for every question: two players, disagreement point normalized to (0,0), some feasible set of utility pairs, and a fair arbitrator who must impose a single point. 8 to 10 questions, each a concrete bargaining problem with actual numbers or a simple frontier sketched in words, and two or three answer options phrased as verdicts about which splits are fair or unfair. Never ask "do you accept the monotonicity axiom" or anything labeled like that — a student who's never heard of Nash or KS should be able to answer every question cold.

Must include at least one case of each of these, though you can disguise the framing however you like:

- a situation where player 1's side of the feasible set expands — their personal best possible payoff grows while player 2's best stays put — and the old fair split gave player 1 some amount; is the new fair split allowed to give player 1 *less* than before?
- a situation where you delete some alternative that no fair arbitrator would ever pick but that isn't dominated (say, one that's amazing for one player and near-disastrous for the other), and whether the fair point is allowed to move;
- a case where the two players' maximum possible gains differ by a lot, so "equal absolute gains" and "equal fraction of what's possible for you" come apart;
- a question probing whether the two players' utility scales are comparable at all (e.g. one player's payoffs quoted in money, the other's in a psychologist's made-up happiness points) and what follows if they're not;
- one fully symmetric problem as a calibration item — and be upfront in the key that this one can't seperate anyone, it's just there to catch people who are answering vibes.

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves: Nash, Kalai-Smorodinsky, egalitarian (equal absolute gains), and the skeptic leaf where any Pareto-improvement-over-disagreement point is as good as any other and the axioms are answering a question that has no answer. If the logical space allows answer combinations that don't map to any leaf, note them and diagnose why nobody's there (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). Also flag explicitly which pairs of solutions the quiz can't pull apart — Nash and KS agree on symmetry, Pareto, and invariance to rescaling each player's utilities, so don't pretend some question splits them when it can't, and don't let the comparability question quietly double as the Nash-vs-KS splitter when it's really the egalitarian-vs-the-rest splitter.

Two more things. If some case you construct turns out not to actually seperate the solutions you intended — e.g. your numbers accidentally put Nash and KS at the same point — say so in the key rather than quietly fixing the numbers and moving on. And end with a short self-critique: where is the scoring key most likely to mis-sort someone, which question is most likely to pull readers toward an answer for reasons unrelated to the underlying commitment (framing, numbers being evocative, whatever), and what specifically would make you restructure the quiz if it showed up in the answers.
