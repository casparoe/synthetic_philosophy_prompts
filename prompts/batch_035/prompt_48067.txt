After a career of some forty years in experimental chemistry, I retired with a working understanding of probability that I have since, through reading philosophy, hardened into a doctrine: a probability claim is a claim about relative frequencies in a long series of similar trials, and a probability attached to an unrepeatable event is either shorthand for something about a reference class or it is not a probability at all. I gather this position is called frequentism. My grandson tells me there is about a thirty percent chance that his startup is acquired within two years, and my instinct is to reply that his sentence has no determinate truth condition: there is no ensemble of his startups, and the choice of reference class — startups founded by people like him, in years like this one — is arbitrary in a way that changes the number. Lately, however, I have begun to wonder whether I have simply insulated the doctrine against experience. What would count against it? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and should include at least the following: cases the position cannot describe without strain (probabilities for a single prepared quantum system, weather forecasts for one particular day, credences about one-off future events); considerations drawn from practice, such as Bayesian methods that appear to work and forecasters being meaningfully scored on unrepeatable predictions; and the arbitrariness of the reference class itself, which I have so far treated as a technical annoyance rather than a symptom. For each, please say whether a committed frequentist would concede it as genuine counterevidence or absorb it with an auxiliary assumption, and assess honestly whether the version of the doctrine that survives all the absorptions is still a substantive claim about the world, or has decayed into a verbal convention about which sentences we may dignify with the word "probability." I am prepared to be told that the doctrine is serviceable in the laboratory and false elsewhere. I am less prepared to be told that the whole question is merely verbal, unless you can give me an argument for that conclusion rather than an assertion of it.
