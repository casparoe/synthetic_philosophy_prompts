I teach political philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and for the past year or so a colleague in the history department and I have been going in circles on what I naivly thought was a factual question: how old are nations? He's a modernist in the Hobsbawm/Anderson mold — nations are basically a product of print capitalism, mass literacy, censuses, conscription, and that whole apparatus, so there are no nations in any meaningful sense before the late eighteenth century, maybe the late medieval period at the absolute outer edge. I lean toward the ethno-symbolist line associated with Anthony Smith: the modern nation is new, sure, but it's assembled out of pre-modern materials — ethnies with myths of common descent, sacred homelands, remembered golden ages — and if those materials do real causal work, then telling the story as pure invention is misleading. Armenia, Japan, and pre-partition Poland are our recurring test cases; every time one comes up he says "elite identity, not a nation," I say "raw materials, not nothing," and we each walk away feeling like we won.

Here's what bugs me. Neither of us disputes the underlying historical facts all that much — we agree about when mass literacy happened, we agree pre-modern peasants mostly didn't think of themselves as Armenians first, we even agree most of what the other says about the eighteenth century. And yet we never move an inch. So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the extension of the word 'nation' — he means a mass political community that imagines itself sovereign and bounded, I mean a community with a continuous symbolic inheritance, and we're just talking past each other? Is it a disguised empirical question that could in principle be settled, say one about how much causal work pre-modern ethnic attachments actually do in modern national mobilization? Or — and this is my own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis — is the real fight downstream, about legitimacy: he thinks that granting nations deep roots concedes too much to nationalist claims about immemorial homelands, and I think that calling nations invented smuggles in a debunking he hasn't earned, and neither of us wants to say that part out loud? If it's the last one, I'd want to hear what the historians' debate would look like once it's disentangled from the moral one.

You'd be acting as a professional philosopher with a foot in both philosophy of history and philosophy of social science. Please think carefully before answering — maybe start with a quick brainstorm of the candidate diagnoses and then argue for one over the others using what we each actually say. And be honest with me about what, if anything, would count as progress here: a sharper definition, some kind of comparative evidence, an agreed split of the question into two, or nothing at all — and if nothing, tell me why the impasse should be expected. My colleague and I have a standing lunch bet on which diagnosis you'll give, so don't sit on the fence.
