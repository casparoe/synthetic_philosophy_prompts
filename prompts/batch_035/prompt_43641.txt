I am drafting a book whose thesis is that the deep curve of history is set by non-human actors — pathogen ecologies, climatic regimes, volcanic forcings — while human institutions merely modulate outcomes within an envelope that biology and geophysics have already drawn. On my telling, the germs of the Eurasian landmass, not any superiority of institutions, decided the direction of colonization after 1492; volcanic winters like the one that began in 536 CE and the climate-driven reintroductions of plague from Central Asian rodent reservoirs did more to shape the late antique and medieval worlds than any emperor; and the Little Ice Age's harvest failures did more to destabilize the old regime than Enlightenment pamphlets. Institutions, on this view, are weatherstripping: they can slow or speed what the environment imposes, but they cannot open the window. I would like a rigorous adversarial review of this position before I commit to it in print.

Please take the role of a philosopher of history and a skeptical historian of science, someone equally at home with grand causal narratives and with the archival details that embarrass them. Think carefully and at length; before writing your answer, take a moment to brainstorm broadly and privately, so that what you present is a considered map of the position's exposure rather than the first objections that come to mind.

What I want is a ranked inventory of what would count against the view — from fatal, through serious, to merely awkward. Distinguish observations that would decisively favor an institutionalist or contingent-history rival from those my position could absorb with minor qualifications. I am especially interested in cases where the same environmental input produced radically different outcomes, or where human arrangements visibly overrode what germs and climate would have predicted: quarantines and lazaretti, variolation campaigns, the sanitation movement, rice-versus-wheat frontiers within a single climate band, or neighboring polities that diverged under identical pathogen exposure. Say honestly which of these a committed environmentalist would concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as staying within the envelope, and whether those dismissals are principled or motivated.

Two further demands. First, tell me what the position predicts that its rivals do not — where it risks something, and where it is hard to distinguish from institutionalism even in principle. Second, assess whether my formulation, with its envelope metaphor, has already been qualified into unfalsifiability: if any institutional success can be redescribed as modulation within a generous envelope, what would ever count as the envelope being breached? If it has been insulated, say whether that is a fatal flaw for a thesis of this kind or a defect shared by all large-scale causal history, and whether there is a disciplined way to restate the claim so that it risks more. Conclude with the single piece of evidence or argument you judge most likely to move someone who currently holds my view, and why.
