I've been reading LessWrong and the slate-star-sphere long enough to have watched the IIT-versus-everyone fights, the Global Workspace threads, and the recurring is-consciousness-just-attention debates come and go. One thing I've noticed: higher-order theories get dismissed quickly in these circles because everyone rehearses the same two cases — the absent-minded truck driver and blindsight — declares the theory defeated, and moves on. I want a sharper instrument. Build me a thought experiment to the following specifications, all of which must be met:

1. The scenario: one paragraph, everyday life, no lab equipment, no lesions, no drugs, no sci-fi neural editing. A normal subject going about their business has a first-order perceptual representation of X while simultaneously having a higher-order representation of their current experience as being of Y, where Y differs from X. The mismatch must arise from something mundane — a mishearing, an over-eager categorization under attentional load, a snap mislabeling — not from pathology or exotic intervention.

2. You may not merely stipulate that the higher-order representation exists. You must motivate its presence from what is independently plausible about attention, categorization, and judgment in the situation you describe, so that a skeptic about higher-order states can't just deny the setup.

3. The walkthrough: state what the higher-order theorist must say (the subject is consciously experiencing Y), what the first-order theorist must say (the subject is consciously experiencing X), and how much strain each has to absorb. If it helps, say whether your case targets the inner-sense version, the thought version, or both — but take a stand on which higher-order variant is most exposed here.

4. A second phase: introduce one further ordinary event — the subject reaching for the object, or being asked an immediate question about it — and show what each theory predicts about the subject's report and behavior, and whether the predictions actually diverge. If they don't diverge, say so plainly and concede that the case separates the theories only on the phenomenal question, not the behavioral one.

5. Identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict once you remove it.

6. Give the strongest counterattack available to the theorist who comes out worse — the best repair on offer, not a strawman — and say whether the case survives it.

The point of the case is to isolate the misrepresentation problem: whether phenomenal character attaches to whatever the higher-order representation says it attaches to, or to whatever the first-order state is actually about. So a scenario that falls back on the usual suspects — blindsight, subliminal priming, dreams, the truck driver — fails the spec, as does one that smuggles in the verdict by flatly asserting that the subject consciously experiences Y rather than generating that verdict from the theory.

Take a definite position throughout; don't end with both theories can accommodate this. Hard limit: 650 words for the scenario and walkthrough combined, so be economical.
