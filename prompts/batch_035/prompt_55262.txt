I'm in a graduate seminar on causation, and for my final position paper I've committed to writing about whether absences and omissions can be genuine causes. Before I present, I'd like an external consistency check on the package of views I've drifted into, because I suspect some of them fight each other but I can't see where. Here's roughly where I stand: (1) Causation is an objective feature of the world, on all fours with things like chemical bonding -- whether X caused Y is not even partly a matter of our interests, what happens to be salient to us, or the purposes of a particular inquiry. (2) Omissions can really cause things: the neighbor who agreed to water my plants while I was away and didn't caused their death, full stop, not in some loose or courtroom-honorific sense. (3) My underlying picture of causation is roughly the transference one: in the paradigm cases, what makes a cause a cause is that something concrete gets passed along from cause to effect -- momentum, energy, charge -- and I regard counterfactual dependence as at best a derivative or imperfect marker of that. (4) I accept the standard counterexamples running in both directions against counterfactual accounts: some things that are but-for dependent on an outcome aren't its causes (the usual backup and preemption cases), and some causes aren't but-for dependent on their effects. (5) I think double preventers can be genuine causes: if an interceptor destroys a missile that would otherwise have destroyed a ship, the interceptor's hit is among the causes of the ship's surviving. (6) Finally, while the neighbor's failure to water my plants was a cause of their death, the simultaneous failures of the millions of other people who also didn't water my plants were not causes of anything, and I'm quite confident that asymmetry is correct rather than an artifact of who I happen to be annoyed with.

What I'd like from you is an audit, not a lecture on the literature I could look up myself: go through the six commitments, identify which pairs or larger subsets are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please also flag, as you go, where the audit has to take sides on questions that people who work on causation genuinely disagree about -- which parts of your diagnosis would be near-uncontroversial among specialists and which reflect one contested line of thought rather than a settled view -- since for the presentation I need to know which of my liabilities are real and which are just the particular cuts you chose to make.
