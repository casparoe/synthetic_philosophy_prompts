I teach a graduate seminar on confirmation and statistical inference, and I am assembling a case study for my students that goes deeper than the standard "null hypothesis significance testing is broken, therefore Bayes" polemic. The episode is the psychology replication crisis, anchored on the 2011 publication of Daryl Bem's nine experiments purporting to demonstrate precognition, the Bayesian reanalysis arguing that his data did not in fact support the psi hypothesis, the subsequent failures to replicate, and the reform movement those failures catalyzed — preregistration, registered reports, larger samples, and the multi-lab replication consortia. The lens is the dispute between frequentist and Bayesian statistics, and I want the analysis to run in both directions: theory illuminating case, and case testing theory.

Please structure your analysis around the following four tasks.

First, do the mapping carefully. Under each framework, state precisely what Bem's statistically significant results were supposed to license, and what the reanalysis and the failed replications did to that license. Distinguish explicitly between "the data would be surprising under the null" and "the hypothesis has higher posterior probability than its rivals," and explain what a failed replication amounts to under each reading — a posterior update, or evidence that the original procedure's advertised error guarantees were illusory. A disciplined answer will say what counts as the evidence, what counts as the hypothesis, and what counts as the procedure, rather than sliding between them.

Second, identify which features of the crisis each framework genuinely illuminates. Candidates worth your attention: the claim that optional stopping invalidates p-values but not Bayes factors, and whether that asymmetry actually mattered in this episode or is a textbook point with little purchase on what broke; the argument that Bem's data looked weaker under a Bayes factor than under a p-value, and what that asymmetry does and does not show about the frameworks themselves; and Paul Meehl's old observation that in the social sciences the null hypothesis is never exactly true, so that a sufficiently powered significance test of a directional prediction approaches an even chance of "succeeding" for reasons unconnected to the theory under test — a point that cuts against a certain habitual use of frequentist tools but does not obviously vindicate Bayesian ones. Where the frameworks each get something right, say so; I am not looking for a verdict for one side.

Third, say where each framework is silent or actively misdescribes the episode. Much of what actually broke was a matter of design and reporting rather than inference: researcher degrees of freedom, selective publication, and, in the worst cases, outright fabrication of data. Neither prior probabilities nor long-run error rates have anything direct to say about a fabricated dataset. And note an awkward fact for the popular Bayesian retelling of the crisis: preregistration, arguably the reform that has worked best, is usually justified in frequentist terms — it fixes in advance the procedure whose error rates are being guaranteed — whereas it is not obvious what a committed subjective Bayesian should say about the demand that the analysis be fixed in advance, since the posterior over the data one already has does not depend on the order in which one formed one's intentions. Is that asymmetry real, or can the Bayesian give an internal justification of preregistration? Do not force the frameworks to speak where they are silent; I would much rather read an honest "this part of the crisis had nothing to do with the dispute" than a strained mapping.

Fourth, close by saying what the episode shows about the statistical dispute itself. There are at least three readings on the table: (a) the crisis is a confirmation of Bayesianism, which is why its early diagnosis was Bayesian; (b) the crisis indicts a degenerate institutional practice of frequentist inference — significance thresholds, optional stopping, selective publication — and not frequentism as such; and (c) the crisis shows that the whole frequentist–Bayesian framing was largely orthogonal to what broke, and that the reforms that worked required at most the machinery, not the philosophy, of either camp. Say which reading the episode best supports, and — crucially — say what would have to have gone differently, in the actual history, for one of the other readings to be the right one.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. Precision about statistical concepts counts for more than citation names; where you invoke a specific episode detail (Bem's design, the reanalysis, the replications), supply the salient facts as part of your exposition, since your reader should not need to consult anything else.
