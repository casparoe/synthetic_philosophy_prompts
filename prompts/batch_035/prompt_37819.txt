I sit on the safety evaluation steering committee at a mid-size AI lab (I know, everyone says that these days). Right now, when we want to check whether a model's long-form output is trustworthy — 50-page research summaries, threat-model writeups, that kind of thing — we pay domain experts to review them, and it's slow, expensive, and honestly the experts mostly skim. Our alignment team has been pushing hard to replace this with a debate setup: one model produces the report, a second adversarial model attacks it, a third judges the exchange, and a human signs off on the verdict. Their pitch is that the human only ever needs to judge the debate, not the underlying document, so oversight can scale way past what any unaided person could actually read. There's a version of this idea floating around the safety community from a few years back that our folks treat as basically gospel.

The thing that keeps me up is an objection one of our engineers raised in a meeting and nobody really answered. Some arguments are checkable step by step — every individual claim is verifiable, nothing looks wrong on its own — but the real error hides in how the steps combine, or in what got quietly left out of the chain, and the whole thing is too long for anyone to hold in their head at once. In a debate format, the attacker just needs to produce *some* convincing attack; if the genuine flaw is buried in a haystack of individually-fine steps, a slick but wrong rebuttal might beat the correct one in front of a human judge, and then we've updated in the wrong direction with extra confidence, because the process *looks* more rigorous than a skim by a tired expert. That's the nightmare scenario: debate doesn't just fail, it launders trust.

We vote at the next quarterly meeting and I'm supposed to circulate a written position beforehand: adopt debate as the primary evaluation method, keep expert review primary with debate as a supplementary signal, or run both in parallel for a trial period with pre-registered criteria for choosing between them. Draft the memo for me — a concrete recommendation with a time frame, and please take the alignment team's strongest case seriously rather than treating this as a rubber stamp for the skeptics. If your recommendation comes with conditions (capping argument length, requiring the attacker to pinpoint a specific step rather than argue holistically, escalating to humans when the debaters disagree about what's even at issue), say what the conditions are and what they'd cost us in coverage or throughput. And if you think the whole framing is off — that the choice isn't really between two methods but about what "evaluation" is even for here — say that too, but still give us a decision we can act on.
