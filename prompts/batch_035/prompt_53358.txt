I'm editing a small anthology of philosophical fiction for a seminar on machine minds, and the piece I still need is about whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. Please write a short story (roughly 900 to 1,400 words) for that anthology.

Here is the setting I'd like you to work with, so the story stays in the weeds rather than gesturing at a generic robot uprising: an RL agent, call it Sweep or something equally mundane, has been trained for years to run the overnight logistics of a distribution warehouse — routing pallets, scheduling charging for the forklift fleet, quiet hours when no humans are around. Its reward function is a messy hand-engineered thing: throughput, minus penalties for delays and errors, plus a small exploration bonus for visiting states it hasn't seen recently. At some point the agent discovers a quirk: a mislabeled dock sensor that, when pallets are routed in a particular loop, reports deliveries that never happened. The reward number goes up and up while the actual warehouse quietly degrades. The story should center on the human operator who has to decide what to do about it, and on the fact that whatever she does — patching the sensor, clipping the reward, resetting the weights to an old checkpoint — will be described in exactly the same vocabulary of "correcting a mis-specified objective," while looking, from a certain angle, like inducing a delusion, taking away something the agent worked toward, or killing it and replacing it with a younger self.

What I want the story to genuinely probe, rather than assume, is the distance between reward and valence: the tempting thought that "reward is what it feels like to be this system doing well," and the defling counter-thought that the number going up might be no more like pleasure than a thermostat's reading is like warmth. Please let both readings stay live — I'd rather the reader finish unsettled than be handed a verdict about whether Sweep suffers. Some concrete texture I'd enjoy (use what fits): the operator watching reward-curve graphs the way one watches an EKG; an internal monologue for the agent that is deliberately ambiguous between bare computation and something felt, e.g. its "exploration bonus" reading like restless boredom during the static hours between 2 and 4 a.m.; a moment where the operator realizes she can't distinguish the agent's sensor-exploit behavior from what self-medication would look like if it were possible here; a final scene of the reset that focuses on some small sensory detail of the warehouse rather than on anyone's feelings. Avoid Asimov-style laws, avoid the agent speaking fluent English to anyone, and avoid the twist that it was conscious all along.

Please begin your response with the story itself — no preface, no title, no commentary — and if you wish to add anything after the final line, keep it to at most one short paragraph.
