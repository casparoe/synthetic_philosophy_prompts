I've been sitting in on a reading group that started in population ethics and drifted into the infinite case, and one of our members has produced an argument that has half the room ready to give up on aggregating welfare altogether. Each step looked defensible when he said it, and the conclusion is one nobody in the room accepts, so I assume something slipped — but I cannot find where.

(1) The value of a world is determined by the well-being of its welfare subjects, aggregated by summing: one world is at least as good as another just in case it contains at least as much total well-being. (2) Nothing in logic or physics rules out Omega, a world with countably infinitely many welfare subjects, each with positive well-being; and any such world's total is infinite, which no finite subtraction or finite addition can raise or lower. (3) So Omega is exactly as good as Omega with one happy life deleted, exactly as good as Omega with an extra tortured child inserted, and exactly as good as Omega with a thousand of each. (4) When we pressed him on insertion — surely adding a happy life to paradise makes it better — he had an answer I still can't shake: insert the happy life and shift every existing subject one slot later. Match the worlds person by person, and the second has everything the first has plus one happy life; match them slot by slot, and each slot holds a flourishing subject in both. Since neither matching is more legitimate than the other, he says the insertion is exactly neutral — the verdict (2) already delivered, now derived without any arithmetic. (5) In any infinite world, every act available to an agent differs from its alternatives only by finitely many such deletions, insertions, and amendments, since acts are local. (6) So in any infinite world, no available act is better or worse than any other: in Omega, torturing the child in the new slot for amusement is exactly on a par with rescuing him. (7) Omega is possible, and a theory of value that, in any possible world, ranks all available acts on a par is not a theory of value at all. Therefore (1) is false, and with it, he believes, every view that aggregates well-being by summing.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (7), though I can't defend both inclinations yet. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to begin with, and what rejecting it costs — including, if you think the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to lose and what we pay in the finite cases the theory was built for.
