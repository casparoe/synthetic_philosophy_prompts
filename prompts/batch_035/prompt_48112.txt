I serve on a hospital's clinical ethics committee, and I've become bothered by an asymmetry in how we treat advance directives that everyone on the committee can justify only by convention, never in principle. When a patient wrote a directive while competent that refuses treatment — declining dialysis should she develop advanced dementia, for instance — we honor it almost without hesitation, even when the patient, now demented, is cheerful, content, asks for the treatment, and would visibly suffer from our refusing it. The earlier self, we say, had the authority to decide for this one, and the current self's disagreement doesn't matter. But when a directive requests rather than refuses — asks that everything possible be done, including aggressive interventions the now-demented patient visibly hates, or, where it is legal, requests a life-ending act at a specified point the current patient no longer wants — we suddenly weigh the present patient's wishes and welfare heavily, and often let them override the directive. So the earlier self's "no" binds, while its "yes" barely does. What makes this stranger is that outside medicine we do let past selves bind future selves in both directions: promises, contracts, and marriages entered into earlier can obligate a reluctant later self to act, not merely to refrain. Why medicine treats the earlier self as a veto-holder but not a commander is what I can't articulate.

I'd like someone to reconstruct the theory of the earlier self's authority that this practice presupposes — what view of whose interest is at stake, and of which self gets to speak for the person, would make the asymmetry principled rather than arbitrary. A few candidate diagnoses occur to me, but I don't trust my own judgment on which is right: perhaps the practice assumes the competent self has privileged access to the person's enduring values, so its refusals track what the person really wants while its requests are mere predictions, correctable by the present patient's actual welfare; perhaps it assumes a protected baseline of bodily integrity that only the current occupant can waive, so earlier selves may decline but never authorize; or perhaps it is not about authority at all, but a policy about who should bear the cost of our uncertainty over what the demented patient would really want. Please say which of these, or something else, the practice actually requires; whether its different parts presuppose consistent things; and where it is underdetermined, such that several rival theories of personal identity or autonomy would equally license the same rules. Then tell me what would have to change in the committee's day-to-day practice if we replaced the implicit view with a strict one on which the earlier competent self's recorded values bind in full, or with a purely presentist one on which the current patient's experiences and wishes are all that counts.

Throughout, please indicate how confident you are in each step of the reconstruction, and single out the point you are least sure of — I would rather know where the ground is soft than receive a seamless-looking theory.
