so I'm on the volunteer board of a small community arts fund, we give out micro-grants a few times a year, and last month we had a genuinely uncomfortable fight about one of our panelists. she openly favors applications from artists in our county, and when someone accused her of bias she pushed back that it's a *preference* — she thinks art rooted in a particular place is a real aesthetic good, and that our fund's whole reason for existing is sustaining the local scene. half the board thinks that's totally fine and even admirable, half thinks it's textbook bias, and nobody could articulate what the actual difference is. I brought it up with a friend from my book club and she floated the cynical take: that "preference" versus "bias" is just a label difference, basically PR — a preference is just a bias we happen to like. I don't buy that but I also can't refute it, which bugs me.

so here's what I want you to build me. construct a pair of characters whose moral psychologies are matched as closely as you can manage — same moral judgment, held with the same strength, expressed in the same behavior — but where one of them is clearly biased and the other clearly has a legitimate moral preference. constraints: don't let the difference be that one judgment is correct and the other isn't (that would beg the question against my friend, and besides, in the panelist fight people are clearly just split on who's right); don't let it be that one judgment serves the character's own interests, since neither state should benefit its holder at all; don't let the two differ in any factual belief or piece of evidence; and keep it realistic — a grant panel, a hiring committee, a sports club, something like that, no sci-fi and no ideally rational agents. one paragraph for the pair is plenty.

after the scenario, I need you to show the thing actually works rather than just asserting it does. run the pair through at least three candidate criteria for the preference/bias line — I'm thinking something like (a) a scrutiny test: whether the person would keep the state after honestly thinking it through, (b) a sensitivity test: what the state causally tracks, i.e. what would have to change in the world for it to change, and (c) whatever you think the real criterion is if it's neither of those — and for each one say which character it sorts which way and why. then tell me which single feature of the pair is doing all the work, what happens to the case if you delete that feature, and how my cynical friend would attack the case and whether that attack actually lands. the whole point is to test her slogan, so if your scenario secretly just stipulates the bias/preference difference instead of generating it from a principled criterion, it's useless to me — I need to be able to point at the specific thing that separates them and say "that, that's the difference, and here's why it matters."
