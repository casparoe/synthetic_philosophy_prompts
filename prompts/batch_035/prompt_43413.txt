I've spent enough years on rationalist blogs and forums that the wild-animal suffering arguments feel like old friends: that predation, disease, and starvation mean pristine nature may be a net source of suffering rather than a benign idyll; that because most wild animals are small, short-lived, and wildly fecund, with most offspring dying before maturity, the aggregate welfare of an ecosystem could well be negative; and the corollary people find hardest to swallow, that habitat destruction, by shrinking the number of wild lives lived at all, might reduce total suffering — a conclusion most of us resist on intuition while admitting the intuition does no argumentative work.

I recently rewatched Princess Mononoke and realized it is nearly a perfect test case for that whole debate, which nobody seems to have run. The standard reading is environmentalist: Lady Eboshi's Irontown strips the forest to feed its iron works, the animal gods fight a losing war against it, and the beheading of the Forest Spirit is a kind of deicide that the ending partially atones for. But watch what the forest actually contains. A boar god rots from an iron ball lodged in his body until hatred turns him into a demon. Moro's wolf pack hunts. The boar clan, half-blind and consumed by wounded pride, marches to a mass death in a battle they were manipulated into. Even the film's curse mechanic — suffering that metastasizes into a consuming hatred that spreads to whatever it touches — reads like a thesis about the self-propagation of misery.

Read the film through the lens of ecosystem net welfare. Does it take any position on whether the suffering of the forest's animals matters morally, or on whether razing it would be good or bad for them? Which scenes are evidence either way — the demon boar, the boar clan's suicide charge, the Forest Spirit's healing, the final regrowth? Is the film committed to a flourishing ecosystem being valuable full stop, or does it leave the aggregate question genuinely open? Where does it resist the welfarist reading, and the honest critic has to concede the resistance? And here is the question I most want your view on: by making the animals gods — wolves who reason, a boar who would rather die charging than flee — the film gives wild suffering faces, biographies, and agency that the philosophical literature's r-selected millions lack. Does that personification dramatize something the welfare calculus actually misses, or does it smuggle in a picture of wild animals as persons that the vast majority of them fail to satisfy, making the film's sympathy inadmissible evidence? I'm writing this up for my blog and want a reading that takes both the film and the welfarist argument seriously, and says clearly what the film adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't.
