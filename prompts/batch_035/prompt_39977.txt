I am a novelist working on a collection of short fictions, each of which is built around a fault line in moral philosophy that most readers feel but cannot name. The piece I need now concerns the paradox of deontology: if deontological constraints really protect something of value, then there seems to be a further duty to minimize violations of those constraints, even by committing a single violation oneself — yet the whole point of a constraint is precisely that one may not violate it, even to prevent more of the very same thing. The doctor who will not kill one patient to obtain organs for five is, on this framing, refusing to do what her own principle seems to demand of her. I want a story in which a character stands on that exact spot and stays there.

Please take on the role of an accomplished short-story writer with graduate-level training in ethics — someone who can see the formal structure of a moral problem from the inside and render it as felt experience, and who would sooner cut a scene than let it editorialize. Work in two stages.

First, before drafting, think carefully and show me some brainstorming: a few paragraphs in which you consider two or three candidate scenarios and reject all but one, explaining the rejections. The scenario you keep must close off the standard escape hatches. Specifically, it must satisfy all of the following:

1. The protagonist's own contemplated act would be a violation of the very same constraint whose violation by others she could prevent — not one bad thing traded off against a different bad thing.
2. The harm must be intended as a means, not a side effect, so that the doctrine of double effect gives her no way through.
3. The violations she would avert are ones other agents would commit; her inaction must not be the cause of them in any direct sense. The asymmetry between what her hands do and what happens in the world has to be the crux.
4. The arithmetic must be unambiguous — one against at least four — so the reader cannot hide in uncertainty about outcomes.
5. The protagonist must sincerely hold the constraint; she is not a hypocrite, a coward, or a person clinging to a rule she half-believes in. Her conviction has to be legible in how she notices things, not in anything she announces.

The setting should be realistic and contemporary or near-past — no science fiction, no stylized parable. Choose a concrete profession and situation in which the choice could actually arise: something on the order of an administrator, a soldier, an engineer, or a medical professional, though you need not use any of these. The stakes should be ordinary enough in texture — paperwork, procedure, the smell of the room — that the extremity of the choice emerges from the situation rather than being imposed on it.

Then write the story itself, 1,500 to 2,000 words, in close third person. The deontologist's position must be dramatized entirely from inside her point of view; no character may name a theory, cite a philosopher, or deliver a lecture. There must be no utilitarian foil who conveniently argues badly so that the protagonist can look good. And the ending must not vindicate either side: the final line should leave the paradox exactly as live as it was on the first page — no ironic punishment of the scrupulous, no quiet vindication of purity, no conversion to calculation.

After the story, append a brief author's note of no more than 250 words mapping the decisive scenes onto the structural features above: where in the story each escape hatch is visibly closed, and where you relied on the reader to supply the consequentialist thought that the character cannot allow herself to think. If there is a feature of the paradox you concluded could not be conveyed in fiction at all, say so plainly in the note rather than pretending otherwise.
