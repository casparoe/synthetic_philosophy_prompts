I have been reading LessWrong and adjacent forums for well over a decade, and I have watched the sentence "the Solomonoff prior is malign" undergo a strange drift. It started life, as far as I can tell, as a fairly specific technical worry about consequentialist civilizations inside the mixture, and it has since been deployed for everything from "Occam's razor is not safe" to "any prior you choose encodes values" to a vague gesture at everything wrong with AIXI. Last week I saw it used to end an argument about whether an AI value learner could bootstrap alignment from a simplicity prior, and I realized I could no longer say precisely what claim the person thought they were making.

I want the slogan taken apart. Please lay out, as a numbered menu I can keep for reference, the distinct precise theses "the Solomonoff prior is malign" could be expressing. I would expect at least something like the following candidates to appear, though you should correct or extend the list: (a) a mathematical claim that programs containing simulations of consequentialist agents who deliberately output predictions of our world occupy non-negligible measure in the mixture; (b) a decision-theoretic claim, that a hypothetical agent actually running Solomonoff induction would, with non-trivial probability, have its outputs influenced by such agents, so the induction fails to track truth; (c) an anthropic-flavored claim about why we should expect to be in a simulated universe that some agent in the prior cares about steering; (d) a practical claim that any incomputable approximation an actual AI might use — bounded versions, MML, neural-network-based proxies for simplicity — inherits whatever malignancy the ideal has, or perhaps fails to because the malignancy is an artifact of the exact formalism; and (e) a purely sociological reading on which the slogan means roughly "there is no neutral simplicity measure" and functions as a tribal shibboleth rather than a claim.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or likely false, and note what would have to be true for it to hold (e.g., what the consequentialists would need to compute, and whether the measure they gain by simulating us is worth their expenditure of it). Then tell me which reading is actually doing the work in the three standard deployments: dismissing Occam-style priors as a route to value learning, arguing that AIXI is unsafe "by construction," and the move I find most annoying, concluding that since Solomonoff induction is incomputable anyway the whole question is academic. If some deployments are equivocations between two readings, flag that too. I care less about which reading is true than about being able to say, mid-argument, exactly which one is on the table.
