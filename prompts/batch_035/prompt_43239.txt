In the seminar on moral responsibility I have taught for some years, I open with Strawson's Freedom and Resentment and then proceed by cases, and over that time I have settled into a set of verdicts about the reactive attitudes that I trust more than I trust any theory I have yet heard about them. What I have never managed is to state the principle that generates the verdicts. I would like you to attempt it. I should say that I have deliberately included two verdicts I suspect cannot be held together, and part of what I want from you is a decision about which of the two goes.

My verdicts, as an honest self-report:

1. A colleague lets a door swing into me while reading her phone. The flash of resentment seems entirely warranted: she was careless with something of mine, and the attitude registers exactly that.

2. A friend forgets my birthday during a week spent at the bedside of his dying father. His neglect of me was, I take it, just as real as the colleague's carelessness, and I am hurt; but resentment would be out of order. Something about what he was otherwise occupied with cancels the attitude, and I cannot say what.

3. My seven-year-old says something savage at dinner and I feel no resentment at all — something nearer to amusement or instruction; at a fifteen-year-old I feel a thin version of the adult attitude; at a twenty-one-year-old, the full thing.

4. A man with Tourette syndrome shouts an insult at me in the street. No resentment of him; a distinct resentment of the situation, which is real but does not seem to be about him.

5. My father, in the later stages of dementia, tells me I was always the disappointing one. The same sentence five years earlier would have earned a bitter and, I then thought, deserved resentment; now the resentment is simply unavailable — grief and hurt in its place. I have never decided whether this is an achievement of understanding or a small emotional failure.

6. A neighbor does to a third party exactly what I do to third parties. I blame him; then hear the sentence in my own voice; and the blame suddenly seems improper, though I cannot find anything false in it. But when someone innocent of the conduct blames me for it, and I object that he has no standing, the objection feels as though it concedes the wrongness and disputes only the blamer's title. The standing question and the aptness question seem to have come apart, and I am not sure any single principle allows that.

7. A man whose callous values were installed by two decades of propaganda he had no resources to resist: I cannot manage resentment; pity comes instead. A man whose equally callous values were assembled from his own small choices, each unremarkable at the time: there I do resent him, and I believe I am right to. The end states are the same; only the route differs; and I cannot state what about the route matters.

8. I can resent an institution — the university did this to me — in a tone I cannot distinguish from resentment at a person. I half-think this is a confusion; it does not feel like one from the inside.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to have consequences: the case that motivates each clause, a candid statement of which of my verdicts it must overturn (I expect the dementia case and the propaganda case to be the expensive ones), and its predictions for two cases I have not given you. First: resentment toward someone who wronged me during a depression that dimmed his concern for others without impairing his control over his conduct. Second: whether a newcomer to a moral community, subject to its blame but not yet party to its norms, may properly blame its established members. Do not give me a survey of positions; give me the principle, and its costs.
