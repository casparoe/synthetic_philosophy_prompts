I'm writing an essay on Bernhard Schlink's The Reader and want to read it against Jaspers' postwar typology of guilt rather than the usual vague talk of inherited German shame. In The Question of German Guilt (1946), Jaspers distinguishes four kinds: criminal guilt, for concrete deeds, judged in court; political guilt, the liability every citizen shares for what one's state does, worked off in consequences rather than punishment; moral guilt, for what one is individually answerable for before one's own conscience and peers; and metaphysical guilt, the guilt of those who remained alive while others were murdered, which no human forum can judge. Where do the novel's central figures actually fall? Hanna is on trial for a criminal act -- the women left locked in a burning church during the death march -- yet the trial turns on her admitting to a report her illiteracy made it impossible for her to have written, and on Michael's silence about what he knows. Does Schlink deliberately muddy criminal with moral guilt there, and does that muddying work toward exculpating Hanna or toward showing how inadequate the legal category is to what she did? Michael's own silence at the trial looks like a clean case of moral guilt, but the novel insists his shame is larger than that one act: he belongs to the generation born during the war, condemned to judge the perpetrators while unable to free itself from loving them. Can someone who never stood by while the murders happened bear Jaspers' metaphysical guilt at all, or does the novel show the second generation suffering a guilt that has no slot in the typology -- and is that a genuine extension of collective guilt to later generations, or the vicarious, self-regarding guilt the novel has often been accused of aestheticizing? I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the typology seriously, says plainly where Jaspers' categories illuminate Hanna's and Michael's situations and where the novel resists them, and ends by saying what the novel adds to the question of whether a nation can be guilty across generations that Jaspers, writing for contemporaries in the rubble, could not have said himself.
