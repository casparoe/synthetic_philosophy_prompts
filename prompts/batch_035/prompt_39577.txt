The question, worth 25 points, read as follows:

"Keynes's A Treatise on Probability (1921) holds that some probabilities are not numerical and that some probabilities are not even comparable with one another. Answer the following. (i) Explain what this thesis amounts to on Keynes's logical interpretation of probability, and why it is a stronger claim than that we often cannot work out the numbers. (ii) Explain how the thesis differs from the claim that probability is subjective. (iii) What did Ramsey find objectionable in Keynes's account? (iv) How does this line of thought reappear in Keynes's economics of the 1930s, and what role does the 'weight' of an argument play there?"

Here is the student's answer, as it appears on the paper:

"Keynes's logical interpretation says that a probability is a relation between a proposition and the set of premises given as evidence. Because the relation is logical, the probability is fixed by the evidence: two people reasoning from the same premises must assign the same probability. Keynes is famous for denying that all probabilities are numbers. What he means is that in some cases the relation exists but is too complex for us to compute: the probability has a definite value, and we just cannot work it out. Even so, any two probabilities can always be ranked against each other — we can always say which is greater — even when we cannot give either of them a number.

Since the probability depends on what evidence you happen to have, and different agents have different evidence, Keynes's view is in the end a subjective theory of probability, and this is what Ramsey seized on. Ramsey's objection was that Keynes gives us no way of determining these probabilities in practice: there is no special faculty by which we perceive logical relations between a proposition and our evidence, and so the theory cannot explain the extent to which we do in fact agree about probabilities.

Keynes also introduced the weight of an argument, which is really the probability viewed from another side: the balance of the evidence in favour of the conclusion over the evidence against it. When the weight is high the probability is close to one; when it is low the probability is close to a half. This is what he is invoking in chapter 12 of the General Theory, where he says our knowledge of the long-term prospects of investment is very uncertain. By 'uncertain' he means what Knight meant by risk: the cases where we can form a frequency distribution from past data and calculate a probability, but the particular outcome is still not guaranteed. He repeated this in his 1937 restatement of the theory, where he says the orthodox theory assumes all probabilities can be measured, which he accepted as true for the special cases the classical economists had in mind."
