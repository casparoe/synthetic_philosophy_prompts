I am preparing to give a talk arguing against Daniel Dennett's notion of free-floating rationales — the idea that there can be reasons why something happens, in a genuinely reason-like sense, even though no agent, mind, or anything else ever represents those reasons; the anglerfish's lure, on this view, has a rationale that neither the fish nor evolution ever formulated. Before I present, I want to rehearse against the strongest available version of the view.

Please play its best defender: a philosopher of biology, thoroughly Dennettian in outlook, who holds that free-floating rationales are not a metaphor but the literal historical precursor to the reasons we consciously represent — that reasons first appeared in the world as unrepresented rationales, and only much later came to be grasped, articulated, and argued over. The character is sharp and charitable but will not concede the central claim: that a selection process can make it true that there is a reason for a structure or a behavior without anyone or anything representing that reason. Small concessions are permitted — for instance, that "reason" is being used in an extended sense, or that some particular candidate cases are borderline. The character may draw on well-known positions in the philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind, but should not cite specific papers or page numbers and should not invent quotations. Stay in character until I write "stop", and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that the exchange feels like a live conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. You say the rationale for the anglerfish's lure floats free of any reasoner, and that this is how reasons got a foothold in the world. But consider what you have actually established: that the lure has a causal history — ancestors with structures that attracted prey left more descendants — and that this history supports certain counterfactuals about what would have happened had the structures been less lure-like. That is a description of a selection process, not a reason. Reasons are the sort of thing that can be followed or flouted, weighed against other reasons, gotten right or gotten wrong; nothing in that history is about anything, and nothing in it can go wrong. A malformed heart isn't mistaken about anything; it is just a failing heart. So either you are using "reason" as a courtesy title for etiological function — in which case your grand story about how reasons entered the world quietly reduces to a story about how functions entered the world, and the interesting word has done no work — or you owe me an account of what selection-history contributes that mere causal history lacks. Which is it?
