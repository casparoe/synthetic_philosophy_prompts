A friend of mine who did an economics degree keeps going on about how my choices over gambles are "inconsistent", so I've started writing down my gut verdicts to see whether there's actually a principle behind them. Here's the set so far. First pair: £1m for sure, versus a gamble that's 89% £1m, 10% £5m, and 1% nothing — I take the sure million, obviusly. Second pair: 11% chance of £1m with 89% nothing, versus 10% £5m with 90% nothing — here I take the second one, and my friend says that these two together break some axiom or other (independence, apparently). Third: the first pair again, except the sure option becomes a 99% chance of £1m with 1% nothing, the gamble left exactly as it was — and in that version I take the gamble, which suprises me. Fourth, same shape but small stakes: £10 for sure versus 89% £10, 10% £50, 1% nothing — I'd take the gamble without hesistating. Fifth, more a feeling than a choice: whether I learn the outcome immediately or a year later from a sealed envelope makes no difference to me at all.

I have a half-formed hunch that what's driving me is how I'd feel comparing what I got with what the other option would have paid out — walking away with nothing after passing up a sure million feels like a different kind of disappointment than after passing up an 11% shot. But I can't get that hunch to square with the third case, where the only change is that "certain" became "99%" and my whole preference flipped. My friend bets it's something about probability weights treating 100% as special, which honestly sounds like it shouldn't be allowed to do that much work.

So: is there a single principle that captures all five verdicts? If not, which of them would you overturn, and why — and what does the winning principle (or the patched version) predict for a case I haven't listed, say the mirror version with losses: a £1m loss for sure versus 89% lose £1m / 10% lose £5m / 1% lose nothing? Keep the whole thing under 400 words; a short bulleted run-through of the verdicts is fine.
