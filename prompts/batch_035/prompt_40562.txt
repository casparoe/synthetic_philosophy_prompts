My two flatmates, Ravi and Elena, have been arguing on and off for a month about whether our neighbor Marta killed our tomato plants, and they've asked me to referee, which I am not up to. While the rest of the household was away for three weeks, Marta — who had explicitly promised to water the plants — never did, and they died. The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Ravi said Marta did not cause the plants' death, because causation connects events, and an omission is not an event: nothing she did killed them, because she did nothing. (2) Elena replied that had Marta watered them, they would have lived, and that this counterfactual dependence is precisely what being a cause consists in. (3) Ravi answered that the test proves too much: every person on Earth also failed to water the plants, so by Elena's criterion each of them caused the death too — several billion co-killers of a single tomato plant — which is absurd, so the criterion must be wrong. (4) Elena said the absurdity is real but has a fix: causation by omission tracks deviations from a background of what was supposed to happen, and only Marta was supposed to water; the rest of humanity was under no such obligation, so their omissions do not count as causes. (5) Ravi said this concedes his point in different words: if you cannot say who caused the death without first saying who was supposed to act, then cause-talk has stopped describing the world and started grading conduct — Elena is not doing metaphysics but distributing blame — and notice that her verdict would change if Marta had promised under duress, or if the promise had lapsed, even though nothing about the plants, the water, or the dry spell would be any different. (6) Elena's last word was that Ravi's own picture has an equally odd feature: on it, the plants died from dehydration, which just is the absence of water, so the one thing he is willing to call the cause is itself an absence — meaning his account commits him to absences doing causal work after all. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying for each of the six steps whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification; then explain what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write your answer in American English.
