Okay so I want a satirical piece for this one. Write mock minutes of a meeting of a governmental animal welfare advisory board that has been told its remit now extends "by degrees" down the sentience scale. Like, the legislature passed something vague saying welfare protections should track sentience "in all its gradations," and the poor committee has to figure out where the lines actually go.

Format it as real committee minutes: attendees, apologies, items numbered, motions proposed and seconded, action points at the end. Maybe 600ish words. Deadpan bureaucratic voice throughout — the humor should come from the committee's escalating desperation as they work their way down the scale, not from anyone cracking jokes.

The structure I want: they breeze through vertebrates in about two minutes (that part is basically settled and the minutes should show them being bored), then it gets progressively harder. Fish they handle with a precautionary principle vote. Then insects — here's where I want the real philosophical content to show. They argue about whether the line goes at insects, or between insect orders, or at octopuses being weirdly smart, and someone keeps proposing "collapses when prodded" as an operational criterion and someone else keeps pointing out a thermostat does that. That's the bit I actually care about: the running joke that every behavioral criterion they propose — nociception, trade-offs, learning from harm — gets met with "the thermostat does that" or "the plant does that," until someone finally says the quiet part out loud: that they can't find a behavioral marker that separates sensing from feeling, and the chair notes this is "the problem of other minds" and moves on.

Then the last items: plants (someone brings up signaling between trees and is asked to leave it out of the minutes), and the thermostat itself, which a junior member insists on putting under "any other business" because her office thermostat "seems to suffer." The vote on the thermostat should be the emotional climax — taken seriously, defeated on a technicality, and the member asks to have her dissent recorded, and the minutes note that the committee was unable to specify what the technicality was.

Two constraints. First, the satire has to be fair to the actual philosophical positions: the person arguing for graded moral status should get to make her case properly (unequal degrees of concern, the difference between a boundary and a threshold, why "some mattering but less" isn't the same as "none"), and the person resisting should also get real arguments (indeterminacy doesn't mean anything goes, the difference between uncertainty about a fact and absence of the fact). Don't let everyone in the room be an idiot — let them be competent people failing at a genuinely hard problem, which is the actual joke. The target is the forced sharpness: the philosophical view says moral status comes in degrees, but the law has to say "protected: yes or no," and the minutes should dramatize that mismatch without ever stating it.

Second, keep the thermostat stuff philosophically literate. The thermostat is the classic limit case of a system that detects and responds to a stimulus with no inner life, so use it that way — as the reductio that keeps eating their criteria. Don't do anything cute with it like having it actually be conscious.

End the minutes with the action points almost all reading "defer to next meeting," and a final line noting the meeting ran over and the janitor unplugged the thermostat in the meeting room, which no one present considered an ethical event, "the significance of which was noted and deferred."

Give the committee a plausible-sounding name and keep the whole thing in-universe — no real organizations, no real people, and don't cite any actual declarations or scientific papers. Everything the reader needs should be in the minutes themselves.
