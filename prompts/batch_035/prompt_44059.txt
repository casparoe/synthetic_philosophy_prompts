I've ended up holding a pretty strong view about natural kinds and I want to stress-test it before I go around repeating it at dinner parties. My view is basically that chemical elements are the paradigm of a real kind — that the periodic table carves nature at genuine joints — and that other candidate kinds (species, diseases, maybe some social kinds) are real exactly to the extent they approximate that element-like structure: a hidden underlying nature that explains a open-ended cluster of properties and settles what counts as the same kind. I got here from chemistry of all things, via a couple of details that stuck with me: chlorine's standard atomic weight isn't even a single number but an interval (something like 35.45 give or take a bit) because the isotope mix varies depending on whether your sample came from seawater or from rock, so the "properties of the kind" smear across samples; and didymium, which sat in the periodic table as a bona fide element for decades before being split into neodymium and praseodymium. Those cases feel to me like the joints showing through our mapping errors, not like evidence against joints existing at all.

So: what would actually count against my view? I want the observations, cases, or developments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be — including things that arguably have already happened. I can imagine pressure from a few directions and I genuinely don't know how much weight each deserves. From the isotope side: hydrogen and deuterium are the same element, but heavy water is a different substance as far as living things are concerned, and chemists sometimes treat isotopes as different chemical kinds outright, which makes me wonder wether "element" is a joint of the world or a joint of chemistry-at-lab-conditions. From the superheavy end: oganesson exists for milliseconds, only a handful of atoms have ever been made, and it has essentially no macroscopic chemistry — is a kind with almost no members and no visible properties still carving anything, or is its kindhood just inherited from the table's architecture? And a bigger one: if it turned out that under sufficiently extreme conditions all matter becomes an indistinguishable plasma, so that elementhood only makes a difference across the narrow band of conditions we happen to occupy, has the carving become ours rather than nature's? Which of these would a committed defender of the view concede as genuinely counting against it, and which would they wave off — and on what principled basis, not just motivated reasoning?

One more thing I'd like an honest answer on. My view has this reflex available to it: "elements are real kinds; when the mapping gets revised, as with didymium, that just shows the real joints were elsewhere all along." If every revision can be absorbed like that, is my view being qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that a fatal flaw, or is it just what it looks like for a metaphysical view to stay responsive to science? And since I'll be repeating this at dinner parties, please flag for me which parts of the surrounding territory are actually settled versus genuinely contested among people who know this stuff — I assume whether species are natural kinds is still fought over, and whether the Kripke/Putnam story about element terms is right is presumably not unanimous either, and I'd rather not walk away mistaking some contested take for the consensus.
