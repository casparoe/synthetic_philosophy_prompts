In most jurisdictions, a person may sign an advance directive while competent — instructing caregivers, say, to withhold antibiotics or artificial nutrition if she later develops advanced dementia — and the instruction remains legally binding even at a stage when she no longer understands what a directive is. Clinicians are told to enforce it against a patient who now seems content, enjoys her meals, and reaches for the spoon, on the theory that the earlier self had the authority to decide for this one. Yet the very same hospitals, deciding for a patient who was never competent, proceed by the patient's current best interests; they would dismiss as outrageous a competent person's attempt to bind her future self in other domains — a directive demanding that her sweets be taken away, or that a future romantic partner be kept from her bedside; and ethics committees in practice give the demented patient's current objections real weight whenever the directive is anything less than explicit. What conception of the self, or of the authority of the earlier self, does the practice of precedent autonomy actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: is it that the directive-writer and the demented patient are literally the same person, so that autonomy is simply being exercised over time; something weaker, like self-binding on analogy with contract or gift; or the claim that a life can be going badly from one's own earlier critical standpoint even when each of its moments is pleasant? Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with: does the boundary-drawing institutions actually do — demanding clear and convincing evidence, never withholding comfort care, letting current objections muddy things — track the reconstructed view, or is it quietly borrowing from the rival view that only the present patient's interests count? And if we adopted that rival view outright, what in current practice would have to give?
