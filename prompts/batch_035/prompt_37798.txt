Write an essay on whether verbal testimony (śabda) is an irreducible pramāṇa or merely a disguised form of inference (anumāna), and use the case of machine-generated text to help you reach a verdict.

The classical controversy: the Cārvākas rejected śabda outright as a source of knowledge, and the Buddhist epistemological tradition, admitting only perception and inference, treated our trust in a speaker's words as an inference — roughly, from this person's utterances having been reliable, or from the speaker's being an āpta, to the truth of what is said. The Naiyāyikas and Mīmāṃsakas insisted that śabda is sui generis: it yields knowledge of the speaker's meaning through the apprehension of the words themselves, not through a cognition of the speaker's trustworthiness that we first establish and then argue from. I want you to weigh this seriously on both sides. The reductionist has an embarrassment of resources: we do not, phenomenologically, run an inference every time we understand a sentence; but plenty of automatic inferences fail to announce themselves too. The anti-reductionist has the bootstrapping problem: on the Nyāya account the connection between words and their referents is learned from the speech of competent elders — but if that learning is itself testimony, the view looks circular, and if it is inference, the reductionist asks why the same courtesy cannot be extended to every later act of understanding. Consider the regress explicitly: what could ground the very first linguistic knowledge if testimony were always inference from previously observed reliability, and how does each tradition propose to stop the regress?

Then use a case the classical theorists never faced. You ask a large language model a question, it produces a fluent answer, and you believe it. The model has no āptatva in anything like the classical sense — no knowledge of what is true in the way an embodied knower has it, no benevolent intention to inform you, and certainly no concerned stake in your epistemic welfare; it was trained to imitate human text at scale, which is compatible with its saying things because they sound right rather than because they are. Yet the answer transmits real information you could not have gotten otherwise. So what is the pramāṇa at work? If you say śabda, you have testimony without a trustworthy speaker, which the classical account forbids; if you say anumāna, you have an inference whose premises (about the model's reliability, the training data's quality) most users neither possess nor consciously deploy, which looks like a promissory note rather than an actual inference; and if you say the user is simply careless, then it is worth asking how much of ordinary human testimony works the same way, with the āpta's credentials more assumed than known. Take the machine case as a probe: whichever theory of śabda — reducible or irreducible — better accommodates it, or better explains our justified discomfort with it, gains real evidential support.

Requirements: give both the classical and the machine case real argumentative weight; do not let the anachronistic example float free of the Sanskritic framework, but do not force the framework to pronounce on questions it never anticipated without saying that that is what you are doing. Reach a definite conclusion — either śabda is reducible to inference or it is not, and your verdict should be defended partly by what it lets you say about the language model — and justify it.
