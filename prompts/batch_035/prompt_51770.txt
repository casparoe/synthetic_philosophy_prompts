I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and my supervisor runs a weekly reading group that ends with a ritual: she gives a snap yes-or-no ruling on a single attribution of the form "Does this thing have the function of doing that?" She never explains her reasons, and we have started to suspect there is a system in it. Next week I'll be presenting, and I'd like to go in with predictions. Her rulings so far:

1. The vertebrate eye, forming images: yes.
2. Bone marrow, producing red blood cells: yes.
3. An implanted pacemaker, regulating the heartbeat: yes.
4. Male nipples in adult humans, any function at all: no.
5. The human chin, any function at all: no — she added, unprompted, that it is a byproduct of how the jaw grows.
6. The human appendix, digesting plant matter: no.
7. Lysozyme in human tears, breaking down bacterial cell walls: yes.

Next week's docket, on which I want your predictions:

A. A mutation that arose within the last year in a population of beetles — it has not yet spread or been weeded out by differential survival or reproduction — and that happens to make its carriers unpalatable to a predator introduced around the same time. Does the allele have the function of deterring the predator?

B. The vestigial pelvic bones of a baleen whale. Do they have the function of supporting walking? I've also asked her, as a follow-up, whether she would say they are malfunctioning or simply functionless, so predict that too.

C. The sounds produced by a beating heart, on which physicians rely when diagnosing disease through a stethoscope. Are those sounds a function of the heart, or a mere effect of it?

What I want from you, in American English:

- First, reconstruct the candidate rules that fit her track record so far: at a minimum the etiological or selected-effect family and the causal-role family, plus any further rule the record does not yet exclude (for instance, one keyed to present contribution to survival and reproduction). For each ruling above, note where all the surviving rules agree and where one of them would have to be stretched — her "no" on the appendix, for example, should be easy for some rules to absorb and awkward for others, and the pacemaker ruling is presumably doing some quiet work of its own.
- Then take A, B, and C in turn: say what each surviving rule predicts, give your best guess at her actual verdict, and justify the guess.
- Last, propose one further case she has never ruled on that would do the most to discriminate among the surviving rules — one that splits them where A, B, and C may not — and explain exactly why it splits them.

Please don't lean on any particular book or article, and don't give me a reading list or citations; I'm after the reasoning, not a bibliography.
