I'm a PhD student in affective neuroscience — my thesis is on anhedonia, specifically how flat versus steep reward signals affect learning and motivation, so I spend most of my days thinking about what reward gradients actually do — and I've developed a side interest in the philosophy of wellbeing. At a conference afterparty a couple of weeks ago the conversation drifted to David Pearce's Hedonistic Imperative, and I realised I only know it secondhand, through people at parties. So I went and read a longread in a pop-science magazine that summarised it for me, and I want to check how much of it to trust. Here's the relevant passage, as close as I can remember it:

"Pearce's argument is that suffering is a design flaw. Evolution wired negative states into sentient creatures because they were useful for propagating genes, not because they're good for anyone, and a system built by blind selection has no authority over how we should feel. So the moral thing to do is use biotechnology — gene editing now, more invasive interventions later — to abolish suffering across the whole biosphere, not just in humans. As a negative utilitarian, Pearce holds that pain is the only thing with disvalue, so his target isn't extreme suffering but all sub-optimal experience; the endpoint he envisions is constant, uniform bliss, essentially the whole biosphere wireheaded — pleasure fully decoupled from anything happening in the world, since any coupling to the world reintroduces the risk of the bad stuff. And because the case rests entirely on hedonistic utilitarianism, if you value anything else — truth, achievement, autonomy — you have no reason to sign on. Indeed, on Pearce's own premises a painless extinction of all life would rank above a future containing any suffering at all, which is why most philosophers treat the HI as a reductio of utilitarianism rather than a serious proposal."

Something about it felt off — I have a vague memory of Pearce insisting the project isn't about flat bliss, and the wireheading bit especially caught my attention because whether reward gradients need negative valence to carry information is a live question in my feild, and my hunch is they dont. But I don't trust a half-remembered party conversation over a published article, and pop-science pieces are usually right about the headline and wrong about the mechanism, or vice versa. So could you go through the passage claim by claim: which parts would Pearce and his sympathetic readers accept as basically right, which are simplifications a defender would grumble at but live with, and which are plain misrepresentations? I'd especially like the wireheading/uniform-bliss claim untangled (does he really want valence decoupled from the world, or does he want information-sensitive gradients preserved?), and your view on whether the negative utilitarianism attribution is doing the work the article thinks it is — including whether the painless-extinction inference is something Pearce is committed to or just an objection dressed up as his position. And at the end: once the corrections are in, does the article's closing move survive — that the HI is best treated as a reductio and can be safely ignored by anyone who isn't already a hedonistic utilitarian?
