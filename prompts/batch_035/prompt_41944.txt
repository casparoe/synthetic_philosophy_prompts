I've been keeping a ledger of my gut verdicts about when consent actually counts, and I'd like someone to find the principle behind them — or tell me honestly that there isn't one. The cases are mostly about deception, with a few about coercion and competence thrown in, because I suspect the three interact.

First, the verdicts. A partner removes a condom mid-act against an explicit agreement: the consent was invalid, obviously. A woman tells a partner she is on birth control when she is not, intending to conceive: also invalid — and notice that I hold this one with total confidence even though my friends treat it as a joke rather than an analog of the first case. A man impersonates his identical twin in a darkened bedroom: invalid, though the physical act experienced is molecule-for-molecule what was agreed with someone else. A surgeon lets a supervised trainee perform the delicate part of an operation while telling the patient afterward that he did it himself: invalid, I think, even though the surgical plan, the risks run, and the outcome were all as consented to. Now the ones I'm less proud of. Someone sleeps with a person after being told a flat lie that the person is unmarried, when they have a firm policy against sleeping with married people: my gut says invalid, but weakly. Someone sleeps with a person after being told a lie that the person is in love with them: I call this valid — a terrible thing to do, but the consent stands. Someone gets sex by lying about being a retired astronaut: valid, and barely even a hard case.

Then coercion and competence. A signature extracted at gunpoint: invalid. A signature extracted because it's the only grocery job within two hours' travel and the wage is exploitative: valid, though it sticks in my throat. A mildly sedated but fully oriented patient consents to an additional emergency procedure mid-surgery: valid. That same patient, before sedation, signs a blanket "do whatever the surgeon thinks best" form: valid, I want to say, even though it doesn't look informed in any meaningful sense — it looks like the opposite of informed.

So the puzzle I can't resolve: the marital-status lie and the love lie are both flat lies about facts that mattered to the decision, and I give them opposite verdicts. The trainee-surgeon case changes nothing about what happens to the patient's body, and I still call it invalid, while the astronaut lie changes the patient's — the partner's — understanding of the person entirely and I shrug. The blanket form is uninformed and valid; the contraception deception is arguably better informed than most sex and invalid. I've toyed with the familiar line that invalidates deception about the nature or character of the act itself but tolerates deception about the incidental features or the deceiver's qualities — but I can't get the trainee case to sit on either side of that line without squirming.

What I'd like: state one or a few candidate principles precisely enough to test, tell me which of my verdicts each one captures and which it has to overturn (I'd rather lose the love-lie verdict than the gunpoint one, and I'd rather lose the blanket-form verdict than either, if that helps you weight them), and then run the best candidate on cases I haven't listed — consent obtained after a fortune-teller's genuine-feeling prophecy that refusal would curse one's family; a subject consenting to a trial while misunderstanding what randomization means; a tipsy but competent-seeming bet at a card table; a duelist's acceptance delivered under social ruin for refusing. Where a verdict of mine looks like prejudice rather than principle — the contraception case being the obvious suspect — say so plainly. Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the single point in your account you are least sure of, the one that would most change your answer if it went the other way.
