novelist here, and I've been mulling Nietzsche's three uses of history for life -- the monumental, the antiquarian, the critical -- by testing them against my own gut reactions, because I use all three in my work and I can't tell if my instincts add up to anything. Here are my verdicts. When I write about WWI nurses and let the courage of it swell into something half-myth, that feels fine, even necessary for the book. When I spend three months on 1890s Vienna street slang that changes almost nothing in the manuscript, that feels fine too -- a guilty pleasure, but fine. When I write against my hometown's founding story, exposing what the town founders actually did, that feels fine and even owed. But a nationalist epic that monumentalizes great men of the nation: not fine, it makes me queasy. Antiquarian research that curdles into hoarding -- a writer who knows everything about their period and can no longer write a sentence: not fine. And critical history that demolishes everything until the writer can't love any tradition enough to write from inside it: also not fine, and that one worries me most. My tentative principle is that a use of history is good exactly insofar as it feeds new work -- but that principle condemns my Vienna slang rabbit hole, which was pointless for the book, and I refuse to give that up. So: is there a principle that saves all six verdicts? If not, which one should go, and what does the repaired principle say about a case I haven't listed -- say, a novelist who knowingly falsifies a historical event in service of a truth she thinks matters more? Please say how confident you are in the principle you land on and point to the single part of it you'd be least sure of.
