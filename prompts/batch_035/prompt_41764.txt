I'm a product policy analyst at a mid-sized technology company, and I need help pinning down a slogan before I use it in a memo. Last week, in a meeting about our recommender system, an engineer defended the algorithm by insisting that it is politically neutral — it just optimizes for engagement, and whatever politics people find in its outputs is politics they brought with them. A colleague shot back with the title of Langdon Winner's 1980 essay, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", declaring that artifacts have politics, and the room promptly split between people who thought that settled the matter and people who thought it was a rhetorical flourish.

Before I take a side, I want to know what the claim "artifacts have politics" actually commits you to. Please lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise theses that phrase could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading, say what it asserts, whether it is plausible, and what evidence about our recommender system would show that the system has politics in that sense. At minimum, I want you to distinguish the reading on which an artifact merely bears the fingerprints of the people who built it; the reading on which the design or arrangement of a technical system can settle political questions for a community without anyone intending it to; and the strongest reading, on which some technologies are only workable at all under certain forms of social or political organization, so that adopting the technology is already, tacitly, adopting the social order.

Then tell me two further things. First, which of those readings, if true, would actually answer the engineer's neutrality claim — and is "it just optimizes engagement" a rebuttal to any of them, or does it merely relocate the question to who chose engagement as the objective and what a system built to maximize it will tend to amplify? Second, flag which readings amount to technological determinism, in which the artifact imposes a social order on its own, and which instead keep the politics flowing from human choices while denying that those choices stop at the boundary of the technical. Finally, note plainly where you think my colleague's retort was doing real argumentative work and where it was trading on the ambiguity between these theses.
