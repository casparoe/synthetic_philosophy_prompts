My friends and I have been going around about who gets to blame whom, and I've realized my gut verdicts on cases don't obviously add up to anything, so I want to see if there's a principle hiding in them or if I just have to bite some bullets. The cases, with my honest reactions: my friend who lies to his girlfriend constantly has zero business calling out another friend for lying to his girlfriend — that one's easy, textbook hypocrisy. But a guy I know who lied habitually years ago, owned it, completely changed, and now talks openly about it? I think he actually does have standing to blame liars, maybe even more standing than someone who's never struggled with it. Then there's someone who is a terrible person on a totally different axis — say a guy who's cruel but scrupulously honest — blaming someone for lying. My gut says he has standing, that his cruelty doesn't disqualify him, which is weird because his hands are plenty unclean, just not for this. And then a casual acquaintance who's morally fine, blameless even, calling out someone they barely know for cheating: I feel like saying "this isn't really your business," even though the meddler is less of a hypocrite than anyone else on my list. So it can't just be about moral purity.

There are two more that I keep going back and forth on, which is why I want a principle rather than vibes. One: a woman who was tempted to cheat on her husband, came very close, but stopped herself — does she have standing to blame a friend who actually went through with it? Part of me says of course, she resisted precisely because she takes the thing seriously. Another part says she's one weak moment away from being the person she's blaming, and if my reformed liar has standing, she should too, but it feels different and I can't say why. Two: someone whose own past cheating came out years later, after they'd already blamed their brother for cheating in the interim — I want to say their past blame was fine at the time, they weren't a hypocrite then, but boy does it look bad now. Which makes me wonder if standing is fixed at the moment of blaming or gets retroactively poisoned.

So: what's the actual principle, or set of principles, behind these verdicts? I've half-notice there seem to be two different things going on — something about being guilty of the same thing, and something separate about whose business it is — but I can't tell if they're really two conditions or one, and my verdicts pull in opposite directions (the blameless meddler fails one but passes the other; the honest-but-cruel guy seems to pass both or neither depending on how you cut it). Please give me a principle stated precisely enough that it settles cases, tell me which of my verdicts it can't save and which of those I should actually give up, and then run it on one more case I haven't mentioned: a whole group of people who all quietly cheated on the same exam years ago, where one of them publicly blames another one for cheating on a different exam now. Does having the group's shared dirty secret change anything compared to one individual with the same past? I genuinely don't know what I think and want to know what the principle says before I decide whether I believe it.
