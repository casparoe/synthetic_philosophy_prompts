Been reading alignment-adjacent stuff for years now (lesswrong, then the Alignment Forum once it split off, the whole ecosystem) and there's something about how frontier labs talk about racing that I still can't fully articulate, so I want someone to actually untangle it for me. The practice in question, concretely: labs publish responsible scaling policies where they commit to not training for or deploying the next capability tier until a set of safety evals come back clean-ish, and they fund safety teams and do dangerous capability evaluations and so on. At the same time, whenever anyone asks why they're still pushing capabilities as fast as they can, the answer is always some version of the competitiveness argument: if we slow down unilaterally, someone less careful gets there first, and the world is strictly worse off, so the best we can do is keep the frontier in the hands of people taking safety seriously. So the practice is "race, but with brakes we promise to use."

What I want is the implicit theory underneath that. It seems to presuppose some claim about how capability progress and safety progress relate — that the process that produces more capable models is also what generates the alignment knowledge, so the alignment tax is small, front-loaded or shrinking, and mostly paid by whoever's behind the frontier. If capability and safety knowledge were strongly separable, and safety mostly came from deliberately slowed, careful, smaller-scale work, wouldn't the "we must stay in front" argument kind of invert — you'd want the reckless ones to hit the wall slowly while the careful one deliberately stays a step back? So: is the practice presupposing that safety is a complement to capability (scales with it), or just that alignment is a tax you can only afford to pay if you're competitive? Which rival views of the capability-safety relationship would license the exact same practice, and which would rule it out? And is the practice underdetermined enough that a total doomer about the coupling could still endorse it, or does racing only make sense within a fairly narrow band of beliefs about that coupling?

There's also a second layer I'm suspicious about. The eval-threshold side of the practice — "we don't deploy past what we've demonstrated safe" — treats safety as something you establish by demonstration, absence of demonstrated danger, whereas the racing side treats the danger as comparative, "safer than the counterfactual lab that replaces us." Those feel like two different views of when risky action is permissible, and I'm not sure they're consistent. And the counterfactual itself: "if not us, then a worse actor" presupposes something about how individual lab decisions aggregate into outcomes — a view where each actor's choice is only evaluable relative to what the others do — which sits weirdly next to the same labs insisting that their individual choices (releasing weights, say) are morally loaded in themselves, full stop. Reconstruct the commitments for me, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined vs actually stuck with a substantive premise, and say what would have to be true for the whole package to be coherent.
