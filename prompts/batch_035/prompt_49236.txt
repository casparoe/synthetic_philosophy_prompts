I teach ethics at a small liberal arts college, and this semester I got roped into chairing our academic integrity panel — a weekly gig where we hand out sanctions for cheating and plagiarism cases. Here's my problem: the framework everyone on the panel uses instinctively is "could the student have done otherwise?" We let off the kid whose laptop auto-synced a corrupted file mid-exam, and we hammered the kid who googled answers on a closed-book final. But I've been teaching Frankfurt cases for years, and I know the principle of alternative possibilities doesn't actually work like that — the whole point of the counterfactual intervener stories is that you can be responsible for something even when you couldn't have avoided doing it, because the action flowed from you rather than from the intervener. So our implicit test is both too strict and too loose at once. Too strict, because sometimes a student couldn't have done otherwise and should still be held responsible. Too loose, because sometimes a student technically had alternatives and we still shouldn't blame her — the coerced kid whose "options" were cheating or losing her scholarship isn't off the hook just because the door was unlocked.

I want to fix this before the fall semester starts. What I'm asking for is a decision procedure the panel can actually run in the roughly 20 minutes we get per case, with no philosophers on it besides me and a dean who reads all our write-ups, so it has to be in plain language. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "not responsible," and — this is the part I care most about — where it has to stop and hand the case to human judgment because the question has stopped being factual and become one of values. It should preserve whatever is genuinely right in Frankfurt's insight (roughly: responsibility tracks whether the misconduct was sourced in the student, not whether alternatives existed) while keeping the distinctions that do real work for a disciplinary body: actual coercion vs. Frankfurt-style inability to do otherwise, genuine impossibility vs. mere difficulty, and the cases where we'd never have caught the innocent version anyway.

Then run it on three real-ish cases from last year, since a procedure that hasn't been run is just an essay. (1) A student who pulled answers from a leaked key during a take-home exam, where IT later discovered the exam portal had been compromised in a way that would have substituted the leaked answers into whatever he submitted — he cheated, but he also couldn't not have cheated. (2) A student who handed her problem set to a friend to copy after the friend threatened to report an earlier (lesser) incident of collaboration between them — blackmail-adjacent, but she did hand it over voluntarily in the end. (3) A student with an undiagnosed processing disorder who copied long passages verbatim from a study website because she genuinely believed that's what studying was — the panel went easy on her and I've never been sure that was right. Give me the procedure's verdicts on all three even where I won't like the answer, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and please don't restate my question back at me.
