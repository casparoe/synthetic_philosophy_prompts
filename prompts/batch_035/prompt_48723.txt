I'm a high school junior taking AP Physics, and last week our teacher was comparing two ways of explaining the same set of observations — one involving fewer moving parts than the other — and justified preferring the first by saying that the simplest explanation is usually the correct one. A classmate immediately objected that "God did it" is about as simple as an explanation can get, so if the razor really favored simple explanations, it would favor that one; the teacher replied, somewhat vaguely, that the razor is not about explanations that are easy to say but about ones that don't introduce unnecessary machinery, and then moved on. Nobody in the room found this fully satisfying, including me.

Since then I've noticed the same one-liner being used to mean quite different things. In a statistics video I watched, it apparently meant that overly complicated models fit noise rather than signal. In an online argument I stumbled into, someone claimed it meant that nature itself is simple, so simple theories are more likely to be true, and someone else countered that this is just an article of faith about the universe. And on a computer science forum, people seemed to be measuring simplicity by how short a computer program would have to be to fully generate the theory's predictions — under which "God did it" apparently comes out not simple at all, though I don't quite see why.

I'd like to be able to walk into class with something more rigorous than my teacher's aside. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that the slogan could actually be expressing — for instance, claims about what "simple" means (fewer entities, fewer adjustable parameters, shorter description length, easier for humans to grasp), about whether simplicity is a feature of the world or of our limitations, and about why simplicity should count in favor of truth at all — and for each reading, say whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false? Then please tell me which of those readings, if any, would actually have supported my teacher's preference between the two explanations, and which readings survive my classmate's "God did it" objection rather than collapsing under it. A structured list would be ideal, since I want to bring it to class.
