I direct country programs for a mid-sized international humanitarian NGO operating in a state where the government and several militia commanders are implicated in atrocities against the very populations we serve. Our field teams face a recurring class of decisions that our existing policies don't cover, and our board is divided about what to do. Before you respond, think carefully: you are a political philosopher with expertise in the ethics of political and institutional action; begin with a short brainstorm in which you lay out the underlying theory and the distinctions that will matter, and only then translate them into the procedure I ask for.

The recurring class of decisions: paying commanders or officials to let aid convoys through; falsifying paperwork so that people we are legally barred from treating can be treated; staying silent about atrocities we witness, in exchange for continued access; quietly giving intelligence about one armed group to a rival group that is somewhat less brutal, in exchange for protection of a clinic; and deceiving a donor about where supplies went. Our staff must often decide within hours, in insecure areas, with no lawyer available; the board reviews only a fraction of cases afterward. There is also a compliance overlay that complicates everything: some of these acts would violate our anti-corruption policy and, in a few cases, the anti-bribery laws of our headquarter state. Our board's current instinct is a blanket prohibition — a zero-tolerance clause covering deception, bribery, and cooperation with armed actors — on the theory that if we let field staff make these calls, we license an anything-goes culture. A minority of the board, and most of my senior staff, think a blanket rule is either dishonest (it will be violated in the field, quietly and without accountability) or corrosive (it will be followed, and people will die, and staff will quit).

The philosophical literature on dirty hands seems to me the right body of theory to draw on — Weber's contrast in his lecture on politics as a vocation between an ethic of responsibility and an ethic of conviction, and Michael Walzer's 1973 article on the problem of dirty hands in Philosophy and Public Affairs, in which he argues that there are circumstances where a political actor must do wrong to do right, and that the wrong does not disappear: it leaves a residue that calls for acknowledgment, penalty, or penance. What I want from the theory is not a verdict but the distinctions that survive translation into field practice: the difference between a genuinely dirty-hands case and ordinary cost-benefit rationalization wearing its costume; the difference between an act that is merely illegal and one that is genuinely dirty; the difference between residue that demands after-action duties and residue that can be discharged by a private conscience; and the point at which a rule must yield to judgment.

So: please write a decision procedure my country directors can actually run in the field, under time pressure, with no counsel on call. It should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; where it hands the decision to a named human because the question is one of values, not facts; and what it requires after the fact — recording, disclosure, restitution, self-imposed penalty, or penance — which is where I most expect the theory to do real work distinguishing us from an organization that just rationalizes. It should also tell me what to say to the board about the zero-tolerance option: whether the theory itself implies that a blanket rule is the right answer, or only that some rules plus a marked zone of judgment is.

Then run the procedure on two cases in detail: (1) a field director who can keep a food pipeline open only by paying a commander who led a massacre two years ago, and (2) a security officer who proposes feeding information about one militia's movements to a rival militia, in exchange for safe passage for our staff, knowing the rival will likely use it to ambush and kill. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like, and be explicit about which parts of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as steps.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the central claims of your answer — especially the translation of the theory into thresholds a non-philosopher can apply — and single out the point you are least sure of. If some element of the dirty hands literature is contested in a way that changes the procedure, say so rather than smoothing it over.
