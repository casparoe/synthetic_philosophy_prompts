I'm a second-year MPP student and I've gotten obsessed with a pattern in how goverments spend on life-saving that nobody in my program can explain to my satisfaction. When the lives are identified — the twelve boys in that Thai cave in 2018, trapped miners, a kid falls down a well — agencies will spend basically without limit on the rescue, and nobody blinks. But when the lives are statistical, the same governments do cost-per-life-saved math with a cutoff, and routinely decline safety measures that would save far more people per dollar than the rescue cost. So a system that happily spends millions to save a few identified people will refuse to spend less to save more unidentified ones. (And no, I don't think it's just the transplant thing — I understand why we don't take one healthy patient's organs for five sick ones; the interesting case here is spending, not harming.)

My political theory professor said offhand that this pattern looks "structurally like Scanlon's contractualism" — his line that in a choice between preventing something very bad happening to one person and producing smaller benefits for many, you can't just add the smaller benefits up and let them outweigh the big loss, because each person's claim has to be answerable to that person individually. That got me excited but also suspicious. It sounds too good: the practice gets dressed up as a principled refusal to aggregate, when it might just be identifiable-victim psychology — we care when there's a face on the news, and the budget follows the cameras. So here's my actual question: what theory of fairness or value does this identified-vs-statistical spending pattern presuppose? Does it really need something like contractualism's non-aggregative view of claims, or is that reading one of several the practice is compatible with, most of them less flattering?

Please don't hand me a menu and let me pick — take a position and defend it. Tell me which theory you actually think the practice is committed to, whether the commitment is consistent across both halves of it (unlimited rescue spending on one side, threshold-based safety spending on the other — do those presuppose the same view, or is there a real tension there?), and what a policy regime would have to change if we rebuilt it on the rival theory, whichever you think that is. If the contractualist story is wrong, say so plainly and give me the alternative.
