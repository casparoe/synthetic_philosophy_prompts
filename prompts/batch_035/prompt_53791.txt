I argue about ethics online way too much and I keep running into the same argument and I want to know if the specific move in it has a name. The setup: someone says utilitarianism is creepy because it just adds up suffering across different people and lets one person's huge loss be outweighed by lots of small gains elsewhere, as if the boundary between persons doesn't matter. Then the utilitarian fires back with an analogy: look, you already accept trade-offs between experiences that are separated in time within one life. You'd take a headache today to avoid three headaches next month, nobody thinks that's monstrous, so what exactly is the difference between trading off across your-times and trading off across people? The pain in ten years isn't literally present now either, and you still think it counts for you. Sometimes it gets put more ambitiously, that the difference between me-now and me-in-ten-years is ultimately no deeper than the difference between me-now and you-now, so treating the second boundary as somehow more load-bearing than the first is just bias. Then the anti-utilitarian side starts going on about how persons are separate and pain doesn't cancel across that boundary, and it just loops forever.

What I'm after is a name for the analogy-move itself, not the general debate. I know the reply side has a label, the separateness of persons, that gets thrown around constantly. And I've gathered that Sidgwick maybe said something in this ballpark a long time ago, and I think Parfit's stuff about personal identity being less deep than we assume gets pulled in as backup for the analogy. But is there an established term for the move of arguing that interpersonal aggregation is no different in kind from intrapersonal aggregation over time, and a standard account of where it succeeds or fails? If it doesn't have a name of its own, coin one for me, with a definition and boundary cases — like, does it cover only the positive claim that the two kinds of trade-off are symmetrical, or also the mirror-image move where critics of utilitarianism lean extra hard on how unified a person is across time to make the cross-person contrast look sharper? And where does it leave someone who accepts the Parfit-style view about identity but still doesn't buy the analogy — are they allowed to do that, or did they give up the ground they need? I'm not looking for just a term and a citation, I want to know what exactly the claim commits you to, what it doesnt, and how it hooks into the separateness stuff that always comes up in reply.
