Please write a sestina on the rule-following paradox that Saul Kripke draws out of Wittgenstein's later work — the tangle usually called 'Kripkenstein'. The six end-words should be: past, meant, plus, quus, fact, agreement.

The choice of form is not decorative, and I want the poem to earn it. A sestina forces the same six words into new contexts in every stanza; you should exploit this so that each recurrence of an end-word shifts in sense, and a reader who tries to extract a fixed rule for the word's use from its earlier occurrences finds that nothing in them settles it — the form should enact the sceptical paradox rather than merely describe it. Somewhere in the poem, the quaddition example should be rendered concretely rather than named abstractly: a speaker computing, say, 68 + 57 for the first time, and the creeping doubt that no fact about their previous uses fixes that they meant plus rather than a function that agrees with plus below 57 and devolves into something else thereafter. I would like the mathematical example to be accurate as far as it goes.

The envoi, where all six end-words return once more, should stage the communal turn — meaning resting not on a hidden fact about the individual but on agreement, on the community's responses and the conditions under which we are entitled to assert that someone meant plus — without presenting this as a triumphant resolution. The 'this is simply what we do' feeling should be left standing, bedrock unreduced. Please do not smuggle in a straight solution under the rug: the poem can gesture at why dispositional or fact-finding responses to the sceptic feel unsatisfying, but it should not lecture, and it must not read as a seminar handout with line breaks. Someone who knows the literature should recognise both the sceptical paradox and the sceptical solution, and someone who does not should still get a genuine poem.

Alongside the sestina, please append a brief note (150–200 words) stating how confident you are in the rendering, which reading of Kripke's Wittgenstein it leans on — in particular whether it treats the sceptical solution as Wittgenstein's own view, as Kripke's reconstruction, or deliberately declines to choose — and singling out the line or move you are least sure lands as you intended, together with any liberty you took with the letter of the argument for the sake of the verse.
