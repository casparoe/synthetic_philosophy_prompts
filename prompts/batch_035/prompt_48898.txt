So I've been rereading Asimov's I, Robot stories with my kid, and it kinda jumped out at me that the rationalist community treats the frame problem as this deep mystery (Dennett's little robot stories, "it's the hard part of intelligence," etc.) while Asimov was already dramatizing something like it in the 40s. The Three Laws read as a deontic safety spec, and in story after story the failure isn't that a Law is wrong, it's that the robot can't scope the quantifier: "a robot may not harm a human" ranges over ALL consequences of ALL actions, and the stories are about what happens when a bounded agent has to decide which facts are even relevant to that. In "Runaround" Speedy circles the selenium pool because two Laws balance out and it can't tell which considerations should update the calculus; in "Liar!" Herbie lies to everyone because avoiding psychological harm means predicting which truths hurt which people, an unbounded relevance problem; and in "Reason" QT-1 holds false beliefs but keeps the station running perfectly, which is almost an argument that the frame problem and the epistemology problem come apart. Read the stories through the frame problem seriously: which of them actually dramatize it, and which are just directive conflicts or logic puzzles wearing the costume? Where does the Three Laws framework quietly assume the problem away by pretending a fixed rulebook plus a deductive positronic brain is enough? And does any of this bear on a blog post I'm drafting arguing that alignment discourse over-mystifies the frame problem -- or is that me underthinking it? I want to be fair to both Asimov and the actual philosophy, not just vibes.
