Here's something that's lived rent free in my head since I was a teenager, and I'd like someone to actually argue it out with me properly.

Back in the 80s Scientific American ran a thing called the Luring Lottery. Anyone could enter by postcard, and the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, paid to one randomly drawn entry. The kicker was you could submit as many entries as you wanted, you just wrote the number on your postcard. So entering a million times makes you way more likely to win, except now the prize is a dollar, and everyone who figured that out flooded the magazine with postcards listing absurdly huge numbers (several people wrote a googolplex), the prize became effectively zero, and they couldn't even determine a winner. The whole point of the column was that if everyone had just sent in a single entry, one lucky reader would've gotten the full million. Hofstadter called the idea behind that superrationality. He also had a toy version he called the Platonia dilemma: a trillionaire gathers 20 people and says if exactly one of you wires me by noon tomorrow, that person gets a billion dollars, but if more than one does, or nobody does, everyone gets nothing. Apparently the superrational answer there is to send the telegram with probability 1/20.

I've never taken a philosophy or game theory class, so maybe I'm missing something basic, but after years of turning this over I've come to think Hofstadter was actually onto something and the people who wave it off as magical thinking are being too quick. My own version goes like this. No, my choice can't cause anyone else's choice. But you also can't treat your own choice as evidence-free when you know you reached it by reasoning, and other people are running similar-enough reasoning on the same problem. When I decide to send in one postcard, that doesn't cause anyone else to, but it IS a sample of what people-who-think-like-me do. If the lottery were only played by my exact clones, my choice would perfectly predict the field and cooperation is obviously right. Real people aren't clones, so my choice is a noisy predictor, and it seems like the sensible move is somewhere between totally ignore the similarity (what standard game theory says) and pretend we're clones (what superrationality says), scaled by how much overlap there actually is.

So what I want is the strongest case you can make that superrational cooperation is genuinely rational and not just wishful thinking or a nice social convention. Please take on the objection that it only works between exact mental clones, and tell me whether my noisy-predictor amendment actually fixes that problem or just smuggles it back in. Also, if you think the whole thing breaks somewhere, say where — for instance, does it matter that the people in the lottery never met and had no way of knowing how similar their reasoning was, or that in the Platonia case you'd have to be pretty confident 19 other specific strangers are on board for abstaining to be anything but throwing your billion away?
