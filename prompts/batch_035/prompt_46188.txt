I spent the better part of four decades in physiology and clinical research before retiring, and I have lately been reading philosophy of medicine in earnest. Before I go further, I want the specification I never thought to demand while I was practicing: what must an account of health and disease actually deliver to count as adequate? Write the desiderata. I am less interested in a tour of naturalism and normativism than in the requirements list itself: the cases a theory of disease must classify correctly (or explain why classification fails), the distinctions it must respect, the biological and institutional facts it cannot contradict, with the case behind each. To keep the list from becoming anodyne, please at least weigh the following, drawn from my working life:

- The reference-class problem. Normal values are defined relative to a population — age, sex, sometimes ancestry — and the choice of population can move the same measurement across the boundary between health and disease. Any adequate account must say what fixes the reference class, and it must do so without collapsing into the claim that disease is mere deviation from a local average, since we rightly refuse to call the tallest villager diseased.

- Pre-disease and risk states. Osteopenia, pre-hypertension, asymptomatic bacteriuria in elderly women. These categories were partly manufactured by panels and partly by genuine longitudinal evidence, and they expand when definitions are relaxed. An account must either mark them off from disease proper in a principled way or bite the bullet and say why most of what internal medicine treats is not, strictly, disease — and it should say which of those two it is doing.

- Evolutionary function and its limits. Accounts in the naturalist tradition lean on species-typical selected function, but selection built trade-offs, not optima: the recurrent laryngeal nerve's detour, the vertebrate eye's blind spot, senescence itself. If "function as designed by selection" is the criterion, the healthy body is a museum of design failures, and an account has to say why the design flaws of the species are not thereby diseases, while the design flaws of the individual are.

- Contested conditions. Homosexuality's removal from diagnostic manuals, the Deaf community's insistence that deafness is a culture rather than a pathology, grief as a target for treatment. Whatever the right verdicts, these cases were and remain hotly contested, and mere statistical deviation does not explain the heat. An account of disease owes an explanation of why the boundary in these cases is contested at all, not just a ruling.

- The insurance and gatekeeping function. Whatever else it is, the concept of disease decides what medicine is obligated to treat and what insurers must pay for. Anchor the adequacy conditions to that purpose if you like — a definition good enough to draw the boundary of clinical obligation — but say explicitly whether you think that purpose distorts the resulting list.

Two further demands. First, commit. Tell me which of the desiderata are genuine and which are the prejudice of one camp, rather than laying out the options for me to choose among. Second, tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I confess I suspect that value-neutrality (the demand that disease judgments be scientific rather than moral) cannot be satisfied alongside the demand to explain the contested cases, and I want that suspicion confirmed or dismantled with reasons, not diplomacy. Finally, name the single requirement on your list that excludes the most existing theories, and defend that ranking. If you think any of my five candidate requirements is confused or misbegotten, say so directly and say why; I would rather have a corrected list than a deferential one.
