Quick bit of background so this makes sense: I'm a senior structural engineer at a mid-size consultancy, and since a restructure last month I'm now the final sign-off on safety assessments before they go to clients. Not everyone in my position — I'm one of three checkers and the other two have been at it longer than me. Fairly often (maybe twice a week) one of them reviews my work and disagrees, or I review theirs and then they push back, and I have to decide whether to revise my judgment or dig in before a call with the client. Stakes are real but not always huge — sometimes it's whether a safety factor needs revisiting, sometimes it's whether a crack report is worth flagging at all.

Here's my actual problem. Twice now I've caught myself in a state I can only describe as: I still believe my original judgment was right, but I also believe that, given that two more-experienced people disagree with me, I shouldn't be confident in it. And I have no idea what I'm supposed to DO with that. If I act on my original judgment I'm acting on a belief I don't think I'm entitled to hold that confidently; if I defer, I'm signing off on something my own reading of the evidence says is wrong. A friend who did philosophy told me this is apparently a whole literature under the name epistemic akrasia (believing something while believing you shouldn't believe it, or 'stepping on your own evidence'), and that some philosophers think akrasia is always irrational, others think a fully rational person can be stuck with it. But I can't run a philosophy seminar in the ten minutes between the disagreement landing and the client call.

So what I want is a procedure. Something like a checklist I can actually work through when a disagreement comes in: what counts as evidence about my own reliability vs. evidence about the actual engineering question, how to weigh the two against each other, and crucially what the outputs are — do I defer, do I hold my position and document why, do I escalate to a third checker? I want the procedure to be honest about the points where it can't decide for me and just hands the judgement back. And I want it to say something about the asymmetry: it's easier to defer when I'm wrong than to explain to a client why I let a junior's assessment through when I thought it was wrong, and I suspect that skews me.

Then please run it on three real-ish cases from my last month: (1) my opposite number, who has ten years on me, disagrees about whether a fatigue assessment needs redoing, and my case for my position is solid but built on one assumption he thinks is unconservative; (2) I find out from our QA records that on the specific class of checks I'm reviewing, my past calls have been wrong about a third of the time — more than I'd have guessed — and I'm now mid-review of two assessments of exactly that class; (3) a disagreement where the other checker has a commercial incentive to clear things fast (he's on the bid team for the follow-on contract), so my "evidence about his judgment" is muddied by an obvious conflict of interest. Case 2 especially, because I don't think flatly revising my confidence downward actually tells me what to write in the review comment, which is the thing I have to produce.

If it helps, I don't need the procedure to resolve whether akrasia is 'really' irrational — I need it to work either way on a Tuesday afternoon with a deadline. But if some bit of the theory (like the difference between evidence that attacks the engineering claim itself vs. evidence that just makes me doubt my reading of it) makes the procedure better, please build it in. Also happy to hear where the procedure will definately misfire — I'd rather know its failure modes than pretend it's airtight.
