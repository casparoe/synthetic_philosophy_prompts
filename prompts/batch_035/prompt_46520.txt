A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about luck egalitarianism, and I want to see the position I lost to argued properly rather than as I presumably caricatured it. We both accepted the familiar core of the view: inequalities traceable to brute luck call for compensation, while inequalities traceable to the informed choices of the people who make them do not. The dispute was over where the line between the two kinds of luck actually falls.

Her position was this: a choice counts as option luck, and so generates no claim to compensation, only to the extent that it expresses the chooser's own settled, reflectively endorsed values. Whatever in an outcome is attributable to the gap between what a person chose and what she would have chosen had she been acting from the values she actually endorses is not option luck at all but brute luck of a distinctive kind — bad luck in one's motivational machinery — and is therefore compensable. On her view, the gambler who hates that he gambles and would not endorse his own conduct on reflection is owed something, while the gambler who stands behind his losses wholeheartedly is owed nothing at all, whatever their respective bank balances.

Please argue for her side. I want the argument to do two things in particular. First, it should derive the endorsement-gap principle from premises luck egalitarians already accept, rather than smuggling in an alien theory of autonomy or authenticity; if it helps, the natural starting points are that option luck is only genuinely option when the outcome flows from the agent, and that what flows from the agent is a matter of what she stands behind rather than what merely happens in her. Second, it must answer the objection I pressed hardest and never got a satisfying reply to: that the principle rewards regret and subsidizes second thoughts, so that two people with identical losses, identical information, and identical opportunities end up with different entitlements depending only on how they feel about themselves — which looks less like egalitarianism than like a policy of consoling the squeamish while letting the unrepentant starve.
