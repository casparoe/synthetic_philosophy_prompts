For most of my working life I was a physical chemist, and Bayesian conditioning was simply part of the craft: a prior from the calibration data, a likelihood from the spectrum, and a posterior you were entitled to report. Nothing in that practice ever required conditioning on evidence that was itself a conditional statement — a spectrum is just a spectrum — so I was unprepared for a puzzle I have met in the philosophy literature, van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem. A soldier is dropped by parachute into territory split between enemy Red forces and friendly Blue forces, and each side's territory is further divided into a Second Company sector and a Headquarters sector; she initially assigns equal probability to the four resulting quadrants. Her radio then delivers one message: if she is in Blue territory, the odds are three to one that she is in the Headquarters sector rather than the Second Company sector. Nothing is said about Red territory. Yet when she conditions on the message in the standard way, her credence that she is in Red territory comes out below one-half — she appears to have learned about a region the message never mentioned. As I understand the moral, this refutes Bayesian conditioning as a general rule of rational updating: it demonstrably yields the wrong posterior for at least one kind of evidence, namely conditional testimony. So my question is a practical one: which replacement update rule should a rational agent adopt for conditional evidence — I gather imaging and minimizing relative entropy are the leading candidates — and would it be defensible to keep ordinary conditionalization for the unconditional evidence I handled all my career while switching to the replacement rule whenever a conditional shows up, as a kind of hybrid procedure? The hybrid strikes me as theoretically unstable, and I would like to know how to patch it up cleanly.
