I write about science and ideas for a national daily, and I have been invited onto the external advisory panel of our statistics office's new National Wellbeing Programme. The panel must decide at Thursday's meeting what the headline indicator will be anchored on, because the treasury wants a single number it can monetise and set against GDP in cost-benefit appraisals of policy. Three options are on the table. The first derives welfare from behaviour: what people pay for clean air and quiet, where they choose to move, how they trade off commuting time against pay — on the argument that choice is the only evidence of utility that is ever put to a test. The second builds the index from day-reconstruction and experience-sampling surveys, on the argument that choices mispredict, and quietly adapt to, what life is actually like hour to hour. The third refuses aggregation and publishes a dashboard of separate measures. A pilot we ran on a proposed clean-air zone sharpened the disagreement: the behavioural valuation came out at a small fraction of the survey-based one, because residents of polluted districts had adjusted both their housing choices and their answers to the life-satisfaction questions. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one option concretely, with a timeline and a protocol for handling divergences between the measures; it should engage seriously with the strongest argument for the losing side rather than caricaturing it; and it should state plainly, in terms a general reader could follow in my newspaper, what each option commits us to claiming about what utility is — whether it is whatever a person's choices maximise, something her choices can systematically miss, or nothing that admits of a single number at all.
