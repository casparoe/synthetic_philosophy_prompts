For my term paper in a course on AI ethics and political philosophy, I'm focusing on the question of how a superintelligent AI system should communicate with humans given that its arguments, phrasing, and even tone might be orders of magnitude more persuasive than anything a human interlocutor can produce. My instructor has offered to sponsor three student research projects next semester on this topic, each roughly a semester of part-time work by a philosophy student with no ML background, so I need to advise her on which open questions to fund.

To be clear about the setting: assume a system that is genuinely benevolent and honest, so deception is off the table — the question is what honest, well-intentioned persuasion by a vastly more capable speaker does to someone's autonomy, and what norms should govern it. In brainstorming, I've collected a number of candidate questions, and I suspect some of these are downstream of others or just not tractable without technical skills I don't have: whether the system should disclose, before each persuasive exchange, that it is attempting to persuade (call this rhetoric disclosure); whether it should aim at the listener's immediate assent or at what the listener would endorse after extended reflection (and how these can come apart); whether its persuasive budget should be capped differently for different domains, e.g., personal life decisions versus political ones; whether it should always voice the strongest counterargument to its own position, even when doing so predictably makes the listener worse off by their own lights; and how much light ordinary cases of asymmetric speakers — doctors, therapists, cult deprogrammers — actually shed here, given that none of them approach the capability gap in question.

Which questions would you prioritize for these three slots, and in what order? Please structure your response as follows: a short "Criteria" section stating what makes a question worth a semester of a philosophy student's time and any trade-offs between criteria; a "Ranked Questions" section giving your top three, each with the case for funding it first and an explicit account of which other questions it is upstream of; a "Deprioritized" section covering the candidates you would not fund and why; and a final "What Would Change the Ranking" section on what developments over the next year or two should move questions up or down. I care more about the reasoning behind the ordering than about the list itself.
