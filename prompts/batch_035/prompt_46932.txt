I'm writing a novel set in a provincial British jobcentre, and my protagonist, Marion, is a benefits officer who has accumulated, over two decades, a track record of rulings on sanctions — the suspensions or reductions of claimants' payments for noncompliance. I want her behavior to be internally consistent as a character, and I've reached the scene where she must rule on a new claimant, so I'd like your help working out what she would actually do.

Her record so far, as I've drafted it in earlier chapters:

1. She sanctioned a young man living with his parents who missed three consecutive appointments and ignored every letter; when finally seen, he admitted he simply hadn't bothered.
2. She sanctioned a woman who falsified her job-search log, inventing employers and forging an initial here and there — though it later emerged the woman had been nursing her dying father and had no time to search, and her sister was covering the rent.
3. She sanctioned a man who truthfully reported declining a night-shift warehouse job because of his caring responsibilities for his mother — but he had also claimed his induction letter never arrived, and the delivery records proved otherwise. His brother contributes to the household income.
4. She did not sanction a destitute single mother of two who missed an appointment because her youngest child was hospitalized, and who called ahead to say so.
5. She did not sanction a woman who truthfully reported declining a cleaning job whose wages were less than her bus fare to it — because the woman had already accepted a better position beginning the following month, and her son pays the household bills.

The new claimant is a man in his late fifties, a machinist whose trade collapsed, long-term unemployed, no children, no family support. He attends every appointment without fail. At his latest review he volunteered — nobody asked — that he had turned down a caretaker position, because after travel costs and the taper on his benefits he would have been roughly twenty pounds a week worse off in work than out of it; he was candid, almost apologetic, and said he would take the job the moment the arithmetic worked the other way. He is behind on his rent and close to genuine destitution.

Please predict Marion's ruling. More importantly, tell me what rule or rules she seems to be implicitly running — I can see at least a good-faith-effort reading, a candor-versus-concealment reading, and something about where the sanction actually lands, but I want your independent analysis of the candidate principles and whether her record genuinely fits each one, including any I haven't noticed. Note that her precedents may underdetermine this case: every past sanction for declining work also involved some deception or concealment, so this may be the first clean test between rival readings. Give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring that rule over its competitors, describe the further case that would best discriminate between the rules — the incident I could give her in a later chapter to reveal which principle she has really been following all along — and, since this is a novel and not a policy memo, tell me briefly what each candidate rule would mean for who Marion is, if she turned out to have been running it.
