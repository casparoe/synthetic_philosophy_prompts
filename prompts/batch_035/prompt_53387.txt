I spent 35 years as an analytical chemist before retiring, so my instinct whenever I hear "vagueness" is that it's just measurement error wearing a costume -- every quantity has a true value, your instrument just isn't good enough, and philosophy should get out of the way of the mass spec. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy for fun though, and a popular book I just finished explains the debate over vague words ("bald", "heap", "tall") like this:

"Epistemicism holds that there is a sharp boundary: there is an exact number n such that a man with n hairs on his head is bald and a man with n+1 hairs is not. We can't currently locate the boundary, but that is simply because we haven't been careful enough -- with better definitions and precise counting, the cutoff is in principle discoverable. Supervaluationism is the more common-sense view: borderline sentences lack truth values, they are neither true nor false. So 'Luke is bald' may be indeterminate, and by the same token 'Luke is bald or Luke is not bald' also comes out neither true nor false -- meaning vague language is one place where the law of excluded middle genuinely fails. Epistemicists are stuck defending an invisible razor edge they can never point to, which is why hardly anyone accepts the view. Given that quantum mechanics has taught us that nature itself is fuzzy rather than sharp, the natural home for vagueness is fuzzy logic, where 'Luke is bald' can be 60% true, and the sharp-boundary debate simply dissolves."

Something about this bugs me but I honestly cant tell if it's just my lab instincts talking. The epistemicist line is weirdly appealing to me -- in my world there's always a fact of the matter even when your instrument is garbage, and "we haven't measured carefully enough yet" was basically my job description for three decades. But the book makes epistemicism sound like a fringe position held by cranks, and the quantum bit at the end sounds a bit too pat, like it's doing rhetorical work it hasn't earned. Can you go through the passage piece by piece and tell me what's accurate, what a defender of each view would object to, and whether the book's conclusion -- fuzzy logic wins because nature is fuzzy -- actually follows from anything it said along the way? Please assume I know no formal logic beyond intro level, so explain the machinery in plain language as you go.
