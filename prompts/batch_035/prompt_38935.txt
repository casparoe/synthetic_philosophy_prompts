I'm on the advisory board of a small foundation that funds conceptual work in the science of consciousness — philosophy of mind and theory development, not new experiments. Our next cycle can support about four researchers for five years, and the board has decided to concentrate on the cluster of disputes around global workspace theory: its standing against higher-order theories and recurrent processing theory, and Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness. Given that budget, which open conceptual questions in this area would you prioritize, and in what order?

I care more about the reasoning behind the ranking than the list itself. For each question you put forward, please explain (a) why it matters — how much other work in the field implicitly depends on how it gets resolved; (b) how tractable it is for philosophers working with the existing experimental literature rather than running their own studies; and (c) what empirical or conceptual developments over the next few years would move it up or down your list. I'd also like you to identify what you would deliberately deprioritize, and to justify that too: for instance, I have a suspicion that some of the disagreement is terminological — a dispute over which property deserves the label "consciousness" rather than over the properties themselves — and that this dispute absorbs effort that would be better spent elsewhere. But I'm open to being told that the terminological fight actually carries substantive weight.

Two specific tensions I'd like your prioritization to address. First, suppose the global workspace framework turns out to be a theory of access consciousness only, with phenomenal consciousness left untouched: which downstream projects would that genuinely disrupt — for example, the interpretation of unconscious-processing paradigms like masked priming, the search for neural correlates of consciousness, or the assessment of residual awareness in patients with disorders of consciousness — and which would carry on regardless? Second, cases that are sometimes claimed to dissociate access from phenomenology, such as the richness of peripheral vision or the fine-grained detail in visual experience that subjects can't fully report, seem to me like they should be central evidence here; I'd like to know whether you agree, and if so which of these putative dissociations you'd have the researchers work on first, given that each requires engaging with a different and contested experimental literature.

The deliverable I'm hoping for is a defended agenda, not a survey of positions: which two or three questions should these five years go to, and what would convince you mid-cycle that the ordering was wrong?
