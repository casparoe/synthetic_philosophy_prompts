My department head answers every question of credit and blame with the slogan that there is no "I" in team. When we miss a deadline, we are told that nobody should concern herself with who dropped which ball, because the team failed as a unit; when we exceeded our targets last quarter, the same department head singled out one colleague for the largest bonus. I am not asking you to adjudicate the office politics. What I want is a menu of the precise claims that slogan could be expressing.

Lay out the distinct readings, from the truistic (you cannot spell the word "team" with the letter "I") through the normative (individuals ought to subordinate their private goals to the team's goal) to something genuinely metaphysical: that a team can intend, believe, and be responsible for things over and above what its members individually intend, believe, and are responsible for. For each reading, say whether it is platitudinous, substantive but defensible, or simply false, and say which readings would license spreading blame across the team while also permitting unequal rewards — or whether the slogan, once precisified, can only support one of those two uses.

Please take seriously the strongest reading: that collective intentionality cannot be analyzed distributively, as each member intending her own part while expecting the others to do theirs. To test it, consider five relay runners who each intend to run their own leg well and each expect the others to do the same, and who between them drop the baton twice. Is there any fact of the matter about what the team did that is not exhausted by facts about what each member did, intended, and failed to do? If you think the distributive reading actually suffices there, explain what the anti-summativist thinks it leaves out — and whether any of it matters to my department head's inconsistent uses of the slogan.
