I am a high school senior writing my extended essay on the split-brain cases, and I have hit a wall that I now suspect is not really about the brain at all.

The background, as I understand it: starting in the 1960s, some patients with severe epilepsy had their corpus callosum severed, cutting the main neural link between the hemispheres. In the famous experiments, this produced striking dissociations — a patient could name an object shown only to the left visual field (routed to the right hemisphere) only if allowed to respond with the left hand, since the verbal left hemisphere had no access to what the right hemisphere had seen, and the right hemisphere, in turn, could not report what it knew in words. From this, the early researchers, including Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga, drew a large conclusion: each hemisphere houses its own stream of consciousness, so cutting the callosum leaves one body with two minds. Thomas Nagel wrote a well-known philosophical paper on these cases in the early 1970s in which he suggested that the question of whether there is one mind or two cannot really be answered, because we lack identity conditions for minds — we do not know what would count as one conscious subject continuing or splitting.

Since then, the debate seems to me to have gone in a circle. On one side are those who accept some form of the two-streams view, drawing on the laboratory dissociations. On the other side are those who point out that outside the laboratory the patients behave as unified persons — they walk, talk, plan, and joke like anyone else — and that some apparent communication between the hemispheres may persist through subcortical pathways. More recently, I have read that some philosophers defend a "switch" model, on which there is only ever one consciousness, which shifts between the hemispheres. Gazzaniga himself, meanwhile, emphasized the left hemisphere's role as an interpreter that confabulates a coherent narrative, which some take as support for disunity and others as support for unity.

Here is what I want, and my supervisor cannot give it to me. Everyone in this debate agrees on the experimental results. Everyone agrees the patients are conscious. Nobody seems to have moved in fifty years. So I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who is right. Specifically: is the disagreement substantive or verbal? That is, when the two-sides camp says "two consciousnesses" and the switch camp says "one," are they genuinely disagreeing about the world, or are they operating with different criteria for counting subjects — one side counting something like simultaneous experience, the other something like functional integration — so that both could be right under their own readings? Alternatively, is a hidden empirical question doing the work, namely what actually happens in these patients' daily lives outside the lab, so that the stalemate reflects missing data rather than confused concepts? Or is the real issue Nagel's: that the question "one mind or two" is ill-posed until we are given identity conditions for minds, in which case both camps are answering a question that has not yet been made precise?

Please give me a reasoned diagnosis, supported by how each camp actually characterizes the evidence — the laboratory dissociations on the one hand, everyday integration on the other — and tell me what the diagnosis implies about progress. If the disagreement is verbal, what distinction or stipulation would dissolve it? If it is empirical, what finding about subcortical pathways or behavior outside the lab would count as decisive? If it is ill-posed, explain why fifty years of careful work has not produced the needed identity conditions, and say whether that is a fact about the limits of the psychology or a fact about the concept of a subject of experience. I would rather be told that my essay's question needs to be reformulated than be handed a survey of positions I could have assembled myself.
