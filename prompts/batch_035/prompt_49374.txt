My roommate and I keep going around on this and I'm pretty sure he's smuggled something in, but I can't pin down where. The argument:

(1) You're morally responsible for an outcome only if you intended it, or could have foreseen it, or had enough control to prevent it. (2) When a learning system does something harmful — a trading algorithm tanks a market, a car handles a corner in a way nobody specified — the designers didn't intend it and, beforehand, couldn't foresee it (letting it figure things out was the entire point), and the user couldn't control it (that's what autonomous means). (3) So neither the designers nor the user are responsible, and the machine isn't a moral agent: responsibility gap. (4) But now take a company where an employee does something clever, unauthorized, and harmful — something nobody up the chain intended, foresaw, or controlled. The firm hired him precisely because he'd act in ways his managers couldn't anticipate, which is the same structure as (2). So there's a responsibility gap here too. (5) Yet we hold organizations responsible for their employees' unauthorized acts all the time — that's basically what vicarious liability is — and nobody thinks that's a category error. (6) So either (1) is false, and then the machine case is no gap either, or we have to say firms aren't responsible for rogue employees, which my roommate happily accepts and I really don't.

He says the conclusion is just that responsibility gaps are an ancient feature of delegation and the robotics people rediscovered them with better branding. My instinct is that something moves between (2) and (4) — something about the difference between a thing that surprises you and a person who decides — but every time I try to say it, he asks whether the foreseeability condition in (2) should also apply to the people who "designed" the employee (his parents? his teachers?), and I lose the thread. Where does the argument actually slip, if it does? And if it doesn't slip, be honest about which premise to give up — I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (6), but I want to know exactly what I'm paying for that.
