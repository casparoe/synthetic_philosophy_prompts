I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since like 2015, done a couple of AGI safety fundamentals cohorts, and now I do volunteer grant reviews for a small AI safety microfund a few hours a week. Mostly we get early-stage proposals from independent researchers, maybe 5-10 a week, and I have maybe 30 minutes per proposal before I write a short recommendation note. Our fund's mandate is explicitly about the narrow alignment problem, and here's the thing that keeps tripping me up: the word "alignment" is doing wildly different jobs in different proposals. By narrow alignment I mean roughly the intent-alignment sense — making sure the system is competently pursuing what the operator actually intends, not the broader value-alignment sense where you're trying to get the system's values to match humanity's long-run reflective values or whatever. So a proposal that makes the model produce more helpful and less offensive outputs will say it "improves alignment," and a proposal about whole-brain-emulation governance will also say it addresses "alignment," and I have to figure out in half an hour which sense the authors mean, whether the narrow problem is what they're actually working on, and whether they've noticed the difference at all. A scary number haven't, and it's awkward to reject a proposal partly because its authors are using the word wrong when the underlying work might be fine.

So: pretend you're a philosopher of AI who also does hands-on alignment research, and write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal in ~30 minutes. I want a sequence of questions to ask about the proposal text, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, such that at the end I can classify the work as (a) targeting intent alignment, (b) targeting value alignment, (c) targeting something else entirely that the authors are calling alignment (and what it actually is — capability work? ethics/taste? governance?), or (d) too confused/underspecified to tell, plus a flag for whether the authors seem aware the distinction exists. Please brainstorm the candidate questions first before locking in the sequence, and think hard about the failure mode where the procedure classifies surface features (the buzzwords used, the model class discussed) instead of the actual target of the work — a proposal can use all the right vocabulary and be aiming at something else entirely, or use sloppy vocabulary and be squarely on target.

Then run the procedure on three test proposals so I can see how it behaves: (1) a project fine-tuning a language model onApproval ratings from a diverse panel of humans to reduce sycophancy, (2) an interpretability project trying to extract and formally specify what the model's learned objective seems to be, and (3) a proposal for AI debate between two systems to surface human-invisible considerations before a decision is made. For each, show me the intermediate steps, not just the verdict. And I want at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I won't like — I'm sympathetic to the debate-style stuff personally, so if the procedure says it's not narrowly-targeted work, show me that honestly rather than rigging the questions. Also tell me where the procedure can't settle things and I just have to use judgment, and what the most common way is that I'd misclassify a competent proposal. No reading lists needed, I know the literature well enough — I want the procedure itself, usable as a literal checklist I can keep in a tab.
