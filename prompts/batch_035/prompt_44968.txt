I teach the philosophy of probability, and a student in my graduate seminar has produced an argument about Bertrand's paradox that I cannot stop chewing on. Every step looks like something I would mark correct on an exam, and yet the conclusion appears to prove far too much. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The background, briefly. In Bertrand's chord problem, one inscribes an equilateral triangle in a circle and asks for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord is longer than the triangle's side. Three classical applications of the principle of indifference give three different answers: choosing the chord's endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference gives 1/3; choosing a radius uniformly and then a point uniformly along it, drawing the perpendicular chord through that point, gives 1/2; and choosing the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk's area gives 1/4.

The student's argument runs as follows:

(1) Where the evidence gives no reason to prefer any of a set of exclusive, exhaustive alternatives, assigning them unequal credence misrepresents one's epistemic state. Indifference within a partition is rationally mandatory, not merely permitted.
(2) Each of the three parametrizations partitions the space of chords so that (1)'s antecedent is satisfied within the partition, and so each application is, on its own terms, impeccable.
(3) The choice among the three parametrizations is not settled by evidence either; nothing in the problem's statement privileges the endpoint description over the midpoint description. So by (1), we must be indifferent among the parametrizations themselves.
(4) The way to be indifferent among rules that issue in different answers is to average their outputs. Hence our credence that the chord is longer than the side should be 13/36 — the mean of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4.
(5) The move is perfectly general. Applied to the wine-and-water paradox, it would average the classical answers there; applied wherever defensible priors disagree, it would settle the dispute by arithmetic, extracting a determinate credence from what looked like genuine underdetermination.

The conclusion strikes me as proving too much in at least two ways. First, on the student's own account, the rational credence is hostage to intellectual history: a fourth parametrization, however contrived, would move it; before anyone proposed any parametrization there was apparently no rational credence at all, and the first proposal would have set it outright. Credences about the circle would then change on news about what mathematicians have dreamed up, with no new evidence about the circle. Second, (5) seems to conjure information out of nothing: if indifference can manufacture a number like 13/36, it can manufacture determinate priors wherever we had no right to them.

And yet I cannot locate the break. My colleague in physics, who works in the maximum-entropy tradition, tells me that Jaynes's old line about Bertrand's problem is that the problem's own symmetry group — rotational, scaling, and translational invariance — is supposed to privilege a unique measure, making the problem well posed after all. But she also concedes the published rejoinder that the same three invariances can be used to derive all three classical answers, so I am unsure how much weight her objection to step (3) can carry. Meanwhile the student, who is aware of all this, happily grants that any symmetry constraint I propose is itself one more candidate for the meta-level averaging in (3)–(4), to be diluted along with the rest.

I want to keep (1), at least in a weakened form: that indifference is mandatory or at least permitted once a partition is somehow given. My suspicion is that something changes between the first application of (1) — within a partition — and its second application — across partitions — but I cannot say what, since the second application looks like a literal instance of the principle I teach. So I am asking for the following: name the step I should reject; explain why the step looked legitimate, in a way that identifies what exactly (1) speaks to — partitions of outcomes within a fixed model, or something broader; and then price the alternatives. If the right move is to reject (3)–(4), tell me what the person who takes that exit owes us — if indifference among rules does not mandate averaging, does it mandate anything, or is there no fact about how to be indifferent at that level at all? If instead the right move is to reject (1) as stated, tell me what that costs: is the student entitled to the further conclusion that indifference is never mandatory, only permissible, so that any of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 — and perhaps 13/36 as well — is a defensible credence? And say whether, on either diagnosis, the student is right that (5)'s generality is where the argument shows its hand.
