I am a recently retired analytical chemist who spent the better part of my career at a metrology institute, working on calibration chains and the propagation of measurement uncertainty. Since retiring I have been reading epistemology, and I have just encountered the debate between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimony: whether the justification a hearer acquires from a speaker's say-so must ultimately be grounded in the hearer's other sources — perception, memory, induction — or whether testimony is a fundamental source of justification in its own right, not bottoming out in anything else.

I want you to restate this debate in the vocabulary I actually worked in, as faithfully as you can: primary standards and working standards, calibration and traceability, interlaboratory comparisons, the compounding of uncertainty from link to link in a chain, and the difference between an instrument's accuracy and its precision. Here is the mapping that has been forming in my mind; take it seriously, but do not flatter it. Reductionism about testimony looks like the demand for metrological traceability: no reading counts as a measurement unless it can be traced through an unbroken chain back to a primary standard. For the reductionist, the primary standards are the hearer's own observation and reasoning, and every speaker is a working instrument whose readings are only as good as the independent grounds I have for trusting that particular instrument. Anti-reductionism, by contrast, looks like the old artifact-based SI: before 2019, the kilogram was defined by one particular platinum-iridium cylinder kept in a vault at Sèvres. Nobody verified that cylinder against anything more fundamental; it partly constituted what a kilogram was. Trust in it was not derived from independent evidence, and it was not therefore irrational. Perhaps trust in ordinary speakers is like that: not derived from anything more basic, but partly constitutive of the practice that makes the very notion of a reliable source available.

After the translation, give me what I can only call the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate survive the move into calibration vocabulary, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in it at all. Two matters I especially want addressed. First, in a genuine traceability chain, uncertainty compounds at every link according to error-propagation arithmetic. If chains of testimony were like that, much of what I take myself to know — that the Earth's core is largely iron, that I was born on the day my birth certificate says — should have accumulated uncertainty far past any usable bound, and yet it plainly has not. What does this disanalogy show, if anything? Second, we metrologists could eventually redefine the kilogram against a fundamental constant and thereby retire the artifact standard; the anti-reductionist about testimony appears to deny that there is any analogous further standard against which trust in speakers could be measured. Does that mark a real difference between the epistemology of testimony and the epistemology of measurement, or have I misunderstood one side or the other?
