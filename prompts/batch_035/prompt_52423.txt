A friend of mine who does ML safety keeps telling me that mechanistic anomaly detection is incoherent, and I can't quite put my finger on where he goes wrong. The context, in case it matters: the idea, as I understand it, is that you flag inputs where a model is doing its work via a different mechanism than the one it used on the training distribution — a backdoored model that answers normally until it sees the trigger, or a model whose good benchmark scores came from some shortcut feature that quietly stops working off-distribution. Instead of watching outputs you watch internals. Fine so far. His argument:

(1) A mechanism, whatever else it is, is some pattern of components and connections in the network — a physical thing implemented in weights and activations. (2) Two inputs are handled by the same mechanism only if the same components do the same causal work on both. (3) No two inputs ever make the same components do the same work: activations are continuous and input-specific, so every input produces a numerically different activation pattern at every layer, and the causal profile always differs at least somewhere. (4) So no input is ever handled by the same mechanism as any other — there is no mechanism-sharing at all, only degrees of numerical similarity between token-computations. (5) Therefore on any test input the model is, by definition, using a "different mechanism" than it used in training, and a detector built to flag different-mechanism inputs fires on literally everything. (6) And since it can't fire on everything, what actually gets thresholded is a similarity score in some representation we chose — the notion of "mechanism" is doing no work, and mechanistic anomaly detection is just ordinary out-of-distribution detection wearing a lab coat.

The reason I think this proves too much is that he's happy to generalize it. By his lights no program is ever run twice on the same noisy hardware, no two patients ever have the same disease since their fevers differ numerically, and no two utterances of a word are the same word. He bites all those bullets cheerfully, which makes me suspect the bullets are being handed to him by a bad step rather than by reality. My instinct is the trouble is somewhere between (2) and (4) — soemthing about the granularity at which "same component, same work" gets evaluated — but every time I try to say "mechanisms are types, not tokens," he asks me what fixes the typing, and I don't have an answer. So: which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and what would a principled individuation of mechanisms even look like, such that anomaly detection comes out neither trivially all-anomalous nor trivially nothing-anomalous?
