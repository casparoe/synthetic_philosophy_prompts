I am currently enrolled in a course on campaign finance and interest-group politics, and a single analogy seems to structure nearly every reading we do: the political system as a marketplace. On this picture, campaign contributions are investments, lobbyists are brokers or middlemen, voters are consumers, candidates and parties are firms competing for market share, and election outcomes are the product being sold. My professor notes that the analogy gets used to license two opposite conclusions, which is what confuses me. Writers sympathetic to deregulated spending lean on it to argue that political money is like commercial speech: competition among spenders aggregates information, voters can sort good candidates from bad the way consumers sort good products from bad, and restrictions on spending are like restrictions on advertising — paternalistic and prone to entrenching incumbents. Critics of big money in politics use the very same analogy to argue that donors are simply buying votes and access, that the "political market" is rigged in favor of concentrated wealth, and that a system where influence tracks purchasing power is corrupt even when every transaction is formally voluntary.

I would like a serious audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on either policy position. Specifically:

1. Make the mapping explicit. What plays the role of the consumer, the product, the price, the firm, the broker, and competition? Some of these mappings are strained — voters, for instance, do not pay for what they "buy" with their votes, and each voter gets the same representative regardless of whether they supported them, so it is unclear votes are purchases at all.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks down. I want you to separate disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusions being drawn from ones that do not. It probably does not matter much that candidates are not literally firms; it may matter a great deal whether there is any functioning price mechanism, whether "consumers" can exit to a competitor the way they can switch brands, whether non-buyers (non-donors) bear costs from other people's transactions, and whether the "product" is a private good or a public one.

3. Ask what the analogy smuggles in. Does calling it a market already presuppose that exchanges are legitimate transactions rather than something more like bribery or toll collection? Does it import assumptions about efficiency, consumer sovereignty, or the desirability of competition that we would otherwise have to argue for?

4. Assess whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the deregulatory argument depend on the parts of the mapping that hold, or only on the parts that do not? Same question for the corruption argument.

5. Finally, if the market analogy is badly leaky, propose a better one — for instance, is politics better modeled as collective action, as public goods provision, as an auction, or as something the market frame gets almost exactly backwards?

I should mention that we have discussed cases like Buckley v. Valeo and the general framework of treating spending as speech, so you may treat those as background; but I am chiefly interested in the structure of the comparison itself, not in the case law. Please be rigorous about which claims the analogy can carry and which it cannot.
