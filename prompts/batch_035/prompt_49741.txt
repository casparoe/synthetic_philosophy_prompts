You are a professional speechwriter with a background in political philosophy, and in particular a close reader of Simone Weil's wartime essay on the abolition of political parties. I need you to draft an opening address, and I need you to think hard about the traps before you write it, so please begin with a brief brainstorm of the structural risks before producing the speech itself.

The occasion: a regional citizens' assembly, whose 100-odd members were selected by civic lottery, is convening for the first of eight weekend sessions on the future of the region's water supply. The assembly is strictly nonpartisan, but the room is not politically innocent: several members are longtime party activists, and local party officials and journalists are seated in the gallery. I am a philosophy professor who has been asked to give the ten-minute opening address, and I want that address to put Weil's argument to work rather than merely to invoke it.

The structural requirements, which follow from the Weilian material and which you should treat as hard constraints:

1. The speech cannot contain a single partisan applause line, on any topic, and cannot sneer at parties or at party members. Weil herself conceded that parties are often full of people of good faith whose civic work is real; the speech must honor that while insisting on the distinction between thinking alongside others and thinking *as* others think.
2. Weil's core worry is that collective machinery substitutes slogans and party spirit for the solitude in which judgment actually happens, and that people in a collective do not notice their own judgment being displaced. So the speech must dramatize that mechanism in concrete, everyday terms a plumber and a schoolteacher would both recognize — no jargon, no more than a passing mention of Weil by name if any.
3. There is an obvious irony to be managed rather than ignored: "think for yourself" is exactly the kind of slogan Weil was warning about, and the assembly is itself a mini-collectivity that will develop its own orthodoxies, factions, and deference patterns over eight weekends. The speech must acknowledge this and then convert it into two or three small, concrete procedural commitments the assembly agrees to try — for example, something about moments of private written judgment before group discussion, or about how members signal that they have changed their minds.
4. The register should be plain, warm, and brief: roughly 700 words, addressed to citizens, not to scholars.

Please produce the brainstorm first, then the speech. Do not include references, further reading, or citations of any kind.
