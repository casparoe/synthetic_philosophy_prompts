In a seminar on archival methods, discussion of my dissertation chapter was brought to a halt when another participant ended it with the remark that 'history is written by the victors'. The chapter tries to reconstruct something of the working lives of enslaved women on a Jamaican sugar estate from plantation ledgers, day books and the owner's correspondence — sources produced, of course, by the slaveholders themselves. I am reluctant to concede that nothing can responsibly be extracted from such records, but I am equally reluctant to treat the proverb as empty rhetoric. When people repeat it, the sentence seems to be doing at least three different kinds of work: sometimes as a platitude about which documents happened to survive; sometimes as a claim about the categories and vocabulary in which even the subordinated can appear at all in the record; sometimes as a blanket dismissal of any reconstruction of the past from hostile sources. Please disentangle the distinct precise theses that could be hiding in that one sentence, from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is plausible, and whether it would genuinely support refusing to attempt a chapter like mine. I would also like your view on which reading my critic was most probably invoking, and what an adequate reply to her, on that reading, would have to show.
