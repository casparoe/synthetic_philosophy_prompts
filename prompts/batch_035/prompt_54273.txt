I work as a policy analyst at a mid-sized international development agency. Every few months a project lands on my desk that will, in a fairly direct way, change who exists in future generations — resettling a village, building a dam that displaces people, a maternal health program that changes birth rates, that kind of thing — and we have an ethics review step, but our guidance was written as if the only question were "does this harm identifiable people who already exist." I half-remember the nonidentity problem from Parfit's Reasons and Persons and I think it breaks that guidance: if a policy decision changes the timing of conception, the people who exist *because of* the decision can't straightforwardly be said to be harmed by it, since the alternative for them was never existing at all. Nobody on the review board can run this reasoning cleanly, and I suspect we're either rubber-stamping things or refusing things for no coherent reason.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics who has also spent time embedded in actual policy shops, so you know the difference between an argument and a procedure someone can follow at 4pm on a Friday. Before writing the procedure, brainstorm briefly: lay out the different routes people have taken to the problem (person-affecting views, impersonal principles, ex ante consent-style reasoning, "no wrong without a wronged party" vs dropping that assumption) and note which ones can survive being turned into decision steps for non-philosophers, and which die in translation. Then actually write the procedure.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can run on a project proposal in maybe an hour, without any philosophy background beyond a one-paragraph briefing you should draft. Concretely it needs:

- an initial screen for whether the decision is identity-affecting at all, with a rough threshold for "plausibly changes who exists" so reviewers don't rabbit-hole on every project that merely nudges demographics — and an honest note on how crude that threshold is
- for identity-affecting decisions, questions that still get traction even though the "victim" can't be pointed to (I'm thinking things like: is there some possible future person, whoever they turn out to be, for whom life goes badly? does the decision look wrong when evaluated before anyone knows which people will result? does it fail standards we'd insist on for whichever people end up existing?)
- clearly marked exit points where the procedure says "stop, this is a values question, hand it to the board" — I specifically want the procedure to flag where it is smuggling in a controversial commitment (like whether to count happy extra lives as a benefit) rather than pretending to be neutral
- the failure modes: where the procedure will misfire or give absurd answers if followed mechanically, so reviewers know when to distrust it

Then run it on three cases: (1) resettling a flood-prone village, where relocation will predictably shuffle who marries whom, and conditions at the new site are expected to be harsh but above bare subsistence; (2) a lithium mine that enables decarbonization decades out but does serious local ecological damage now; (3) a maternal health program that will substantially change the size and composition of the next generation in the region — and for at least one of these I want the case where the procedure gives an answer the review board (and probably me) won't like, with an explanation of whether that's the procedure being right or being broken.

Please don't give me citations or a reading list — assume I can't check references. If you need to gesture at a philosopher's position, describe it in plain words instead. Keep the whole thing usable: headings, numbered steps, the worked cases clearly seperated. Think carefully about the order of the steps — I suspect which question you ask first actually does a lot of work here.
