I'm a journalist — mostly on the science-and-ideas beat for a general-interest magazine — and this fall I got roped into co-teaching an evening adult-ed seminar called "Big Questions" (think intro philosophy for curious grownups). We just finished a unit on personal identity that included two sessions on Buddhist no-self (anatta), built around the chariot dialogue from the Milindapañha. I'm now sitting on a stack of take-home exams and I want a second pair of eyes on one of the answers before I commit to a grade.

The question I wrote was:

"A. Anatta (no-self) and the person.
(a) Explain the chariot analogy in the dialogue between King Milinda and the monk Nagasena, and how it is supposed to support the doctrine of no-self.
(b) An objector argues that the analogy proves too much: if a self is just a convenient name for the five aggregates, then by the same reasoning a chariot is just a name for its parts, and then nothing at all exists — the argument dissolves the whole world, not only the self. How might a Buddhist reply to this objection?
(c) Does anatta give you any reason to care about your own future suffering no more than you care about a stranger's? Defend your answer."

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as recieved:

"In the Milindapañha, King Milinda, who was a Greek king, asks the monk Nagasena who he is, and Nagasena replies that his name is only a designation. When the king presses him, the Buddha gives the analogy of the chariot: when the axle, wheels, body, and the other parts are assembled in the right way, we call it a chariot, but no matter how thoroughly you search through the parts, you never find a chariot-essence hiding in any of them. The same is true of the person: it is a designation for the five aggregates, namely form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. This is anatta, which is really just the impermanence doctrine: since everything changes moment to moment, there cannot be a stable self persisting through time.

The objector says this dissolves the whole world. But Nagasena could answer that the chariot does exist in an everyday sense — after all, it can carry the king to the palace — it just does not exist as some essence beyond its parts. To call something a designation is not to call it nothing. So the self is real enough for everyday purposes, like answering to your name, but there is no deeper thing underneath.

Finally, since there is no self, there is no continuity between the me of today and the me of tomorrow, and so a Buddhist has no reason to care about her own future pain any more than about a stranger's. That is why, for a Buddhist, compassion is not just nicer than selfishness; it is the only attitude that takes the facts seriously."

We grade on a 0–100 scale with letter equivalents (90+ is an A, 80–89 a B, 70–79 a C, 60–69 a D, below that an F).
