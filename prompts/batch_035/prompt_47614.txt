My friend is obsessed with the old Meno puzzle — why is knowledge supposed to be better than a true belief that happens to be right — but she refuses to argue theory, she only ever gives verdicts on cases. I've been keeping notes on which beliefs she says are "better to have" than someone's lucky guess:

- the traveller who carefully studied a good map of the route to Larissa, versus the traveller who just guessed and got there anyway: she says the map-reader's belief is clearly better
- an old farmer who is certain the river floods every spring, purely because it always has — he cant explain why, nobody taught him, he just expects it, he's never yet been wrong, and he moves his livestock up the hill every year on the strength of it: better than a gambler's one-off lucky guess
- someone who glances at a clock that stopped exactly twelve hours ago and truly believes it's noon: NOT better — she says that one is worth no more than a lucky guess
- a sailor whose good-luck ritual reliably indicates storms, though he thinks it's magic and couldnt explain the correlation to save his life, and he re-rigs his boat by it every voyage: better than a lucky guess

Next week she comes to dinner and I get to pitch her a new case: an amateur historian who spent months properly verifying that a fairly obscure merchant visited a particular town in 1763 — her method was airtight, the belief is true — but the fact will never guide anyone's actions, won't support any further beliefs anyone will actually draw, and she'll never think about it again, versus a rival who just guessed the same thing truly. Can you predict what she'll say about that one? I'd also like you to work out what rule she's actually running — I can see at least two or three that fit her verdicts so far — and tell me which further case would best tease those rules apart. Please answer in British English.
