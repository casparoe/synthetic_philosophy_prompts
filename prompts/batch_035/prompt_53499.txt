I run a student effective altruism group at my university, about 14 people, mostly second-years who've read a bit of 80k-style material but never actually tried to use the frameworks. I've got a 90-minute session coming up on cause prioritization, specifically the importance / tractability / neglectedness (ITN) framework. The problem is that everyone nods along when it's explained on a whiteboard -- multiply the three scores, rank the causes, done -- but nobody feels where the framework actually bites: the fact that the three factors fight each other, that neglectedness is doing something weird (the more people who pile into a cause, the worse it looks, which feels backwards until you sit with it), and that the scores are basically made-up numbers whose product gets treated as knowledge. So I want a game, not a lecture.

Here's what I'd like you to design. Act as an experienced workshop facilitator who's run cause-prioritization exercises many times and knows all the ways they fail. The game should use fictional causes -- invent maybe six of them, deliberately varied so that some are huge and horrifying but utterly intractable, some are small but easy wins, some are neglected for arguably very good reasons (nobody works on them because they don't matter), and at least one that's the fake version of a cause that gets a lot of real-world attention, so the participants can't just pattern-match to what they've read online. Participants should have to commit to numeric scores for each factor at some point, because I want them to feel the arbitrariness of putting a number on tractability. It would be great if the game had at least two rounds, where between rounds you inject a shock -- e.g., a fictional billionaire drops $500M into one of the causes -- so they have to watch their neglectedness scores collapse and figure out what that does to their ranking, including the uncomfortable implication that success makes a cause look worse.

Rules, materials (assume a whiteboard, sticky notes, printed scorecards, no laptops), a fictional budget with some payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money but still gives people something to win or lose, and a time breakdown for the 90 minutes. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific claims of the ITN framework -- and importantly, onto its known weak points, like whether the factors are really independent, whether multiplying them makes sense when the scores aren't on any shared scale, and whether neglectedness tracks "weirdly overlooked" versus "rightly ignored." Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect some participants will refuse to give numbers at all, others will just argue about the fictional causes' details), and be frank about which feature of real cause prioritization the game just cannot reproduce -- a 90-minute game can't give people the actual uncertainty of the real world, and I want to know what I'll have to supply in the debrief that the game itself can't.
