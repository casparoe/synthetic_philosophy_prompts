I edit a small epistemology blog read mostly by advanced undergraduates and other grad students, and I want to run an interactive feature titled "Which view of peer disagreement do you actually hold?" My readers constantly tell me things like "I'm basically a conciliationist, except about math" — I suspect most of them are sorted by which label they find flattering rather than by what they would actually do in the cases, and the quiz is meant to expose that. So the diagnostic questions have to do all the work.

Please design the quiz to the following specification.

Format and discipline of the questions:
- Ten questions, each a short first-person concrete case with two or three answer options. At least one must be a numerical-confidence question (options given as confidence levels, e.g. "I'd go from 90% down to 85%," not verdicts), and at least one must be about suspension vs. continued outright belief rather than about degrees of confidence.
- No question may use any of the labels of the debate — no "conciliationism," "steadfastness," "equal weight," "higher-order evidence," "epistemic peer" (describe the peer's credentials instead, concretely), and no philosopher's name. A reader who has never heard of the literature must be able to answer every question.
- Don't recycle the literature's stock examples verbatim (the restaurant tip calculation, the blind wine tasting, the jazz-club parking spot). Fresh variants in the same spirit are exactly what I want.

Required case-types — each of these must appear exactly once among the ten:
1. A case in which you learn the actual content of your peer's reasoning, and it seems to you to contain a specific flaw you can articulate but cannot get them to see in discussion.
2. A case in which you are one against eleven, all disagreeing with you in the same direction, and — this is the twist I care about — where you have independently checked and rechecked your own work.
3. A case of disagreement about peer disagreement itself: the reader must be confronted with equally impressive people who give opposite answers to the very question the quiz is asking, and must decide whether their own answer is destabilized by that fact.
4. A case about an evaluative rather than a purely factual question (for instance, whether a particular grant proposal is fundable), so the quiz can test whether the reader's position is meant to generalize across domains.
5. A case in which you learn of the peer's view only secondhand, through a reporter whose reliability you cannot assess — to probe whether the source of one's evidence about the disagreement matters.

Scoring key:
- It must be runnable with paper and pencil — a pattern-matching key, not a script.
- It must map answer patterns onto at least: the equal weight or split-the-difference view; the view that first-order evidence can swamp the peer's dissent so that little or no movement is required; the right-reasons view on which you should hold steady when your own reasoning was in fact sound; the lonely steadfast position on which reasonable people can simply remain sure they are the lucky ones; the position on which you can never justifiably rule out that you are the unreliable one, and must therefore dig in; and full withholding. Associate each leaf with the philosophers who defend it in the literature, as far as you can.
- The key must honestly flag which pairs of positions the quiz cannot separate, and say why (for instance: which concrete case would have been needed to pull apart two views that agree on everything my ten questions test?). I would rather print an honest gap than pretend the instrument is sharper than it is.
- Finally, include a short section on the empty cells of the logical space: combinations of answers that would be perfectly consistent but that no published position occupies. For each, one sentence diagnosing why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?

Keep the tone of the quiz questions neutral: the answer options should be equally self-flattering, since my readers will otherwise use vanity as a heuristic.
