My closest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the old dust-speck question — whether some enormous number of people, each suffering a purely trivial harm (say, a dust speck caught in the eye for a few seconds), could be all-things-considered worse than one person being tortured for fifty years. We got much further than either of us expected, and we want a document that captures exactly where we arrived. I'll describe our positions as precisely as I can. Your task is to draft the joint statement we could both sign — not to adjudicate between us.

My position is aggregativist about suffering. Every instance of suffering has negative value; disvalue need not be linear in intensity (I happily concede that a harm's badness may grow more than proportionally with how bad it is), but I deny any lexical gap: there is no severity threshold below which harms stop counting across persons. So for any suffering, however mild, there is some number of simultaneous instances of it whose total badness exceeds that of a single, much more intense suffering. Note that I claim only that such an outcome would be *better*, not that anyone is obligated to produce it.

Her position is a threshold view with a twist. She accepts that trivial harms are bad and matter impartially, and she accepts aggregation above a certain severity — she readily agrees that a sufficiently large number of day-long severe migraines, distributed across different people, can outweigh one person's fifty years of torture. But she denies that below some severity of individual harm, any number of such harms, across any number of persons, can outweigh a harm above the threshold. She takes the threshold to be real but vague, the way a sorites boundary is: a dust speck is clearly below it, a day-long migraine clearly above it, and she is honestly unsure about the middle. Her guiding idea is that the tortured person has a claim that cannot be defeated by any number of claims each too weak to engage with it: individual claims gain in weight only once each is strong enough to be commensurable with the opposing claim in the first place.

Two things we noticed along the way that we want reflected in the document. First, late in the argument we suspected part of our dispute was verbal: when she says the specks don't "outweigh" the torture, she may partly mean a claim about what one would be *permitted to impose* in a forced choice, whereas I mean a comparison of how *bad* the two outcomes are. We want the joint statement to disentangle these cleanly, and, if you judge that some of our residual disagreement is genuinely verbal rather than substantive, we want that said explicitly, with which part and why. Second, when I pressed her with a continuum of cases, dialing the individual harm up from speck to paper cut to headache to broken bone, she replied that vagueness at the boundary does not refute the boundary — there is no sharp line between day and night, but the distinction is not thereby an illusion. I found this move stronger than I expected, and I want the crux it generates stated exactly.

Please produce the following:

1. A numbered section of claims we both accept. These should be substantive — enough that neither of us is signing something empty, but nothing either of us would balk at. Include whatever we clearly share: that trivial harms are genuinely bad rather than neutral, that each person's suffering counts equally and impartially, that she rejects any *pure* lexical absolutism (she agrees some durations or intensities of the severe harm are mild enough that even she would let the numbers win), and anything else you judge we genuinely converged on.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair to its own view — this is the part we will scrutinize most, so phrase each side's position the way that side would phrase it, not as the other side would caricature it.

3. Our cruxes: the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of us. We believe there are roughly three: (a) whether the ranking of outcomes is a function of individual welfare levels alone, or is sensitive to a relation *between* persons' claims; (b) whether a vague threshold can do real work at the clear cases even while conceding a wide vague middle where our verdicts converge; and (c) the intra-personal test — she accepts that one person's own countless trivial sufferings over a lifetime can sum to something very bad for that person, and I press that if aggregation is fine within a life, adding more persons should not switch it off. State each crux precisely, and correct us if you think our list is wrong — merged, split, or missing one.

4. For each crux, at least one concrete case or argument that we both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance, in that side's own voice. For crux (b), use the bridging case we discussed: an astronomically large number of people — larger than any number either of us can picture — each enduring a moderately painful but non-disabling headache for one hour, versus one person tortured for fifty years. We both agree this case bears on where the threshold sits; write down what each of us predicts we would say about it, as we would each state it.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with their cases and predictions. Throughout, keep the drafting neutral — we should each recognize ourselves, and neither of us should be able to complain that the document tilts.

Finally, close the document with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase (at most six words) naming the single deepest crux between us — the one whose resolution would move the most. We intend to disagree about that too, but we'd like to see what you say.
