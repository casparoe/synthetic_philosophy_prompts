I'm drafting the framework chapter for a volume on the ethics of organ transplantation, and the chapter has to do something I haven't seen done carefully: set out, before taking any sides, the full specification that a definition of death — together with the criterion that operationalizes it and the bedside tests that apply it — has to meet if it is to be fit for both law and vital-organ procurement. The background, so you can see what's at stake: the standard US statute equates death with either irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration or irreversible cessation of all functions of the whole brain, including the brainstem; but almost every element of that formula is now contested somewhere in practice. In donation after circulatory death, the patient's heart is stopped and, after a wait of only a few minutes justified by the evidence on autoresuscitation, the patient is declared dead under a circulatory criterion even though the cessation is permanent only because it was decided not to reverse it — irreversibility in the strict sense would require an hour or more of no-flow time, by which point the organs are lost. In deep hypothermic surgery, circulation is deliberately stopped for the better part of an hour at temperatures where the patient would, under ordinary criteria, count as dead, and yet the patient is rewarmed and walks out of the hospital. Brain-dead patients have been somatically maintained for weeks — in one much-discussed sort of case, to allow a fetus to reach viability. And anencephalic infants, who never had the capacities a higher-brain criterion makes constitutive of being alive as a person, expose the difference between losing a function and never having had it.

What I want from you is the desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a judgement for each as to whether it is non-negotiable or reflects the prejudice of one camp. At a minimum I'd want you to cover: respect for the three-level distinction between the definition of death, the criterion that implements it, and the tests for applying the criterion (a theory that quietly slides between levels has cheated); compatibility with settled biological fact — that death of the organism is not an instantaneous event but a process taking place at different rates in different tissues, so that a criterion demanding a single determinate moment of death is imposing a line on a continuum and owes us an account of why the line is principled rather than convenient; determinability at the bedside by feasible tests, since a criterion that is correct but untestable within the window in which transplantable organs degrade is fit for metaphysics and useless for medicine; compliance with the dead donor rule, that the retrieval of vital organs must not itself be the cause of death — or else an honest statement of which desideratum gives way when it conflicts with the others; the distinction between the death of the organism and the death of the person, and a decision about which one the definition is a definition of; the distinction between irreversible and merely permanent cessation, which in the DCD setting makes the moment of death depend on a prior decision not to intervene; and objectivity — the thought that whether someone has died should not be hostage to facts about what anyone will choose to do next.

The part I most want to see is where the list bites itself. My suspicion is that at least two pairs of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied: that no criterion can make irreversibility the marker of death while keeping the time of death independent of human decisions, since irreversibility just is a modal fact partly constituted by what will in fact be done; and that the requirement of a determinate moment of death cannot be had together with the process character of biological death without the line being drawn by convention — in which case the list itself, and not any particular criterion, is the philosophical result. Say whether you agree, and if you think some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, show which and what should be dropped. Then, and only briefly, run the three familiar candidates — whole-brain, circulatory, and higher-brain criteria — against the list, noting which single requirement does the most to exclude each. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard; the scoring is for a later section and I can do it myself.
