For roughly twenty years I have taught philosophy, mostly at a mid-sized state university, and every so often a case comes along that my colleagues and I chew over at the coffee machine for weeks. I would appreciate your help thinking one through, because I find that my training in ethics, while it gives me the vocabulary, does not settle this for me in practice.

A student of mine is applying to doctoral programs in philosophy. He is diligent, genuinely decent, and eager — but his written work is mediocre. His papers are clear enough, yet rarely rise above careful summary of the assigned readings; when he does argue, he tends to miss the obvious objection. Realistically, a strong program would be a poor fit for him, but admissions committees at lesser-ranked programs lean heavily on letters, and mine, given my subfield, would carry real weight with several of them. When he asked me for a letter, I agreed — in part because declining, given our customs, would itself have told him what I think of his prospects, and I did not want the request to do that work.

Now, drafting the letter, I see that I have several options. I could write him a frankly positive letter; that would be a straightforward lie, and I won't do it. I could write a letter every sentence of which is true but which I know will be read as damning: a paragraph about his punctuality, another about how reliably he attended seminar, a closing line noting that he has the makings of a patient grader of introductory coursework. Nothing in it would be false; the reader would take the whole as a signal that he cannot do philosophy, and I would intend them to take it that way. Or I could decline to write after all, which by convention says roughly the same thing as the faint-praise letter. My question is whether the second option — asserting only things I believe true while knowingly inviting an impression, and planning for the reader to receive that impression — is morally different in kind from the outright lie, different only in degree, or not different at all. Some of my colleagues insist the distinction is a comfortable rationalization: a way to get the effects of deception without the stain of having asserted a falsehood. Others reply that what matters is precisely that I never assert anything I believe false, and that the reader's inference, however reliably I can predict and arrange it, is the reader's own act. I would also like your view on two refinements: whether my position changes because the impression I am inviting is, as it happens, one I believe accurate (I am not inducing a belief I think false — only getting the reader to it without saying it); and how you would rank the misleading letter against outright silence, given that in our profession silence itself speaks. I should add that I bear this student no ill will — if anything, I am reluctant precisely because he is kind and hardworking — so please take that off the table as an explanation of what I'm tempted to do.
