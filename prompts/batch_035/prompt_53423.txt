Hey. I'm a grad student and my department's aesthetics reading group has been stuck on the aesthetic testimony question for what feels like forever, and I'm starting to think the problem isn't the arguments — it's the shape of the disagreement itself. So this is a request for a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right.

Quick map of where we're stuck, so you know what I mean. One side (call them the pessimists) hold soemthing like an acquiantance principle: a justified belief that a work is good has to be grounded in your own experience of it — you engage with the work and judge for yourself. They back this up with a few different things: that aesthetic belief is supposed to come with understanding (you should be able to say why it's good, in your own words, with reference to features of the work itself), that critics disagree wildly so there's no track record of convergence the way there is in, say, chemistry, and a more life-ish thought that a person whose entire aesthetic life consists of borrowed verdicts is missing the point of having aesthetic responses at all. The other side (optimists) say testimony is just a normal epistemic source: I've never been to Tokyo but I know it's in Japan, so why can't I know Ulysses is a masterpiece because trusted friends and critics, whose taste I've sampled and calibrated over years, tell me so? Every restriction the pessimists propose looks ad hoc — it would forbid trusting a friend about a restaurant or a movie, which we all do constantly, and when the pessimists retreat to "well, those aren't REAL aesthetic beliefs" the whole thing starts to smell like a definitional trick. We've been through this loop maybe four times this year. All the moves are known. Nobody has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two groups are answering different questions under one heading. The pessimists are talking about appreciation and what makes an aesthetic life worth living, the optimists about justified belief, and the phrase "aesthetic judgment" papers over the gap. But I'm not sure, because sometimes the pessimists do seem to be making a straight epistemic claim (critics don't converge, so deference is a shot in the dark), which would make the whole thing an empirical question in disguise, and sometimes the optimists quietly concede the life point and defend only the belief point, which feels like losing by winning. So which is it? Verbal? A hidden empirical disagreement about convergence? Genuinely substantive and just hard? And what would count as actual progress — a distinction, a study, a change of question — or should we expect none?
