I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've internalized the usual lessons about priors, replication, and the file drawer — which is precisely why I'm having trouble calibrating my attitude toward the guest on my podcast next month. He is a moral philosopher in the utilitarian tradition who has also spent several decades as an officer of a psychical research society, and he models himself explicitly on Henry Sidgwick, who managed to be both a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research (in 1882) and the author of perhaps the most rigorous systematic treatise on utilitarian ethics of his century. My guest's thesis is that Sidgwick's double life was not an eccentric diversion but a natural extension of utilitarianism: if consciousness can exist independently of the body, or if information can be acquired in ways our current physics does not accommodate, then the scope of the moral calculus — who or what counts, what our obligations extend to, whether death really is the end of a welfare subject — changes enormously, and the empirical question becomes ethically urgent. He further contends that the accumulated experimental record, from the Victorian seance investigations to the ganzfeld debates to Daryl Bem's 2011 precognition studies, is more probative than mainstream science is willing to grant.

I want to interview him in a way that takes this thesis seriously without rolling over for it. Please draft eight to ten interview questions with the following constraints:

1. At least one question on the Sidgwickian connection itself — specifically, on the dualism of practical reason (the apparent rational gulf between what prudence dictates for oneself and what the general good dictates for everyone) and on why my guest thinks evidence about survival of death or disembodied minds would bear on resolving it, rather than being an empirical curiosity orthogonal to ethics.

2. At least one question pressing him on the replication crisis, using Bem's work as the fulcrrum: he must say whether he accepts that the methodological lessons that led the psychology mainstream to discount Bem's results — optional stopping, flexible analysis, publication bias — apply with equal force to the older parapsychology literature he leans on, and if he thinks psi studies are somehow exempt, why.

3. At least one question posing an explicitly Bayesian challenge: given the historical base rates of fraud, self-deception, and selective reporting in this literature, from Victorian mediumship through the mid-century card-guessing labs to the present, what likelihood ratio would the surviving evidence need to have to move a well-calibrated prior, and does he think it clears that bar?

4. At least one falsification question: what pattern of findings, over the next few decades, would he regard as strong evidence against the reality of psi or of survival — and if the honest answer is that no pattern would, what distinguishes his position from an unfalsifiable one?

For every question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give — he is clearly steeped in the literature and will have ready answers, so the follow-ups should anticipate those answers rather than merely asking him to elaborate. Please avoid gotcha framing and avoid questions that let him retreat into a comfortable plea for more research funding; I want the strongest version of his position extracted and then genuinely stress-tested.
