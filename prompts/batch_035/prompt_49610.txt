Some years ago I completed a master's in philosophy before leaving academia, and I have recently had cause to regret both the leaving and some of what I took away. For a decade I have had treatment-resistant depression that medication never touched, and last spring I enrolled in a clinical trial of deep brain stimulation. The surgery went well. At my next programming appointment I face a choice between two settings, and the choice has made me realize I may not have a coherent view of what a life is for.

The situation, briefly: the team distinguishes between what they call liking and wanting. One setting reliably produces a calm, undifferentiated contentment while flattening motivation and emotional range — I would be serene but progressively indifferent to the projects and relationships that used to structure my life. The other preserves engagement and range of feeling but leaves residual lows. The higher setting is, in effect, the contented fog; and it is permanent unless I ask them to change it. So before the appointment I sat down and wrote out what I actually believe about well-being, and the list disturbed me more than the surgery did.

1. Hedonism, straight: the only thing good for a person, in itself, is the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience. Facts about desire-satisfaction, achievement, and contact with reality matter, if at all, only through their effects on how life feels from the inside.
2. Mill's qualitative distinction, which I have defended since graduate school: pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity, and a modest amount of complex, engaged pleasure — reading, friendship, working a problem through — can outweigh a large quantity of simple comfort, because of the faculties it exercises.
3. The resistance people report to Nozick's experience machine is bias — status quo preference, plus an attachment to our own imagined specialness. People confronting real analogues of the choice, like chronic pain patients who choose comfort over engagement, show what anyone would actually choose. If I am right, the person permanently wired into blissful stimulation — the human version of the rat that abandons food and young to press the lever — is not thereby worse off.
4. The deceived businessman: the man whose plans all succeed while the story of his life is rotten — faithless partner, false friends, a fraud about to be exposed — is badly harmed even while he feels wonderful. Harmed by how things actually are, not merely by the eventual crash.
5. Death is bad for the one who dies because of deprivation. Dying painlessly and instantaneously at forty is bad for me, though I never experience anything unpleasant in the dying, because of the good life I lose.
6. The contented oppressed person — whose desires have contracted to fit narrow and unjust circumstances, so that she wants little and gets most of what she wants — is not doing well. And I do not think this is because her pleasure is unusually faint; it seems to me the defect is in the pleasure itself, or in what it is a pleasure in.
7. And, uncomfortably: when I imagine the higher setting, I find I would refuse it, and the refusal does not strike me as bias. It strikes me as the considered judgment that the serene, indifferent version of me would be worse off in a respect that matters.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on the theories behind these. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as though they were equally bad. For each, say what would have to give and what it costs — I already know that abandoning hedonism requires replacing it with something, and I am not interested in being told to abandon it as though that were free. Two questions I specifically want answered. First, whether (5) is really a problem for (1) or whether a hedonist can say everything I want to say about deprivation. Second, whether (2) is one of the problems or part of the solution: could the quality-of-pleasure metric absorb the businessman in (4) and the contented oppressed person in (6) — and, for that matter, ground my refusal in (7) — or does that move quietly convert hedonism into an objective-list theory wearing a hedonic label? Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I have three weeks before the appointment, and I would like to walk in with a position I can defend to myself whichever setting I choose.
