Covering physics stories for a general audience, I sometimes get physicists saying cheeky philosophy stuff in interviews. Latest one: I'm writing a piece about JWST spotting galaxies as they were 13 billion years ago, and one cosmologist I talked to joked that "to be is to be perceived," so by Berkeley's lights those galaxies only came into existence when the telescope imaged them. Editor loved the line, I put it in my draft, and now I want to actually understand what I'm quoting before it goes to print. Because it seems like the slogan could mean a bunch of really different things? Like maybe it's just the boring point that all our evidence about anything comes through perception, maybe it's the claim that physical objects are nothing over and above their dispositions to look and feel certain ways to us, or maybe it's the full-on ontological thesis that existing just IS being perceived by a mind -- which, if you take the no-minds-yet early universe seriously, apparently forces you to say God was perceiving the galaxies the whole time, or that they popped into being retroactively?? And there might be a verificationist reading too where talk of unperceived stuff is just meaningless noise. Can you lay out the readings as a list I can work from, say for each whether it's trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or just false, and tell me which reading (if any!) my cosmologist is actually leaning on when he makes the joke about the telescope -- and whether the joke survives on that reading or is just trading on ambiguity?
