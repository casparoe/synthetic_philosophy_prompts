In debates over autonomous-vehicle ethics, a familiar move is to say that self-driving cars will confront trolley problems at scale: the philosopher's bystander at the switch becomes an engineer setting how a crash-avoidance system trades off risk to the passenger against risk to pedestrians. In my department, one colleague uses this analogy to argue that trolleyology has finally become practical ethics — that we need a settled answer to the footbridge case before allowing such cars on the road. Another uses the same analogy to argue the reverse: that the trolley problem is so stylized that importing it into engineering discussions actively distorts policy, since real crashes involve probabilities, degraded sensor data, and no clean five-versus-one framing. Audit the analogy for me. Give the explicit mapping — who or what plays the bystander (the engineer, the learned policy, the car itself?), what plays the switch (a parameter, a training objective, a whole design culture?), what plays the certainty of outcomes. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for each colleague's conclusion from those that don't: probabilistic risk-shaping versus a forced binary choice; the aggregation of many small risks over many events versus one dramatic decision; and, importantly for the doctrine of double effect, whether anything in the system intends a death in the sense that distinction requires, given that the programmer aims at no particular outcome at all. Finally, tell me whether either colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other comparison — actuarial risk regulation, public-health trade-offs, something else — would serve the engineering debate better.
