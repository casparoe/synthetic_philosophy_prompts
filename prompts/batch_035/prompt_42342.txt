So I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly features — and my younger brother has hit me with a question I can't get out of my head, and I'd love your help untangling it.

Background: he dropped out of college a couple years back, he's been living at our parents' place, and recently he told me his actual life plan. There's a startup (he won't name it, could honestly be vaporware) supposedly building full-dive brain-interface simulations. The pitch: once it works, you lie down somewhere, the machine runs a complete life for your brain — you sketch the broad strokes (career, where you live, who you love) and it takes it from there, and from the inside it's supposedly indistinguishable from reality. And there's an option I find almost creepier than the rest: you can have the memory of entering the machine wiped, so you'd live the entire thing believing it was real. No halfway state, no nagging doubt in the back of your mind, ever.

He's completely serious about signing up the day it exists. When I pushed back, he asked what exactly he'd be giving up, and I floundered. My gut said something like: you'd want to have actually written the novel, not just have the rich experience of having written it. But he had an answer ready: from the inside, "actually writing it" just is a particular sequence of experiences — the stuck months, the breakthrough, the finished manuscript. If the machine produces that exact sequence, there's nothing left over for him to be missing.

I write about this stuff for a living and I couldn't answer him on the spot, which stings. Can you help me figure out what's actually at stake here? Is his move — that a life just is its experiences from the inside — something serious people have a considered reply to, or does it only sound airtight? And is there anything I can say to him that does more work than "it feels wrong"? It seems to me there are two questions mushed together: whether he'd be worse off in that tank, and whether something else about it matters anyway. I'd like to keep them seperate, because I suspect he wins one of them and loses the other — I just can't tell which is which.
