I teach a mid-size seminar in philosophy of science — fourteen students, one two-hour session, tables that can be rearranged into small groups. We're about to take up the pessimistic meta-induction, and I don't want to hand them the argument on a slide; I want them to build the track record themselves and then notice what they're standing on when they go to endorse today's theory. Design a multi-generational simulation that does this.

The setup I have in mind is a fictional history of science on some topic — say, the nature of heat, or inheritance, or the composition of the heavens, but with the theories and instruments invented by you rather than borrowed from real history, so students can't pattern-match to "phlogiston was wrong" and coast. Students work in research teams of three or four. The room passes through five or so successive "generations": in each generation, teams receive a hand of data cards, choose among two or three live rival theories, and stake credibility points on their choice, with points paid out according to how well the theory explains that generation's data. Then a "new instrument" card is revealed — a better telescope, a finer calorimeter, whatever fits your invented history — which discloses a new datum that the previous generation's winning theory cannot accommodate. The theory is retired to a public ledger at the front of the room, with its points recorded. Each new generation must choose again, using the same standards the last one used: fit to available data, unification, fecundity. Crucially, give the final generation a distinct option the earlier ones lacked: they may purchase a "seal of permanence" on their theory at a steep cost in points, which pays out a large bonus only if their theory is never overturned. They must decide knowing the ledger.

I want the rules, materials, and payoff scheme in enough detail that I can run it from your write-up without improvising: the actual data cards and what each says, the theories on offer in each generation, how points are awarded and lost, how the ledger is maintained, and how long each generation gets. Keep the fictional science simple enough that a philosophy student can evaluate it in ten minutes, but real enough that theory choice is a genuine deliberation rather than a coin flip — I'd like the generations to differ in how hard they are, so that some choices are easy and one at least is a near tie, since the argument's sting is worst when the winning theory was chosen by impeccable lights.

Then the debrief. It should map moments in the simulation onto the pieces of the argument: the ledger is the historical record of successful-then-false theories; the decision facing the final generation is the inference problem the meta-induction poses; the fact that every retired theory was chosen by the same standards as the current one is what makes the induction "meta." I also want the debrief to stage the realist's replies against what happened in the room — the no-miracles appeal to the fact that the winning theories really did earn their points, the selective realist's claim that some parts of retired theories survived into their successors (design the fictional theories so that some but not all of each one is carried forward, and see whether students notice), and the point that maybe the current generation's theory differs in kind from the retired ones. Let the debrief ask whether any of that would have rationally stopped the final generation from hesitating over the seal.

Finally, two candid sections. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: students refusing to buy anything at any price because they've guessed the twist (and how to keep the early generations oblivious — I'm tempted to run them in genuine ignorance and only reveal the seal option at the end, but tell me if that creates problems); students treating points as a game to be won rather than an epistemic commitment; a generation that picks a theory for laughs. Second, what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in discussion: presumably the fact that real scientists cannot see the ledger as a completed pattern, that we don't know we aren't an early generation, and whatever else you think the simulation structurally distorts. I'd rather hear the distortion honestly than discover it mid-session.
