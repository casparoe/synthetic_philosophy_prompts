I am a retired geophysicist who spent most of my career reconstructing paleoclimate from ocean sediment cores, and who has lately — perhaps too late — fallen into the philosophy and historiography of the last few centuries. I have volunteered to give a six-part lecture series on "world history for the scientifically minded" at our local lifelong-learning institute, and I have run into a problem I cannot solve myself: every way I arrange the material seems to smuggle in a standpoint, and I cannot tell which arrangements merely hide theirs better than others.

Concretely, I have been weighing five strategies for structuring a global history:

1. Terminological and curricular repair: switching to BCE/CE dating, retiring language of "discovery" and "dark ages," and rebalancing reading lists — while the overall arc stays much as it was.
2. Additive inclusion: keeping the traditional spine (Mediterranean antiquity, medieval Europe, the Renaissance, the Enlightenment, industrialization) but interleaving substantial chapters on Song China, the Abbasid caliphate, Mali, the Mughals, and the Mexica.
3. Divergence analysis: abandoning the long arc of "the rise of the West" in favor of a late, contingent comparison — asking why northwest Europe pulled ahead only around 1800, with attention to coal, colonies, and global flows rather than European exceptionalism.
4. Inversion: treating the Indian Ocean world or China as the demographic and economic center of gravity for most of the period, with Europe a resource-poor peninsula on the margin until recently.
5. Big history: beginning with the Big Bang, stellar nucleosynthesis, plate tectonics, and evolution, so that human history occupies only the final minutes of the account; on this scale, questions of whose history it is arguably lose their grip.

My own temptation is toward the fifth, since it looks the most like the unified natural-science narrative I am at home in. But I have a nagging worry, which I will state in my own idiom: in physics we learned that there is no privileged frame of reference, and I suspect that "telling the past from everywhere at once" may be like claiming a view from nowhere — a claim that, on inspection, always turns out to be someone's view from somewhere, just unacknowledged. Scale, in particular, does not automatically confer neutrality; zooming out far enough can simply dissolve the problem rather than solve it, the way averaging over a turbulent field can hide the turbulence.

So here is what I would like from you, and I would like you to commit rather than survey. First, rank the five strategies from most to least genuinely Eurocentric — that is, by how far each actually escapes the standpoint it is trying to escape, as opposed to relocating or cosmetically masking it — with a sentence of justification per placement. Second, tell me which single strategy I should build my lecture series around, and accept the costs of that choice explicitly. Third, give me your settled verdict on the framing question: is a history told "from everywhere at once" a coherent ideal that these strategies approximate in varying degrees, or an incoherent one that each strategy fudges in its own way? If it is the latter, say what the defensible substitute ideal is. Do not hedge into a list of considerations; I want your actual ranking, your actual choice, and your reasons.
