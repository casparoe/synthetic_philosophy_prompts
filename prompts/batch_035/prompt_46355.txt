For the past few months I have been trying to extract a decision principle from my own verdicts on variants of Newcomb's problem, and the cases refuse to line up. I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. My verdicts: in the classic case, with a predictor known to be right about 99% of past choosers, I one-box without much hesitation. In a variant where the predictor is right only 51% of the time, I two-box — at that accuracy the predictor is barely better than a coin, and the dominance argument seems obviously to win. In a medical variant where a reliable test tells me I carry a lesion that both causes a strong craving for cigarettes and, independently of my behavior, causes cancer — so that my smoking would be evidence of the lesion but would do nothing to my health — I smoke, or would if I wanted to. In a transparent-boxes version where I can see the million sitting in box B and the thousand in box A, I take both, and I feel no regret about abandoning the million that is already visibly mine. But in a version where the "predictor" is really a time traveler who simply observed my choice and reported it back before the boxes were sealed, I one-box again without hesitation — somehow the mechanism matters to me, and I cannot say why. And in a version where the predictor rewards two-boxers and punishes one-boxers, just to keep me honest, I two-box, though I notice this is the same structure with the labels swapped, and I am not sure the swap should matter. I can see how an evidential view would get my first verdict and how a causal view would get my second and third, but each loses at least one verdict I care about, and the time-traveler case embarrasses both. Please state the principle, or a short list of candidates, precisely; tie each clause of it to the case that motivates it; tell me what it predicts about a variant I have not mentioned — say, transparent boxes where box B is visibly empty, so that one-boxing means walking away with nothing; and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and why you think it is the verdict, rather than the principle, that should go. The case I keep going back and forth on, and would most like your principle to adjudicate, is a predictor that is right only 60% of the time, where box B holds a billion dollars — enough that a twenty percent edge feels like it should swamp the guaranteed thousand, and yet something in me still reaches for both boxes.
