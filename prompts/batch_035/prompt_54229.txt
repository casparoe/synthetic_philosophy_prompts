In the city where I live, as in many jurisdictions with discretionary zoning, whether a proposed apartment building gets built is decided at a public hearing following notice mailed to the owners of property within a few hundred feet of the lot. Those neighbors—the immediate abutters and, in some schemes, anyone else who shows up—may speak against the project, and the board may deny it on grounds like inconsistency with neighborhood character, added parking pressure, or diminished views. The people who would actually live in the building have no notice mailed to them, no right to speak, and indeed no way of being identified, since they don't yet know they want to live there; the same goes for the businesses that would employ them and the region's employers who would hire them. Meanwhile the very same approvals process, when it happens to trigger environmental review, is required to weigh diffuse and future interests: impacts on people miles away and not yet born, through traffic projections, emissions accounting, and the like, all evaluated for parties who never attend a hearing and get no notice either. And in other areas of the same legal system we draw the stakeholder line differently again: a defendant's sentencing hearing hears from the victim but not from the victim's neighbors, and a firm's hiring decisions are nobody's vote but its own.

I want to understand what this patchwork presupposes. Focus on the zoning case, with the others as points of comparison. What implicit theory of who has a claim on a land-use decision does the notice-and-hearing practice rest on: is it that those who bear concentrated, localized costs deserve a veto while those who bear diffuse benefits get nothing; that standing follows propinquity or property rather than affectedness; or something like a presumption in favor of the status quo, under which existing residents count as the city's constituents and merely possible residents count as no one's? Reconstruct the theory (or theories) the practice actually needs, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined—where several rival accounts of urban stakeholding would license the same one-sided hearing. Then consider the strongest objection to the most natural reconstruction: on a principle that everyone affected by a decision deserves some say, the prospective residents are plainly affected and plainly silenced, but they cannot be identified, consented to nothing, and did not exist when the neighborhood took its shape—so does the asymmetry reveal that the affected-interests principle is incoherent as applied to cities, or only that it demands representatives rather than direct participation (a tenants' union, a lottery-chosen panel of would-be movers, an advocate for future residents)? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in the hearing process if the presupposed view were replaced by that rival: who gets mailed what, who may object on what grounds, and whether any version of neighborhood input survives.
