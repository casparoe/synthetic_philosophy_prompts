I am a novelist drafting a book set in a fading river town in Iowa, and one thread of the plot turns on a dinner-table argument between two sisters and a retired philosophy professor who taught the well-being literature for forty years. Over the course of the novel, each sister accuses the other of having wasted her life, and I want the professor to respond not with a lecture but with a single, carefully built case that he lays out once and then refuses to repeat. To make that scene work, the case has to do genuine philosophical work rather than serve as scenery, so I am turning to you rather than improvising it myself.

Here are the specifications. Construct two lives that are comparable on every axis except the ones at issue—similar length, similar health, similar material comfort, real relationships in both—so that neither life is a strawman of misery or of bliss. The first sister should have undergone a shift in her desires: she once wanted something demanding and rare, then came to want, sincerely and without self-deception about the facts, the smaller life she ended up with, and she is content. The second sister kept her original ambitions, has some genuine accomplishments to show for them, and is chronically dissatisfied. No experience machines, no radical deception, no science-fictional devices, no perfect information from any oracle—the case must be the kind of thing that actually happens to people, and both women must know the facts of their own lives. The case must be built so that hedonism ranks the first sister higher, a straightforward actual-desire-satisfaction theory also ranks her higher, while an informed or idealized-desire theory ranks her lower—and so that a thoughtful reader feels the pull of both verdicts rather than being railroaded toward one.

After the scenario itself, which I would like as a single paragraph of no more than 150 words, suitable for being spoken aloud at a dinner table, walk through the reasoning of each theory and show why it delivers the verdict it does. Then tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and how a defender of actual-desire theories would fight back—for instance, by questioning whether an informed-desire standard is still tracking what the person herself cares about rather than what she should care about. Close with one or two sentences the professor could say when one sister accuses him of having stacked the deck. Keep the entire response, scenario included, under 600 words.
