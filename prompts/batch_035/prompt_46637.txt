I work in a state government "red tape" review unit — small team, we get handed a docket of maybe forty legacy rules each quarter, most of them decades-old regulations nobody remembers passing, and our job is to recommend scrap, keep, or rewrite. The minister wants a procedure, not vibes, because right now it's basically whichever analyst gets the file doing twenty minutes of googling and going with a hunch, and we got burned last year when we scrapped something that turned out to be the only thing holding some behaviour in check. No disaster, but enough of a scare that the minister now wants sign-off on every docket item.

The obvious starting point is Chesterton's fence — don't remove a fence until you know why it was put up — plus the Burkean bit underneath it about institutions encoding knowledge nobody can articulate. But as stated it's useless to us. It just says "find out first", and we don't have the time or the archives to find out for forty rules a quarter, half of which have no discoverable legislative history at all. And it doesn't distinguish between the situations we actually face, which after a while in this job look like three seperate animals:

- fences that guard against a harm that is still live, even if nobody left alive can say what the harm was;
- fences that no longer guard against anything but have become load-bearing — businesses have organised themselves around the rule, so removing it doesn't restore some pristine pre-rule state, it creates a brand new disruption, and the disruption lands on the people who complied;
- fences that mark a boundary — the rule exists as a settlement between two factions who would otherwise fight, and its real function is the truce, not the words in the regulation.

Write us a decision procedure the team can actually run. For each step: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure stops with scrap / keep / rewrite / hand to the minister. Constraints worth knowing: an analyst gets at most one day per rule, there's no access to internal agency history beyond the public file, and recommendations have to survive a minister who wants headline scrapping numbers ahead of the next session. Then run it on three cases: (a) a 1970s rule requiring two signatures on some paperwork, rationale undiscoverable, everyone in the affected industry thinks it's silly; (b) a licensing rule whose original public-health justification is clearly obsolete, but existing firms have built whole compliance departments around it and new entrants would love it gone; (c) a rule that looks pointless on its face until you dig and find it's the quiet compromise that ended a long-running fight between two industries. Include at least one case where the procedure gives the answer the minister won't want, and be explicit about which step isn't a factual question at all — when "is this harm still one we care about" turns out to be a value call, that goes to the minister, not to us. And tell us honestly what the procedure can't do: the kinds of rule where one day of diligent process still produces a confident recommendation that is, at bottom, a guess.

Please answer in British English.
