Two friends of mine — a professional sports bettor who swears by the Kelly criterion and an academic who works on decision theory and is a fairly orthodox expected-utility maximizer — have argued for years about bet sizing and, more fundamentally, about what rationality under risk requires. They have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the joint statement they will both sign.

Here is how I want you to write that document. The Kelly side holds that a rational agent should choose so as to maximize the long-run geometric growth rate of wealth — that compounding makes multiplicative, not additive, growth the quantity that matters, and that this is a structural fact about agents who persist through time rather than a psychological preference. The expected-utility side holds that rational choice maximizes the probability-weighted expectation of utility, that the utility of wealth is whatever shape the agent's actual preferences reveal it to be, and that even a positive-expectation bet can be rational to decline if the agent's utility function is concave enough — without any appeal to long-run averages.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: the claims both parties can sign without reservation — about what the Kelly criterion computes, about the fate of a gambler who repeatedly stakes a fixed fraction of wealth, about the divergence between arithmetic (ensemble) and geometric (time) averages of wealth, and about anything else you find they genuinely share.

2. A disagreements table, in which each remaining disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the Kelly bettor would accept as a fair statement of his own view, and once in wording the expected-utility theorist would accept as a fair statement of hers — plus a column for which disagreements are substantive and which are, on inspection, merely verbal (for instance, whether an expected-utility maximizer whose utility happens to be logarithmic in wealth simply is a Kelly bettor under another name).

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, each paired with a case, bet, or argument that both parties agree bears on it, together with each party's prediction recorded in advance. At minimum, address these cases:
   - The coin that pays +50% of current wealth on heads and −40% on tails: positive arithmetic expectation per flip, yet ruinous over a long enough run for an agent fully reinvesting. What exactly does each side think this example shows, and what, if anything, would each concede about it?
   - Samuelson's colleague, who refused a single 50-50 bet of winning $200 against losing $100 but said he would accept a hundred such bets. Both sides agree the colleague's position needs explaining; each should record whether they think it is defensible, and what their explanation is.
   - A bettor offered a fixed sequence of 100 coin flips with a 51% heads edge, who must precommit to staking a constant fraction f of current wealth on each flip. Give each party's recommended f and their predicted distribution of outcomes, and state what the divergence reveals.
   - A securitized version of the same opportunity: the bettor can instead sell shares in a single flip to many investors, or bundle many flips and sell stakes in the bundle. Each party should record whether this changes the rational fraction, and why.

Keep the tone of a signed consensus document: formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, no scoring of rhetorical points, and each side's position stated as its holder would state it. Where you believe a dispute dissolves once the words are fixed, say so explicitly and identify the fixing.
