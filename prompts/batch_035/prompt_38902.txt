I've been teaching Dennett's quining-of-qualia argument in my phil of mind seminar, and I keep coming back to the Chase and Sanborn coffee taster case he uses to argue the whole notion of qualia is confused. Quick sketch in case it's fuzzy: Chase and Sanborn are professional coffee tasters for Maxwell House. Chase decides the coffee now tastes just as it did when he started the job, only he's become more discerning and no longer likes that taste. Sanborn decides the coffee itself tastes different to him now -- worse, not to his liking -- and he can't tell whether his taste buds changed or his internal standards for judgment drifted. Dennett's point is that nothing in their introspective access can settle these differences, because our judgments about our experiences are deeply confounded with the experiences themselves, and the "intrinsic, ineffable, private" properties qualia were supposed to be don't survive this pressure. My question: does this actually show that qualia are an incoherent posit, or only that our introspective reports about them are unreliable? I want you to take a side and defend it. Don't give me the standard survey of pro and anti-Dennett positions -- commit to either the eliminativist reading or the "unreliable access, real phenomenology anyway" reading, and explain where exactly you think the argument overreaches (if it does). One thing I'd especially like you to address: if Chase can't distinguish between his qualia changing and his judgment-calibration changing, is that a fact about qualia, or just a fact about the limits of discrimination in memory-based comparison generally?
