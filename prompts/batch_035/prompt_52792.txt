At a workshop on computational models of creativity, I recently watched two researchers argue past each other, and I want to see the disagreement done properly. The topic was Douglas Hofstadter's account of creativity, the one he lays out in Variations on a Theme as the Crux of Creativity (a chapter of Metamagical Themas, building on his Scientific American column of the same name). The core of that account is the knob-twiddling model: a concept is not a definition but a position in a multi-dimensional conceptual space, one whose dimensions have graded distances that shift with context; thinking of a variation on a theme -- a new A, a Chopin mazurka, a composite face, a novel -- is a matter of nudging a large set of "knobs," most of them superficial and quickly returned to center, a few of them central and consequential, with the nudging partly guided by internal pressures from the activated concepts themselves and partly stochastic. Hofstadter illustrated this with his "First Lady" thought experiment, where subjects are asked to describe a woman who looks like a blend of Hillary Clinton and Michelle Obama: before describing the composite, they find themselves specifying, one adjustment at a time, what they would have to change about the Clinton concept to get there -- hair, skin tone, age, cheekbone structure -- which suggests that even an intuitive "snap" of recognition is underwritten by an implicit sequence of small, local moves in a space of features. He and his Fluid Analogies Research Group built models (Copycat and its successors) in which such moves are made by many small agents competing in a workspace, with a temperature-like measure that loosens the system when it is far from a good structure; and he has insisted, in the face of programs like David Cope's EMI that compose convincing pastiches by recombination, that the crux of creativity is exactly this local, pressured twiddling rather than long jumps or brute recombination.

I want a written dialogue between two proponents of opposing views on this, staged as a conversation over coffee after the workshop, and I want both of them to be genuinely good at this. Neither side should be a strawman, and they must respond to each other's actual points -- concessions, reformulations, and pointed replies, not parallel monologues.

One participant is a computational cognitive scientist in the FARG tradition. She holds that the knob model, extended in the ways Hofstadter himself extended it, is essentially right: that what looks like a "long jump" across conceptual space is usually an artifact of plotting the path in the space of the mature, end-state concept system -- the coordinates that make the two endpoints look distant are not the coordinates that guided the moves when they were made, and the space itself can reshape (distances collapse, groupings reform) while the twiddling is going on. She can point to the layered structure of the knobs, to the fact that which local moves happen is unguided in detail, and to the thought that pressure-guided stochastic twiddling in a space that can reorganize itself already suffices for the space to rebuild itself, so no deus ex machina is needed. She may also deploy EMI as evidence that the response humans have to creative work sits nearer the surface than romantics about creativity like to admit.

The other participant is a philosopher of discovery, skeptical of the model's reach. His strongest lines should include: (1) that the model may capture the mechanics of style and retrieval but is silent on how the navigable conceptual space arises in the first place -- that the crux of creativity may be the process that grows the space rather than the steering within it, so that knob-twiddling explains the ornament but not the architecture; (2) that the "who applies the pressure" question hides a regress -- pressures are just other concepts activating, which is fine, but then the model owes us an account of why some regions of the space exert pressure at the right moment, and appeal to pressure risks smuggling in a homunculus in softer clothing; and (3) that EMI cuts both ways -- a lay audience's inability to distinguish a machine-generated mazurka from Chopin at first hearing measures surface style, not the depth that rewards a lifetime of listening, so the experiment underdetermines the conclusion she draws from it. He should be no romantic about lightning bolts: he concedes that major advances typically come with long chains of small local changes (the Wright brothers' yearly modifications are his favorite exhibit) and he does not believe in uncaused leaps; his claim is about where the explanatory weight falls.

Both of them should be allowed to make the strongest move available to their side, including revising their own position when the other lands a real point. The dialogue should end without a cheap synthesis: instead, let them converge on stating precisely what kind of evidence or model would settle the matter between them -- what a knob-twiddling system would have to do, or fail to do, to count as explaining the origin of a new conceptual space rather than only travel within one.

Write it in a polished register; medium length, enough to let the dialectic develop. Do not have either party cite bibliographies or list references; if either invokes a historical example, keep it one an educated reader would know without looking anything up, and introduce it in enough context to be intelligible on its own. Please do not give me a moderator's verdict at the end.
