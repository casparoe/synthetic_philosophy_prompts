My political obligation seminar has me stuck on a fair play argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find where it breaks.

(1) If you knowingly accept substantial benefits from a cooperative scheme that other participants make real sacrifices to keep running, you owe them your fair share of the burdens.
(2) Anyone living in a functioning state knowingly accepts substantial benefits — roads, security, courts, epidemic control — that their law-abiding compatriots make real sacrifices to provide (taxes, foregone opportunities, sometimes military service).
(3) Your fair share of the burdens of a cooperative scheme can't be less than complying with its rules when everyone else does.
(4) The rules of the scheme include the laws, all of them, not just the ones you happen to like.
(5) So every beneficiary of a functioning state has an obligation to obey the law as such — political obligation grounded in fairness, holding even for laws the person believes are unjust.

Here's my problem. Run the exact same argument on a neighborhood where a gang provides protection that genuinely cuts violent crime, sustained by the shopkeepers who pay up: it seems to say the shopkeeper who's been threatened into paying owes the gang compliance, since she knowingly accepts the reduced crime and could move away. Or run it on a woman in a country whose laws bar her from voting and most professions: she still gets the roads and the security, other women comply with the discriminatory laws at real cost to themselves, so fairness obligates her to comply with her own disenfranchisment. If the argument works, it grounds not just political obligation but obligations to rackets and to injustice, which can't be right.

Tell me which step to give up. Lead with your answer in the first sentence, then walk me through why that step looked fine in the abstract and what rejecting it costs. I especially want to know whether the fix still leaves us with an obligation to obey just laws in ordinary cases, or whether patching the mafia case wrecks the whole fair play story. And if you think the state and the gang can be pulled apart without smuggling in a premise like "the scheme must be reasonably just" — which I suspect is cheating, since that premise would be doing all the work — explain why I'm wrong about that.
