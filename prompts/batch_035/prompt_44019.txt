I am a high school senior taking an elective called Ethics in Literature, and for my term paper I have to test a philosopher's theory against a novel rather than summarize either. I have chosen Anna Sewell's Black Beauty (1877) and Kant's account of our duties regarding animals, and I would like your help developing the central reading before I draft.

Here is the pairing as I understand it. Kant holds that we have no direct duties to animals: they are not persons or ends in themselves, and their pain does not, by itself, make an action wrong. Our duties concerning them are indirect duties to humanity — cruelty to animals is wrong because it damages the agent, deadening compassion and disposing us to treat other humans cruelly. He sets this out in the Lectures on Ethics and the Doctrine of Virtue, though I gather the Lectures are reconstructed from student notes, so there is interpretive leeway. Note that on this view animals do feel pain; the question is only whether their pain counts morally for their sake or only for ours.

I want a reading that is fair to both sides, and that ends in a verdict rather than a survey. Please address, at minimum, these four points:

1. The vindication case. In the novel, kindness and cruelty seem to travel together across species: the masters and grooms who are gentle with horses (John Manly at Birtwick, the cab driver Jerry in London) are also the best people to other humans, and the cruel ones are coarse all around. Doesn't this pattern look like evidence for Kant — cruelty to animals as a symptom and a rehearsal of cruelty to people?

2. The pressure point. Ginger is ruined by harsh treatment early in life, and near the end of the book Beauty comes across her body on the knacker's cart. When the reader grieves at that moment, the grief seems to be for Ginger herself — for what her life was like for her — not for what her suffering did to anyone's character. Can the indirect-duty view explain that grief, or does it ring hollow precisely where the novel is most powerful? I want this confronted directly, not softened.

3. The bearing rein. Much of the cruelty in the book is driven by fashion and status rather than sadism: the checkrein is used because a high head carriage looked elegant, by owners who barely register the horse at all. If the wrongness of animal cruelty lies in its effect on the agent's character, what does the view say about suffering caused by thoughtlessness and vanity, where there seems to be no cruelty in the person to coarsen? This is my own worry about Kant, and I want to know if it is actually a good objection or whether a Kantian has a ready answer.

4. The narrative form. The book is narrated in the first person by the horse, recounting his own life with hopes and fears. Does the novel's very form beg the question against Kant by presupposing the horse has a perspective on his own good — or can a Kantian accept the device, on the grounds that having sensations is not the same as being an end in yourself?

Finally, two requests about honesty. First, if any of my plot descriptions does not match the novel as you know it, please flag the discrepancy rather than building on it. Second, at the end of your response, add a short note stating how confident you are in the reading's central claim, singling out the one interpretive move you are least sure of, and acknowledging any point where the reading leans on a contestable understanding of Kant.
