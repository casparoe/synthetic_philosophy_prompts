I'm taking an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of harm, and we've just finished a unit on the distinction between doing and allowing — the idea that there may be a morally important difference between killing someone and letting them die, even when the outcomes are the same. For the creative portion of our grade, we've been asked to write a short story (roughly 1,500–2,000 words) that dramatizes this distinction rather than merely mentioning it. I've been told my draft shouldn't read like a philosophy paper with characters; the distinction has to live in the plot and in the texture of the protagonist's experience, and the story shouldn't resolve into a tidy moral either way.

Here's the scenario I've sketched, which you should feel free to adapt and enrich: A veteran operator is working the night shift at a small rural water utility during a severe storm. A flood has compromised the treatment intake, and untreated runoff is beginning to seep past a failing valve into the distribution branch that serves the valley — including a nursing home and about four hundred households. Her instrumentation is partially offline, so she can't be certain how bad the contamination is or how fast it's moving; she only knows people will start falling gravely ill over the next several hours. There is one intervention available: by manually opening a bypass, she would actively redirect the flow into a much smaller branch that serves an upstream enclave of roughly thirty seasonal cabins. The contamination that is currently "just happening" would become contamination she deliberately steered toward a smaller group of people. To complicate the character of her agency, she has been opening and closing these valves by muscle memory for over twenty years, so the physical act of doing would feel almost automatic — I'd like the story to explore whether that changes the moral phenomenology, or whether it should.

A few further constraints: I'd like a second character, an off-site engineer on the phone, who argues from a straightforwardly consequentialist standpoint and pushes her hard toward the bypass, while never using philosophical vocabulary — their disagreement should be concrete and practical in tone. Structurally, I'd love the letting-die to unfold slowly, over hours, while the doing would be over in a matter of seconds, so that the story's pacing itself mirrors the distinction. Finally, please include a moment where she considers a third path — doing nothing directly but falsifying her logbook so the contamination's arrival would look like an equipment failure no one could have caught — and let the story sit with the question of whether that bureaucratic omission feels different from the other omissions. The ending should leave a thoughtful reader genuinely torn, not secretly coached toward one side.
