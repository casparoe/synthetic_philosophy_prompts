My roommate spent like three hours on customer support last week because someone social-engineered their account and took a rare item out of their inventory, and the support team eventually restored it and banned the other guy. Which got us into a whole argument, because the game's terms of service -- like basically every MMO's -- says flat out that virtual items are the company's property, have no real-world value, and that your account is just a revocable license. So the same institution officially declares these items to be nothing (no value, not yours, can vanish at any time), and then runs a whole enforcement apparatus -- investigations, restorations, bans, appeals -- that treats them as exactly the kind of thing that can be stolen from you and owed back to you. Meanwhile real-money trading of those same items is banned in one game and officially supported via an auction house in the next one, and when a game shuts down its servers, everything everyone "owned" is destroyed with no compensation and nobody thinks that's theft.

So here's what I actually want to know: what ontology of virtual objects does this whole pattern presuppose, if any? Is the support-and-restoration practice only coherent if virtual items are something like property -- or could it be fully explained as just a company managing customer feelings, regulating behavior, keeping players subscribed, without any commitment about what the items ARE? And is the pattern actually inconsistent (they're nothing here, they're yours there) or do the different bits presuppose compatible views -- e.g., "it's a service we provide" covering both the ban on scamming and the shutdown-with-no-compensation? I kept saying the practice needs at least a defensible story about virtual harms being real in some sense, my roommate kept saying it's all just contract and branding and the metaphysics is idle, and honestly I couldn't tell which of us was begging the question.

Try to reconstruct whatever implicit theory makes the practice intelligible -- including how "theft" of something with no value is even supposed to work -- then tell me what parts of the practice that theory strictly requires versus merely fits well with, and where the whole thing is underdetermined enough that several different ontologies of virtual objects would license the exact same behavior. And if you replaced the presupposed view with your rival's, point at which concrete parts of the practice would have to change -- like, would support stop restoring items, would the shutdown become compensation-worthy, would the RMT bans lose their rationale?
