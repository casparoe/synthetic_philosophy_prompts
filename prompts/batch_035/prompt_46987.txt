In nearly every parliamentary democracy, most legislators run under a party label and are expected to vote with the party line even on questions where they privately disagree, with dissenters risking the withdrawal of the whip, expulsion, or deselection at the next election; only a small set of issues is formally carved out as a matter of individual conscience. What theory of political judgment and representation does this practice of party discipline presuppose? That voters elect a program rather than a person, so a legislator's private judgment has no special standing? That collective discipline filters out individual bias and corruption? That the party, not the member, is the true representative? Consider Simone Weil's wartime argument in On the Abolition of All Political Parties: she claimed that any institution making adherence to a shared doctrine a condition of belonging necessarily corrupts its members' relation to truth, because staying in good standing requires treating the party's collective position as correct rather than arriving at one's own judgment, and that parties are above all machines for the pursuit of power in which doctrine serves the party's growth rather than any concern for truth or justice. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the whipping system, and assess whether it presupposes precisely what Weil denied is possible — that a member can treat the party line as a genuine reason while her judgment remains her own — or whether a defensible theory of party discipline can be reconstructed on which the practice is consistent with honest judgment. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, which rival theories of representation would equally well license it, and what would have to change in parliamentary practice if Weil's view were taken seriously.
