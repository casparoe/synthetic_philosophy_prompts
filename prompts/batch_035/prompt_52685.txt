I'm in an evening reading group that has spent this term on twentieth-century philosophy of language, and we have reached Wittgenstein. Before our next meeting I read ahead in a well-reviewed popular book on the analytic tradition, and its two pages on the private language argument struck me as unusually clear and confident — perhaps too confident. In substance it says the following:

'Wittgenstein's private language argument is the centrepiece of the Philosophical Investigations (1953). A private language, in his sense, is one whose words refer to what only the speaker can know about — in the paradigm case, his own inner sensations. The argument is that such a language is impossible: a sign like "S", coined purely to mark a recurrent private sensation, could never acquire a correct use, because whatever seems right to me would thereby be right, and a sign that cannot be misapplied is not a meaningful sign at all. The point emerges vividly in his famous thought experiment of the beetle in the box: each person has a box no one else can look into, and if everyone uses the word "beetle" for the thing in his box, the thing in the box drops out of the language-game entirely — which shows that sensations, conceived as private objects, play no role in our psychological vocabulary. Wittgenstein is thus best read as a behaviourist about the mental: what we call naming a pain is really the replacement of pain-behaviour by its verbal expression, and the criteria for applying sensation-words are entirely public. This has the striking consequence that there could be no such thing as private, first-person knowledge of one's own states: whatever I could not in principle manifest in behaviour or in words, I could not mean, and so could not know.'

I found this genuinely illuminating, and parts of it match what our group leader has said about the "S" diary and the difference between a solitary language and a logically private one. But a friend who studied this at university tells me the behaviourist reading is a known misinterpretation, and that Wittgenstein himself protested against it somewhere in the Investigations; she also said the conclusion about first-person knowledge doesn't follow at all. I can't adjudicate this myself. Could you go through the quoted paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a Wittgenstein scholar would accept, which are defensible simplifications, which are genuine misreadings (and what the corrected version would be), and — most importantly for our discussion — whether the book's striking conclusion about first-person knowledge survives once the errors are repaired? It would help if you flagged any places where the paragraph puts into Wittgenstein's mouth something he explicitly denied, and if you said where the beetle example really leaves the private object when the argument is stated correctly.
