I'm a third-year PhD student in machine learning, working on mechanistic interpretability, and my advisor has lately been building our group's research statement around an analogy I can't fully evaluate on my own: features in a trained network are like organs in an organism, in the specific sense that some philosophers of biology give to biological function. On that view, the heart has the function of pumping blood not merely because that's what it happens to do, but because pumping blood is what it was selected for over the lineage's evolutionary history. My advisor's transfer is this: gradient descent plays the role of natural selection, loss plays the role of fitness, and a "dog-head detector" feature in layer 11 has the function of detecting dog heads because detecting dog heads is what caused that direction to be retained and strengthened across training rather than washed out. Training, on this picture, is a period of intense selection pressure, and the trained network ends up full of well-adapted little organs — so that interpretability is anatomy, and a features-and-circuits writeup is a physiology paper.

He uses the analogy to defend a strong realist position in our internal debates: when we extract a feature and characterize what it responds to, we are not projecting a convenient story onto otherwise storyless weights; we are discovering what the features are for, in the same sense in which a physiologist discovers what the heart is for. Two of us in the group are uncomfortable with this and can't quite say why, and I've been assigned to write up the objection properly for our next reading group.

Please audit the analogy in whatever structure you find natural, though a correspondence table would help me organize the writeup. Some specific things I want covered. First, the mapping itself: what plays the role of natural selection, of fitness, of reproduction and heredity, of variation, of the population and the lineage, of drift, of exaptation, of spandrels and vestigial organs, of the selective environment. Second, where the mapping breaks: it seems to me that descent with modification requires a population of replicators and generations of them, whereas a single training run is one lineage, with no reproduction and no generations in the usual sense — the "population" is at best the ensemble of possible weight configurations explored by the optimizer, and I don't know whether that substitution is a technicality or a dissolution of the analogy. Relatedly, selection in training operates on whatever lowers loss on this dataset, so features adapted to spurious correlations — a "wedding" feature that is really a watermark feature, say — would come out with perfectly legitimate selected functions that are nothing like what we'd want to claim in an interpretability paper. Does the analogy then support realism about functions while quietly undermining our confidence that the function we read out is the function that's there? Third, there's a complication from our own subfield that my advisor glosses over: features under the superposition hypothesis are not discrete, non-overlapping organs; they're packed into the same neurons in an overlapping, interference-laden way, which looks less like anatomy and more like something the organ picture has no counterpart for. Does that break the analogy's structural claims, or is it just the organ of a creature we haven't met?

Finally, and most importantly: tell me whether the realism conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Please distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the realism claim from the ones that don't (I don't care, for instance, whether the selection is "blind" in any robust sense). If the conclusion survives, I'd like to know whether it survives because the analogy is good or because realism has independent support that the analogy was merely illustrating; if it doesn't, I'd like to know whether some repaired version of the function talk — a weaker, dispositional or causal-role notion, or a notion keyed to the training distribution rather than the evolutionary past — could still carry the weight my advisor wants it to carry. And if you think there's a better source domain than evolved organisms for making sense of what features are — artifacts with designers, trained athletes, riverbeds shaped by water — I'd like to hear it, with the same audit applied to it. I have a philosophy-of-science background from my undergraduate days but no one in my current program to check this against, so please be precise about the moving parts rather than gesturing at them.
