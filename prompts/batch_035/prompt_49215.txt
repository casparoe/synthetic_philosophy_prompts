So I've been turning something over for a while and finally wrote up my notes, half of which were supposed to become an essay, except now I can't tell if there's an actual argument in there or if I'm just being a crank about television. Hoping you can give it a read and tell me straight.

The notes are below. Background: I don't have any philosophy training, I just think about stuff on my commute. A while back I went down a rabbit hole about Rousseau, because I'd heard he was against theater -- like, famously against it, wrote a whole thing arguing Geneva shouldn't build a theater, at a time when every enlightened person thought theaters were obviously good for you. From what I remember his take was something like: watching sympathetic characters on stage lets you spend your compassion cheaply. You cry for the fictional orphan, feel like a wonderful generous person, and then walk right past your actual neighbor's actual problems. Also something about how theater is fine for big cities like Paris because those people are already lost to each other anyway, but a small republic runs on people actually being together, and a theater gives them a way to be "together" without touching each other. I might be mangling him, feel free to tell me if I am.

Anyway that snagged on something in my own life. My notes, more or less:

1. When I binge a show all weekend I come out of it feeling like I've spent time with people. The characters feel like friends. But obviously I haven't socialized at all, I've been alone on a couch for 11 hours. Is the feeling itself the harm? Like I've been fed the sensation of connection and it seems to take the edge off the need for the real thing?

2. Here's my own addition, which I haven't seen anywhere (maybe because it's dumb, you tell me): maybe what matters isn't what's on the screen at all, but the posture of being an audience. Whenever you're a spectator you're practicing being moved by people who are not there and cannot be affected by you. Empathy at a fixed distance, where nothing you feel can ever land anywhere. Do that enough and maybe the distance starts feeling normal even with people who ARE there.

3. Counterpoint to myself: I watch stuff with my wife, and my mom's book club is basically a TV club now, and those shared shows give us half our conversations. So spectatorship can clearly be social glue. Maybe the variable is alone vs. together, not watching vs. doing.

4. My current attempt to hold both: maybe watching is like a REHEARSAL of feeling vs. a DISCHARGE of feeling. It only goes bad when it substitutes for the real thing instead of warming you up for it. But then the question is what tells the difference, and honestly I can't answer that. Is it a time window? A check on whether you acted on the feeling within the week?

5. Worry: is this just puritanism with extra steps? People said the exact same stuff about novels ruining everyone, and about video games, and it keeps being wrong.

What I want from you: comments on whether the rehearsal/discharge distinction is a real distinction or just wordplay that sounds like one. Whether the "audience posture" idea in point 2 is interesting or already old news. Whether my Rousseau section is doing actual work or is just me borrowing his coolness, and whether I've got him roughly right or am putting words in his mouth. Also point out where the argument is weakest -- I'd rather fix it than defend it. And do you think it generalizes to stuff like sports fandom and doomscrolling the news, or does it only work for fiction?
