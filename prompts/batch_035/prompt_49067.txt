I read The Selfish Gene as a teenager and honestly I've never shaken it -- I think of natural selection as fundamentally acting on genes, with individual organisms as sort of disposable vehicles, and any trait as ultimately explainable by what it does for the copies of the genes that build it. My brother-in-law is a bio grad student and he rolls his eyes whenever I talk like this. He says gene-level selection is just a bookkeeping choice, that you can always redescribe organism-level or group-level selection in genic terms and vice versa, so arguing for "the" unit of selection is like arguing about whether a window broke because of the brick or because of the laws of physics. We had a long argument about this at thanksgiving and it kind of unsetttled me, because I realized I don't know what would actually change my mind on either side. Like, if someone showed strong group selection in the wild, I'd probably just say "fine, but that's still genes getting copied, from the gene's point of view" -- and maybe that means my view can't lose, which I gather is supposed to be a bad sign.

So here's what I want. Take it as given that I hold the gene's-eye view pretty firmly, and tell me what would count against it. Concrete stuff please -- actual kinds of findings, cases, or arguments, not just "group selection happens" as a slogan, since I've heard that a million times and always dismissed it as either super rare or secretly just kin selection in disguise. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with the most damaging one first and no intro or conclusion around them. For each one, be honest about whether a committed gene-selectionist like me would concede it counts against the view or would explain it away, and if you think the view has been softened so much over the years that nothing could ever refute it, make that one of the five points.
