I have been reading Less Wrong, Overcoming Bias, and their descendants for well over a decade, and I have noticed that our community has an official answer to the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, delivered with great confidence and almost never defended: dissolve the binary. "Belief" is a relic of an agent with limited channel capacity; the real state of a rational mind is a credence in [0,1], and once you say that, the paradoxes evaporate — of course you can assign 0.999 to each of a thousand ticket-losings and 0.999 to the conjunction being false somewhere, because numbers compose and "beliefs" don't. The literature, meanwhile, has been circling since Kyburg's lottery in 1961 and Makinson's preface in 1965: some restrict aggregation of rational acceptance (Kyburg himself), some reject the idea of rational acceptance altogether and go probabilist, some keep binary belief but tinker with thresholds or contextualism or paraconsistency, and some — the ones I find hardest to dismiss — insist that high credence just does suffice for rational outright belief, so the preface author's set of beliefs really is inconsistent and there is nothing much wrong with that.

Here is what I want diagnosed, and I want a verdict, not a survey. The two paradoxes have the same formal skeleton — each member of a large set is individually very likely, the full set cannot all be true — yet nearly everyone's intuitions run in opposite directions across them. In the lottery we are comfortable saying you should not believe of any particular ticket that it will lose; in the preface almost nobody wants to say the author should withhold belief from each carefully checked claim of their own book, since that would make scholarship impossible. If our judgments about the very same structure flip depending on whether the low-probability errors are independent by explicit randomization or by mere messy causation, that is either a deep datum about rational acceptance or a demonstration that our intuitions are tracking something other than rational acceptance (salience, blame, conversational propriety — I genuinely don't know which).

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the fifty-year impasse is mostly a verbal dispute between people who use "belief" for an action-guiding, all-or-nothing commitment and people who use it for a graded disposition, and the probabilist dissolution that my community treats as a knockdown win is actually a refusal to answer the question the binary-belief people were asking, namely what an agent with bounded time and a lossy channel should store and act on. If credences are the whole story, someone still has to say when the lossy discretization is done well, and that question is recognizably the same one Kyburg was asking. But I can also see the case that this is a substantive disagreement about whether rationality requires consistency at all, of the kind that no amount of formal machinery will settle.

So: give me your committed diagnosis. Is the standoff verbal, substantive, or a pile of unrelated squabbles under one name? Which of the three original principles — accept the very likely, reject the jointly inconsistent, aggregate the acceptable — should actually be revised, and what does the lottery/preface asymmetry in our intuitions tell us about which? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as real progress here, or make the case that the debate is doing permanent useful work precisely by refusing to resolve. Please take a definite position and defend it; do not lay out the options and leave me to choose, and do not default to the Bayesian dissolution just because it is locally fashionable — I have read that one a hundred times and I want to know whether you actually believe it.
