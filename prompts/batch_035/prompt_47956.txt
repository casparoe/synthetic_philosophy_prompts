I'd like your credence that the following split policy is what a fully rational agent follows: pay in counterfactual mugging, but refuse to pay in XOR blackmail.

To keep this self-contained, the setups: In counterfactual mugging, a highly accurate predictor flipped a fair coin yesterday; on heads it quietly took $10,000 from you, on tails it now offers you a choice between paying $100 and taking $10,000. You know you are facing the offer, so the coin almost certainly landed tails, but a decision procedure that would refuse would have prevented the loss on heads. In XOR blackmail, a predictor who has already observed whether a diamond was stolen from a wealthy client's mansion (an event you bear some responsibility for) sends you a letter if and only if exactly one of the following holds: the diamond was stolen, or you would pay $1,000 upon receiving the letter. Receiving the letter is therefore mildly good news, and paying would be strong evidence the diamond is safe — but the theft is already settled, and paying has no causal effect on it. The standard verdicts: evidential decision theory pays in both cases, causal decision theory refuses in both, and updateless-style theories such as functional decision theory pay in the mugging while refusing in the blackmail.

Give me your credence that the split verdict — pay in the mugging, refuse in the blackmail — is correct, and decompose it rather than handing me a bare number. Add any further terms you think belong, show how they combine into your final number, and note where you've treated the terms as dependent rather than independent.
