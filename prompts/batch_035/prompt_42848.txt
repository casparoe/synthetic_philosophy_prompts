"The paradox of deontology is this: deontological theories place constraints on what agents may do, like a constraint against killing the innocent. But sometimes violating the constraint once would prevent several other violations. For example, if I could kill one person to stop five other killings, the deontologist says I may not. So a deontological world will contain more killings than it needs to, which shows the theory is self-defeating: it makes the very thing it cares about (killings) more common than necessary. In fact it's a formal inconsistency in the theory, since it both says killings are wrong and says we shouldn't minimize how many killings occur.

The deontologist's standard response is that constraints are absolute: no amount of prevented violations can justify violating a constraint oneself. This blocks the paradox, because if the constraint can never be overridden, there is no question of minimizing violations, and the apparent conflict disappears. Kant held something like this: the categorical imperative forbids using people as a means, and killing one person to stop others uses them as a means, so its forbidden no matter what.

I think this response succeeds. The consequentialist assumes that if deaths are bad, agents should minimize deaths, but this just smuggles consequentialism in. The deontologist can also point to the doing/allowing distinction: when I kill, I do wrong; when others kill and I refuse to intervene, I merely allow. Since merely allowing a violation isn't itself a violation of the constraint, the deontologist who refuses to kill doesn't do anything wrong, and the 'extra' deaths are the killers' doing, not hers. So the theory isn't self-defeating after all."
