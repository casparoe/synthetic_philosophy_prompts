For some two thousand years a single charge has shadowed Madhyamaka: that its negations consume themselves. Nāgārjuna refuses all four corners of the tetralemma — the Tathāgata after death exists, does not exist, both, neither — and declares all things empty of intrinsic nature (svabhāva). Nyāya and Vedānta critics answered that this leaves him either holding a fifth position, "none of the four," which must itself be empty and so refutes nothing, or else asserting emptiness from a standpoint that is exactly the kind of grounded, intrinsically real assertion he denies is possible. The Madhyamaka reply is equally old: that emptiness being empty is not a concession but the point, since an emptiness with svabhāva would be just one more thing to be emptied; and that the negation is non-affirming, taking away without positing anything in place, so that it needs no ground to stand on. The exchange has been revived in modern dress as a puzzle about whether rejecting all four corners is itself a proposition with a truth value, a performative contradiction, a rejection of the question's shared presupposition, or a speech act aimed at attachment rather than belief — and as far as I can tell, nobody moves: each side restates its conception of what a negation requires, the other restates that this is precisely what is at issue, and the wheel turns again. My tentative hypothesis is that the dispute turns on a single unstated difference — whether intelligibility itself must be grounded in something un-negated — and that the two sides' verdicts on every concrete case follow from that difference rather than from any disagreement about the cases themselves. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says, and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: substantive, verbal (perhaps about the words "position" or "assertion"), or two distinct questions — one logical, one therapeutic — running under a single name. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress here: a formal treatment of the four corners, a sharp distinction between refusing a question and answering it negatively, a clarification of what svabhāva amounts to, or nothing short of one side abandoning the conception of negation with which it began.
