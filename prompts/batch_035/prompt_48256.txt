Please argue that in two-party bargaining problems where the feasible set is asymmetric — where one side's best attainable outcome substantially exceeds the other's — the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, rather than the Nash bargaining solution, should be the default solution concept for both predicting and prescribing the settlement.

Anchor the argument in a particular kind of change to the bargaining problem: an exogenous improvement in one party's prospects. Imagine two firms negotiating how to divide the surplus from a joint venture, and suppose that, shortly before the final round of talks, one of them gains access to a new and lucrative outside use of the resources it would contribute. This expands the feasible frontier in that party's direction — its ideal point, the best it could get in the bargaining region, rises — while the disagreement point and the remainder of the frontier stay roughly where they were. Under Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives axiom, the settlement is exactly what it would have been had the expansion never occurred; under Kalai–Smorodinsky's individual monotonicity condition, the settlement moves in favor of the party whose prospects improved. Argue that the Kalai–Smorodinsky response is the right one, and defend individual monotonicity against the most familiar objection to it: that IIA reflects a defensible kind of decision-theoretic consistency, since a bargainer whose preferences over the surviving alternatives are unchanged should not revise her choice merely because some alternatives have dropped away.

Along the way, address three further points.

First, make the strongest case you can for the Nash solution before answering it — in particular, its derivation as the unique solution satisfying a short list of compelling axioms — and then explain why that list loses its force in exactly the asymmetric settings at issue here. Note that both solutions coincide on symmetric problems, so your argument must do all of its work at the point where asymmetry enters; and be precise about what that asymmetry is, given that both solutions are invariant under positive affine transformations of utility. The asymmetry that matters must be a feature of the feasible set itself, not merely of how the parties' utilities happen to be scaled.

Second, take seriously a critic who objects that Kalai–Smorodinsky's reference point is a fiction. The solution works by measuring each party's share of its maximum possible gain over the disagreement point, where that maximum is computed while holding the other party at its disagreement payoff. But no rational bargainer actually aspires to an outcome that would require the other side's complete capitulation, and real bargainers often have only a dim sense of the frontier's extremes. Explain whether the solution's fairness rationale survives this complaint: is the ideal point a normatively meaningful benchmark, or merely a convenient mathematical construction whose plausibility is doing unearned work?

Third, consider a wrinkle the textbook treatment usually skips: what happens when it is the disagreement point, rather than the frontier, that moves — say the same new outside opportunity also improves one firm's fallback payoff. Discuss how each solution behaves under this second change, and whether the combination of the two changes makes the contrast between the solutions sharper or blurs it.

Conclude by stating clearly what class of bargaining situations, if any, would lead you to abandon the position and grant Nash's solution priority. Do not end on a merely non-committal note.
