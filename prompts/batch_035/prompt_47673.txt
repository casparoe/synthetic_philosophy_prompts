So I've been reading up on citizens' assemblies, the sortition-based mini-publics like the Irish one that dealt with abortion or the French climate convention with its 150 randomly drawn members, and I can't figure out what these things actually think legitimacy IS. Help me work out the implicit theory, because the pieces don't obviously fit together.

Look at the actual machinery. Members are drawn by lot from the population, but not by a pure lottery: organizers impose stratification quotas so the assembly roughly mirrors the country on gender, age, region, education, that kind of thing. Then a large chunk of the people who are drawn decline to serve, and they get replaced with other random draws from the same strata — so the final body is a random sample of people willing to give up their weekends for months, which is not a random sample of the country. Once seated, the members hear from experts who were chosen for them by an advisory board of academics and officials, the agenda and question put to them is fixed in advance, and professional facilitators structure how the discussion proceeds. And when it's all done, the assembly issues recommendations that elected politicians remain free to accept, modify, or quietly bury — the French case is the famous one, the president had promised to pass the proposals on unfiltered and then parliament kept only a fraction of them, and the body was disbanded.

Here's my puzzle. If the legitimacy of these assemblies comes from the lottery — they're a statistical mirror of the people, so their judgment is in some sense the people's judgment — then a bunch of the machinery is doing anti-mirror work: the quotas say a raw sample isn't good enough (so it's not really representativeness by chance that we trust), the self-selection through attrition skews toward the civic-minded (mirrors of the population don't volunteer), and the curated experts and facilitators mean the raw "voice of the people" is heavily stage-managed. But if the legitimacy instead comes from the quality of the deliberation — diverse ordinary people, properly informed and facilitated, reasoning together — then why insist on randomness at all? You could get informed, diverse, well-run deliberation from an application process, or from a panel of specially chosen good deliberators. And either way, why does the elected parliament keep the final word, if a thousand-something voters in a constituency are a much noisier and less informed signal about the public's considered will than a stratified, briefed, deliberating sample? The whole design seems to say "this body knows better than parliament what the people would think if they thought hard" and simultaneously "so obviously parliament stays in charge."

So: what view of democratic representation or legitimacy does this bundle of practices presuppose, if any? Maybe descriptive representation (the assembly mirrors the population), maybe something more like a trustee or proxy model (the assembly is what the people would conclude under good conditions, sort of an idealized snapshot of the general will), maybe it's just advisory input like any lobby group with nicer branding. Which parts of the machinery are strictly required by whichever view you reconstruct, and which parts are just pragmatic add-ons that several rival views could live with? Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined? And what would have to change — in the selection, in the agenda-setting, in the relationship to parliament — if you rebuilt the institution around one of the rival views, say a pure mirror view or a pure deliberative-quality view? I want to know if the current design is a coherent hybrid or just an unstable compromise between a distrust of politicians and a distrust of actually handing ordinary people power.
