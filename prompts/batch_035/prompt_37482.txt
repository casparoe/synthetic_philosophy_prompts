I serve on the terminology and standards committee of a mid-sized international human rights organization. We are finalizing the chapter of our annual report on a country whose government, over the past six years, has forcibly removed a linguistic and religious minority — call them the Krastavi — from their ancestral territory into designated relocation zones, closed their schools, banned their language from public life, demolished or repurposed their houses of worship and heritage sites, and subjected Krastavi women to coercive sterilization campaigns. There has been no mass killing: elevated deaths among the displaced stem from deprivation, exposure, and the denial of medical care rather than from executions. Our job at Thursday's meeting is to decide, once and for all, what our report will call this.

Three positions have hardened on the committee. Our legal director argues that the 1948 Genocide Convention is the controlling yardstick: genocide requires the specific intent to destroy a protected national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such, and the punishable acts it lists center on killing, serious bodily and mental harm, and measures intended to prevent births. Coercive sterilization plainly engages the last of these, but the rest of the pattern — expulsion, forced assimilation, cultural erasure — falls, on her reading, under the heading of ethnic cleansing and persecution, and she notes that the International Court of Justice's 2007 judgment in Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro treated systematic displacement and even atrocities as evidence that could support an inference of genocidal intent, without treating displacement itself as genocide. Her conclusion: we should not use the word, and its misuse could expose us to charges of partisanship and even undermine future prosecutions.

Our advocacy director answers that this gets Lemkin backwards. Lemkin's original conception, developed before and through the Convention's drafting, understood genocide as a two-pronged crime — the destruction of a group's physical existence and the destruction of its national, cultural, religious, and political life — and the drafting history records that provisions on cultural destruction and on political groups were deliberately excised as the price of adoption, largely for reasons of Cold War politics and colonial sensitivities. On his view, an organization that takes the Convention's narrowed text as settling the question has mistaken a political compromise for the concept itself; the Krastavi case is the paradigm of what Lemkin was trying to name, and a refusal to say so is itself a moral choice with consequences, given that naming a situation genocide signals to members, donors, and states that the duty to prevent is engaged.

A third bloc, mostly field staff, worries about inflation: the word's power depends on its scarcity, and we have already seen it used, in their view, rhetorically in ways that cheapen it for cases of systematic mass killing; they want us to coin or adopt a separate documented term for this class of atrocity and reserve genocide for the physical core.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action for the report's terminology, with the reasoning laid out so that the losing factions can see their strongest considerations taken seriously rather than dismissed. Along the way it should take a position on the underlying questions the committee keeps deferring: whether legal categories should govern a non-governmental organization's descriptive vocabulary at all; whether Lemkin's broader conception survives as a live moral category even where it is not a legal one; and whether the inflation worry is a claim about the word or about our credibility as its users. Please also include a short set of criteria that we could apply in future cases, so that we are not re-fighting this from scratch each year. Keep it to something the committee can actually read before the meeting — call it three pages.
