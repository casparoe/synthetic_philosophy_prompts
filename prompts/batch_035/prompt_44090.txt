I've been circling the Liar paradox for a couple of years now — LessWrong got me there via Gödel and Löb, and since then I've worked through the main theories of truth at the level an interested amateur can manage. There's a pattern I keep hitting that I can't find a name for, and the more I look, the more it seems like the central phenomenon rather than a side effect of particular proposals.

Every treatment of the Liar works by having the theory say something about the Liar sentence — that it's undefined, that it's both true and false, that its truth predicate lives at the next level up in a hierarchy. And then, as soon as you take the very resources the theory used to say that and point them at the saying itself, an isomorphic paradox pops up one level higher. The gap theorist declares the Liar undefined, but then can't, in the object language, endorse the claim that the Liar isn't true — the obvious-seeming statement of the diagnosis is exactly what the object language can't express without collapsing back into contradiction, so the diagnosis retreats to a metalanguage, where the same construction awaits. The both-true-and-false camp (Priest's) says the Liar is a glut, but the theory only stays consistent by denying that 'false' excludes 'true' in the pathological cases — while the whole motivation for calling them gluts leans on the ordinary, exclusive readings. Tarski-style hierarchies push the truth predicate up a level, but then there's no single sentence saying that every level's sentences get classical values at their own level; saying that needs a language above every level. And the shape is eerily familiar from Löb: a system that could certify its own reliability in its own vocabulary would thereby collapse.

So, questions. Does this recurring one-level-up reproduction have an established name in the truth literature? How is it supposed to be related to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem — is there an actual theorem that any expressively adequate, consistent treatment of its own truth regenerates the pattern, or is it only an argument shape that keeps succeeding case by case? And where are the edges: if a counterexample is built from brand-new vocabulary — some theorist's pet operator like 'really true' or 'has a settled value' — is that a genuine instance of the pattern, or just a fresh paradox about a fresh word, given that the theorist can disown the new vocabulary? I won't be satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, how it differs from mere self-reference, which theories it does and doesn't afflict, and — if the literature truly hasn't named it — a proposed term with a definition and boundary cases.
