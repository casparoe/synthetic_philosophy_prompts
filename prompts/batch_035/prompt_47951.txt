I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a feature on current theories of consciousness. For background I've been reading a popular book by a cognitive scientist, and in a chapter on the higher-order theories it offers this summary:

"Higher-order theories say that a mental state becomes conscious when, and only when, the mind takes notice of it. A pain is not conscious in itself; what makes it conscious is a further mental act — a thought about the pain — that turns back on it. So consciousness is essentially introspection: every conscious sensation is one we have observed ourselves having, and could in principle describe in a report. Because forming thoughts about one's own mind requires considerable conceptual sophistication, the theory also implies that animals and infants, which lack it, have no conscious states at all — their pains still occur, but they are felt by no one. In effect, the theory dissolves the hard problem by definition: instead of explaining why pain feels the way it does, it declares that feeling is nothing over and above thinking about a state — something a suitably programmed computer could manage just as well. That is why most researchers now regard higher-order theories as a philosophical dead end."

Before I lean on any of this in print, I'd like a careful audit of the passage. Please go through it claim by claim and sort the material into three categories: what a working defender of a higher-order theory would accept as a fair statement of the view; what such a defender would wince at as a simplification that obscures something the theory insists on; and what is straightforwardly a misrepresentation. I'd particularly like your judgment on the sentence that equates consciousness with introspection and reportability, on the claim about animals and infants, and on the closing charge that the theory solves the hard problem by definition. Finally, tell me whether the book's concluding verdict — that the theory explains nothing and has been abandoned by most researchers — follows from whichever parts of the summary are actually accurate. If some of the passage is right in ways a lay reader like me might not expect, I'd be grateful to know that too, since I don't want to correct an error that isn't one.
