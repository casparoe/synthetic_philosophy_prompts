Two people in my cohort got into a long argument at our reading group about what they kept calling "the presumption of independence," and since I'm the one writing this up for our notes, I'd appreciate a referee.

The setup they both accepted: when we can't prove two quantities are independent, we often just presume it — like treating the digits of sqrt(2) as random noise, or assuming two independently-written bugs in different modules won't fire at the same time, or assuming the errors in two different heuristics won't line up. The fight was over whether that presumption can be justified at all.

Marco's move was that it's not a bare assumption but an inference: if something passes every cheap test we can throw at it — no visible pattern, no shared cause we can identify — that's genuine evidence of random-ish behavior. He compared it to symmetry arguments in physics, which everyone accepts: we presume nothing breaks the symmetry unless we see a reason, and that's treated as legitimate reasoning, not faith.

Jules said heuristic tests are exactly the wrong instrument, because the world contains things built to pass them. A pseudorandom generator's output looks independent under every cheap test, yet it's fully correlated with the seed — so "it passes our tests" is compatible with "some observer who knows the trick can predict it perfectly." For Jules the presumption is only safe when nobody has adversarially chosen the example, and we usually can't tell whether that's the case.

Marco replied that this proves too much: by Jules' standard statistical mechanics collapses too, since a demon could always have arranged the microstate. He said the right standard is "no correlation findable by us," not "no correlation, period," and that once you accept that, the presumption becomes a perfectly good inductive practice.

Jules said "findable by us" isn't stable — it changes depending on who's doing the finding and how much compute they have — so Marco's standard amounts to "our ignorance of correlations is evidence against them," which Jules compared to looking for your keys under the streetlight and concluding the darkness is safe.

They stopped for coffee before resolving anything and I'd definately like to hand them something at the next meeting.
