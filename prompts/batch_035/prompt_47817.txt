I'm a novelist, and my current book is about two sisters in their seventies who've shared a house for forty-five years — one nursed the other thru cancer, they pool their finances, they're each other's next of kin in every way that matters. A lawyer friend told me that if they were a couple, they'd get the whole marriage package: survivor's pension, inhertiance tax breaks, hospital next-of-kin rights. As sisters they get none of it, and no contract fully replicates it. So here's what I keep circling and can't land: now that same-sex marriage is legal in a lot of places, the old "marriage exists for procreation" answer is officially dead. So what does marriage law, as it actually stands, implicitly presuppose the institution is FOR? I can think of candidates — a state-certified romance, a caregiving pact, chosen kinship — but each gives me a different plot for the sisters, honestly. If it's about care, the sisters seem robbed; if it's about romance, the state is in the business of blessing love affairs, which is strange; if it's about chosen family, why exactly two, and why not the sisters? Tell me which theory the legal package really presupposes (as opposed to merely fits), where the pieces are inconsistent, and what the sisters' case exposes that the same-sex marriage debate didn't. No citations or case law, I just want the conceptual structure. At the very end, give me a one-line answer in the form \answer{...} — three or four words naming the theory of marriage's purpose the law actually operates on. That goes in my notebook.
