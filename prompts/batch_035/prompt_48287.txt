I'm a graduate student writing a paper defending the view that Dutch book arguments do real justificatory work: that synchronic vulnerability to a sure-loss book establishes the probability axioms as norms of rational credence, and that the diachronic extension establishes conditioning as the norm for updating. My advisor regards this as a defensible position circa 1950 and a museum piece now, and I want to know whether he's right. Specifically, I'd like a ranked map of what counts against my view, from fatal through serious to merely awkward, with the objections that committed proponents themselves concede (I gather many Bayesians grant that the diachronic argument is weaker than the synchronic one) clearly separated from the ones only critics press.

The things I'm aware of and want assessed honestly: the pragmatic-epistemic gap — the argument seems to show I'm exploitable, not that I'm incoherent in any truth-connected sense, and I want to know whether the depragmatizing moves (recasting the vulnerability as a kind of inconsistency among contents rather than among bets) retain anything of the original book or just relabel it; the suspicion that the sure-loss theorem is a piece of arithmetic, so that violating the axioms guarantees a book only because of how credences are stipulated to map onto odds, leaving it unclear what epistemic work the gambling table is doing; the diachronic objections — that the bookie needs access to my future credences, that my later self arguably faces a separate budget and a separate rationality, that Jeffrey conditioning and other updates slip past the diachronic book, and whether van Fraassen's Reflection-style principles patch this or make it worse; and the escape routes of refusing to bet, hedging, lexicographic or intransitive preferences, and the bet-size restriction that "sufficiently small stakes" papers over. For each of these, tell me whether the preference-based reformulations, where the agent is confronted with choices among acts rather than offered bets, genuinely close the gap or quietly change the subject from credence to choice.

Finally, the falsifiability question. If the Dutch book argument is doing real justificatory work, my view should predict something that its rivals don't — some difference in how agents who satisfy versus violate the norms are evaluable, some case where the argument's presence or absence matters. Is there anything like that, or has the view been qualified into insulation ("it's a toy model, but it points at something deeper")? And if it has, say whether that insulation is a defect for a justification of this kind or just the ordinary fate of idealized models. Conclude with the ranking, and be honest if the right verdict is that the synchronic half survives while the diachronic half should be cut loose — I would rather learn that now than in my advisor's office.
