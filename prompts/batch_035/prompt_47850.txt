I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, thesis on distributive justice, and I keep circling back to the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism and I've basically concluded the literature on it froze around 1984. The shape of it: utilitarianism lets losses to one person be offset by gains to another, but there is no aggregate subject whose life goes well or badly — there are just separate persons, each living exactly one life — so trading my losses against your gains is supposedly a different kind of thing from trading a bad decade in my own life against the rest of my life being good. Rawls pressed a version of this when setting up his argument against the classical principle of utility, Nozick made it vivid with the utility monster and the fear-of-experiment case, and then Parfit, in Reasons and Persons, argued that personal identity is less deep than we assume, which was meant to shrink the moral distance between persons and take the sting out of the objection. Since then, as far as I can tell, nothing has moved. Utilitarians either say separateness is just a fact about where experiences happen to be bundled and carries no moral weight, or they grant it some weight and say consequences swamp it anyway. The critics keep repeating that cross-person compensation is different in *kind*, and keep illustrating with the same three cases from 1974.

I have a hypothesis about why it's stuck and I want it tested, not a verdict on utilitarianism. My hypothesis: the two camps are answering different questions and neither notices. The critics are making a claim about the *form* of moral reasons — about what can stand in a compensating relation to what — while the utilitarians are making a claim about which states of affairs we ought to bring about. If that's right, the debate presents as substantive but never generates a case either side would have to concede, which would explain the fifty-year stalemate better than "it's just hard" does.
