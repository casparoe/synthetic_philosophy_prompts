Something I keep noticing and I can't work out if it has a proper name or if I'm just pattern-matching badly. In a certain kind of contest, the rules seem to be: whoever holds on longest collects the prize, but everybody who's in keeps paying the whole time, and — this is the bit that makes it weird — if you give up, your losses don't just sit there as wasted money, they effectively convert into the winner's trophy.

My brother-in-law runs charity fundraisers, and one of his games is auctioning off a £20 note where the runner-up ALSO has to pay their final bid (he puts it toward the charity, so nobody feels too robbed). He says it regularly goes past £50. I first thought oh, that's just people being irrational, but then I started seeing the same shape everywhere. Two people in a facebook argument going at it for six hours over something trivial — the "prize" is basically being visibly right, and the first one to stop replying is treated as having conceded, so all their invested hours just get handed to the other person as a win. My manager and a colleague once fought for two weeks by email over a £60 expense claim that both of them were surely spending more than £60 of salary on. Custody disputes where both parents torch the college fund fighting over who gets the kids' weekends. Even arguments in my pub quiz team about whether to challenge an answer, where nobody can back down without the other side getting the point.

I know the sunk cost fallacy, but that only seems to cover half of it. Sunk cost is about me and my past costs dragging me forward — but in the cases I'm describing the competitive bit is doing real work: the reason I can't walk away isn't just my own wasted effort, it's that walking away actively hands the prize to somebody who has ALSO been paying, and everybody watching knows it. And it's not a straightforward zero-sum contest either, because both parties end up down in total, only one of them collects anything. Brinkmanship also comes to mind but that feels like it's about the risk of mutual destruction, whereas here the mutual loss isn't a risk, it's the default that happens every time.

So, questions: does this exact structure have an established name, and if so, how do people who study it carve it off from plain sunk cost reasoning, from winner-take-all competition, and from brinkmanship? If it doesn't have a name of its own — or if the name only covers the auction version and not the flame war / lawsuit versions — then I'd want you to say so, and coin a term that covers the whole family, with a proper definition and some attention to where the edges are. Like: does it still count if the loser only pays half their bid? Does it still count with three bidders, where quitting benefits two rivals at once? Does it count when there's no audience, since in the flame war cases it feels like the spectators are what makes quitting so costly? I'm not after just a label, I want to know what the thing actually is and isn't.
