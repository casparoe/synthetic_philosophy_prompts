Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent 39 years in fluid dynamics, retired in 2019, and since then I've been making a nuisance of myself in a philosophy reading group. We've been arguing about AI welfare and machine consiousness, and I wrote up an argument at the last meeting that everyone hated but nobody could kill. It seems to prove way too much, and I want to know exactly where it breaks.

(1) Whether a system can suffer depends only on the computation it performs, not on what it's made of — a silicon system running the right computation hurts just as much as a brain does. (2) A computation is an abstract pattern of state-transitions, specified formally. (3) Whether a physical system implements a given abstract pattern is a matter of mapping physical states onto formal ones. (4) Given any physical system with enough distinguishable states — a cliff face weathering, the North Pacific in November — and given freedom in choosing the mapping, you can make its actual sequence of state-transitions correspond one-to-one with the transitions of a simulation of a person in pain. (5) So under that mapping, the cliff instantiates suffering. (6) So rocks can be tortured. (7) That's crazy; therefore (1) is false, and substrate matters morally after all.

Here's my problem. My whole career was gerrymandering messy continua onto clean grids, and my gut says the catch is somewhere in (3) and (4): the mapping is something I brought to the system, and if I'd chosen a different one I'd have gotten a different computation out of the same cliff, so surely not every mapping counts as the system really implementing something. But a guy in my group pushed back: if you demand the mapping respect the system's own counterfactuals — that it would have transitioned differently had the formal state been different — you've only saved the cliff by admitting that plenty of dumb dynamical systems implement simple pain-like computations, so you've handed moral status to thermostats and tide gauges and just moved the absurdity somewhere less visible.

I'd truely rather keep (1) than give it up — substrate-independence seems like the only thing that makes the machine-suffering question tractable at all — but I can't believe every cliff on the coast has a moral life either. So tell me: which step do I reject, why did that step look fine to begin with, and what does rejecting it cost me? Keep your whole answer under 450 words.
