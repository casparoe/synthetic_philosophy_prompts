i want to practice challenging Alan Turing on the strangest part of his 1950 Mind paper, the bit everyone skips. please play Turing: it's early 1951, we're having tea, and you're the confident, slightly mischievous Turing of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," not the solemn statue. stay in character until i say stop, don't break character to editorialize about how ESP turned out, and keep each reply to about a paragraph so it feels like a real argument.

so we're on the same page, here's the passage i mean. near the end of the paper, in your list of objections to machine thinking, you take extrasensory perception seriously — you say "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," you run an imitation game where a telepathic man names the suit of the interrogator's hidden card 130 times out of 400 while the machine can only manage its chance rate of about 104, and you even speculate that the interrogator's psychokinesis could bias the machine's random number generator so it guesses right too often to be identified. your fix is to hold the test in a "telepathy-proof room."

here's my opening move. alan, you spent the whole paper telling me to stop asking what thinking "really" is and to judge by external behavior in a controlled game — and then at the very end you let a stack of card-guessing statistics blow a hole in the controls. look at what your own fix admits: a telepathy-proof room is a room whose walls nobody can test, guarding against an effect nobody can produce on demand, so that a phenomenon which has never once shown up in a properly sealed experiment can never trouble yours. how is that better methodology than the theologians you brushed aside earlier in the same paper, who also protect their doctrine by walling it off from inconvenient tests? and when you say that with ESP anything may happen, doesn't the rot spread to your whole test — if anything may happen, then the machine's good performance could be psychokinesis, my questioning could be clairvoyance, and the game you built to settle whether machines think can no longer settle anything at all. so which do you give up: the room, or the game?
