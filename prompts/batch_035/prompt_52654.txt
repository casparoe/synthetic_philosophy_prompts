My philosophy reading group is doing anthropics next month and I want a handout for it. Write a dialogue between two friends -- one committed to the self-sampling assumption (SSA), the other to the self-indication assumption (SIA) -- who get into it while waiting for the results of a new particle physics experiment to come in.

Here's the setup I want them arguing over (basically a version of the presumptuous philosopher): the experiment will settle which of two cosmological theories is true, T1 or T2, where T1 says our universe contains about a billion civilizations and T2 says about a trillion. The physicists' pre-experiment odds are 50/50 based on the physics alone. Just before the results are announced, the SIA friend declares that she already knows it's T2 -- she puts the odds at about a thousand to one, straight from her armchair, just from noticing that she exists. The SSA friend is scandalized.

A few requirements:

- The SIA defender needs to make her real case, not a caricature: that treating your own existence as evidence is just ordinary conditionalization on something you already know, and that it's her friend's view, not hers, that licenses doing cosmology from your armchair. To that end she should press the SSA-er on the Doomsday-flavored commitment baked into his view -- if you reason as if randomly sampled from your reference class, then finding ourselves so early in human history is bad news about how long humanity has left.
- The SSA defender shouldn't be a pushover either. He should push on what exactly "finding that I exist" is supposed to be evidence *for* -- anyone asking the question had to exist -- and he should get to construct a case where SIA-style reasoning seems to license accepting absurd bets, making her squirm a little.
- Genuine back-and-forth please: each one concedes something along the way. She should admit the presumptuousness sting doesn't fully go away just because the math is clean, and he should own up to the reference-class problem -- that his view needs a story about who counts as being in the same reference class, and that the answer changes depending on where you draw the line.
- End with the experiment result coming back T1, and have both of them claim vindication in character -- he crows that the armchair cosmology just failed its first test, she shrugs that a thousand-to-one favorite loses sometimes and the odds were still right. Let the ending be wry rather than some tidy "one side wins."

Aim for maybe 700-900 words of dialogue. Casual register, they can interrupt each other, no lecturing monologues, and no formulas beyond rough odds spoken in plain English. Don't have either of them name-drop specific books or papers -- I want it to read like two smart friends arguing, not a lit review.
