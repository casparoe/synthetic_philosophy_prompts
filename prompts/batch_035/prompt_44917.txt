I teach a survey course in philosophy of mind, and in the opening weeks I like to reach back before Chalmers and Nagel to give students a sense of how old the "hard problem" really is. This year I've put a passage from Leibniz's Monadology (1714) on the syllabus, and I'd like to make sure my own reading of it is sharper than it currently is before I walk into the classroom, since my training is mainly in ethics and I've never worked through the Leibnizian corpus systematically. The passage in question, from section 17, reads as follows:

It must be confessed, moreover, that perception, and that which depends on it, are inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is, by figures and motions, And, supposing that there were a mechanism so constructed as to think, feel and have perception, we might enter it as into a mill. And this granted, we should only find on visiting it, pieces which push one against another, but never anything by which to explain a perception. This must be sought, therefore, in the simple substance, and not in the composite or in the machine.

There are two things about this passage that I'd genuinely appreciate help thinking through, since I want to present it to undergraduates as a live argument rather than a museum piece. First, I'm unsure what kind of argument this is supposed to be. The mill image is obviously vivid, but is Leibniz offering something like an argument from conceivability — that we can coherently conceive of all the pushing and grinding without any perception being present, so perception can't be constituted by it — or is he instead making a claim about the limits of explanation, i.e., that mechanical descriptions, however complete, simply never mention the perceiver's point of view? These feel like importantly different arguments, and I can imagine a contemporary philosopher pressing each version against functionalism and against identity theory respectively. Second, the closing inference puzzles me: from the premise that perception is not explicable "in the composite or in the machine," Leibniz concludes that it must be sought "in the simple substance." Even if I grant the mill observation, why should the failure of mechanical explanation point specifically toward perceiving simples rather than, say, toward some further fact about the composite as a whole, or toward our explanatory frameworks simply being inadequate? A student of mine raised exactly this worry in office hours, noting that modern emergentists seem to deny precisely the inference from "not explicable in the parts" to "located in a simple," and I didn't have a confident answer for her. Could you help me reconstruct the reasoning as Leibniz would have understood it, and also tell me where, in your judgment, the argument is most vulnerable — where exactly does the analogy between the mill and the brain do more work than it is entitled to?
