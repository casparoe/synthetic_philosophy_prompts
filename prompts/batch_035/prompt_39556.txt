I keep going back and forth on when someone is truly responsible for what they do, so I tried just writing down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases to see if there's a pattern:

- Drunk driver who kills a pedestrian: fully to blame, and even more so because he chose to get behind the wheel.
- A man who starts assaulting people because a tumor is pressing on his brain: not to blame at all, though obviously he still needs to be stopped.
- A woman who was horrifically abused as a child and has grown into a genuinely cruel adult: I want to blame her less than an ordinary cruel person, but I can't get all the way down to zero, which already feels inconsistent of me.
- A guy who, as a teenager, coldly and soberly decided to become a ruthless person, and then actually pulled it off: somehow feels MORE blameworthy than someone who just drifted into being ruthless, even though the decision itself came from how he already was at fifteen.
- A psychopath who literally cannot grasp that other people's suffering matters: I want to say not blameworthy, but I also want to blame him more than the tumor guy, and I can't justify that to myself.

My tentative principle is something like: you're responsible only if the character flaws behind your act trace back, at some point, to a choice. But then I read about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and if I'm understanding it right it goes roughly: you act the way you do because of how you are, you can only be responsible for what flows from how you are if you're responsible for how you are, and you never can be, because how you are came from upbringing, genes, luck — and even if you try to reshape yourself, the reshaping is done by a self you didn't make, so the regress never bottoms out in a self that made itself. Conclusion: nobody is ultimately responsible for anything.

Here's my problem. I can't find a principle that saves all my verdicts, and the Basic Argument looks like it forces me to throw out nearly all of them, since in every case the causes trace back to something unchosen eventually, the teenage decider included. So: is there a principle that captures the pattern of my verdicts, maybe by weakening what responsibility requires so it stops demanding an impossible self-made self? Or does the Basic Argument just win, and I have to overturn some verdicts — and if so, which one's go and which survive, if any? I'd also like to know what your principle says about a case I haven't listed: someone who does one terrible thing in a state of extreme sleep deprivation that they never would have done rested, and who is horrified by it afterwards.
