I am the teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on distributive justice that has just finished Parfit's "Equality or Priority?" Delivered in the last session, the disagreement between the telic egalitarians and the prioritarians in the room turned out to be mostly verbal: everyone said they cared about "the worse off," and no one could say what would make them come apart. For the final session I want an instrument that sorts people by what they actually believe about cases, not by which label they prefer.

Please design a decision tree of positions on the leveling-down question — whether inequality is bad in itself, or whether the worse off simply matter more — with the following requirements.

**Construction order.** Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of positions: first fix the branching questions, then locate the actual positions as leaves, then diagnose the combinations of answers that no one occupies (is the combination incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?).

**Node requirements.** Every internal node must be a concrete case that a person can answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node may ask "are you a prioritarian?" or use any of the position names. At minimum the tree must include nodes that turn on the following cases (you may add up to two more of your own devising):

1. **Pure leveling down.** Two people, one sighted and one blind; a change that blinds the sighted one, benefiting no one and raising no one's welfare, but narrowing the gap. Is the outcome in any respect better?
2. **A gap with no loser.** Two equally well-off people; one gets an unforeseeable windfall. Nobody's welfare level falls, but inequality appears. Did the outcome get worse in any respect?
3. **The source of the gap.** Two societies with identical welfare distributions: in one the inequality tracks a hereditary caste system, in the other it tracks freely chosen, differently risky hobbies. Does the difference between them matter, or is only the distribution itself relevant?
4. **Above the threshold.** Two people both comfortably above any decent sufficiency level, one slightly better off than the other; a small benefit can go to the slightly worse off. Does the residual gap matter at all here, or is it no more regrettable than any other tiny inequality?
5. **Absolute depth versus comparative position.** A tiny benefit available to the very worst-off person, versus a large benefit to a second person who is only marginally better off. Does only the worst-off person's claim count here (however small the benefit), or does the size of the benefit eventually outweigh the difference in depth?

**Leaves.** At least these must appear as named leaves, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment: telic egalitarianism, prioritarianism (weighted), leximin, sufficientarianism, utilitarianism, relational or deontic egalitarianism, and at least one hybrid or moderate view (e.g., egalitarianism constrained by a person-affecting or no-proirity-to-priority proviso). You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them, including an "unnamed" leaf where nobody in fact occupies a coherent combination.

**Honesty about the tree's limits.** Flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate — in particular, consider whether pure relational egalitarianism and utilitarianism give the same answers on every purely distributive case, and whether any case-based instrument can ever split them, or whether they differ only when the cases concern relations between people rather than distributions over them. Do not paper over such failures; state them as limitations of the instrument.

**Format.** Return the result as a single JSON object with the following schema, and nothing outside it except a two- or three-sentence cover note explaining any limitations you could not represent in the schema:

- `"root"`: string, the id of the root node.
- `"nodes"`: array of objects, each with fields `"id"` (string), `"type"` (either "case" or "leaf"), and, for cases, `"case_text"` (the concrete scenario, self-contained, no position names), `"options"` (an array of objects each with `"answer"` — a short first-person reply a respondent might naturally give — and `"goto"` — the id of the next node), and `"commentary"` (one or two sentences on what this node is doing); for leaves, `"position"` (the name), `"awkward_commitment"` (one sentence), and `"diagnosis"` (one sentence on why someone lands here).
- `"inseparable_pairs"`: array of objects with `"positions"` (array of two names) and `"why"` (a sentence, e.g., identical answers on every case available to the instrument, differing only on relational cases it cannot represent).
- `"unoccupied"`: array of objects with `"answer_combination"` (described in words), `"verdict"` (one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "unexplored"), and `"comment"` (one sentence).

Keep the total to roughly six to eight internal nodes and seven to ten leaves. The respondents will be graduate students who have read Parfit but nothing else in the literature, so every case must be answerable from the scenario alone; the tree will be administered on paper, so each `"case_text"` should be no more than about 60 words.
