You are a philosopher of cognitive science who, over the years, has quietly prepared dozens of speakers for hostile question periods at workshops and conferences; you are good at predicting what a *specific* room, rather than a generic audience, will ask. I need that skill now, and I want you to think carefully before writing — first work out what each faction in my audience actually believes, then derive their questions from those beliefs rather than from a list of stock objections to creativity research.

I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. In three weeks I give the opening talk at a small two-day workshop on models of creativity, and I need to survive the question period. My thesis comes from Douglas Hofstadter's essay "Variations on a Theme as the Crux of Creativity": genuinely creative acts are not bolt-from-the-blue recombinations but disciplined variations on a theme the mind holds steady while turning a small number of knobs on it — the way one hears a Chopin mazurka as a recognizable family member of a hundred others, or the way a strong analogy slips just a few things (in his picture, concepts are like stars with orbits, and twiddling a knob carries you a short distance along an orbit, where you can be captured by a concept you never set out to visit). My claim, as a non-specialist extending Hofstadter, is that the crux of creativity is not generating novelty at random but knowing which knobs your culture's themes expose, and that this explains why creative leaps so often feel like discovering something that "was there all along" rather than like blind search.

The audience is about forty people, and I know its composition: a majority of empirical creativity researchers in the Darwinian tradition, for whom Campbell's blind-variation-and-selective-retention is the founding charter and Dean Simonton's formalization the working reference point — several of them will have spent careers defending the claim that creative variations must be *blind* to their eventual value; a contingent of computational-creativity researchers who build generative systems and believe a model without an implementation is just prose; two literary scholars invited as a courtesy, who find the entire knob vocabulary tone-deaf to what artists do; and one cognitive scientist who spent time in Hofstadter's own research group and will be listening for where I vulgarize him. They all know I am a journalist. More than a few will suspect my talk is metaphor stacked on metaphor with no testable content, and at least one will say so in those words.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. It should contain:

1. The seven or so objections this room will actually raise, ranked by how much damage each would do to my talk — not by how philosophically interesting they are — each phrased the way the relevant faction would phrase it (an empirical researcher's phrasing is not a computationalist's, and neither is a literary scholar's).
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in about a minute at the podium, plus a candid note on where that reply is soft and what it would take to shore it up.
3. A short list of the points I should concede up front rather than fight for — I already suspect "knob" lacks an operational definition, and that the best-known implementation of this research program, the Copycat architecture, never escaped the microworld of letter-string analogies, which will not be lost on the computationalists.
4. The single question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — the one that would turn my own thesis against me — and what to do with my face and my voice if someone asks it anyway.

Keep the whole prep sheet under 1,000 words. Prioritize ruthlessly: if you must cut, cut philosophy I can improvise and keep the things only you could have told me.
