I teach a philosophy of science elective that spends two weeks on evolutionary psychology — the "is it science or is it just-so stories" fight. The class is about 25 students, mostly second-years, one 80-minute session, and they've already read the pop version of the debate, so they know the *words* but I don't think they've ever felt the actual problem. They can recite "an explanation that can explain anything explains nothing" and then, five minutes later, produce a fluent adaptive explaination for whatever I put in front of them. That's the gap I want the activity to hit.

I've been toying with a structure and I want you to either make it work or tell me it doesn't and design something better. The sketch: I hand out "findings" that I invent beforehand — plausible-sounding stuff with fake specificity, like "in a survey of 1,800 adults, eldest children were 14% more likely than youngest children to say they'd stay in an unhappy relationship for the kids' sake" — and small groups get ten minutes to produce the best adaptive explanation they can for each one, where "best" is scored by the other groups voting (no real money; design some token payoff scheme). The twist I'm hoping will land: half the room gets each finding stated one way and half gets it with the direction flipped — the other half reads that eldest children were 14% *less* likely to stay — and nobody knows the room is split. My bet is both halves end up equally confident with equally good stories. Then the reveal.

What I need from you:

- The full runnable design: rules, timing across the 80 minutes, what I have to prep, how many findings to use (work out a couple more invented examples concretely, since I'll just steal yours), and the voting/payoff mechanics.
- The debrief: map what actually happened onto the real distinctions — post-hoc pliability vs. making a risky prediction in advance, and the fact that "alternative explanation" (byproduct, drift, constraint) is a live option in the actual literature and not just a dunk. The students should come out seeing that the problem isn't that adaptive explanations are unscientific per se; it's that *these* ones earned nothing.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time i run it — I'm guessing at least one group smells the trick, or the stories come out so lazy that nobody's impressed by their own work — and how I should handle each.
