I'm preparing a handout for an undergraduate discussion section on the philosophy of death, centered on Samuel Scheffler's book Death and the Afterlife. Scheffler's guiding observation is that even people who expect no personal survival nonetheless assume what he calls a collective afterlife: that humanity will continue after they die. To expose this assumption, he offers two cases. In the doomsday scenario, you will live out your natural life, but thirty days after your death everyone else dies as well. In the infertility scenario, no new children will ever be born, but everyone now alive lives out a full lifespan. His afterlife conjecture is that in either case much of what we currently value — scientific research, political causes, building institutions, even the enjoyment of music — would come to seem pointless to us in advance, and that this reveals a dependence of our present values on the assumed survival of humanity.

I would like you to write a decision tree for the handout that starts from the question "Is there anything bad about human extinction beyond the deaths it involves?" and routes each student to a position. Please satisfy the following constraints:

1. Every branch node must be a concrete case or hypothetical that a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to. No node may ask which label or theory the student endorses. Along the way, the tree should test at least the following axes, using whatever cases you devise: (a) whether the residual badness of extinction survives if one will never learn of it, versus only if one falsely believes it is coming; (b) whether it matters who the future people are — whether a stable population of strangers in distant nations satisfies the relevant dependence, or whether cultural or national continuity with one's own community is required; (c) whether the dependence runs through one's own present projects and their conditions of meaningfulness, or through the loss of valuable experiences the future would have contained regardless of anyone's projects; and (d) whether the dependence is forward-looking only, or whether similarly weighty, non-death-related losses in the past would also register as bad.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the actual positions within it. The leaves should include at least: the strict addition-only view on which extinction is nothing over and above the particular deaths it comprises; Scheffler's own afterlife conjecture; a meaning-of-life or legacy view on which what is lost is the point of our ongoing projects; and an impersonal-value view on which what is lost is the valuable future itself, quite apart from its effects on present valuers. Additional leaves are welcome.

3. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, rather than inventing a diagnostic question that only appears to distinguish them.

4. Identify at least one answer combination that the tree permits but that corresponds to no position anyone actually holds, and say in one or two sentences whether the vacancy strikes you as a sign of incoherence, of unattractiveness, or simply of unexplored territory.

5. Aim for eight to ten branch nodes and no more than seven leaves, and keep the whole instrument to a length a student could complete in about five minutes. The point, throughout, is to sort students by what they say about the cases, not by which position they like the sound of.
