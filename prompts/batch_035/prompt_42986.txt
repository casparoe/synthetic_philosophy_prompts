Hey — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm working on a piece about how the League of Nations gets read as a Kantian experiment that failed. Everyone writes that story, but I want the actual mapping done carefully for once, because I suspect the usual version is sloppy.

So here's the ask. You're a professional philosopher specializing in Kant's political philosophy, and I want you to analyze the founding and early collapse of the League of Nations (roughly 1919 through the Manchuria and Abyssinia crises) through the lens of Kant's essay on perpetual peace. Think carefully before answering, and maybe start with a short brainstorm of the mapping before committing to the analysis.

Some facts to anchor the case, so we don't drfit: the League was created at the 1919 Paris Peace Conference, its Covenant was folded into the Versailles Treaty, decisions in the Council required unanimity, there was no standing force and enforcement relied on member states volunteering, the US Senate refused to ratify so America never joined, Germany was excluded at first and only admitted in 1926, Japan walked out after its 1931 invasion of Manchuria went unpunished, and Italy left after the weak response to its 1935 invasion of Abyssinia. The mandates system put the former colonial territories of the defeated empires under the supervision of the victorious powers.

Now the mapping I want. Kant's preliminary articles include the ones against standing armies, against war-driven national debt, against interference in other states' constitutions and internal affairs, and against peace treaties that quietly reserve the matter for a later war. His definitive articles call for republican constitutions in each state, a federation of free states rather than a single world state, and a cosmopolitan right of hospitality. Go article by article: which features of the League and of the settlement it was embedded in satisfied each, which violated it, and which simply had no counterpart. I'm especially interested in whether the Versailles settlement — the reparations, the war-guilt clause, the dictated character of the treaty — should be read as exactly the kind of peace treaty Kant warned against, one that plants the next war inside itself, and whether the mandates system is straight-up interference in other states' affairs dressed up as tutelage.

Then be honest about where Kant breaks as an explanatory model. If republican constitutions plus a pacific federation are supposed to do the work, the glaring anomaly is that the great republic of the age sat the whole thing out, while a bunch of constitutional monarchies and a French republic that was exhausted did the joining — and the unanimity rule meant the federation had no teeth anyway. Is that a problem for Kant, or was Kant never talking about an organization like the League in the first place — is his foedus pacificum not a proto-UN at all but something much looser, more like a standing disposition among states than an institution with a headquarters? And then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory — a confirmation (no genuinely Kantian state of affairs ever existed, so no surprise it failed), an anomaly, or a case his framework just never anticipated? Don't force the theory to speak where it's silent. If you think a later development, like the empirical democratic-peace finding, explains the episode better than Kant does, say so and defend it — but keep the two claims clearly seperate, one about Kant and one about what actually happened.

Last thing: no reading lists, no citations, and don't put anything in quotation marks as Kant's words — paraphrase him in your own words throughout. I can't have you inventing quotes, and I can't check them.
