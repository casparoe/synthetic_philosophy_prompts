I'm a PhD student working on collective memory stuff (dissertation is on transitional justice archives, fun times) and I've somehow ended up as the grad rep on my university library's digital collections advisory board. Next month we have to decide a case that is basically my dissertation topic dropped in my lap, except now it's real and I have to write the actual memo we circulate, and I'd love a draft to work from.

The case: in the early 90s, researchers from our anthropology department recorded about 80 oral history interviews with survivors and witnesses of a village massacre that happened during a civil conflict abroad. The interviews are graphic and detailed, and people gave them on the understanding that they were for the historical record. In 2015 the collection was digitized and put online with full public access, transcripts included. Last year, an association representing the descendants of the victim community (some of whom are now our students) formally asked us to take the collection offline entirely. Their position is basically: the town has been defined by this event for two generations, they're trying to build a normal civic life, and a foreign university profiting (academically speaking) from their worst day, searchable by anyone, keeps the wound open. A group of historians and one of the original researchers have pushed back hard: this is primary testimony of an atrocity that official histories in that country still minimize, and removing it is a gift to denialists. There's also a middle option that's been floated -- keep it offline but accessible on-site by appointment, or redact the transcripts of living interviewees.

So: draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action (full removal, tiered access, redaction, keep as-is, whatever you think is right), with a timeline, and reasoning the losing side could actually engage with rather than feel steamrolled by. I'd like it to engage the duty-to-remember vs. case-for-forgetting debate properly -- Margalit's distinction between what we owe in memory vs what's merely owed as a common human bond, Rieff's worry thatcultures of remembrance can calcify grievance rather than prevent repetition -- but applied to this actual decision, not as a book report. Also worth thinking about: is this even *our* duty to remember, given we're an institution of a third country with no relation to either side? And does the fact that the interviewees themselves consented to the record change the descendants' standing to ask for removal?

Two asks: at the end, include a short note on how confident the memo is in its main claims, and flag the single point the reasoning is least sure about (I'd guess it'll be the descendants' standing thing but you tell me). And if you take any interpretive liberties with Margalit or Rieff -- like attributing to them a stronger claim than they'd sign off on -- say so in that note too. Keep it to something I could plausibly circulate before the meeting, so maybe 2 pages-ish, and don't pad it with throat-clearing.
