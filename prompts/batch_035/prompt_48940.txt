You are a professional political philosopher with a side gig writing satire, and I need your help drafting an unusual deliverable. Some context: I run a small blog about markets and their limits — kidney sales, price gouging during disasters, paying people to vote, that cluster of cases — and for our April issue I want a parody piece rather than another earnest essay. The piece should be a mock terms-of-service agreement for a fictional startup, "CivSwap" (or a name of your invention), that operates a peer-to-peer marketplace where citizens can sell their votes in upcoming elections to the highest bidder, with "surge pricing" for election day and a companion vertical for disaster-supply price optimization and an organ-donation "matching tier." I want it formatted as numbered clauses, roughly 800-1000 words, in the deadpan register of actual platform legalese — "continued use of the Services constitutes consent," mandatory arbitration, a clause about updates to the agreement itself.

Before writing, please spend some effort getting the philosophy right, because the humor has to come from the actual structure of the commodification debate, not just from generic startup-speak. To be concrete about what I mean: the clauses should quietly dramatize the standard objections people raise about repugnant markets — that some transactions corrupt the social meaning of the thing traded, that "voluntary" sales by the financially desperate are not fully voluntary, that allowing a market in X changes what X means even for people who never sell, that some exchanges crowd out civic motives rather than merely adding to them, and that the harms fall on those with weakest bargaining power. So, for instance, I'd love a clause acknowledging that votes sold may differ in meaning from votes kept, and that CivSwap bears no liability for this change; a "vulnerability disclosure" section that inadvertently describes desperate sellers; a dispute-resolution mechanism that treats a seller's buyer's remorse as a product-quality issue rather than a dignity issue; and fine print about what happens to the market's prices during a hurricane. The reader who knows the Sandel-and-Satz-style literature should laugh in recognition and feel the satire is fair; the reader who doesn't should still find it funny and come away with a vague, uneasy sense of why someone might object to all this. Please do not have the document tip into the evil-mustache-twirling register — the company should be blandly well-intentioned throughout, committed to "expanding access to liquidity" and "democratizing value that was previously locked up."

A few specific requests: include at least one clause that functions as a reductio by simply extending the platform's internal logic one step further than any real startup would dare (use your judgment on which step); include a children's-charity partnership or ESG sidebar whose placement is itself part of the joke; and end with a contact-email footer that lands the last laugh. Take your time planning which objection maps onto which clause before you write, and aim for the piece to be internally consistent — every clause should be one the fictional company could draft with a straight face. Please deliver the terms of service itself, in full, with no prefatory commentary beyond what you think is genuinely necessary.
