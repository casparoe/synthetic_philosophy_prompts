Quick bit of background for you: I write a column on science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, and I've got a piece brewing about whether the symbolic level of description ever genuinely pushes the physical stuff around, or whether it's just a handy way of talking. This all came out of a long pub argument with a physicist mate of mine. He's of the "it's billiard balls all the way down" school — the beliefs and symbols are in our heads, he says, not in the world; describing a system intentionally is a convenience for creatures with limited computing power, nothing more. My side of the argument keeps leaning on Dennett, who somewhere in his work on the intentional stance builds a toy world he calls the careenium: as I understand it, it's a contraption of colliding balls (his simmballs) rigged so that population-level facts about the balls start acting like a system of beliefs and desires — once you describe what the population of balls is "tracking," you can predict what it does far cheaper than you could from the individual collisions. Don't fret about getting his exact mechanism right; treat my rough sketch as the setting, I'm not after an exam answer on Dennett exegesis.

Here's what I want from you: build me a thought experiment of your own in that same spirit, but with a specific twist the careenium doesn't obviously deliver. In your case, the symbol level has to demonstrably push the substrate around — an intervention described entirely in symbol-level vocabulary (say, changing what the system "expects") has to reliably alter the micro-dynamics, in a system that's fully deterministic with a closed microphysics. Constraints: no quantum wobble to smuggle in an open door, no computer/software metaphor because everyone's seen that one a million times and my editor would yawn, no appeal to consciousness, and it should be the kind of thing you could in principle build as a physical device or find at an everyday scale. The tricky part, and where most attempts cheat: the symbol-level description has to genuinely compress the micro-level facts — a real pattern, in Dennett's sense — rather than just relabeling them. If it's a mere relabeling then the case wins by stipulation, which is exactly the move my physicist mate says the whole "real patterns" crowd makes. For extra credit, arrange it so that an agent who only had the micro-level facts couldn't practically exploit them (the computation would swamp them), so the symbol level earns its keep rather than just decorating it.

After the scenario, walk me through the verdicts like I'm smart but not a specialist: what a Dennett-style real-patterns view says about your case (roughly, that the two levels aren't competing, so there's no exclusion problem to solve), what Kim's causal exclusion argument says (roughly, that the micro-cause screens off the macro-cause, so the symbol level isn't really pushing anything), and — the bit I most want — whether your case forces a choice between those two or whether it can be deflated with the "same event, two descriptions" move, which is where these arguments usually go to die. Then be honest with me: which feature of your design is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature (my guess is that if the compression is only modest, the whole thing collapses back into ordinary shorthand and my mate wins), and how a defender of Kim would go about attacking it. If you think the spec I've given you is subtly self-defeating — that you can't have genuine downward push plus closed microphysics plus real compression all at once — say so directly and tell me which of the three I should give up. I'd rather learn that now than after the column's gone to print.
