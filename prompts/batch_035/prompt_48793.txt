For a long-form feature I'm preparing on eliminative materialism — the Churchlands' old wager that "belief" and "desire" are theoretical posits of a bad proto-scientific theory, destined to go the way of phlogiston — my editor has asked me to commission a satirical sidebar that dramatizes the position rather than just summarize it. Please write me that piece: a parody of an internal style guide for a metropolitan daily whose editor-in-chief has converted to eliminative materialism and ordered all propositional-attitude vocabulary purged from the paper's copy. Around 600–750 words, formatted as numbered stylebook entries in the deadpan bureaucratic voice of a real newsroom style manual (entries like "believes, believe in" and "wants"), addressed to reporters and copy editors.

The humor must come from the actual commitments of the position, not from caricature. So the entries should wrestle with real problems: what dispositional or neurocomputational paraphrase could substitute for "believes" in a routine news sentence, and how quickly those paraphrases become unreadable; whether "expects," "hopes," and "intends" are equally tainted or get grandfathered in; the argument that folk psychology explains little and predicts worse, compared with the eventual neuroscience it will be replaced by; the analogy with retired theoretical vocabularies like phlogiston or demonic possession as an explanation of mental illness; and the lingering instrumentalist temptation — keeping "belief" the way we kept "sunrise," as harmless shorthand. Include at least one entry where the editor reluctantly grants an exemption (I'd suggest the horoscope page, the advice column, or a eulogy, but use your judgment), and close with an editor's memo explaining the new policy that quietly, visibly fails to obey its own ban — the memo's breakdown should be the piece's reductio.

Constraints: stay deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, but the irony must remain legible to someone who has never heard of folk psychology; a philosopher of mind should read it as fair to the position and recognize where each joke is doing real work. Do not attribute any invented quotes to real people, and don't cite or recommend outside reading — the piece should be self-contained.
