Been arguing with myself about whether famines should be counted as natural disasters or as political crimes, so let me just list my gut verdicts on cases and see if a principle falls out.

Ireland in the 1840s: political crime, full stop. Food was leaving the country under armed guard while people were starving.

Bengal 1943: also a crime, though a notch below Ireland in my gut. The British didn't cause the crop failure, but between wartime priorities, the refusal to stop exports or ship in grain, and the general indifference to Indian lives, yeah, crime.

Great Leap Forward: this one messes me up. Bigger death toll than the other two combined, and man-made from top to bottom, no natural trigger at all. But the picture I have is of local cadres inflating harvest numbers while the leadership genuinely believed the fantasy, so nobody at the top exactly chose famine. I stilll want to call it a crime, but I notice I'm reaching for it, unlike Ireland where it's immediate. That difference bothers me.

A hypothetical well-governed but dirt-poor country hit by a once-in-five-centuries drought, no early warning system, some hunger deaths despite the goverment doing everything it reasonably could: genuine disaster, not a crime, and I'd resist anyone trying to pin it on anybody.

A goverment sitting on early-warning data that delays imports for two quarters to protect domestic farm prices: crime-ish? Definitely blameworthy, but I can't say who exactly the criminal is.

A besieged city intentionally starved out: war crime, easiest call on the list.

Here's my problem. I also basically accept Sen's observation that famines don't happen in functioning democracies with a free press. But that seems to imply that wherever the capacity to prevent famine exists, famine is political — which would sink my verdict on the drought case, and would also make rich donor countries accessories in every famine they could have relieved and didn't. I don't want that conclusion, but I'm not sure I've earned not wanting it.

So: is there one principle that captures all these verdicts, or close? If not, which verdict has to go and what's the cheapest fix? And please tell me what the resulting principle says about a case I have no verdict on — North Korea in the 90s, where sanctions, mismanagement, and national pride are all tangled up together.
