I'm a philosophy PhD student writing on decision theory, and a friend in the math department has constructed a version of the two-envelope paradox that I can't debunk with the standard tools, for the annoying reason that he never makes the standard mistake. His setup: a benefactor flips a fair coin until it first lands heads. If heads first appears on flip n+1 — that is, after a run of n tails — he puts 3^n dollars in one envelope and 3^{n+1} in the other, shuffles them thoroughly, and hands me one. I may open my envelope before deciding whether to swap. My friend's argument, as he wrote it out:

(1) Suppose I open my envelope and see 3^k dollars for some k ≥ 1. Then either my envelope is the smaller member of a pair produced by a run of k tails, or the larger member of a pair produced by a run of k − 1 tails; those are the only two ways the number 3^k can be sitting in front of me. (2) A run of k − 1 tails is twice as likely as a run of k tails — one extra fair flip has to go a particular way — and, conditional on my seeing 3^k, nothing else distinguishes the two hypotheses. So the odds are 2 to 1 that my envelope is the larger of its pair: with probability 2/3 the other envelope holds 3^{k−1}, with probability 1/3 it holds 3^{k+1}. (3) The conditional expected value of the other envelope is therefore (1/3)·3^{k+1} + (2/3)·3^{k−1} = (11/9)·3^k, which beats the 3^k in my hand by 2/9 of it, every single time. (4) This computation favors switching for every amount I could possibly see, and if I see $1 the other envelope is $3 outright. (5) If switching is favored after every possible observation, then it is favored without looking: opening the envelope can only ever confirm the swap, so the unopened swap must already be the right move. (6) But the two envelopes were produced symmetrically and shuffled; sight unseen there is nothing to choose between them. Worse, once I have switched, his own table applied to my new envelope tells me to switch back, and then again, around the circle forever. His conclusion: expected-value reasoning issues an endless cycle of mutually reversing commands and then, at (6), forbids the very first of them; so conditional expectation is inconsistent as a guide to action, and this, he says, is what the two-envelope paradox was always trying to say — he has finally written the version with no blunder in it.

He is right that he never assumed the other envelope is equally likely to hold double or half; every probability he uses comes from the coin, and I have checked them. I also re-derived his k = 2 case by brute enumeration — holding $9 arises either as the larger member of the pair from one tail (probability 1/8) or the smaller member of the pair from two tails (probability 1/16), giving odds 2 to 1 and an expected value of $11 for the other envelope — and it checks out. So the usual lecture about how the conditional probabilities aren't really 50–50 gets no grip here, which is precisely what unsettles me.

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked so innocent, and what rejecting it costs. I would much prefer to keep the conditional computations in (2) through (4) — the arithmetic looks airtight and I verified one case by enumeration — so if the fault lies there, I want to know exactly how arithmetic I trust could mislead. And tell me whether the damage is what he claims, a wound to decision theory itself, or whether it stays local somewhere between (4) and (6), and what the local lesson is. One more thing I can't interpret: when I try to work out what my envelope is worth before opening it, the series refuses to settle into a finite number, and I can't tell whether that is my weak mathematics or the point.
