I'm preparing a seminar session that pairs Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — roughly, the claim that much of what gives our lives and projects their point for us depends on the assumption that other people will continue to live after our own deaths — with Cormac McCarthy's The Road, a novel set in a world where that assumption has collapsed: the biosphere is dying, and there is at best a thread of a human future. The puzzle I want to work through with my students: the father's defining project is keeping his son alive and morally oriented — being one of the good guys, carrying the fire — in a world without a plausible collective future, and he pursues it with undiminished seriousness; the novel then ends with the boy taken in by another family and a closing image of brook trout in mountain streams, in a natural world that will outlast every person in the book. Does the novel vindicate the conjecture, complicate it, or quietly refute it? Give me the reading as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the strongest evidence within the novel for the conjecture, (2) the point at which the novel most resists it, (3) whether the father's project is best read as a counterexample to the conjecture or as its illustration, (4) what the ending implies about whether the afterlife that matters must be a human one, and (5) one thing the novel dramatizes that the philosophical argument alone cannot. Refer to scenes in your own words rather than quoting.
