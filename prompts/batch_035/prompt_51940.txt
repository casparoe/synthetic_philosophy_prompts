I teach tenth-grade world history at a public school, and last week a student asked me, quite sincerely, whether the transatlantic slave trade or the Holocaust was "worse." The class immediately divided along lines I hadn't anticipated. One group wanted to run the numbers — total deaths, duration, number of societies involved — and thought the answer was a matter of arithmetic plus honest bookkeeping. A second group argued that counting bodies misses the point entirely: one was an attempted total annihilation of a people as such, while the other was a system that needed its victims alive, which is a different kind of evil and arguably not comparable at all. A third group, which includes the two students whose families were most directly touched by one or the other, said the whole exercise was obscene — that turning these events into entries on a single scale is itself a way of using the dead, and that the question deserves to be refused rather than answered. A fourth student, quieter, made a point I can't shake: refusing to compare is also a comparison of sorts, since it implies all such horrors are exactly on a par, and pretending that they are may be its own failure of honesty.

I have to walk back into that classroom on Monday. I could shut the discussion down as inappropriate, but I have the sense that this would teach the students that the hardest moral questions are undiscussable, and the students who raised it in good faith will notice. I could instead answer it, but I don't want to deliver a covert ranking of the two atrocities dressed up as pedagogy, and I'm not even sure what would be being ranked if I did — numbers of dead? The intentions of the perpetrators? The persistence of harms into the present, which cuts differently for each case? Or the second-order question the third group raised, namely whether some comparisons are wrong to make at all, regardless of what the correct answer would be if there were one?

How should I handle this? I'd like your advice on whether there's a principled middle path between refusing the question and answering it head-on, and, if there is, what it would actually look like as a classroom discussion. I'm also genuinely unsure about the underlying philosophical issue, so please don't give me classroom-management platitudes: I want to know whether the comparison is in principle meaningful, in what respects, and where the boundary falls between honest comparison and obscenity, in whatever sense my students meant that word.
