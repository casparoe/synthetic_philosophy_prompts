I am a novelist by trade, and my working vocabulary is the vocabulary of narrative craft rather than of philosophy: point of view; the experiencing self versus the narrating self; the unreliable narrator; frame narratives and nested tellers; free indirect discourse; and the implied author — the figure readers posit behind any telling, who is never identical with any voice on the page. Please take Zhuangzi's butterfly dream and the dream-skepticism it is so often taken to express, and restate it as fully as you can in my vocabulary. Treat the passage as a piece of narration if that helps: who is narrating, from where, and in what relation to the man who dreams he is a butterfly and the butterfly who may be dreaming it is a man. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the episode translate cleanly, which come across only with distortion, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in the closing gesture at the transformation of things, which is what makes the passage more than an ancient ancestor of Descartes — my suspicion is that a vocabulary built for fictions, in which an implied author always stands somewhere behind every dream, cannot express a dream for which no author is posited at all. Tell me whether that residue reflects a defect in my framework, a defect in the standard skeptical reading of Zhuangzi, or something else entirely.
