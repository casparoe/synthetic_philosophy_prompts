In our game theory reading group a friend presented an argument whose conclusion is obviously too much, but whose steps I can't bring myself to reject. It runs:

1. In a centipede game with finitely many nodes, the player who moves at the last node gets a strictly larger payoff by taking than by passing, so any rational player takes there.
2. Suppose rational players take at every node from node k onwards. Then a rational player at node k−1 knows that passing hands the opponent a certain gain and leaves herself worse off than taking now; so she takes at k−1.
3. By induction on (1) and (2), rational players take at node 1, and where rationality is common knowledge, both expect this and the game ends at once with the smallest pot.
4. The only structural features the argument needed were these: finitely many moves, and each pass handing the opponent a profitable chance to defect at the next move.
5. Any cooperative venture between two people has those features when modelled as a finite alternating-favours game — there is always a last favour, and each favour invites a profitable defection.
6. So between agents with common knowledge of each other's rationality, cooperation is impossible: no debt is repaid, no promise kept, nobody ever does the first favour.
7. Rational people plainly do cooperate. Something has gone wrong.

I want to keep (1) and (7), and I suspect the trouble is somewhere in (2)'s conditional or in what (4) and (5) quietly take for granted, but I can't say precisely.
