I am a retired marine ecologist who spent most of my career on coastal fisheries before turning to the social-science and philosophy literature on collective action. Reading around the tragedy of the commons and public goods, I keep running into a structure I cannot find a name for, and I would like to know whether one exists.

Here is the pattern. A fishery is overexploited; the fishers respond by drawing up rules and forming an association to make and enforce them. That solves the resource problem, but the association itself generates a new cooperation problem: monitoring and sanctioning are costly, and each member is tempted to let others carry that cost. Sometimes that second problem is handled by a higher body, say a federation of village associations — but then funding the federation is itself a collective-action problem among the villages, and so on up the ladder. Each rung seems typically to be cheaper than the one below it — it is easier to get a handful of associations to fund a secretariat than to get every fisher to monitor every other fisher — but the chain has to terminate somewhere, whether in norms internalized enough that enforcement is nearly free, or at a level where the residual shirking is small enough to tolerate, or in the blunt instrument of state coercion. I have come to suspect that whether a commons is "solvable" is really a question about whether this ladder converges before it outruns the participants' willingness to cooperate.

I am aware of two nearby concepts. One is the second-order free-rider problem from the punishment literature, and the other is what Ostrom calls nested enterprises among her design principles for successful commons governance. But as I understand them, each names only a piece of what I am describing: the second-order problem is usually framed as who pays to punish defectors within a single group, not as an open-ended sequence of governance levels, each of which turns the previous level's solution into a new, smaller commons; and nestedness describes the arrangement of successful institutions without, so far as I can tell, addressing the convergence question. Is there an established term for the recursive structure itself — solutions to a commons that convert it into a new collective-action problem one level up? If there is, I want to know exactly how it is standardly defined and where my fisheries example fits or fails to fit. If there is not, please coin one: give a careful definition, distinguish it from the second-order free-rider problem, from Ostrom's nestedness, and from ordinary talk of federalism or subsidiarity, and mark out the boundary cases — in particular, whether a ladder that terminates in internalized norms rather than sanctions still counts as an instance, and whether a chain that ends in centralized enforcement counts as a termination or as a different kind of solution altogether. A bare label will not help me; what I need is the carving.
