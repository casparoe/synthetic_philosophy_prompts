In a workshop on global justice, a colleague defended the cosmopolitan side with an argument I find suspicious precisely because it seems to prove so much:

(1) The special claims compatriots are said to have on one another — the claims invoked to justify immigration restrictions or welfare priority for citizens — must be grounded either in choice (consent, promises, knowingly accepted benefits) or in the relationship itself. (2) Compatriot relations are plainly not chosen: citizenship is assigned at birth, exit is costly and unworkable for most, and one cannot decline the benefits of public goods. (3) So if compatriot priority is defensible at all, its ground must lie in the relationship: specifically, joint subjection to a common coercive authority and participation in a common scheme of cooperation in which each benefits from the contributions of the rest. (4) A grounding fact grounds the duties it grounds uniformly: whoever stands in the same relationship has the same claims. (5) But each of us also stands in relations of joint subjection and mutual benefit with all participants in the global economic order — trade rules, financial systems, the shared atmosphere — none of it chosen either. (6) So, by (4), whatever special claims compatriots have on one another, we have equally with everyone enmeshed in the global scheme; compatriot priority is preferential treatment for fellow members of one coercive club among several. He is content to conclude that either we owe compatriots nothing beyond what we owe anyone, or we owe everyone what we supposedly owe compatriots — he accepts both disjuncts cheerfully.

When I objected that the state involves a far denser relationship than the global order, he replied that density is a difference of degree, and that a ground admitting of degrees cannot mark a categorical boundary — otherwise the line between compatriot and stranger becomes arbitrary, cutting off our duties to distant rural fellow citizens before our duties to business partners abroad. I cannot accept either disjunct of his conclusion, and yet each premise looks defensible. Diagnose the argument for me: which premise or inference should I reject, what exactly is wrong with it, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (1) and (4); I feel no comparable attachment to (3).
