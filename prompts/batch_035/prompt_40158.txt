I'm on the ethics committee at a mid-size care facility (mostly long-term nursing and dementia care), and a case has landed on my desk that I have to write up before our meeting on Tuesday, and honestly I keep going back and forth on it so I'd rather get my thinking straight on paper first.

The resident, "Ellen," wrote an advance directive 22 years ago after watching her mother deteriorate with dementia. It says, in her own handwriting, that if she ever reaches a point where she no longer recognizes her family, she does not want any treatment to prolong her life, and she specifically mentions not wanting to be kept alive by feeding assistance. Ellen is now in mid-stage Alzheimer's. She recognizes her daughter maybe half the time, her son almost never. She needs help eating — she doesn't refuse food, she just can't manage utensils anymore and will not initiate eating unless prompted and spoon-fed. She's lost significant weight in the three weeks since staff, aware of the directive, started backing off on feeding assistance.

Her daughter wants the directive followed and has accused the facility of dragging its feet out of liability anxiety. Her son says the directive is being misapplied: his mother isn't suffering, she lights up at music, she smiles at staff, and to him stopping feeding her is starving a woman who would open her mouth for a spoon if you offered it. He's also hired a lawyer.

Two things are making this harder than a plain "advance directives are binding" answer. First, a nurse on the unit pointed out something that's stuck with me: when Ellen wrote the directive, she was terrified and grieving, freshly back from her mother's funeral. The person who signed it arguably wasn't calmly weighing her future interests — she was making a promise to her younger self out of dread. Why should that frightened version of Ellen get to overrule the Ellen who is sitting there now, humming along to the radio, apparently content in a way her past self never predicted or understood? Second, our psychologist keeps insisting that Ellen's current "in the moment" pleasure shouldn't count for less just because she won't remember it in an hour — she thinks there's a bias in how we assess quality of life for dementia patients, where we discount experiences the patient herself isn't tracking over time.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the committee. It needs a concrete bottom-line recommendation for what the facility should actually do going forward (including what to do about the weight loss and the son's threatened legal action), not just a survey of views. But it also has to take the strongest arguments on the losing side seriously — whichever side loses, there's a version of this where the losing side is going to feel steamrolled, and I want the memo to give them their real due rather than a token paragraph before the "however." If it helps, our policy defers to valid directives but allows exceptions where honoring one would cause "avoidable distress," which of course nobody has defined.

One more constraint, and I know this is a bit much, but please don't just hand me the standard autonomy-vs-beneficence recap I could've written myself at 2am. The interesting bit here, to me, is what exactly the earlier self's authority is *over* — is it over this body's future, or over the person who will eventually be living in it, and does it matter that the person in the bed now isn't obviously the same "patient" the directive was written for? If the memo can get into that, it'll actually move the committee. Sorry for typos btw, typing this fast between rounds.
