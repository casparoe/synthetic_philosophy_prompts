Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of killing cases, and I'd like to know whether there's one principle behind them all. Switching the trolley so it hits one person instead of five: fine. Shoving the guy off the footbridge: not fine. A villain at gunpoint makes me kill one of six people, and kills all six if I refuse: killing the one is fine, clearly the least bad thing I could do. The same villain, but this time he's going to kill five other people, and the only way I can stop him is by shooting through an innocent human shield: I keep going back and fourth on this one. A sheriff who can frame one innocent man to keep a mob from lynching several people: not fine — even though lying to that same mob to protect its target would be fine. Killing my attacker in self-defense: fine, in a way that killing an innocent bystander who's about to cause my death 'accidentally' would not be. So is there a single principle that captures all of these, and what does it say about the case I can't settle? My hunch is it's something about harms I do myself versus harms other people do, but I can't make that fit the forced-choice case, where I'm doing the killing either way and it still seems fine to pick the smaller number. Tell me honestly if some of my verdicts have to go, and also what the principle predicts for cases I haven't mentioned — torturing one person to stop someone else from torturing five, say, or the human-shield case if the five deaths would come from five different villains instead of just one.
