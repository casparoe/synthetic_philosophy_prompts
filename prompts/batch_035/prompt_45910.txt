I'm a graduate student working on a term paper at the intersection of decision theory and moral philosophy, and I've hit on a tension between two clusters of ideas that I haven't seen brought together, and I'd like an argument developed for one side of it. On the one hand, there's a style of reasoning about the far future that, combined with expected utility maximization, tends toward fanaticism: if the future might contain astronomically many worthwhile lives, then even tiny probabilities of affecting how that future goes can dominate the expected-value calculus, and it can start to look rational to accept enormous present costs, or to prefer long-shot interventions with astronomical payoffs over safer near-term ones. Critics have attacked this 'fanaticism' on its own terms — infinite or unbounded utilities, decision-theoretic pathologies — but I want to explore a different line of resistance.

On the other hand, there's the simulation argument, which concludes that at least one of the following must be false: that almost no civilization at our stage of development goes extinct before reaching technological maturity; that almost no mature civilization runs large numbers of simulations of histories like its own; and that we are almost certainly not living in a simulation. Someone who finds the first two claims quite plausible is thereby pushed toward thinking we probably are living in a simulation. Here's the thought I want taken seriously: if we are in a simulation, then the far future we might hope to shape belongs to someone else's computation, and a civilization capable of running many simulations of worlds like ours might, for all we know, run many further simulations of far-future-like worlds directly. Whatever value resides in far-future-type worlds may then be realized many times over independently of anything we do, so that the expected-value contribution of our own efforts to steer the future is diluted, or at least the stakes we attach to our particular world's trajectory look very different than they do under the assumption that this is the only, or the seed, place where such value gets created.

Please make the argument, as sympathetically as you can, that taking the simulation argument seriously should dampen fanaticism about our own world's far future — that is, that it narrows the expected-value gap between astronomical long-shot gambles and sober near-term work, or otherwise undermines the reasoning that lets minuscule probabilities of astronomical payoffs dominate our choices. Along the way, please engage with the most obvious objections and, where you can, respond to the strongest of them: that this reasoning multiplies value rather than diluting it (more simulations of good futures is more good, not less); that simulated lives might not matter at all, which would cut the other way; and that conditional on being simulated, our evidence about what the simulators value is so thin that no defensible expectation can be formed either way. You needn't cite any literature; argue from first principles, taking the simulation argument's premises as given, and be explicit about which steps of the argument carry the most weight and where a critic would most plausibly dig in.
