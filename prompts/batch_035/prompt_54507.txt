I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar and I'm writing the final this weekend, and I got into a huge argument with a friend of mine (also an epistemologist) about whether pragmatic encroachment can survive testimony cases, and I want that fight on the exam. Her thought: if whether you know depends partly on your stakes, you get cases where a low-stakes speaker knows that p, tells a high-stakes hearer exactly what she has, and the hearer *still* doesn't know -- same evidence throughout, one of them knows and one doesn't, and the one who doesn't heard it straight from the one who does. She thinks that's a reductio. My instinct was the opposite: maybe that's just what the encroacher should predict, because when the stakes go up we really do stop trusting a friend's word and go check the primary source ourselves, and explaining that is a burden for the classical invariantist, not for us. Neither of us convinced the other, obviously.

So: please write one essay question, answerable in about 90 minutes, built around what my friend started calling the "stakes transfer" case (speaker testifies p, hearer's stakes are far higher, evidence identical). It should not be answerable by reciting the bank cases or just labeling positions. Then write a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks that says exactly what seperates an A from a B -- in my head the B answer correctly explains encroachment vs contextualism vs invariantism and then runs out of steam, while the A answer does something real with the testimony angle, like whether testimony transmits knowledge or only evidence, whether the hearer can know by trusting someone who knows, or whether contextualism faces the stakes transfer problem at all or just changes the subject (does the hearer's higher stakes change what "knows" means, or change whether he knows?). Please dont ask the students to cite anything, I only care whether they can actually do the philosophy, not whether they've memorized the reading list.
