My best friend and I spent most of last Saturday arguing about Thomson's lamp — the lamp switched on at one minute to noon, off at half a minute to noon, on at a quarter minute to noon, and so on through infinitely many switches converging on noon — and by 2 a.m. we'd stopped arguing about whether it's physically possible and started arguing about whether it's even conceptually coherent. My position was that the interesting problem isn't that no one could build such a lamp; it's that even an idealized lamp whose entire pre-noon history is fixed seems to have no determinate state at noon, since the description never tells you which press is the last one. My friend's position was that this is just a refusal to apply excluded middle: at noon the lamp is on or it is off, and if our description doesn't say which, that's a defect of the description, not of the lamp. Neither of us convinced the other, and I'm now teaching the thing to a second-year metaphysics seminar (16 students, 75 minutes) where I don't want to lecture the argument at them from the front of the room — I want them to run into the gap between "every moment before noon is fixed" and "the state at noon is fixed" themselves, before any of it is named.

Act as an experienced philosophy instructor and designer of classroom activities, and think carefully before committing to a design: please brainstorm two or three candidate structures first, say which you'd choose and why, then develop the best one into something I can actually run. The activity should have participants perform some truncated, physical version of the supertask — a schedule of button presses with genuinely shrinking intervals, done fast enough that they feel the compression even though it must terminate after finitely many steps — followed by something like a specification exercise, where one group writes out everything that is true of the lamp before noon and a second group must act as engineers who can only use that specification to determine the state at noon. I want them to feel both horns: the strong pull of the thought that the lamp must be on or off, and the discovery that the facts they've been given don't decide which. Include rules, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the argument rather than restating it abstractly. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students refusing to play along with an obviously impossible schedule, someone insisting the whole thing is just about physics, groups smuggling in extra assumptions like continuity to make the specification decide — and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about the feature of the problem no activity can reproduce: we can only ever stage finitely many steps, so somewhere the facilitator has to supply the jump to the limit by fiat, and you should tell me exactly where that jump happens and how to mark it so students notice it as a move rather than receiving it as a given.
