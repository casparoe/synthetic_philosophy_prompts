I teach a grad seminar on democratic theory and, as an experiment, I'm making my two most stubborn students -- one all-affected-interests person, one all-subjected-to-coercion person -- write an adversarial collaboration instead of another shouting match. They keep talking past each other and I want to hand them a decent template of what the document should look like, so draft it for me.

For context if you need it: the question is the boundary problem, i.e., who is entitled to a say in a democratic decision. Camp A says the demos is everyone whose interests are affected by the decision (so if my country's trade policy tanks your fishing industry, you get a say, citizenship be damned). Camp B says that's hopeless as a principle and the demos is everyone subjected to the coercive authority of the state -- coercion is what makes the special claim of democracy kick in, since the point of giving everyone a vote is to justify coercion to them. Each side thinks the other's principle gives either far too small or far too large a demos: A people say B's demos is arbitrarily capped at borders (the long-term resident foreign worker is coerced in every way that matters, and B people actually agree about her, they just have to bend the principle); B people say A's demos is unbounded and unknowable (affected interests ripple out everywhere -- trade, climate, currency -- and nobody can run an election over "everyone with a stake").

Please write the actual joint statement they could both sign:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims -- things both camps accept about the problem, about why it's not just definitional gerrymandering, about the cases where they in fact converge (I suspect the long-term resident guest worker is one, and maybe children are another for complicated reasons, but work out what they'd actually say).
2. The exact disagreements, each phrased so the wording is neutral enough that the side holding it wouldn't demand a rewrite -- this is where they always fail, so be careful: no framing that smuggles in "the demos should track interests" as if it were uncontroversial.
3. The cruxes. My guess is there are two big ones: (a) whether being affected by a decision creates the same kind of claim as being coerced into complying with it, or whether those claims differ in strength or ground; and (b) whether indeterminacy is fatal to a principle of inclusion or just a normal feature principles have (B people act like vagueness is a refutation, but every principle is vague at the edges, including theirs). But if you find a better crux, use it.
4. For each crux, at least one concrete case or argument both sides agree is a fair test, with each side's prediction written down in advance. They should use real-ish hard cases: future generations, tourists passing through, citizens living abroad, people affected by a policy but exempt from its enforcement, maybe sanctions on a third country. I want it on record what each camp predicts about the hard case BEFORE they argue about it, so nobody retrofits.
5. If some of their dispute turns out to be verbal -- e.g., they mean different things by "affected" and part of the disagreement dissolves once you disambiguate -- say which part and why. My own hunch is at least a third of their seminar fights were about the word "affected" and not about anything substantive, but don't let my hunch drive the document.

Keep it signed by both: no drafting-slips, and don't let one side get all the good concessions. The B person tends to concede too much in writing and then retract in discussion, and the A person tends to write as if their principle were common sense -- draft accordingly, i.e., with actual symmetry.
