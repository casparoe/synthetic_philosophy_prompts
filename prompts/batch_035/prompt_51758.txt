I've been trying to work out what my actual standards are for believing things about the distant past, because when I list my gut verdicts they don't obviously add up to one coherent rule. Here they are:

- If a Babylonian astronomical diary written within days of some event disagrees with a Greek historian writing two centuries later, I take the diary, no contest. Contemporary beats later, pretty much always.
- Herodotus says Xerxes marched into Greece with millions of men, and I just don't believe it -- I side with the moderns who say it was maybe 150-300k. I basically discount ancient numbers across the board.
- And yet I believe Herodotus' story about the Phoenicians sailing all the way around Africa, and my reason is one tiny detail he included while saying he personally doubted the whole tale: that while sailing west around the tip, the sailors had the sun on their right, i.e., to the north. That's exactly what you'd see in the southern hemisphere, and it's the kind of detail you don't invent. So one throwaway detail from an author I otherwise distrust beats his own explicit figures?? Feels inconsistent.
- When the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle mentions a solar eclipse and modern retrocalculation confirms one hit that area that year, I feel like the entry is confirmed -- but honestly I'm only sure about the eclipse itself, not the battle and the omen attached to it.
- Two medieval chroniclers report the same marvel independently, and I still don't buy it, because "independently" usually turns out to mean they both drew on the same older legend. So much for multiple attestation being the gold standard.
- Australian Aboriginal oral traditions describing the sea rising and cutting off islands -- things geologists date to something like 7,000+ years ago -- I'm inclined to think these preserve genuine memory of real events. So I'll take an oral tradition over millennia but laugh off a written legend that's only 800 years old. That one bugs me.
- The Vinland map: the ink turned out to contain a modern pigment, so fake, case closed, even though the parchment itself is genuinely old.
- The James ossuary: box is ancient, the important part of the inscription is allegedly carved much later, so the artifact is real but tells us nothing about the person supposedly named on it. That verdict feels right but it's weird next to the sun-on-the-right one, where I trusted exactly one carved detail.

So: is there a single principle behind all this -- something like which features of evidence track truth -- or am I just patching together whatever verdict feels good case by case? Give me the principle, or a couple of candidates, stated precisely enough that it actually decides these cases rather than gesturing at them. Tell me which of my verdicts, if any, it can't accommodate and would have to overturn. Then run it on the case I keep flip-flopping on: the Kensington runestone, where the runes themselves look 19th-century to basically every linguist, but there's a geological argument (going back to an early 1900s weathering study, and revived more recently) that the carved surfaces are way too weathered to be modern. Which side does your principle come down on, and why? And finally, invent one further case of your own -- the kind my principle(s) should be able to handle -- and tell me what verdict it delivers.
