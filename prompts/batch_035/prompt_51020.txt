I'm a PhD student in political theory, and a chapter of my dissertation defends a modest epistemic case for majority rule on a committee I actually sit on — an eleven-person faculty panel that votes on grant applications. Writing the chapter, I've been going back to the Condorcet jury theorem constantly, and I've reached the point where I can recite its moving parts but have lost any feel for which parts matter most. Before someone competent reviews this chapter, I'd like to straighten out in my own head how fragile the theorem really is, assumption by assumption.

Please rank the theorem's core assumptions by how badly a violation of each one damages its conclusion — roughly, the guarantee that a majority vote among many voters is overwhelmingly likely to identify the correct answer, given that each voter is more likely than not to be right. Consider at least the following: (a) voter competence, i.e., each voter's probability of choosing the correct option exceeding one half; (b) independence of the voters' errors from one another; (c) the existence of a determinate fact of the matter — a "correct" option that everyone is voting on; and (d) the shared agenda or common framing of the question, such that all voters understand themselves as answering the same question rather than subtly different ones. Rank these by the severity of the damage done when the assumption fails, and explain in a few sentences why each lands where it does. In your reasoning, please distinguish failures that merely weaken the asymptotic result (for instance, situations where relaxing an assumption still leaves a weaker but recognizable version of the theorem standing) from failures that undermine it outright or change its practical upshot entirely, as with correlated errors among voters. If you think the ranking depends on the application — say, an independent jury of fact versus a committee voting on what the best policy is, where the "correct" answer may be partly constituted by the vote — feel free to give a split ranking, but then justify the split.

For what it's worth, my advisor insists that independence is the load-bearing wall and that everything else is carpentry, but I've started to suspect that she underestimates how much the shared-agenda assumption does in practice: on my committee, half the members think they're ranking projects by expected scientific payoff while the other half think they're rationing a fixed budget to maximize institutional prestige, and I've never been sure the theorem has anything to say to a group like that. I'd be curious whether your ranking vindicates her intuition, mine, or neither. Please don't just gesture at the literature; I want your own assessment, stated plainly enough that I can disagree with it.
