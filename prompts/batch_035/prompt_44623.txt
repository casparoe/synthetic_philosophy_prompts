I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, working on a long-form piece about what the law and moral philosophy each make of situations in which every option is wrong, and I'd like a case analysis of a historical episode rather than the usual survey. The episode: in April 1841 the American emigrant ship William Brown struck an iceberg off Newfoundland and sank; nine crewmen and some thirty-two passengers crowded into a leaking longboat, and when it began to founder, the first mate ordered the crew to lighten the boat, whereupon the seamen forced a dozen or more adult male passengers overboard -- no lots were drawn, and every crewman stayed aboard. The survivors were rescued; one sailor, Alexander Holmes, was the only crewman who could be found and tried, and in United States v. Holmes (1842) he was convicted of manslaughter after the court held that necessity is no justification for homicide -- yet he was sentenced to only six months and a nominal fine, and no one, including the court, seems to have thought the boat should simply have been allowed to sink. Analyze this episode as a case study in the debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. Lay out the mapping carefully: what the impossibilist position -- that there is always an all-things-considered verdict, so the sailors merely faced a terrible choice, not a conflict of obligations -- says they should have done and what went wrong in what they did; what the dilemmist position says about whether Holmes violated an obligation no matter what he chose; and how the residue view, on which he may have acted rightly while still having something to feel guilty about, handles the fittingness of his guilt and of the court's token punishment, which looks designed to register wrongdoing without serious blame. Then run the analysis in both directions. Where is each theory silent or strained -- for instance, does the episode's ugliest feature, that the crew saved themselves and chose victims without any procedure, belong to the dilemma at all, or does it show that much of what looks like a dilemma is really a problem of fair selection, and would drawing lots (as the sailors in the later English lifeboat case, R v Dudley and Stephens in 1884, considered but never did) have dissolved the moral residue or merely laundered it? And finally, what does the episode show about the theories themselves: does the law's split verdict -- guilty, but barely punished -- confirm that dilemmists are right that obligation and blame come apart, or does it suggest the courts simply refused to decide the question and that calling this a genuine dilemma confuses tragedy with contradiction?
