Some years ago, out of curiosity about what money really is, I read an odd old book by the Irish bishop George Berkeley called The Querist, from the 1730s, written entirely as numbered questions about Ireland's economy. I have no training in economics or philosophy — I keep a small shop for a living — but the book has stayed with me, and lately, watching the news about digital currencies, I have been trying to work out what he would say about things he could never have imagined. I would like your help thinking it through.

His verdicts on the cases he actually considered, as I understand them, are roughly these: (1) gold and silver have no virtue in themselves beyond setting people to work, and a nation can sit on mines and still be wretchedly poor, or have none and thrive; (2) the true idea of money, as such, is that of a ticket or counter — a device for reckoning, recording, and transferring entitlements — with gold, silver, and paper all alike being merely tickets; (3) bank notes are money, indeed most of the money then in Ireland, paper though they are; (4) less money circulating swiftly does as much work as more money circulating slowly, so what matters is the movement, not the quantity; (5) money is useful only insofar as it stirs up industry, letting people share the fruits of each other's labour, and anything else that did that would serve just as well; (6) those who work with neither heads nor hands deserve to be expelled like drones from a well-governed state, and creating new wants in people is the likeliest way to make them industrious; (7) arbitrarily changing the denominations of the coinage is a public cheat; and (8) he sketched a plan for a national bank for Ireland, and was sharp about whether a bank built from voluntary private subscriptions really deserves to be called national — he generally wanted credit and currency governed for the public good rather than managed for private gain.

Now the new cases I keep chewing on. The first is Bitcoin: a purely electronic token, backed by no state and no land, with a fixed total supply, bought mostly to be held rather than spent, its price swinging wildly — though it is also, in its strange way, keeping a worldwide industry of miners busily at work. The second is a central bank digital currency: electronic tickets issued directly by the state, spendable by anyone, stable in value. The third is the kind of local currency some towns have tried, which deliberately loses a little value every month it goes unspent, so that it must keep moving from hand to hand or melt away.

Here is what I want. I am less interested in the verdicts themselves than in the rule (or rules) that fits his track record. So: lay out the candidate principles he seems to be running — I can see at least a "circulating ticket" reading, an "industry is the whole point" reading, and a "credit belongs under public direction" reading, but there may be others — and check them against all eight verdicts above. Then say what each rule predicts for each of my three cases, since I suspect they come apart on Bitcoin especially. Give me your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it, for what he would actually say about each case. And finally, tell me what further case, if any, would most cleanly tell the rival rules apart — the sort of thing I could put to a Berkeley devotee and learn from the answer which principle was really in charge all along.
