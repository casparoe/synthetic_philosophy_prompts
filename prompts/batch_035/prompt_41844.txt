I'm closing a seminar on the moral status of animals and other minds with a session on panpsychism, and I'd like to end it with a poem rather than a summary. Please write a sestina in which all six repeating end-words are drawn from fundamental physics — charge, spin, field, mass, point, wave — and let the form do the philosophical work: the same six words recombine in every stanza without any new word ever entering, so the reader watches the constituents rearranged at larger and larger scales and keeps waiting for a stanza-level subject to appear, and never quite gets one. That is the combination problem, enacted rather than described. Around the fourth stanza, make the ethical turn: if experience goes all the way down, what becomes of the idea of a suffering subject? The poem should resist both available shrugs — that pain at the bottom renders every moral difference trivial, and that it makes no moral difference at all. Let the envoi gather all six words one last time, as envois do, but have it ask rather than answer whether what we owe anything to is the arrangement or what the arrangement is of. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind wouldn't wince at the panpsychism, and honest enough that it doesn't pretend the ethics is settled.
