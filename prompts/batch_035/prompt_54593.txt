I'm preparing a paper on how popular short fiction metabolizes hard metaphysics, and my test case is Andy Weir's 2009 flash story The Egg, read through the lens Schrödinger built up in his late lectures and essays — the material collected in Mind and Matter and My View of the World, where he argues that the plurality of conscious minds is a construction of experience, that at bottom the total number of minds in the world is one, and where he openly credits the Upanishads rather than any result of physics for this conviction, which he also offered as the only honest consolation regarding death. At first glance the story dramatizes exactly this thesis: the god figure reveals to the recently deceased protagonist that he is every human being who has ever lived or will live, so that everyone he has ever loved, harmed, or ignored was himself. But the story gives the thesis a structure that Advaita, and Schrödinger after it, resists: the lives are lived sequentially, like reincarnation; the reveal has a telos, in which living every human life amounts to a maturation that ends in godhood; and, most importantly, the story retains a continuing individual subject — the astonished you whose journey this is — whereas Schrödinger's claim is precisely that the one mind is not a soul accumulating many lives, because the plurality of subjects was never real in the first place: the sequence is Maya, not a curriculum. So I need a verdict, not a survey. Does The Egg popularize Schrödinger's unity-of-mind view, distort it into something it explicitly denies, or quietly argue a different and independent position — a karmic single-soul view dressed in Advaita's clothes? Where exactly does the story resist the lens: in its teleology, in its dramaturgy of a teacher god revealing truth to a pupil, in its narrative grammar of a continuing self, or somewhere else? Does its ethics follow from the metaphysics, or does the story merely invert solipsism into a moral universalism — be kind to everyone, because everyone is you — that needs the metaphysics only as ornament? And what, if anything, does the reveal structure accomplish that the argument cannot: does making the reader inhabit the deceived subject do philosophical work, or does it smuggle in precisely the individual essence the doctrine rejects? Lead with your verdict in the first sentence — one of the three, or a better formulation if you think my taxonomy is wrong — then give the reading that supports it, flag honestly the points at which the text could be pressed into the opposite reading, and say what the story contributes to the debate that Schrödinger's prose does not. No preamble, no restating of the question.
