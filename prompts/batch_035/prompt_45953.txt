I chair the review panel of a mid-sized private research foundation. Roughly once a month the panel — five members, occasionally four when someone's traveling — meets to shortlist applications: typically fifteen to thirty proposals for two or three awards, sometimes a straight ranking of candidates for a single fellowship. Each panelist reads everything, and each of us genuinely has an ordering in our head, but our orderings rarely agree, and the disagreement isn't usually about facts — it's about how much originality matters relative to feasibility, or how much to penalize a proposal for a weak methods section. At the end of the day we have to walk out with a ranked shortlist that the trustees will see, and the applicants who miss the cut will ask why they placed below people they beat on some dimensions. I've read enough to know that this is exactly the terrain of Arrow's impossibility theorem: whenever you're aggregating individual orderings over three or more alternatives into a group ordering, something has to give — no procedure can satisfy all of unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship at once. What I take that to mean in practice is that the game isn't to find the "fair" aggregation rule, because there isn't one; it's to decide, deliberately and in advance, which condition we're going to sacrifice for which kind of decision, and to be able to say so plainly when an applicant or trustee asks.

So what I'd like from you is a decision procedure the panel can actually run, written as a sequence of steps a program officer could follow with a stopwatch and a stack of score sheets. It should cover: how we choose, at the start of a meeting, which condition we're relaxing for that day's decisions and on what grounds (e.g., if we drop IIA by running a Borda-style points tally, a strong rejected proposal can change the relative standing of two others — the procedure should tell us when that's acceptable and what we say about it); where the procedure stops and hands a question back to the panel as a judgment call, marked as such rather than laundered through arithmetic; and what the procedure honestly cannot do, given that any mechanical rule we adopt will sometimes produce a shortlist a majority of the panel actively dislikes. Then run it on three concrete cases from recent cycles: (1) ranking seventeen fellowship candidates where two panelists' first and second choices are separated by a third candidate everyone agrees is mediocre; (2) shortlisting four of eleven project grants where the chair suspects the tally rule will let her own least favorite proposal through and wants the procedure to overrule her suspicion only if that's what it genuinely implies; and (3) a case where the procedure yields an outcome the panel will find distasteful — say, eliminating a proposal most members ranked second because of how the rule interacts with a widely-scattered set of votes — and say what we should tell that applicant.

A few constraints to keep it real: the whole ranking step has to fit inside a ninety-minute block after the discussion, so nothing that requires panelists to make pairwise comparisons over all candidates; the panelists are domain experts but have no patience for formal notation, so the procedure should be stated in plain sentences with the theorem's conditions translated into concrete language (e.g., "no dead weight" instead of "Pareto"); and the trustees require that the shortlist come with a one-paragraph justification that doesn't hide behind "the formula said so." If parts of the procedure are judgment calls dressed up as steps, please say so explicitly — I'd rather present the panel with an honest instrument than a mystifying one.
