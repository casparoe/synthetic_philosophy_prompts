I have recently been invited to sit on a consultative panel advising our government on the wording of a formal parliamentary apology for a historical injustice: a state-run program, ended more than half a century ago, under which children from a marginalized community were removed from their families and placed in institutions in the name of assimilation. My grandmother was taken under this program at the age of six and, as was common, never returned to her parents; I gather that families from the affected community were deliberately given seats on the panel.

Our drafting sessions have stalled over a question of priorities. We have limited political capital and a fixed date for the ceremony, so we cannot fight for everything at once, and the members disagree about which elements of the apology are indispensable and which, however desirable, are negotiable. Before the next session I would like a considered ranking to test my own instincts against, so let me ask you this:

Please rank the following candidate elements from most to least essential to a meaningful state apology, and give a sentence for each on why it lands where it does.

1. A specific factual narrative of what was done, including mechanisms and numbers, rather than abstract regret of the "mistakes were made" variety.
2. An explicit statement that the state, as a continuing body, bears responsibility — not merely that "suffering occurred."
3. Second-person address: the apology spoken to the survivors and descendants, not merely about them in the third person.
4. The word "apology" itself, with a performative first-person plural ("we apologize"), rather than "regret" or "deep sorrow."
5. A commitment to material redress beyond words.
6. Forward-looking commitments: memorialization, curriculum reform, and guarantees of non-repetition.
7. The deliberate avoidance of conditional or exculpatory constructions ("if anyone was harmed," "though intentions were good for the era").

Two complications I would especially value your thoughts on. First, most direct victims of the program are now deceased, and one member argues that the passage of time converts several of these elements from essential to symbolic — that after eighty years, the ranking should shift, though we cannot agree on which items slide. Does it, and how? Second, another member holds that the entire exercise is a category error: a state is a legal fiction, the current citizens did none of it, and an inherited apology is at best theater — at worst, a moral claim on people who owe nothing. If you find any force in that objection, say where it should temper the ranking, and if you find none, say why the "we" who apologizes is not an empty pronoun.

I should add that this is not an academic question for me: whatever text we settle on will be read at a ceremony my family will attend, and I would like to be able to defend the ranking to my grandmother's memory as well as to the panel.
