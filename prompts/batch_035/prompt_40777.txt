Me and my friend Maya got into a huge argument about this last night and I want to sort it out properly. She works on regulatory policy and was explaining how her agency (and basically every agency that does this kind of thing, transportation, environment, health) runs cost-benefit analyses for new rules. They put dollar figures on everything: lives saved, injuries avoided, time not wasted in traffic, even noise reduction. For deaths they use something they call the value of a statistical life — you survey what people would pay to reduce small risks to themselves, and back out what a life is worth, which comes out to several million dollars. Then they add it all up, benefits minus costs, and if the number is positive the rule looks good.

Here's what got us fighting. At one point in the conversation I said "fine, but do you count a dollar for a poor person the same as a dollar for a rich person? A thousand dollars means way more to someone on welfare." And Maya said no — the standard practice is to NOT do that. When agencies have been asked whether to apply what they call distributional weights, i.e., scale dollars by income so a dollar to a poor person counts for more, the official position is basically that we can't do it, because comparing the intensity of utility between different people isn't scientific, it's just not something you can measure. That sounded reasonable to me for about ten minutes. Then I realized the whole thing she'd just described to me is nothing BUT interpersonal comparisons. The statistical life value comes from averaging what lots of different people would pay to avoid risk — so implicitly a poor person's life counts less, because they'd pay less to reduce their risk, and that's treated as commensurable with what a rich person would pay. And treating every prevented death as worth the same several million dollars, whoever the person turns out to be, is already a claim that one person's life and another's can go on a single scale. So the practice refuses to compare people's utility in exactly the case (income) where the case for it is strongest, while quietly doing comparisons everywhere else, and doing them in a way that tilts against the poor. Maya's response was that the willingness-to-pay method doesn't claim to measure utility, it just measures choice, and I honestly couldn't tell whether that's a real distinction or a dodge.

So what I want is this. What view of wellbeing and of interpersonal utility comparison does this whole practice actually presuppose? Is there a coherent position underneath it — maybe something like "we're only measuring preferences, not welfare, and preference intensity is comparable but utility isn't" — or is the practice just philosophically confused, saying "we can't compare" while operating on the assumption that we can? Where exactly is it underdetermined, i.e., which rival views of wellbeing (hedonism, preference satisfaction, objective list) would license the same numbers? And what would have to change in the actual procedures if the agencies took the incomparability claim seriously, versus if they bit the bullet and adopted the weights?
