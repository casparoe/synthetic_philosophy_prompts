I'm a graduate student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and empirical consciousness science, and before I build a dissertation around a methodological line I've drifted into, I want a coherence check on the package of commitments I've accumulated. Here is my profile:

1. I endorse heterophenomenology, in Dennett's sense, as the right methodological framework for the science of consciousness: the field should operate under third-person constraints, treating subjects' reports as data to be interpreted — the way an anthropologist treats an informant's account of the spirit world — rather than as transparent windows onto experience.

2. I accept the accompanying stance of neutrality: the subject's heterophenomenological world, as reconstructed from their reports, is to be treated as a fiction, neither believed nor disbelieved at the outset, with its characters and events acquiring real referents only insofar as our best theory awards them.

3. At the same time, I believe first-person epistemic authority is genuine and not merely something science will explain away: a subject's sincere report of their current phenomenal state is defeasibly authoritative, such that a third-person observer is wrong to override it absent specific defeating evidence. On the question of what my experience is like right now, I trust me over any experimenter.

4. I take the confabulation literature — split-brain rationalizations, choice blindness, the post-hoc verbal-report findings associated with Nisbett and Wilson, and subjects' misplaced confidence that they would detect changes they demonstrably do not — to show that introspective reports are frequently fabricated after the fact and are therefore broadly unreliable as guides to underlying mental processes.

5. I hold that there are facts about what it is like to be a subject that outstrip everything the subject could or would report even under ideal prompting: phenomenology outruns the heterophenomenological text.

6. I am nonetheless confident that neuroscientific evidence will eventually vindicate most of everyday phenomenology — that the majority of these fictions will turn out to be nonfiction, with real referents in the brain.

Please audit this set rather than explain Dennett to me. Identify which pairs of commitments stand in genuine tension and which merely appear to, and order the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what abandoning that commitment costs elsewhere in the set. Be careful to distinguish flat contradiction from tension that a careful reformulation dissolves; I suspect at least one pair in this set that looks inconsistent is not, and that at least one that looks benign is not. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the resulting package is one a serious defender of a third-person science of consciousness could actually hold.
