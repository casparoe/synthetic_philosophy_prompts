I teach philosophy, and this term I've been sitting in on a colleague's public policy seminar where a lot of the material is cost-benefit analysis as actually practiced by government agencies. The thing that keeps striking me is the pattern of when agencies trust behavior and when they trust what people say. To put a dollar value on preventing a death, they mostly use what workers reveal in labor markets — the extra wages people demand for taking riskier jobs. To value cleaner air, they look at how much more people pay for houses in less polluted neighborhoods. But when there's no behavior to observe — say people valuing the continued existence of some obscure species they'll never visit — agencies fall back on surveys asking hypothetical willingness-to-pay, and everyone in the literature seems embarassed by that route, treating it as a tolerated fallback rather than real evidence. So I'd like to interrogate the presuppositions of the behavioral route, the one treated as the gold standard.

What theory of welfare or preference does that practice actually presuppose? An economist would probably say it just "respects preferences," but the inference from choices to preferences seems to smuggle in a lot: that each person has a single stable preference ordering that carries over intact from the labor market to the housing market; that a choice reveals a preference rather than a belief, e.g. a perception of risk that might be wildly mistaken; that the choice wasn't shaped by the worker simply having no realistic alternative to taking the dangerous job. And there's the awkward fact that willingness-to-pay scales with income, so the practice seems to presuppose either that this is fine because welfare just is preference satisfaction, distribution be damned, or something else that needs spelling out.

So my questions: can you reconstruct the implicit theory in enough detail to say which rival views of welfare it quietly rules out (hedonism, objective-list accounts, preference views that screen for adaptive or non-autonomous preferences)? Is the stated-vs-behavioral tension a genuine inconsistency in the practice's implicit epistemology — survey answers are behavior too, after all — or is there a principled line that justifies the differential trust? And finally, if an agency took seriously the idea that preferences are inferred rather than read off, in the way people in machine learning talk about inverse reinforcement learning, where you need a model of the agent's rationality before the inference even gets started, what concretely would have to change in the practice itself? Please answer in American English, and don't just gesture at "revealed preference" — I want the actual commitments, laid out.
