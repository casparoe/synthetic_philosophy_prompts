So I've been reading Ignatius Donnelly's Atlantis: The Antediluvian World (1882) for a seminar on pseudohistory and demarcation, and there's a bit near the start that I want to unpack properly. After listing his thirteen propositions, he defends the whole project like this:

"There is an unbelief which grows out of ignorance, as well as a scepticism which is born of intelligence. The people nearest to the past are not always those who are best informed concerning the past. For a thousand years it was believed that the legends of the buried cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum were myths: they were spoken of as 'the fabulous cities.' For a thousand years the educated world did not credit the accounts given by Herodotus of the wonders of the ancient civilizations of the Nile and of Chaldea. He was called 'the father of liars.' Even Plutarch sneered at him. Now, in the language of Frederick Schlegel, 'the deeper and more comprehensive the researches of the moderns have been, the more their regard and esteem for Herodotus has increased.' Buckle says, 'His minute information about Egypt and Asia Minor is admitted by all geographers.' There was a time when the expedition sent out by Pharaoh Necho to circumnavigate Africa was doubted, because the explorers stated that after they had progressed a certain distance the sun was north of them; this circumstance, which then aroused suspicion, now proves to us that the Egyptian navigators had really passed the equator."

I get the surface point (experts have been wrong to dismiss stuff before, so maybe they're wrong about Atlantis too), but I feel like there's more going on here that I want to spell out for my paper. Can you walk me through the actual argumentative structure of this passage? Like, is it an appeal to past vindications (the "they laughed at Galileo too" move), or is it doing something slightly more subtle by trying to shift where the burden of proof sits -- making dismissal itself look like a symptom of ignorance? Also, where exactly does the reasoning break down, i.e. what's the relevant difference between Herodotus getting partially rehabilitated and Donnelly's Atlantis? I want to say something like: the Herodotus and Necho cases were vindicated by *new independent evidence*, whereas Donnelly is trying to get the same epistemic credit in advance, based only on the pattern of past vindications, but I'm not 100% sure that's the cleanest way to put the disanalogy. It also occured to me that Donnelly never actually tells you what would count as evidence against Atlantis, and maybe that's the real tell. Would love your help sorting out which of these lines of critique is doing the most work, and whether there's any charitably respectable version of his "converging lines of light" method that we shouldn't just wave away as crankery.
