I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a feature about how people choose spouses (or, these days, swipe for them) in an era of effectively endless options — dating apps promising "the best match" rather than "a good enough match" — and the piece keeps bumping into Herbert Simon's old distinction between maximizing and satisficing: the idea that a rational agent with limited time, limited information, and limited computational capacity does better by settling for the first option that clears a threshold than by searching for the optimum. The literary example I can't get out of my head is Charlotte Lucas in Pride and Prejudice. She's twenty-seven, plain, and portionless; she accepts Mr. Collins within days of Elizabeth refusing him, and she explains herself quite coolly: she's not romantic, she asks only a comfortable home. On Simon's terms she looks like the textbook satisficer — a fast, decisive threshold-satisfier who correctly prices her own market constraints — while Elizabeth, who can afford to reject a respectable establishment on the grounds that happiness in marriage is a matter of chance, looks like a maximizer whose search costs are subsidized by her father's income.

But I suspect the neat mapping falls apart, and that's what I'd like you to work out for me. Is Charlotte really satisficing, or is she doing something the decision-science framing flattens — namely maximizing, but over a different objective function, one that prices in security, spinsterhood risk, and her sisters' prospects, so that Collins is not "good enough" but in fact optimal given her constraints? And is Simon's point not that satisficers settle for less, but that "optimal given constraints" is the only kind of optimal there is — in which case Elizabeth isn't a maximizer either, just an agent with a laxer stopping rule and a bigger budget? There's also the uncomfortable middle of the novel, where Charlotte arranges her household — the carefully chosen sitting room, the encouragement of Collins's gardening — to minimize her exposure to her own husband. Is that a picture of contented satisficing, or does Austen show us the costs that the satisficing literature, with its surveys about maximizer regret, never measures? And where does the reading break down: Elizabeth's eventual acceptance of Darcy after her priors get revised by the letter is arguably the most rational act of belief-updating in the book, which fits neither the satisficer nor the maximizer box.

I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the actual decision theory seriously: where satisficing illuminates Charlotte, where it distorts her, and what Austen dramatizes about choice under constraint that Simon's papers don't — say, about how a social order sets the thresholds people satisfice against, so that Charlotte's stopping rule isn't hers at all. If it helps, tell me which scenes to foreground and which to avoid (my editor wants the piece accessible to readers who know the novel only from screen adaptations), and be honest if the satisficing frame is a clever gimmick rather than a genuine lens. The piece runs long-form, so I can sustain a nuanced argument, but I need to leave the reader with something sharper than "she settled, and that's okay."
