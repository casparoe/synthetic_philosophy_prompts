I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about what Darwin himself actually thought about morality and religion -- not what activists on either side claim he thought, which is a totally different thing. Before I write anything I want to fact-check the summary I've been carrying around in my head, which comes from a popular history of Victorian science I read a couple years back and honestly from years of half-remembered profile pieces.

The picture I've got goes roughly like this: Darwin was at bottom a materialist who believed natural selection explains everything about us, including conscience; he thought the moral sense is just an evolved instinct like any other, so there's nothing specially authoritative about it; he lost his faith basically because evolution disproved design, and he called himself an agnostic mostly for social respectability; and he regarded religion as a tribal artifact that would be selected out as humans matured. Which means, on my summary, that his project was a package deal: if you accept natural selection you're signing on to all of that.

I have my doubts. I remember hearing somewhere that Darwin kept a basically warm view of some religious people (his wife, for one), that he was careful in print about the whole subject, and that the story of how he lost his faith is more tangled than "evolution killed it" -- something about his daughter's death being a bigger blow to him intellectually than the theory was. And I have a vague sense that the moral sense story in the Descent of Man is more interesting than "conscience is just an instinct," something about social instincts and memory and language all having to come together, which isn't the same as one heritable module. But I don't trust my memory here.

So, how much of my summary would a historian of ideas who knows Darwin's actual writings accept? Go claim by claim and tell me what's roughly right, what's an oversimplification, and what Darwin would flatly deny -- and then the big one: does the conclusion follow? If the corrected picture is more complicated than the package-deal story, what does that mean for how I should frame the piece? I'd rather not write the ten-thousandth "Darwin the crusading atheist" or "Darwin the secret believer" article, and I suspect both framings are wrong for related reasons. Also worth knowing: which parts of my summary are contested even among the experts, so I don't replace my folk version with someone else's confident folk version.
