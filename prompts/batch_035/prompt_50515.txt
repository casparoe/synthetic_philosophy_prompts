My friend and I spent most of last night on Gödel's ontological argument — the modal one, not Anselm's — and I'd like your honest credence that the argument is sound, together with the reasoning behind the number. Let me set the stage so the question is precise.

By "the argument" I mean the version Gödel circulated in the early 1970s and Dana Scott later transcribed and slightly reformulated: working in second-order quantified modal logic with S5, take "positive property" as a primitive, assume that whether a property is positive doesn't depend on the accidents of the actual world (positivity is itself necessary), assume the positives are closed under entailment, assume the conjunction of any collection of positives is positive, assume necessary existence is a positive property, and define God as the possessor of every positive property. From these one derives first that God's existence is possible, then, via the S5 machinery, that God exists necessarily.

My friend's credence that this argument is sound — valid in S5 with true axioms — is around 0.3. Mine is around 0.03, and what I'd like from you is your own number with a genuine decomposition rather than a hedge. I have in mind at least these terms, and I'd welcome others you think belong:

1. That the formalization is faithful and the derivation valid, setting aside whether the axioms are true. I'd have thought this is close to 1, though my friend insists some of the closure axioms do subtle work I'm glossing over.
2. That "positive property" is a coherent primitive at all. The axioms treat positivity as a very special operator: closed under entailment in one direction, and rich enough that every positive is jointly instantiable. Is there any independent grasp of what this operator is supposed to mean, or does the notion only get content from the role the axioms assign it?
3. That the axioms are plausible on grounds that don't simply amount to wanting the conclusion. The axiom that necessary existence is positive seems to me the worst offender: necessary existence is exactly the feature you need the conclusion's subject to have for the S5 step to work, which makes the axiom look like the conclusion wearing a hat. My friend counters that necessary existence is plausibly a "great-making" property in the traditional perfect-being-theology sense, so the axiom is a substantive claim from natural theology rather than a rigging. I'm not sure I find that independent support rather than a restatement.
4. That the best-known structural objection — modal collapse, the theorem that the axiom set entails that whatever is true is necessarily true — is not a decisive reductio. My friend's response was the one I found most interesting: she's willing to run the argument in a weaker modal logic where collapse fails, conceding that some of the proof's steps need reexamination there, rather than accept that we live in a world where nothing could have been otherwise. Whether that rescue preserves anything worth calling the argument is exactly where our disagreement got heated.
5. That the conclusion, even if everything above goes through, states something it's appropriate to call the existence of God. As far as I can tell we get an entity that necessarily possesses every positive property, with no uniqueness theorem worth the name and no route to personhood, omniscience, or concern for us — the axioms simply don't speak to those. My friend thinks this is a separate and secondary question from soundness; I think it bears on how much work "positive" is doing and hence indirectly on term 2.

There was one further exchange I want to put to you. I argued that the whole construction is best seen as a dressed-up fixed-point argument: define an operator that collects entailment-closed positives, show the definition has a witness guaranteed by the closure and joint-instantiability assumptions, and the "divine" conclusion drops out of the abstract structure rather than from anything recognizably theological. My friend thought this was a cheap deflation: on her view the analogy fails unless positivity is monotone in some substantive sense, and nothing in the axioms gives you that. I admit I hadn't worked out what would make the analogy load-bearing.

So: give me your credence that the argument is sound as I've stated it, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each term above (plus any terms you'd add), then combine them and say which single term the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me whether my friend at 0.3 and I at 0.03 most plausibly disagree about the coherence of the positivity primitive, the independence of the axioms, the force of modal collapse, or something I haven't listed — and what argument or result, if any, would move your own number most.
