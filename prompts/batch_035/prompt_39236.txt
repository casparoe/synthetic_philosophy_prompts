I have recently been appointed to the publications review panel of a regional historical society, and the panel's first task is a contentious one: deciding how to handle submissions and forum posts that dispute established accounts of the Holocaust, the Nanjing massacre, and the Armenian catastrophe — some of which are crankery, some of which are legitimate historiography, and some of which are genuinely hard to place. Before our first meeting I would like a principled instrument to work from rather than a set of gut feelings.

Please build me a taxonomy in the form of a decision tree that classifies a piece of historical writing about these events — an article, a forum post, a self-published pamphlet — by the kind of claim it makes and how it argues for that claim, rather than by the author's motives, affiliations, or reputation. Every branch node should be a question a careful reader could answer from the text alone, without knowing which category the answer leads to: for instance, what the author does when confronted with a document that counts against their thesis, or whether the author demands a standard of proof for claims they dislike that they do not apply to claims they prefer. Build the tree from the logical space first — which claim is disputed (occurrence, scale, intent, classification, context, moral framing) and how the argument is conducted — and only then locate the actual positions as leaves: honest archival revision; denialism in the strict sense; contextualist apologetics that concedes the facts while shifting blame onto the victims; distortion by exaggeration or inflation; and the earnest amateur who repeats a denialist figure without knowing its provenance. Where a combination of node-answers corresponds to no leaf, say so, and say whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two constraints. First, the tree must not smuggle its conclusion into its nodes: in ordinary historiography revisionism is simply what historians do, so no node may amount to asking whether the author revises the received account at all. Second, the tree must earn its keep on the hard cases, so please test it explicitly against the following: the downward revision of the Auschwitz death toll in the early 1990s from the four-million figure of the Soviet-era memorial plaques to roughly 1.1–1.5 million on the basis of archival work — a revision that mainstream historians accept and that denialists also cite approvingly; the death toll of the Nanjing massacre, where serious historians disagree by close to an order of magnitude; the dispute over whether the Armenian catastrophe satisfies the legal definition of genocide, where the disagreement concerns classification rather than occurrence or scale; and a correction running in the opposite direction, such as the postwar deflation of exaggerated atrocity propaganda from the First World War. If your tree classifies the Auschwitz revision and a denialist pamphlet citing that same revision identically, the tree has failed, and you should say so rather than paper over it.

Finally, tell me where the tree is most likely to misclassify a submission, which concrete case would force you to redesign it, and what consideration would change your mind about the branching order you chose.
