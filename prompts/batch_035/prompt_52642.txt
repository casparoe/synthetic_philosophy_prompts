I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long-form feature about what happens to political fairness when consequential decisions — lending, hiring, bail, benefit eligibility — are made by machine learning systems whose reasoning even their designers cannot fully trace. I'd like to center the piece on a counterfactual interview: John Rawls died in 2002, before any of this, and I want you to reconstruct what he would have said. Please answer in his intellectual voice, arguing from the positions and concepts he actually developed, and mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond anything he wrote rather than restating his views. Do not put anything in quotation marks as his words, and don't cite chapter or page numbers — argue from positions, not textual furniture.

Three questions for him, in escalating order of difficulty.

First, the publicity condition. A Theory of Justice insists that principles of justice be publicly known and accepted as binding by citizens — the terms of the basic structure cannot be an insider's secret. A society that allocates jobs and credit through systems nobody, including their operators, can explain seems to fail that at the level of mechanism. But Rawls might reply that publicity attaches to the principles governing an institution, not to every causal chain inside it: we accept medical decisions made by a body we don't individually audit. How would he draw the line, and on which side of it does an opaque model fall?

Second, the difference principle and automation. Assume productivity gains from AI accrue first as capital income to owners of the systems. Are those gains part of the social product such that inequalities in their distribution are permissible only insofar as they benefit the least advantaged — or is that a misuse of the difference principle, which governs the basic structure and not the returns to a particular technology? Relatedly: Rawls identifies the least advantaged by their bundle of primary goods. Where, in his scheme, does "understanding the reasons for decisions made about you" belong — is it a primary good, part of the fair value of the basic liberties, or neither?

Third, the question I most want him to answer, and the one I suspect he'd resist. Some AI laboratories now write fixed sets of principles — constitutions, in their jargon — chosen before deployment, before anyone knows who will gain or lose from the system, and train the model to conform to them. One researcher described this to me as a veil of ignorance for machines. Would Rawls accept this as a genuine application of the original position, or would he insist it is a category error — that the original position models persons choosing terms of association for a society they must then live in, stepping out from behind the veil and bearing the strains of commitment, whereas a principle written for a tool protects no one from anything and is only as just as the humans who wrote it? If it is a category error, is there nonetheless something in his framework — the idea of the basic structure evolving over time, or public reason — that tells us how such systems ought to be governed?

A closing constraint: Rawls explicitly declined to extend justice as fairness beyond persons — he thought we owed animals compassion but not justice. Have him confront that boundary directly. Does a decision system that increasingly performs the work of institutions become, for him, simply a new gadget of the basic structure, or does its quasi-agency force an amendment to his account that he would have refused to make? I need his answer to be recognizably his, not a friendly modernization.
