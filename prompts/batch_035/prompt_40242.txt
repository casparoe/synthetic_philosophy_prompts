I'm an undergrad writing my thesis on medieval trial by ordeal — the thing where an accused person would grab a hot iron or plunge their arm into boiling water, and if the burn healed cleanly, they were judged innocent. My advisor keeps striking out sentences where I describe the priests as "manipulating" outcomes or call the whole setup backwards compared to how we think about evidence. She says that's me smuggling in modern assumptions about what courts are for and what counts as rational, which people back then just didn't share.

Here's my problem though. The reason I picked this topic at all was a paper I read a while back, which argued that the priests had basically stumbled into something like a screening mechanism: since guilty people genuinely beleived in the ordeal (and expected God to expose them), they'd confess or settle beforehand, while the truly innocent would go through with it — so the priests only had to "administer" the ordeal to people who were probably innocent, and things like how the iron was heated and how the wound was inspected gave them plenty of room to steer the result. That whole analysis is built out of modern tools — incentives, selection effects, that kind of thing — and my advisor has no problem with it. She's fine with me importing modern *concepts* to explain what was going on, she just objects to modern *judgments* about it.

So is there actually a principled line there, or is it arbitrary? Like, "screening mechanism" isn't some neutral description — it frames the priests as strategic operators, which is already a pretty loaded way of seeing them. And if I have to drop the modern judgments because the medievals didn't share our standards, why don't I also have to drop the modern concepts, since they didn't share those either? It feels like pulling on one thread unravels the other, but I can't tell whether that's a real tension in my advisor's position or just me being lazy about the distinction. Am I actually allowed to describe the past with vocabulary the actors themselves wouldnt have recognized, and if so, what's the rule for when that's ok?
