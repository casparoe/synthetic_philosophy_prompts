For the past several years I have covered machine-learning research for a general-interest science magazine, and few arguments in that beat have proven as durable, or as unproductive, as the one about whether neural networks have representations. The immediate occasion is the wave of work in which researchers train sparse autoencoders to decompose a model's internal activations into thousands of interpretable directions — "features" for sarcasm, for security flaws in code, for sycophantic praise — reportedly finding more features than the network has neurons, a packing arrangement its proponents call superposition. On one side of the argument stand researchers I will call the realists: they hold that a trained network literally encodes features of the world, and that this line of work is in the business of discovering the network's own ontology. On the other side stand the deflationists: they hold that "representation" is a word researchers use, that a network's internal computation admits many equally serviceable decompositions, and that interpretability imposes a useful vocabulary on a network rather than finding one already there. In my reporting, the two camps turn out to agree about far more of the experiments than their polemics suggest, and to disagree about matters that no experiment as currently designed would settle.

Rather than publish another he-said-she-said feature, I would like to commission the document both camps could put their names to: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a realist and a deflationist about representations in neural networks. Please draft it with the following components.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. It should record what both camps accept about the empirical record — that linear probes can extract substantial information about inputs from internal activations; that intervening along certain activation directions reliably and somewhat selectively changes model behavior, as in the steering results; that models trained on apparently narrow tasks often develop internal structure responsive to facts beyond those tasks; and that interpretability has delivered genuine practical utility. Each of these should be phrased so that a deflationist could sign it without thereby conceding realism, and vice versa.

Second, a disagreements table. Each residual disagreement must be stated so that the side holding it would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own view: the realist's claims should be worded as claims about the network's own decomposition, not merely about a useful one, and the deflationist's as claims about underdetermination among decompositions, not as claims that interpretability is useless.

Third, a cruxes section. My reporting suggests at least three candidates: (a) whether there is a fact of the matter about a network's natural feature decomposition, or whether the choice among candidate decompositions is constrained only by pragmatic criteria such as sparsity and ease of human labeling; (b) whether successful steering shows that interpretability has located structures the network itself uses in its computations, or only that the network is broadly sensitive to directions in activation space — the difference between discovering a mechanism and constructing a control interface; and (c) whether convergence among independent methods — probing, mechanistic circuit analysis, sparse-autoencoder decompositions — would count as evidence that a real ontology has been discovered, and whether such convergence will in fact be observed as the methods mature. Add other cruxes you identify; and if you judge that one of my candidates is not a genuine crux, say so and explain why.

Fourth, for each crux, the concrete test, argument, or case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. The point of the exercise is precisely that these predictions are written down now, where they can embarrass their authors later.

Finally, close with a verdict on which parts of the dispute are verbal: state whether, once the empirical facts about information flow and manipulability are fixed, the two camps are left disagreeing only about whether words like "genuine" and "discovery" apply — and if so, identify exactly which residual disagreement that verbal residue attaches to and which parts survive as substantive.
