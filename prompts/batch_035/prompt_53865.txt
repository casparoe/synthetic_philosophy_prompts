so quick context: I write novels (midlist literary fiction, nothing glamorous) and I've somehow let the local book festival talk me into interviewing a philosopher who argues there is no self. and I mean no self at all -- not the soft "the self is socially constructed" version, but the hard claim that when you go looking for the owner of your experiences, all you find is the experiences, thoughts, sensations, one after another. he's coming from the buddhist anatta side of things but apparently also embraces the western bundle-theory lineage, and I gather he's fond of the chariot analogy from the Milindapaña, where the monk Nagasena tells a king that "Nagasena" is just a label for a pile of parts the way "chariot" is.

the problem: my next novel has a character loosely modeled on this guy, and I want the interview to actually produce good material, not just him restating the view for forty minutes. so draft me 8-10 questions that press on where the view is genuinely uncomfortable. I want at least one question each on:

- the "who gets liberated" problem -- if there's no one home, what is it that suffers, and what exactly is freed from the cycle? karma for whom?
- whether he can keep talking in first person ("I woke up early to catch my train") without quietly smuggling the self back in, and where exactly he thinks the conventional/ultimate two-truths move earns its keep rather than just changing the subject
- what happens to his position on Parfit-style thought experiments (teletransporters, fission cases) -- if identity is just psychological continuity with no further fact, is that agreeing with him or is there still a fight about whether reductionism about persons amounts to no-self?

for each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give (I expect a lot of "that's only a problem for the conventional self, and I never denied that one"). maybe also one question about what he'd say to the objection that this is all just semantics -- that everyone agrees there's no little homunculus, and denying *that* isn't interesting.

at the end, please tell me how confident you are in the above, and single out the question you're least sure about -- like, which one risks resting on a misreading of the buddhist material or of Parfit, since I really don't want to look dumb in front of the guy my next protagonist is based on.
