In my reading group on illusionism, one member keeps leaning on the desktop analogy: the icons, folders, and trash can on your screen are a user interface — indispensable for getting things done, hopeless as a guide to the machine's internals — and consciousness is the interface the brain gives us. She uses it to support three conclusions: (1) phenomenal properties are like the little picture of a folder, real as affordances but not as features of the underlying machinery; (2) asking what qualia are "really made of" is a category error, like asking for the pixel-color of a file on disk; and (3) neuroscience can never explain consciousness, because it is like trying to reverse-engineer the operating system from screen recordings alone. Audit the analogy as she uses it. Give the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the user, the screen, the icons, the drag-and-drop gesture, the technician who can open the case — then identify where the mapping breaks. Attend especially to the user: in the desktop case there is someone outside the interface who could in principle be told the truth about the circuitry, whereas in the consciousness case we seem confined to the interface, with no position outside it to be deceived from; also consider that some interface elements (a progress bar, a fan-speed readout) do track real features of the machine. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for each of her three conclusions from the ones that don't, say which conclusions survive and which the analogy was quietly carrying, and if the argument needs repair, say whether a better version of the comparison can do the work or whether the point requires a different tool altogether.
