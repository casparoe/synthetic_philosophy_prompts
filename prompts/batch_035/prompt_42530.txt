I'm part of a small evening reading group — none of us are academics, we're just people who enjoy history books — and last month we read a popular business history that retells the collapse of Eastman Kodak in the familiar way: complacent executives, too attached to film profits, blind to digital photography. I happened to mention that, as I understand it, one of Kodak's own engineers, Steve Sasson, built a prototype digital camera back in 1975, right inside the company. That set off a long argument between two members, Rosa and Theo, that neither could settle, and I couldn't referee it either. I've been chewing on it on my own since, and I'd like a considered verdict.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Theo said the whole "blind Kodak" story is hindsight at work: once we know a company failed, we can always weave a morality tale about complacency, whereas if Kodak had made the same choices and survived, we'd be praising it for protecting its core business. The story is manufactured by the outcome, not discovered in the evidence, and the fact that the digital camera was invented inside Kodak itself proves the blindness version is literally false. (2) Rosa answered that finding an explanation after the fact doesn't make it wrong — doctors, detectives, and accident investigators reconstruct causes backward all the time, and the test is whether the evidence supports the account, not when it was formed. She pointed to structural facts visible at the time: film's enormous margins, the poor early economics of digital, shareholder pressure to protect the profitable side of the business. (3) Theo replied that if it was all so visible, nearly everyone at Kodak and most of the industry wouldn't have misjudged it, and that explaining the misjudgment as "complacency" is circular — complacency is just the name we give to error once we know it was error, so you can't explain the error by naming it. (4) Rosa conceded part of this: for every famous failure narrative there is presumably some company that made a similarly risky bet and thrived, and nobody writes the mirror-image story. But she insisted her point wasn't circular, because you can identify the causes of the error independently — what kinds of projects got funded, who got promoted, what happened to anyone who pushed digital too hard internally — and those are observable facts, not just the outcome read backward. (5) Theo's closing move was that this still means detailed historical narratives mainly give us the feeling of understanding rather than understanding itself, and that history can at most teach tendencies and base rates, never why this particular outcome happened; Rosa's closing move was that "everything is just tendencies" gives up far too much, since "it was complicated" is not an alternative explanation, and without constructed narratives we would have nothing at all to learn from history.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the five steps, say who is right and where, what each of them is missing, and then give an overall verdict on who is more right. Along the way, I'd appreciate it if you kept two things apart: the findings about hindsight bias that are well established in psychology, and the genuinely contested questions about whether, or when, hindsight considerations actually discredit retrospective explanation in history. Please flag which claims are settled and which are matters of live disagreement among people who study this, and don't present a contested position as if it were consensus — I'm not a scholar, and I'd rather come away knowing where the uncertainty sits than with a false sense of closure.
