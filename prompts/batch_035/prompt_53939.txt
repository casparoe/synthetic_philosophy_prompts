For roughly fifteen years I have held, with growing confidence, that religion is a natural phenomenon in a strong sense: that its cross-cultural recurrence is best explained by ordinary facts about human cognition and social life — our hair-trigger detection of agency, the memorability of minimally counterintuitive concepts, ritual and costly commitment as devices for binding groups together — and that nothing about the phenomenon points to any reality beyond the natural one. I have also taken a further step: that this explanation partly deflates religious claims, since if belief in gods is what you get when such minds as ours run under such conditions as ours, that seems to be some evidence against there being anything answering to the belief. I have taken that step calmly, on other people's behalf, for years.

Two months ago my father died. At the funeral I had an experience I did not seek and cannot fully describe: a sense of his presence that was specific, addressed to me, and more vivid than anything my daydreams or memories ordinarily produce. I have since learned that experiences like this in the bereaved are common, temporally distributed across cultures in roughly similar forms, and often have the felt quality of communication rather than imagination. I notice something in myself that I did not expect: my view is no longer operating as a cool explanation. It now functions as a defensive instrument, something I reach for to keep the experience at arm's length before it can be considered at all. That may be fine — a well-confirmed theory should absorb new data — but it may also mean the view has stopped risking anything, and I cannot currently tell the difference from the inside.

So here is my request. Tell me what would actually count against my position, treating it as two separable claims: first, that religion is well explained by the byproduct-and-group-bonding package I sketched; second, that such an explanation is itself evidence against the truth of the religious claims it explains. For each claim I want the defeaters ranked, from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward: which features of bereavement-like and mystical experiences, which cross-cultural or historical patterns, which conceivable empirical findings about the origins and functions of religion would genuinely strain the explanatory story, and which would instead strain only the deflationary step. Include the possibility that the deflation generalizes too far — that if being produced by minds like ours under selection-like pressures counts against gods, it is unclear why it does not also count against mathematics, moral facts, or my own confidence in evolutionary explanation — and tell me whether that worry is fatal, awkward, or already answered somewhere in the literature I should know about. I would like an honest assessment of what a thoughtful defender of the naturalistic explanation would concede as counting against it, what a thoughtful critic would insist counts against it, and where those two lists come apart. Finally: given that my view can redescribe any recalcitrant experience as more cognitive machinery at work, is there any observation short of a miracle that could count against it — and if not, is that a defect in the view or just what a correct view of this phenomenon would look like? I would rather know that my position is insulated and true than keep it comfortable for reasons I haven't examined.
