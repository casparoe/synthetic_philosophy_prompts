I spent the better part of four decades in population ecology, most of it with a forestry research institute, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of ethics and welfare. One debate I have followed with particular interest concerns whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering, and I have come to believe the two camps in that debate talk past each other in ways that a structured joint statement could expose. I would like you to draft that statement, as it would be signed by both camps after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The first camp — call it the subtraction view — holds that destroying habitat reduces the number of wild animals alive, and that this is good for aggregate welfare: the average wild life, dominated as it is by short-lived invertebrates and small, fast-reproducing vertebrates whose offspring mostly die young, is more likely than not net-negative, so fewer wild animals means less suffering. The second camp — the turnover view — holds that destruction does not simply subtract animals from a ledger; it converts communities. Cleared land rarely stays empty: it is recolonised by early-successional species with high fecundity and near-total juvenile mortality, so total abundance can remain high or even rise while biomass falls. Meanwhile, the animals lost to destruction are disproportionately the long-lived ones whose lives even the subtraction view would concede are more plausibly positive. On this picture, a hectare of degraded scrub seething with short-lived animals may contain more suffering than the hectare of mature forest it replaced, not less.

Please produce a document with the following parts. First, a numbered list of claims both camps could sign — about what is known and unknown concerning wild animal welfare, about the demographic effects of disturbance, and about their shared ethical assumptions. Second, a set of precise statements of residual disagreement, each phrased in a way that the side holding it would accept as fair to its own view. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I suspect one of them is whether the average wild life is net-negative, and another is how disturbance trades off abundance against biomass and lifespan, but you should determine this rather than adopt my guesses. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or general line of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction, recorded in advance, of what that case would show. Finally, a short section stating whether any part of the dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, and if so, which part and why.

I would like one issue handled explicitly. The camps tend to use "habitat destruction" as a single notion, but it covers at least two very different fates: conversion to intensive human use, where the welfare of any introduced domestic animals arguably enters the same ledger, and mere abandonment to secondary succession, where the land fills with whatever colonises it. Please state whether the disagreement between the camps survives that distinction, shifts under it, or dissolves.

For what it is worth, I confess some sympathy for the turnover view — I have watched old fields support enormous autumn cohorts of granivorous rodents that crash hard each winter — but I want a statement both camps could actually put their names to, with the remaining disagreement made exact rather than argued away.
