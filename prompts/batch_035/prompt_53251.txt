Last term I sat in on a faculty reading group that went off the rails in an interesting way, and the exchange has stayed with me. The topic was nominally the familiar triad people draw between honor cultures, dignity cultures, and what the sociologists call victimhood cultures, and whether any of them is morally superior to the others. Two participants did most of the arguing; I mostly took notes.

First, Miriam argued that dignity culture is plainly superior: it ascribes worth to every person regardless of strength, rank, or gender, whereas honor culture is inherently ranked, keeps score through confrontation, and settles disputes by who can back a threat. She granted that duels and vendettas are colorful, but said the underlying logic is domination dressed up as principle.

Felix replied that the comparison cheats. Honor norms, he said, did their work where no state could enforce contracts or protect the weak, so honor functioned as a substitute for law: it made threats credible and promises enforceable without police or courts. Judged as a piece of institutional technology in weak-state settings, it isn't obviously inferior — it's a different solution to the same enforcement problem. And dignity culture's universalism, he added, is expensive: the claim that everyone counts equally only means anything if there are institutions capable of acting on it.

Miriam conceded the enforcement point partially but said it only works for the strong. Self-help enforcement rewards those who can plausibly fight, so the "substitute for law" argument quietly discounts exactly the people — women, the old, the physically frail — whom dignity culture exists to protect. A system of norms whose enforcement is reserved for the capable can't claim neutrality just by calling itself an alternative to the state.

Felix then shifted to the third case: victimhood culture, he said, is what emerges when aggrieved parties can't or won't confront each other and can't rely on authorities, so they appeal to third-party audiences instead. Crucially, he argued, this is only possible in a world of cheap documentation and broadcasting — it is neither self-help nor state enforcement but reputation warfare conducted through crowds. His conclusion was that there is no single scale of moral superiority at all: each culture is adapted to whatever enforcement is actually available, and claims of superiority smuggle in assumptions about which setting you happen to be standing in.

Miriam's closing move was that this dissolves the normative question into sociology, which she refused to accept. Cultures compete, she said: when functioning dignity institutions arrive in an honor-based setting, people abandon the older code — and if honor were genuinely just as good, that migration wouldn't happen. So revealed preference at least weakly favors dignity, whatever relativists say about adaptation.

They turned to me at the end, and I honestly had nothing. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific claim, where does each of them overreach, is anything important being missed by both, and — on the final pair of moves — is Felix's adaptationist relativism or Miriam's revealed-preference argument the stronger way to close?
