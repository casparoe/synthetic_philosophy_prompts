I teach intro metaphysics and I'm so tired of the Ship of Theseus being run the exact same way every semester: replace the planks one by one, rebuild the old planks into a rival ship, ask which is the real ship. My students have basically memorized the answer sheet before I finish setting it up. So I want a variant that separates three theories that are usually allies, and it has to do it with only ONE candidate survivor on the table — no rival ship, no doubles, so the classic "which one?" dilemma never even gets started.

The three theories, and the usual lineup: (1) the spatiotemporal-continuity view, (2) the Hobbes-style matter view, and (3) a four-dimensionalist view on which an object can have scattered temporal parts — including long stretches where it isn't doing the thing that defines its kind, the way people picture a cryonics patient still existing while suspended. In the textbook version of the puzzle, continuity and four-dimensionalism are on the same side (the gradually-repaired ship wins, the plank-reassembly loses) and only the Hobbesian dissents. I want a case that splits continuity away from the four-dimensionalist.

Required features of the scenario: the object gets disassembled for long enough that no continuous functioning path exists — years, not a weekend in drydock; the reassembly uses most but not all of the original matter, with the leftovers burned or turned into a garden bench so nobody can ever assemble a rival; and the rebuild is done by different people for a different purpose, e.g. a museum exhibit that will never actually be used for the object's original function. Keep it realistic and dull in the furniture sense — a family sloop, a refit yard, an estate auction, a warehouse, whatever. One paragraph. No teleporters, no demons, no magic.

After the scenario, walk through what each of the three views says and why they don't converge the way they usually do, identify the single feature of the case that's doing the work (my guess is the gap, but I might be wrong), and show what happens to the case if you remove that feature. Then give me the defender's move: how would a continuity theorist who gets the uncomfortable verdict bite the bullet, and does the bite seem tolerable or fatal?

Last thing, and this is the part where I actually need you to take sides: don't hand me three verdicts and a shrug and tell me it's underdetermined. You tell me whether it's the same boat, and why that answer is better than the other two. If you think my spec sheet can't all be satisfied at once — if a case with only one candidate survivor can't really split continuity from four-dimensionalism — then say which requirement you'd relax and build the best case you can anyway, rather than quietly fudging it. Also flag any spot where the scenario is sneaking in a stipulation that does the theories' work for them, since the whole point is that the verdicts have to fall out of the views, not out of how I stacked the deck. This is for a lecture, so the walkthrough should be something I can actually say out loud to twenty year olds without them glazing over, but dont dumb it down to the point where the interesting machinery dissapears.
