Small ask but it's been bugging me for months. I'm on the board of our county historical society (tiny town, volunteer gig, mostly retirees and me, the youngest by 30 years). We're redoing the interpretive plaque for the town's founding house, and we've hit a wall.

The story everyone here knows — and I mean everyone, it's in the school pageant, it's why the street is named what it is — is that our founder's house was a stop on the Underground Railroad, and there's a "hidden tunnel" in the cellar that runs out toward the creek. Last year a grad student doing a thesis on our county went through the actual archives and, well. The tunnel turns out to be a drainage culvert, put in around 1900. The Railroad story can't be traced past a brochure written for the 1976 bicentennial, by a committee that included the founder's great-granddaughter. Nothing in the letters, the deeds, the diaries — nothing.

At the board meeting, one of the older members waved this all off with "well, history is written by the victors." The vibe was: the paper record is what the powers that be chose to keep, the community's memory is what actually happened, so the grad student's archives prove nothing and the plaque should keep the story. Maybe even more firmly now, as an act of solidarity.

I'm sure that slogan is doing about five different jobs at once and I want to take it apart instead of just having feelings about it. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims "history is written by the victors" could be standing for — I'd guess something like: (a) a platitude that all accounts have a perspective; (b) a claim that the powerful physically suppressed or never created records, so the absence of a record isn't evidence the thing didn't happen; (c) a claim about what even gets counted as an event worth recording; (d) something much stronger, that there's no fact of the matter about the past apart from whoever's telling it, so community memory and the archive are just rival tellings with no referee. There are probably readings I haven't thought of, and I'd want those too.

For each reading, tell me: is it true, is it controversial, and — this is the part I actually need — does it support the board member's move, i.e. setting aside the archives and keeping the story on the plaque? Because my gut says some of the readings cut the other way. The 1976 brochure was written by the founder's family, who owned the mill and basically ran the town for a century — if anyone around here is "the victors," it's them, and the beloved memory might be the victors' story, not the community's. And the reading where there's no fact of the matter seems to cut against everyone equally, which wouldn't by itself tell me to keep the plaque as is.

So: the readings, verdicts on each, and which of them, if any, actually get you "memory over the record" in a case like ours. If the honest answer is that none of them do, I'd rather hear that than a pep talk — I still have to look the grad student in the eye at the diner.
