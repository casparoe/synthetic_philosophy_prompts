i write novels for a living, mostly multigenerational family stuff where what the grandparents did keeps structuring the grandchildren's chapters. lately i've been reading the reparations-for-slavery debate (also the colonial variants, same arguments mostly) and i keep noticing how much of it is vocabulary i already own, just pointed at the real world. help me test that hunch.

restate the main positions on reparations in the vocabulary of craft — backstory, retcon, continuity errors, POV chapters, the unreliable narrator, what a character has "earned" by the final chapter, whether a book owes its reader closure. i'm fluent in all of that, i teach workshops on it, so don't water it down.

translate these one at a time, and tell me when my proposed translation is cheating:

- the straightforward restitution view: a wrong was committed, value was extracted, it's still sitting in the family, the estate, the nation, like an inherited object, and should be returned. my instinct is "inheritance as plot object," the gun in the drawer from chapter one. does that hold up?
- the nonidentity objection: the people who'd be compensated today wouldn't exist but for the wrong, so what exactly is being compensated? this feels like the "no retcons" constraint — the past of a story fixes who the characters even are — but the translation either doesn't click or clicks so easily i distrust it.
- the counterfactual-beneficiary argument: figure out where descendants would be now had the wrong not happened, and the gap between that and reality is what's owed. in my world that's the "shadow draft," the version of the manuscript where things went differently, and every novelist knows those drafts are a lie we tell ourselves about how the book could have gone.
- the forward-looking / restorative account: reparations as whatever repairs the community now rather than whatever repays the past. that's "the backstory only matters for what it does to the present chapter," except every novelist also knows that line is a cop-out when you're using it to avoid writing the flashback.

then the residue report, which is the part i actually care about: what survives the translation cleanly, what comes across only distorted, and what can't be said at all in the terms fiction uses for its own past. and then the real question. my editor keeps telling me readers need "closure," and politicians keep saying the country needs "closure," and i've begun to suspect one of these groups is using the word more honestly than the other. so: does my vocabulary smuggle in assumptions — that the past exists to serve the present, that anything can be revised in the next draft, that a debt is just unfinished plot — that make the reparations debate look easier than it is? or is the residue evidence that the philosophers were borrowing from me all along, that "debt to the past" and "closure" were narrative metaphors from the start, and maybe not load-bearing ones? if some of the debate genuinely has no counterpart in craft-speak, i want to know whether that's my vocabulary missing something or their argument being less clear than it looked.
