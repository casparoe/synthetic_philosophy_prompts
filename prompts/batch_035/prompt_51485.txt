I've been reading about Libet's experiments — the ones where a readiness potential appears in the brain a few hundred milliseconds before subjects report having decided to flex a wrist, with follow-up studies extending the finding to other simple spontaneous movements. As I understand it, the results show that unconscious processes initiate our actions before we're aware of willing them, so conscious will never initiates behavior; at best it watches what the brain has already set in motion. Libet's suggestion that consciousness might still veto an action in progress seems to close no escape route, since the veto would itself be an action, and by the same logic it too would have to be unconsciously initiated. If that's right, the difference between something I consciously chose to do and something I was merely caused to do has no neurological basis, and I don't understand why criminal law, and most philosophers, carry on treating actions as voluntary as though nothing had happened. Is there something about the experiments themselves I'm misreading — the timing of the awareness reports, or what the readiness potential actually measures — or is the distinction between free and unfree action simply outside the reach of this kind of science? I'd rather be told plainly that I've misunderstood the findings than be given a legal-philosophical answer that assumes the experiments away.
