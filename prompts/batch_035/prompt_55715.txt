I'm taking an intro epistemology course, and last week we reached Descartes. During discussion, a classmate used 'I think, therefore I am' to do quite a lot of work in a short time: first to establish that something remains certain even under the most radical doubt, then that his own mind is more certain to him than the physical world, and finally — in an aside about whether an AI could ever be conscious — that any genuine thinker must be capable of recognizing its own existence with absolute certainty, something he claimed no program could do because a program is just executing instructions and has nothing it is like to be it. Our professor wrote only 'cogito' on the board and said the rest of the course would turn on what exactly that little argument is supposed to establish, but we ran out of time. I've been chewing on it since, and I suspect the slogan is quietly doing several distinct jobs at once — some trivially fine, some much stronger and much less obviously true. It seems one thing to say that an episode of thinking is occurring while I doubt everything else, including my body, my memories, even the language I'm thinking in, and quite another to conclude that there is an enduring self, a mental substance, that persists between thoughts and is better known than tables and chairs. A friend in the seminar pushed back with something she called the 'it thinks' objection: that the argument at most licenses saying that thinking is happening, the way we say it is raining without there being anyone who is doing the raining, and that the 'I' gets smuggled in afterwards.

Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring to seminar, the different precise claims 'I think, therefore I am' might be advancing — from the weakest defensible reading (something about the mere occurrence of a mental episode in the present moment of doubting) through intermediate readings (what kind of self, if any, is established, and whether minds really are epistemically prior to material things) up to the strongest ones (that the mind is a thinking substance whose whole essence is thought, or that one has infallible knowledge of the contents of one's own mind)? For each reading, please say whether the argument actually secures it, whether it's plausible even setting the argument aside, and — this is the part I care most about — which of my classmate's three uses it would genuinely support. In particular, I want to know whether any reading underwrites his claim that a machine could never count as a thinker, or whether everything the cogito accomplishes is tied to a first-person standpoint that a machine could in principle occupy too. Relatedly, does the 'it thinks' objection threaten all of the readings or only the stronger ones? And is the 'therefore' even supposed to function as a logical inference from premises, or is it better understood as a kind of performative showing — something that works only when enacted in the first person, like vowing rather than deducing? A structured list, with a verdict on each reading, would be ideal.
