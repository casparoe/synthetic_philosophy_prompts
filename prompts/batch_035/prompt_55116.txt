so I'm a phd student in phil language and I'm drafting a position statement for our Davidson seminar — everyone has to write up where they stand before we hit the essays on radical interpretation and belief, and my advisor told me to sanity check it actually hangs together before I present it to the group. now that I've got it all written down in one place I'm honestly not sure it does. here's roughly where I stand:

(1) interpretationism: meaning and belief are whatever a radical interpreter, starting from scratch with only causal/behavioral evidence, could in principle extract — this isn't just an epistemology of meaning, it's the constitutive story about what meaning and belief are. (2) charity, and a fairly strong version: to interpret anyone at all you have to take most of their beliefs to be true by your own lights, and this isn't a useful heuristic, it's a condition on there being interpretation at all. (3) Quinean indeterminacy: all possible behavioral evidence underdetermines translation — there can be multiple incompatible manuals that fit the data equally well, and no further fact that decides between them. (4) first-person authority: I know what I believe without observing myself or making inferences from evidence, and that access is genuinely asymmetric with an interpreter's access to my beliefs. (5) skeptical scenarios are perfectly coherent: an envatted brain that says 'there's a tree in front of me' has mostly false beliefs, and it's nonetheless fully interpretable — it has meanings and beliefs, just mostly wrong ones.

for context, part of what got me actually thinking hard about this is a side piece I've been poking at about whether the radical interpretation frame applies to LLMs — like whether charity is doing any real work when we take an LLM's output 'I believe the answer is 42' as a belief attribution, and whether indeterminacy is even worse there — but don't let that derail the main question, it's mostly a fun motivation.
