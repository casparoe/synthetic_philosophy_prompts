I am a junior at a large public high school, and during our school's peer-instruction week I have been asked to lead a fifty-minute session for sophomores on the tragedy of the commons and its relationship to public goods. The sophomores will have had one semester of civics but no economics, and they arrive, I suspect, with two instincts: that environmental problems are caused by bad or greedy people, and that the obvious solution is for someone in authority to just make a rule. My own understanding is decent at the level of intuition, but I am finding it surprisingly hard to decide what the explanation's skeleton should be, because the concept seems to lean on other concepts — rivalry, exclusion, the difference between a shared pasture and a genuinely public good like national defense — and I do not know which of those must be put in place first for the tragedy to feel inevitable rather than merely sad.

Rather than explain the topic to me, I would like a plan for how I should explain it to them. Specifically: what order the pieces should go in and what depends on what; which single concrete example should carry the whole session (I am torn between the classic grazing pasture, the office shared refrigerator at my mother's workplace, and the school's own poorly maintained shared bathrooms, and I would like a principled reason to pick one — including what the example must have and what it must lack, since I suspect some candidates quietly fail to be commons at all); and the misconceptions I should expect the sophomores to form, in roughly the order they will form them, with the exact point in my sequence where each should be headed off. I am guessing the big ones are that the tragedy is about individual moral failure rather than structure, that the only two escape routes are privatization and top-down state control, and that any resource held in common is therefore doomed — but I would like to know what I have not anticipated, and how to plant the counterexample of self-governing commons without turning the session into a lecture on institutional design.

Finally, since fifty minutes is shorter than it sounds: tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I am inclined to skip the formal payoff matrix, the terminology of excludability matrices, and climate change as the framing case, since it tends to make fifteen-year-olds either despair or argue — and give me one question I could ask at the end whose answer would show me they understood the structure of the problem rather than merely having felt bad about overfishing. I would also appreciate a note on one short small-group exercise I could run in the middle of the session to let them feel the incentive pull themselves before I name it, provided it can be done with pencil and paper and does not collapse into a debate about who cheated.
