Retired immunologist here -- 35 years of lab work, mostly on B cell maturation, and only got into philosophy after my wife passed and I needed somethign to do with my evenings. I've been reading Dennett on the four kinds of creatures (Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian) and I have a suspicion I want someone to poke at.

The textbook distinction between Skinnerian and Popperian creatures is trials-out-in-the-world versus trials-run-in-the-head before acting. But that seems too coarse to me. The immune system I spent my career on does clonal selection: the body generates antibodies essentially at random, the ones that bind survive and get amplified, and this whole Darwinian search is happening *inside* an animal that is itself learning operantly from consequences. So you've got a Darwinian creature nested in a Skinnerian one, and neither level knows anything about the other. Meanwhile a game-playing program (or I gather some robots) will sample a bunch of hypothetical actions, simulate them crudely, and only act on the best -- but the "hypotheses" are generated blind, mutation-style, and the ones that fail just get discarded. Is that Popperian insight or Darwinian search on a faster clock? I genuinely can't tell where the line is, or if there is one.

My own hunch, and here's where I want your judgment: the four-way distinction might really be about *who pays for a failed hypothesis*. Darwinian: the genome pays, with the organism's death. Skinnerian: the body pays, with wasted effort or injury. Popperian: the simulation pays -- it's cheap, throwaway. Gregorian: someone else pays entirely, because you borrowed their tool or culture and you only burn reputation. If that reframing is right, then the immune system and the sampling game-player are the same kind of thing, just with different currencies of payment. But maybe I'm flattening something Dennett thought was important.

Please explain the whole business in a way that would let me decide whether my cost-of-error framing is a genuine insight or just an old lab guy pattern-matching to experimental costs. Be honest about where Dennett's own scheme is loose -- I'd rather hear the scheme creaks than get a tidy defense of it.
