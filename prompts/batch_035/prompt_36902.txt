Longtime lurker and occasional commenter on LessWrong here (going back to the mid-2010s), and I've noticed 'information wants to be free' making a real comeback in arguments about releasing the weights of frontier AI systems. Most recently, in a discussion of whether a lab should publish the weights of a GPT-4-class model, someone invoked the slogan and treated it as decisive: restricting access to weights is swimming against the current of history, so the lab's real choice is between releasing them now and being rendered irrelevant. I'm fairly confident the slogan is doing less work than that, but I can't articulate exactly how much less, and I'd like to be able to before the next thread.

Please give me a menu of the distinct precise claims 'information wants to be free' could be expressing in this context, laid out as a numbered list ordered from weakest to strongest. For each reading, say what it takes 'information' and 'free' to mean (bits vs. weights vs. human knowledge; zero price vs. legal freedom vs. impossibility of containment), and give a verdict: near-tautological once spelled out, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false. Candidates worth separating, at least as I see it: that digital goods have near-zero marginal cost of copying; that once weights leak anywhere, containment is permanently off the table; that censorship and secrecy have historically lost to duplication; that openness in the long run outcompetes closed systems economically; and the flatly normative reading that restricting access is wrong. If you see other readings hiding in the one-liner, add them.

Then, crucially, tell me which of the defensible readings actually entail that a single lab should publish its frontier weights in the near term, as opposed to merely entailing weaker conclusions — e.g., that once weights leak, hoarding them buys little, or that many actors will eventually have frontier capabilities regardless of any one lab's policy. Those weaker conclusions leave the lab's 2027 decision open in ways the slogan-quoter didn't seem to notice. I'd also like you to flag explicitly whether any reading purports to derive a normative conclusion (publish!) from a purely descriptive premise (bits are cheap to copy), because I suspect that's where the rhetorical force is coming from, and rationalist norms against is-ought slides should apply even when we like the conclusion.

Finally, since the same thread also contained the claim that open weights help defenders as much as attackers, note where that empirical premise — an offense-defense balance claim — would need to plug into each reading for the reading to support publication, and whether any of them survive if the balance actually favors offense.

Please keep the whole response under 600 words, and number the readings so I can reply to them individually in the thread.
