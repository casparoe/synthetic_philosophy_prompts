I should say upfront that I have no academic background in philosophy. A few years ago I began puzzling on my own over questions about our obligations to people who do not yet exist — climate worries started it — and I now help run a small online self-study group in which we set one another written questions and exchange feedback. We deliberately invent our own examples rather than using well-known textbook cases, so that nobody can coast on half-remembered reading.

The most recent question I set concerned Parfit's nonidentity problem, and one member's answer has left me unsure how to mark it. I am not confident in my own judgment on the finer points, and I would like an independent evaluation before I return it with comments.

The question I set was:

"Suppose a couple can conceive now or wait eighteen months. Conceiving now means their child will have a chronic but treatable condition — persistent mild pain kept in check by cheap daily medication — whereas waiting would produce a different, healthy child. Both children would have lives clearly worth living, and the couple knows all this in advance. (1) Explain why it is surprisingly difficult to show that conceiving now harms or wrongs the child who is born with the condition. (2) Does Parfit's impersonal strategy — judging outcomes by how good they are rather than by whom they are good or bad for — dissolve the difficulty, and at what cost? (3) Does the problem get easier, harder, or stay the same when only one or a few identifiable individuals are involved, rather than large anonymous future populations?"

The member's answer, reproduced in full:

"The couple's case is hard because who exists depends on when they conceive. If they wait, the child conceived now never exists at all. So the child with the condition is not worse off than he or she would otherwise have been, since the only alternative for that very child is nonexistence. This is Parfit's nonidentity problem: some choices affect not just the welfare of future people but which future people there are.

The child is not harmed, because the child's life is worth living. A life of managed mild pain is clearly a life worth living, so conceiving now does not harm the child. On the standard person-affecting view — that an act can be wrong only if it is worse for someone — conceiving now therefore cannot wrong the child. (This is also why it is not wrong to conceive when you know the child will have a life of constant agony that is barely worth living; the nonidentity problem is the problem of such cases.)

Parfit's way out is to stop evaluating acts purely in terms of who they affect. Impersonal principles say that an outcome matters regardless of who enjoys it. So we compare the total happiness of the outcome in which the couple waits with the outcome in which they conceive now; a healthy child contains more happiness than the same child with a chronic condition, so the impersonal view tells the couple to wait, and the difficulty dissolves completely, with no one left unaccounted for. There is no serious cost to this move. Impersonal views like total utilitarianism have no embarrassing implications elsewhere, and Parfit himself accepted total utilitarianism for exactly this reason.

On the third question, the problem gets easier when the individuals are identifiable. With large anonymous future populations, you cannot say who exactly is wronged, because you do not know who will exist in which outcome. With one couple and one child, you can point at the actual person. The difficulty in the anonymous case comes from uncertainty about which people exist in which outcome, so removing the anonymity removes the force of the nonidentity issue. If anything, this suggests the problem is really a puzzle about large-scale policy and not about what individuals owe to the particular people they create."
