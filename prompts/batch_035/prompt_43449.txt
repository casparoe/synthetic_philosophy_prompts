I am a PhD student in metaphysics writing a dissertation on counterpart theory, and for the past year and a half I have sat in on a philosophy of religion workshop that has become stuck, with no visible progress, on one question: whether penal substitution, as an account of the atonement, is coherent. The workshop's participants are sophisticated, the literature on both sides is well known to them, and yet nothing has moved. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the doctrine, and I will offer my own hypothesis, though I hold it only as a hypothesis.

The debate, concretely, runs as follows. The doctrine holds that the punishment due to humanity for sin was borne by Christ, who was innocent. The workshop's critics press three objections. First, the transfer objection: guilt is not the sort of thing that can be reassigned from one person to another, so that punishing one person for another's crime is the paradigm of injustice, and redescribing it as "satisfying the law's demands" changes the label without changing the act. Second, the proxy disanalogy: the defenders' stock reply is that a third party can pay my fine without injustice, so a substitute can bear my punishment; the critics answer that a fine is a debt, and debt is by its nature transferable, whereas punishment is indexed to the offender — being punishable is part of what it is to be guilty — so "punishing a substitute" is a contradiction in terms rather than a mercy. Third, the forgiveness dilemma: either the transaction is necessary, in which case God does not forgive but merely collects, or God genuinely forgives, in which case the transaction is unnecessary. The defenders reply that this is a false dilemma because satisfaction and forgiveness operate at different levels — one restores an objective order, the other applies it to particular persons — and that the critics are conflating them. Every one of these moves has been made, answered, and re-made; the positions in the room are exactly where they were eighteen months ago.

Here is my hypothesis, for what it is worth. In a late paper, David Lewis — the modal realist, writing outside his usual territory — offered a reconstruction of penal substitution using counterpart theory, and I think the reconstruction locates the real fault line. As I understand it, the trick is that "punishment is due from x" can be read two ways: as an intrinsic, monadic property of x, or as a relational fact about how the bearer of suffering is related to the guilty party. The critics' objections all presuppose the intrinsic reading — on which guilt is not transferable because there is nothing to transfer it relative to — while the defenders' replies quietly help themselves to the relational reading, and each side treats the reading that favors its current point as if it were the only reading there is. The critics then correctly observe that the defenders' position is inconsistent, and the defenders correctly observe that the critics' objections never touch the position as the defenders actually hold it. My hypothesis, then, is that the workshop's disagreement is not primarily a moral one about justice at all, but a logical or semantic one about whether the relevant predicates are intrinsic or relational, disguised under one heading — and that the moral rhetoric on both sides is what makes the impasse look deeper than it is.

What I want from you is a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the doctrine's history. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of two questions being pursued under one name? Is my hypothesis right, or does it collapse — for instance, because the intrinsic/relational distinction itself cannot bear the weight I am putting on it, or because the critics could concede the relational reading and still press the fairness objection against it? If the hypothesis survives, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal model of "due from," a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the underlying commitments — about whether guilt is the kind of thing that can be borne on someone's behalf — are bedrock intuitions that argument does not reach. And please say honestly whether you think the Lewis-style reconstruction, if it works, would dissolve the debate or merely relocate the standoff to a new set of terms.
