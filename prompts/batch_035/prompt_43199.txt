I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the old Sequences days, and one argument that recurs there without ever being settled — it surfaces in every wild-animal-suffering thread, every digital-minds thread, every argument about whether a superintelligence should tile the universe with hedonium or with satisfied preferences — is the fight between hedonic and preference utilitarianism. My sense is that this fight almost never gets argued at its actual root: whether the badness of pain is exhausted by pain's relations to the subject's attitudes, or whether there is something intrinsically bad in the sensation itself. Pain asymbolia is the natural laboratory for this question, but discussions I've seen always run the experiment in only one direction — lesion the distress, keep the feel, and ask whether what remains is still bad. I want the experiment run properly, as a controlled design.

Construct a thought experiment with four subjects, arranged as a two-by-two: one dimension is the phenomenal character of the pain (the raw sensory-discriminative feel, its intensity and location-quality), the other is the aversive-motivational component (the felt badness, the urgency to make it stop, the distress). The four cells: a normal subject with both components; an asymbolic subject with the full sensory feel but no distress or urge to escape; and then the cell I never see discussed — an "inverse asymbolic" subject who has the aversiveness without the characteristic bodily feel, someone for whom something is urgently, intolerably wrong without anything feeling like burning or aching; and a fourth subject with neither. Constraints: keep all four realistic — ground each cell where you can in phenomena that are actually attested (asymbolia itself, congenital insensitivity to pain, the well-known observation that morphine can leave patients reporting pain that no longer bothers them), and explicitly flag which cells are extrapolation beyond anything documented. No brain swaps, no demons, no sci-fi gadgetry, and no subjects who are unable to report on their own states. One tight paragraph per subject.

Then run the design. State, for each of the four subjects, what the pain (or pseudo-pain) is like and whether it is bad for them, and do this three times over — once from the view that pain's badness is intrinsic to its hedonic or sensory feel, once from the view that the badness just is the aversive-motivational component (the imperative to stop, which asymbolia is standardly taken to support), and once from a preference-based view on which nothing is bad for you unless it contravenes something you care about. Tell me which cell does the real discriminating work between the three views, what happens to the argument if that cell turns out to be empirically impossible, and how a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack the case — whether by denying the possibility of the inverse-asymbolic subject, by reinterpreting their reports, or by biting a bullet. Finally, say what the case implies for the hedonic-versus-preference utilitarian fight I started with: if you had to build a moral patient out of one of the four subjects, which one would you choose, and what does that choice commit you to? I don't want a survey of the literature — I want the case built, the verdicts demonstrated rather than stipulated, and your own conclusion defended at the end.
