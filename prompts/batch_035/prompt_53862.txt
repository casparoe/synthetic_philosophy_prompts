I'm on the renaming review panel at my mid-sized state university, and somehow I drew the short straw and have to draft the written recommendation the full committee votes on in three weeks. The case in front of us is Hartley Hall, our oldest residence hall, named for Elias Hartley, the university's third president (served 1874–1903). Hartley is genuinely hard to categorize: he basically built the modern institution — founded the medical school, doubled enrollment, rescued the endowment after a financial scandal that wasn't his, and wrote the line about pursuing truth without regard to rank or creed that the current president trots out at every convocation. But he also spent two decades of his presidency publicly and energetically opposing the admission of women (we went co-ed in 1915, over the objections of his successors to his legacy), argued in print that the degree would be degraded if it were open to Black students, and vetoed the first two attempts to hire women faculty. Nothing secret about any of it — it's all in the university archive and in his own collected essays.

The committee is split roughly three ways. One faction wants full renaming, one wants to keep the name but add permanent historical context in the building's lobby, and one is dug in on the "man of his time" defense — that judging Hartley by 2020s standards is anachronistic, that nearly every white man of his era held some version of these views, and that if we rename him we'd have to rename half the campus and eventually every institution older than a century. The students who petitioned have an answer I find pretty forceful: plenty of Hartley's contemporaries disagreed with him loudly, women's education and racial equality were live contested debates he took a side in, and he used the presidency to make sure his side won here for a generation. So "of his time" seems to gloss over the fact that he was on the losing end of arguments happening *within* his time.

I need a decision memo the committee could actually act on: a concrete recommendation (rename / recontextualize / keep, or some combination with a timeline for implementation), reasons that engage the strongest version of the man-of-his-time defense rather than dismissing it, and some account of the other side of that exchange — whether the "everyone thought that way then" claim is even true as applied to Hartley, and whether the defense proves too much. I'd also like criteria we could apply to the next renaming case, because there are already rumblings about the gymnasium and a lecture series. Please don't just tell me renaming is obviously right; about half the committe is unconvinced and the memo has to speak to them.
