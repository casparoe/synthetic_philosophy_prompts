I'm a science journalist, mostly long-form stuff on brains and behavior, and I've got a public lecture coming up that's making me nervous. Here's the situation.

A few years back I wrote a well-read piece on the Libet experiments, and now a university's cognitive science program has invited me to give the big public talk at their annual lecture thing. My thesis, roughly: Libet's setup was genuinely elegant, and both sides have been making it carry weight it can't bear. The readiness potential that supposedly "precedes" conscious intention by half a second looks less like a decision being made and more like background neural noise that occasionally crosses a threshold — the stochastic-accumulator line of work — which, if you take it seriously, undercuts the "your brain decided before you did" headlines AND undercuts the smug rebuttals too. Also, the W judgment (when people say they first felt the urge to move) is basically introspection with a clock, which is a funny thing to build a free-will argument on, given the topic. And the part everyone forgets — Libet's own veto finding, the "free won't" — is to me the most interesting and least discussed piece of the whole thing. My bottom line for the talk: taking Libet seriously leaves you with conscious intention as a real causal player but not the sole author of action, and that should annoy hard determinists and libertarian free-will types about equally.

The room: mostly cognitive neuroscience faculty and grad students, two philosophers of action who know this literature cold, a movement-disorders neurologist, and a chunk of general public who've read pop books telling them neuroscience has disproven free will. The philosophers and the neuroscientists will object to different parts of my argument, in different vocabularies, and I need to hear both versions.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet. Please structure it with these sections, in this order, and stick to the rough lengths:

1. "Read of the room" — who in that audience objects to what, and why, ~150 words.
2. "Objections, worst first" — the six or seven objections I'll actually get from THIS crowd (not the generic ones I'd get at a book festival), ordered by how much damage they'd do. Where the philosophers and the neuroscientists have different versions of the same objection, give me both phrasings. Each one with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. This is the bulk, ~700 words.
3. "Concede up front" — the stuff I should hand over in the talk itself so nobody wastes a question on it, ~100 words.
4. "The exchange I can't lose" — the one objection that, if I botch it, sinks the whole thesis, and how to play it, ~150 words.
5. "Praying nobody asks" — the question that would expose the weakest joint in my position, and a damage-control answer, ~100 words.

American English, obviously. Keep it plain enough that I can actually use it live — I talk fast and lean on metaphors, so replies I can deliver while standing at a podium, not footnoted essay paragraphs. And don't pad the soft-spot notes: if a reply is genuinely shaky, say so, that's the part I most need to know.
