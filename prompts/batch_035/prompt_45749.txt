I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds work on existential risk and decision theory. A proposal before us asks for two years of support for two researchers who want to work out whether actors like us should enter into what they call acausal trades: binding ourselves to policies whose payoff depends on causally unreachable agents — civilizations in other branches of the multiverse, simulators we can never signal to, or simply distant agents whose decision procedures are correlated with ours — making the symmetric choice, so that the correlation itself does the work that contracts and enforcement would ordinarily do.

I've followed the LessWrong-adjacent discussion of this for over a decade, and my standing worry is that it slides between three claims without ever separating them: that such deals are possible in principle; that a rational agent is obligated to honor them once made; and that any two sufficiently similar agents are already trading, whether or not they know it. As far as I can tell, the proposal simply assumes the third, which is also the least defensible.

The committee is split. One faction holds that trade requires causal contact, full stop, and wants to decline with a note that we do not fund decision-theoretic theology. The other thinks the idea is continuous with ordinary trade under uncertainty and at minimum deserves a workshop.

Draft our decision memo — fund, decline, or fund with conditions. It should separate the three claims above and say which of them the proposal actually needs; say where the analogy to ordinary trade genuinely holds and where it breaks, so that we are not voting on vibes; give the strongest version of the "trade requires causal contact" objection a fair hearing rather than a caricature; and close with a concrete recommendation about the proposal as written, including, if the answer is no or conditional, what would have to change for it to be yes.
