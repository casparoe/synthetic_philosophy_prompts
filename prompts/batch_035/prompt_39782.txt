i'm helping run a philosophy of religion reading group and our next session is on the cosmological argument, specifically contrasting the kalam version (whatever begins to exist has a cause, the universe began, therefore...) with the contingency version (leibniz, the principle of sufficient reason, contingent beings needing explanations outside themselves, terminating in a necessary being). i want to open the session with a villanelle on the contingency version, and i want the form to actually do philosophical work, not just be decoration.

the two refrains: one should be the question every contingent thing faces — why this, rather than nothing — and the other should be the kind of answer the necessary being is supposed to give. then the tercets: each one should offer up an explanation (the laws of physics, the initial state, a multiverse, whatever you like) that turns out to be itself just one more contingent thing, which is exactly why the question-refrain keeps coming back. that's the regress the whole argument lives on, so the villanelle's obsessive returning should feel like the regress, not just rhyme with it.

the final quatrain is where the two refrains end up next to each other, and this is the crux: the necessary being's refrain is the one place the question doesn't get re-asked. that difference — between an answer that is just one more item in need of explanation and one that isn't — is the entire argument, so i want the ending to make a reader feel why one halts the regress and the other doesn't, instead of just asserting it.

two more things. first, this has to be the contingency version, not kalam: the explanations in the tercets should be simultaneous/explanatory (deeper laws explaining shallower ones, etc.), not a chain of past causes stretching backward in time. someone who knows both arguments should be able to tell which one the poem is doing and why. second, please don't have the poem glibly declare victory. the standard objection — the one russell pressed against copleston — is that maybe the demand for explanations just runs out, or that the totality of contingent things doesn't itself need one. a good ending should leave a psr-skeptic honestly unsettled rather than preached at, even though the form is obviously built to pull toward termination.

and it still needs to be a good poem — one of the people in the group writes poerty and will absolutely notice if the meter is a mess.
