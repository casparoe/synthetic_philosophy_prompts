I write for the science and ideas desk of a mid-sized paper, and I keep running into the same one-liner when I interview researchers and tech people: "truth is what works". A physicist used it last month to shrug off my question about whether string theory is actually true or just a useful calculational tool. A startup guy used it to mean something like "whatever model makes money". And then at a book event a philosopher got quite sniffy at me and said that's a cartoon version of pragmatism, that Peirce and James disagreed about this stuff more than either of them disagreed with the absolutists, and that neither of them would recognise the sentence coming out of the physicist's mouth.

So before I put any of this in a piece I'm drafting on why "post-truth" and "useful fiction" talk keeps bleeding from philosophy into science journalism: help me pull the slogan apart. What are the genuinely different precise claims hiding inside "truth is what works" — from the boring truism that good theories are useful, to something like "useful for whom, over what timescale", to the full-blown "whatever is expedient for me to believe is true" reading that made everyone dunk on James a century ago? For each reading I want a verdict: trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or just false. And crucially, tell me for each one whether it would actually let my physicist dismiss the string theory question, or whether it just sounds like it does.

Bonus if you can sort out which readings belong more to Peirce's picture (truth as where inquiry ends up in the long run, tied to a community and to methods that self-correct) and which to James's (truth as ideas that get verified in experience, that we cash out). It would also help me alot if you could say where the "it works for them so it's true for them" move — the one my editor always wants to use about, say, placebo effects or homeopathy users — fits into your menu, since I suspect it equivocates between two of the readings but I can't pin down which.

Please answer in British English (so -ise endings, "labour", that sort of thing), and keep it structured as a list of readings I can actually reference from my notes, not one long essay.
