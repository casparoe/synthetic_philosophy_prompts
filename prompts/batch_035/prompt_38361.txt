I'm on the board of a 24-person worker cooperative, and at Thursday's meeting we have to decide how to distribute this year's surplus of about €60,000. The obvious option on the table is an equal per-member payout, roughly €2,500 each. But the majority faction is pushing a different scheme: a larger lump payment of around €5,000 to the fifteen members who are not currently using our subsidized childcare arrangement, funded partly by the surplus and partly by cancelling that arrangement entirely. The nine members who rely on it would each lose a benefit worth about €3,000 a year to them. So the majority scheme creates bigger gains for more people than it creates losses — by anyone's accounting, the sum of the benefits outweighs the sum of the burdens, and the average member ends up better off. For what it's worth, nothing else is at stake: the childcare benefit was always discretionary, no one was promised it permanently, and everyone agrees the fifteen recipients are not more deserving in any ordinary sense — they just happen not to need childcare.

The chair of the board, Miriam, who is one of the nine, has been arguing against the majority scheme in a way I can't quite evaluate. Her view, as I understand it, is that adding up everyone's gains and losses as though the cooperative were one enormous person with one bank account is exactly the wrong way to reason here. The €5,000 going to each of the fifteen doesn't somehow travel across to the nine and soften what they lose; each person experiences only their own position, and for the nine the year is simply worse, full stop. She says a decision that makes fifteen people somewhat happier and nine people worse off, made by those fifteen over the objections of the nine, needs some justification other than the arithmetic — and she claims that in a cooperative of all places, where the whole point is that we're not just a collection of strangers trading at arm's length, this kind of bookkeeping reasoning is doubly out of place. Two of the majority faction have responded that her complaint amounts to nothing more than an unwillingness to do arithmetic: if the total is bigger, the scheme makes the cooperative better off, and preferring a smaller total just because the distribution shifts is sentimentality about which particular people end up holding which euros.

I have to vote on Thursday and I genuinely can't tell whether Miriam's argument is a real structural objection or just an eloquent restatement of being on the losing side. So I'd like your help thinking it through. First, can you articulate what the disagreement between Miriam and the majority actually turns on, stated as precisely as you can — what exactly is being assumed when we treat the total as the thing to maximize, and is there anything in the nature of the cooperative, or of the people in it, that could make that assumption fail? Second, work out which of the considerations in play are doing real work and which are noise (does it matter, for instance, that the winners and losers are colleagues rather than strangers, that the decision is being made by the winners, or that the childcare benefit was discretionary rather than promised?). Third, give me your own verdict on how I should vote, together with a candid account of where your reasoning is most likely to break down: tell me what fact about this case, or what argument on either side, would make you reverse your verdict, and be explicit if you think the strongest consideration supporting the side you reject is one your answer doesn't fully answer.
