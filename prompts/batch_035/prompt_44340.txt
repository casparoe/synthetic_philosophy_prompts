I help run a monthly discussion group on science-and-society topics, and last month's meeting ended with a dispute I couldn't referee. The topic was supposed to be how pollsters use generational labels, but Maya, a sociology PhD student, and Jonas, who works in philosophy of science, ended up debating whether generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — are real kinds at all. Neither would concede, and by the later rounds the rest of us had lost the thread, so I'm bringing the exchange to you. I've reconstructed it as faithfully as I can and numbered the moves.

(1) Maya opened by saying generations are as real as anything in social science: people who pass through adolescence around the same events — the arrival of the smartphone, a major recession, a political trauma — come out measurably different in religiosity, trust, and media habits, and these cohort differences persist in survey data decade after decade.

(2) Jonas replied that nothing in that data corresponds to the boundaries of the labels. Someone born December 31st of one year is not meaningfully a different kind of person from someone born two days later; the cutoffs were set by polling organizations for administrative convenience. A category with arbitrary boundaries and no causal joint at its edges is a bin, not a kind.

(3) Maya said plenty of real kinds have fuzzy or gerrymandered boundaries — species interbreed at their edges, ecosystems fade into one another — and what makes a kind real is not a sharp edge but a causal mechanism holding its members together. Shared formative events, experienced at the same impressionable age, are exactly such a mechanism; that is the whole point of talking about homeostatic property clusters rather than essences.

(4) Jonas granted fuzzy boundaries but attacked the mechanism: the shared experience is not shared. A recession hits the twenty-two-year-old and the fourteen-year-old in completely different ways; class, region, and family swamp cohort in almost any prediction you would want to make; the stereotypical Millennial experience is really the experience of one affluent, media-visible slice of the cohort. What glues the cluster together is not a mechanism but a story, told by journalists and marketing consultants with an interest in selling the cohort as a bloc.

(5) Maya answered that the same is true of nations, professions, and most other social kinds — all are partly narrated — and that narrative is not opposed to reality here, because the label feeds back: people are told they are a generation, consume and vote accordingly, and are then addressed as a bloc by politicians and advertisers, which hardens the differences. Constructed does not mean unreal; money is constructed and quite real.

(6) Jonas said the feedback loop makes the label real, not the cohort: the loop would latch onto any arbitrary bin and work just as well, so the reality it produces cannot vindicate the original demographic category. He added that the empirical case is shakier than it looks, because cohort effects are nearly unidentifiable: any attitude gap between age groups could be due to age (people change as they get older), period (everyone changed in a given year), or cohort, and the three cannot be disentangled from ordinary survey data — so even the measurable differences Maya cites underdetermine the kind claim.

(7) Maya's last word was that this underdetermination afflicts all observational causal inference in the social sciences, so Jonas cannot hold it against generations specifically; and that even if the bin started out arbitrary, decades of feedback have laundered the arbitrariness into real boundaries, the way an arbitrary colonial border becomes a real national border with real effects on the people it divides.

Then give an overall verdict on who is more right. Finally, tell me whether there is a distinction or a question both of them missed that would have changed the shape of the debate.
