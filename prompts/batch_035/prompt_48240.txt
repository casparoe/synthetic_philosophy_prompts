Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006) stages a strikingly exact version of Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment, and I'd like a philosophical reading of the film that takes both sides seriously. In Reasons and Persons (1984), Parfit asks us to imagine a machine that scans your body on Earth, destroys it, and reassembles an exact duplicate on Mars out of new matter — and asks whether you traveled or died and were replaced, eventually arguing that there is no deep further fact here: what matters in survival is psychological connectedness and continuity (his "Relation R"), not numerical identity. The film gives us two magicians who, in effect, run the two halves of Parfit's argument as rival acts. Robert Angier, using Tesla's machine, performs nightly a destructive teletransportation: a new Angier appears at the back of the hall while the one who walked on stage drops through the trapdoor and drowns — a fresh Parfit replica created each performance, with the original destroyed. Alfred Borden, meanwhile, turns out to have lived for decades as a Parfit-style fission case: two men (twins) sharing one life, one name, and one public identity, each living what they call half of a full life, which was "somehow enough for us. But not for them."

Here is what I want the reading to address:

1. Angier and the tank. Parfit's own answer would presumably be that each night's Angier survives in everything that matters — the man in the prestige has the memories, the obsessions, the rivalry, the grief for Julia. Yet the film's final act strongly suggests that Angier himself believed he was dying nightly: he keeps a tank for each drowning, Cutter's story about the sailor turns out to be a lie ("He said it was agony" — though please paraphrase rather than lean on exact wording), and the closing conversation with the surviving Borden twin plays like a dying man's reckoning, not a man who knows he will walk out the other side of the theater. Does the film dramatize the thought that Parfit is wrong — that there is a further fact, and Angier felt it every night? Or is Angier's terror a pre-reflective attachment to identity that Parfit's argument is precisely meant to dissolve, with the film deliberately leaving us unsure which of them, Angier or Parfit, is the one being fooled? I'm curious whether the trick/audience framing — "you don't really want to know, you want to be fooled" — can be read as a comment on our intuitions about personal identity themselves.

2. Borden and fission. Parfit's hardest cases involve branching: when one person could be continued by two, identity can't be what matters, because identity is one-one. The Bordens live the conclusion rather than argue it. As the surviving twin puts it, there is nothing easy about two men sharing one life. Read their arrangement through Relation R: does the film show that a life can be good and even ethically serious under a non-identity-preserving regime of continuity, or does it show the cost Parfit underplays — Sarah's "not today," the severed fingers, the fact that no one, including the audience and even Cutter, can know which man stands before them? Note that the film's two magicians are mirror images: Angier destroys the original nightly to preserve the illusion of one continuous man, while the Bordens destroy their individuality to sustain the same illusion. Is Nolan suggesting that both are solving the same problem — what Parfit would call the fact that identity is not what matters, but that we cannot live as if we believed it?

3. Where the analogy strains. Be honest about the disanalogies: Angier's replicas are not psychologically continuous with each other in the way Parfit's Mars-traveler is with the pre-scan person (each new Angier is continuous with the drowned one, but no surviving Angier ever accumulates the ordinary next-day memories); the drowning is slow and involves suffering that Parfit's instant scan-and-destroy does not; and the Bordens' case is ordinary psychological continuity all the way down, with no replication of matter at all — arguably not a personal identity puzzle but a puzzle about deception and shared agency. I want the reading to say where the film is genuinely doing personal identity work and where it is doing something else (an ethics of obsession, or an epistemology of secrets) that the Parfit lens only superficially fits.

Please write it as an essay-length critical reading I could use with students in a personal identity unit — students who know Parfit's teletransporter, the branch-line case, and the fission cases, but have seen The Prestige only once. Take scenes rather than the plot summary as the unit of analysis, and take a position at the end on whether the film adds anything to the Parfit debate that the thought experiments alone cannot — for instance, something about what it is like, from the inside, to live without the assumption that you will continue. Do not quote dialogue verbatim beyond brief paraphrase; the students have access to the film, and I want the argument, not the screenplay.
