ok so I've gotten weirdly obsessed with this while designing my first display typeface, and I think there's a real philosophical question buried in it that I can't articulate properly. here it is: what actually makes every 'a' an 'a'? When I draw an 'a' distorted almost past recognition — squashed bowl, weird stem — readers still read it as an 'a' fine, as long as it fits the style of the rest of my font. But the same shape dropped into Times New Roman looks like a mistake, or even reads as some other letter. It's like a letterform's identity isn't in any fixed outline but somewhere between the abstract category 'a' and the style that the surrounding font establishes, and neither alone determines what you see.

I vaguely remember Douglas Hofstadter had a research project on exactly this — Letter Spirit, I think — where the claim was something like: a letter is an interaction between an abstract category (the "a"-ness shared by all 'a's) and a concrete style (what all the letters in one font share), and recognition is partly top-down, the whole font's "spirit" telling you how to parse each individual glyph. That matches my experience trying to draw letterforms but I don't fully understand the conceptual picture.

Can you explain the actual issue here? Like: is a letter's identity basically a Platonic form, a family-resemblance thing within the style, or a genuinely two-level structure where category and style each only half-determine what's on the page? And is reading a letter then an act of interpretation rather than plain perception — which would explain why the identical squiggle can be an 'a' in one font and a typo in another? I'm not after typography tips, I want the philosophy of how categories and styles interact to make a letterform the thing it is.
