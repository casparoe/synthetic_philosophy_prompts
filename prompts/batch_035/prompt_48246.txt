I'm a software engineer who's spent way too much of my life in git — rebases, force-pushes, squashed commits, orphan branches, detached HEAD states, you name it. Recently I got sucked into a history-of-nationalism rabbithole after a friend mentioned the whole "how old are nations really" debate — Hobsbawm's thing about invented traditions (the Scottish kilt story, the manufactured pomp around the British monarchy) and Anderson's imagined communities, where the nation exists because millions of strangers read the same newspapers and thereby felt part of the same "we". My first reaction was honestly: this is all just version control. An invented tradition is a rebased history — later commits dressed up to look older, squashed into an earlier epoch so the log reads continuous. Print capitalism is the rollout of a canonical remote that everyone starts pulling from. And the perennial fight over whether nations are basically modern constructions or have deep medieval/ancient ancestry is just asking how much of the repo's history got force-pushed in the last couple centuries versus how much is genuine ancestry.

So here's the ask: take the Hobsbawm/Anderson picture and translate it into git vocabulary as faithfully as you can — commits, rebases, force-pushes, forks, orphan branches, the canonical remote, the reflog, whatever else actually fits. Then do the part I care about most: the residue report. What does not survive the translation? I have some hunches I want pressure-tested. (1) In git a rewritten history is detectable and treated as a problem — but an invented tradition only *works* as long as the rewrite goes undetected; the belief that the history is old is load-bearing in a way it just isn't for a repo. (2) A repo with one contributor is still a fully functional repo, but a nation of one person is nothing — I can't find any git object that corresponds to Anderson's imagined community, the whole strangers-feeling-simultaneity thing. (3) Git has something like cryptographic ground truth in the hash chain, and the historians' debate over whether a given national tradition is 200 or 2000 years old seems to be precisely a fight in a domain where that kind of ground truth doesn't exist.

Tell me which of those hunches survive scrutiny, where I've distorted the historians to make the analogy land, and whether the leftover stuff means my vocabulary is missing something or whether parts of the debate are less substantive than they look. And don't soften the translation to protect the historians — if some stretch of the argument really does reduce to "was the history rewritten and by whom", say that plainly. Its the residue I want, not a flattering analogy.
