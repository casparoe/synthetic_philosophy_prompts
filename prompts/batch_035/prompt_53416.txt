I teach a termly session at a lifelong-learning institute, and in three weeks I have fifty minutes on the dispute between presentism and eternalism: roughly, whether only present things exist, or whether past and future things exist in something like the way things far away from me exist. The audience is retired professionals — former lawyers, a pharmacist, one high-school physics teacher — educated, argumentative, and equipped with popular-science reading but no mathematics beyond algebra. I am not asking you to explain the two views to me; I know the terrain. I am asking for a plan for my explanation, and I want it in American English.

Please give me the following, in whatever order serves the plan:

1. The dependency structure. Which pieces must be secured before which others can so much as be stated. For instance: does the audience need the relativity of simultaneity in place before the argument from special relativity can even be formulated, and does the distinction between the felt present — the half-second window in which a melody is heard as a whole — and the metaphysical present have to come first, so that nobody conflates a fact about experience with a thesis about what exists?

2. One carrying example, chosen and defended. My instinct was the block universe as a loaf of bread being sliced, but I suspect that picture hands them eternalism as an image rather than an argument, and the slicing quietly implies a privileged cutter, which is precisely the point in dispute. Pick a better single concrete case that can carry the whole session, and tell me what it must have and what it must lack.

3. A misconception map, ordered as the misconceptions will actually arise rather than as they are most easily listed. The ones I anticipate: that Einstein proved the block universe, when in fact relativity of simultaneity is a theorem while eternalism is one interpretation of its import; that if eternalism is true, nothing ever happens and change is an illusion; that the block universe entails fatalism, in the sense that my deliberations are already on the page and therefore make no difference; and that presentism is simply common sense and therefore costs nothing. For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off, and with what move.

4. A verdict on the Andromeda-style example — two people passing each other on the street whose personal presents disagree by days about events in a distant galaxy. Should I use it at this level at all, or does it produce more vertigo than insight? If you advise using it, tell me what I must add about why the disagreement cannot be turned into signaling, and where that caveat belongs in the arc.

5. What to openly refuse to cover. I assume general relativity, Gödel's rotating universe, and the full taxonomy of A-theories — the moving spotlight, the growing block — are beyond fifty minutes. Tell me whether to name these exclusions aloud, and why doing so might matter for this particular audience.

6. The closing check: one question I can ask in the last five minutes whose answer would show me they have grasped the actual dispute rather than merely enjoyed the pictures — something that would catch, in particular, the slide from eternalism back to the feeling that time flows.

Throughout, please write in American English.
