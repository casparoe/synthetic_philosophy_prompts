I would like your help thinking through a specific worry that has come up repeatedly in our research group's planning meetings.

We train language models using a learned proxy for human approval: the proxy is fit to a finite set of human preference judgments, and we then optimize the policy heavily against it. Our safety lead consistently objects to increasing optimization pressure on the proxy, and he ends debate by invoking Goodhart's law, almost always in its slogan form: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Occasionally he reaches for Goodhart's original formulation instead: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." His implicit argument runs: the proxy is a measure; making it the target of optimization is what we are proposing; therefore the proxy will stop tracking what we actually care about; therefore we should not push optimization harder against it.

I am sure that one sentence is doing several different jobs, and that the final step — that we should cap optimization pressure — does not follow equally from all of them. Please lay out, as a structured list I can bring to our next meeting, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing. I would like you to distinguish at least the following kinds of readings, and add any you think I have missed: (a) a near-tautological reading, on which the point is just that a proxy and its target are distinct concepts and can come apart somewhere; (b) a statistical reading, on which even with no response from the system, selection on a noisy proxy systematically overstates how much of the target you have captured, so the gap grows precisely where we are selecting hardest; (c) a causal or adversarial reading, on which an optimizing system actively finds regions where the proxy and the target diverge, so that divergence is not just observed but sought out and exploited; and (d) any reading on which the act of targeting itself, or even of measuring itself, destroys the correlation regardless of any optimization pressure.

For each reading, please say: whether it is trivial, substantive but plausible, substantive but doubtful, or simply false; whether it applies to our situation, where the "system being measured" is not a human gaming an institutional metric but a policy being trained against a fitted proxy; and, crucially, whether accepting that reading would actually support my colleague's conclusion that we should reduce optimization pressure on the proxy, as opposed to supporting something weaker (that we should expect the proxy to degrade at the margin, and should measure that degradation directly) or something stronger (that we should never optimize against any learned proxy at all). Finally, please tell me which reading he is most plausibly invoking when he uses the slogan to close discussion, and what experiment or observation would adjudicate between the readings in our setting.
