I'm on my department's hiring committee, and we're in a procedural fight that has turned strangely philosophical. Our chair proposes that we vote proposition by proposition — for each pairwise comparison of candidates, take majority votes on 'A has the stronger research record,' 'A has the stronger teaching record,' 'A is the better fit for our needs,' and then let the majority's verdicts on those premises determine the overall ranking — rather than each member submitting a whole ranking and aggregating those. When two of us objected that his procedure could produce a result contained in nobody's ranking, he waved us off with the wisdom of crowds: five independent heads, each judging the same dossier, will land nearer the truth than any one head arguing the others down.

I've realized I don't know what that slogan is actually claiming, and I'd like a precisification I can circulate as a memo before the next meeting. Lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing — from the trivial (aggregating independent estimates cancels idiosyncratic error, the way averaging crowd guesses about the weight of an ox does) through the Condorcet jury-theorem reading (with its independence and competence conditions) to a purely normative reading (majority procedure is legitimate whether or not it tracks truth) — and give each a verdict: platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or false as stated. Then say which reading the chair's procedure actually needs, and whether the judgment-aggregation literature sinks it. In particular, explain how proposition-wise majority voting can deliver a collectively inconsistent verdict from individually consistent voters — the three-judge court case from the doctrinal paradox is the classic illustration — and construct a concrete five-member profile for our committee in which a majority votes yes on each of the three premises while a majority of members rank candidate B over candidate A, each member's ranking consistent with their own premise votes. Close the memo by assessing the escape routes — conclusion-only voting, treating premises as decisive, aggregating whole rankings instead of propositions, supermajority requirements — and say for each what it costs in whatever it was the wisdom of crowds was supposed to buy us. The chair is a reasonable man; I want to defeat the procedure, not him, and I'd rather hand him the strongest honest version of his own slogan before showing exactly where it breaks.
