I would like help identifying a concept, if one exists, in epistemology. I keep encountering a particular structural move in discussions about what entitles us to our beliefs, and I cannot find a standard name for it. The familiar background worry is this: if a belief is justified only by another belief, then justification seems to run into one of three unattractive outcomes — an endless chain, a circle, or a brute stopping point where some belief is simply declared self-justifying. The move I have in mind accepts the setup but denies the three-way choice. It goes as follows: the chain does not stop at a special self-justifying belief; it stops because the last item in the chain is not a belief at all. For instance, my belief that there is coffee in the kitchen rests on my belief that the mug on the counter is full, and that belief is not supported by a further belief but by my seeing the mug — a perceptual episode, something that happens to me rather than something I hold true. The defender of the move claims this dissolves the trilemma instead of picking a horn: nothing is unjustified, nothing is circular, and nothing is self-justifying, because the chain is a chain of beliefs and it terminates in an item of a different category altogether. The standard objection I have seen is that the episode only helps me if I believe I am having it, and that belief itself needs support, so the chain is one link shorter than the defender admits.

A second version of the same move, which makes me think they belong together, uses states other than perception: a mathematician says her belief in a lemma is supported not by another belief but by an intuition that it is true; a person says his belief that he is in pain is supported by the pain itself. In these cases the terminator looks less like an episode and more like a standing state with propositional content — it seems to say that things are a certain way without quite being a judgment that they are. I am not sure whether these count as instances of the same strategy or as a different one.

I have gathered that there is an old worry in this neighborhood involving the phrase "the given," but as I understand it, that worry is about whether non-belief states can be carriers of justification at all, whereas what I am after is the specific maneuver of terminating the regress by shifting categories — treating the last link as a fact about one's situation rather than as something one believes. So, to be clear about what would satisfy me: please tell me which established concepts come closest to this maneuver, how each of them differs from it, and which one fits best; and if none fits cleanly, say so and coin a term, with a definition that settles the boundary cases I mentioned (perceptual episodes, mathematical intuitions, and states like pain) and that marks the maneuver off from both self-justifying foundationalism and from views on which the regress is terminated not by anything in the subject's mind at all but by the reliability of the process that produced the belief. A bare name will not help me; I want the carving. Please answer in American English.
