In game theory, a standard way to narrow a game is iterated elimination of dominated strategies, where a strategy counts as dominated even when no pure strategy beats it and only a mixed strategy does: some probability mixture over the player's remaining strategies yields a strictly higher expected payoff against every possible play by the others. Deletion is then repeated in the reduced game until nothing further can be deleted. Textbooks present the survivors as the rational choices, and mechanism designers build on the same footing: an institution is certified safe when the deletion process terminates in a single profile, as with a second-price auction, where truthful bidding is what survives.

Something about the practice puzzles me. As a description of actual people, its own practitioners disown it. In the Traveler's Dilemma — two people independently name a value between $2 and $100 for a damaged antique; matching names are both paid that amount, differing names are both paid the lower, with a $2 bonus to the lower namer and a $2 penalty to the higher — the iterated reasoning unravels to $2/$2, and no economist expects or advises that outcome; in the classroom game of guessing two-thirds of the average, the same unravelling predicts 0, and nobody predicts 0. Yet the same community trusts a dominance-solvable mechanism with untrained participants.

Please reconstruct the theory of rationality and of the epistemic situation that this practice presupposes. In particular, I would like you to address: (i) how many layers of "each knows that the other knows that each is rational" each round of deletion consumes, and whether unbounded common knowledge of rationality is a strict presupposition of the practice or merely its most convenient model; (ii) what the mixed-dominance step adds — it seems to presuppose that a player can implement an exact mixture, costlessly, with a randomizing device at hand, in a setting where an opponent gains nothing from detecting the randomization: is this a claim about players, about one-shot play, or just an idealization of the model; (iii) an ordering subtlety: a strategy can be deleted because a mixture containing a second strategy beats it, and a later round may delete that second strategy too — does the practice presuppose that earlier deletions remain justified through the chain, and what view of the transparency of reasoning does that require; (iv) what, if anything, reconciles the analytic and design uses of the same reasoning — that publicity of the rules substitutes for common knowledge of rationality, that concentrated incentives and structure manufacture rationality, or that the designer needs only each participant's clarity about their own incentives, with no mutual knowledge at all?

Distinguish, throughout, what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, say where it is underdetermined — which rival views of practical rationality would license the same certifying use — and say what would have to change in the practice if players could not randomize, or if only first-order rationality could be assumed.
