My brother-in-law and I have been arguing about the mind for close to four years now, mostly on long drives, and I want to capture where we've gotten to. He holds that mental states are defined by their causal roles: pain is whatever internal state is normally caused by tissue damage, makes you withdraw, makes you avoid the situation next time, and makes you lie down and moan — and anything that plays that role, in a squid or a robot or whatever, thereby has pain, full stop. I'm not so sure, and my side (as I've come to hold it) is that the role description is always stated at some rough grain, and that once you get to the grain that matters, the physical details stop being incidental: it's an open question whether the pain I have and whatever the octopus has are two states of the same kind or merely two states with similar jobs, the way a bone eye-lens and a glass lens both "focus light" without being the same kind of thing. Both of us think the other side smuggles in its conclusion, and we've recognized that a lot of our fights have been about which words belong to which camp, so we'd like a clean record rather than another shouting match on the shoulder of the interstate.

We have a set of stock cases that we keep going around. There's the octopus, whose pain-like machinery was built by evolution completely separately from ours, down to painkillers that work on it only after strange delays and only some of the ones that work on us. There's the silicon-substitution case, where you replace my neurons one by one with chips that keep the same spike timings, and I still say "I feel fine" at every step — though I've started to worry that both of us secretly predict the same thing about that case, and that we're only pretending to disagree. There's the slow system: a machine that implements what looks like my exact functional organization but runs a thousand times slower, so a "pain" episode lasts a week. There's the one he invented to needle me, the village where thousands of people with radios and index cards pass messages following the rules of my brain, keeping the whole system going for a whole afternoon after I'm dead. And there's the stroke patient we both know, whose brain routed her speech around a damaged area by a different route with visibly different timing, and who says she experiences her words the same as before — a case where each of us claims the other one's theory makes the wrong prediction but neither of us can say precisely which observation would have gone the other way.

Please write the document I think we should have produced long ago: a joint statement we could both sign. Start with the claims we genuinely agree on — about what a causal-role description is, about what the substitution and stroke cases show, about what evolution's reuse of old parts for new jobs does and doesn't establish. Then set out, in wording each of us would actually accept for our own side, exactly where we still differ: whether sameness of role at any grain is enough for sameness of mental kind, whether the "pain" label is being used for a role or for a realizer and whether that ambiguity explains half our fights. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — and for each crux, name the case, test, or argument we both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, including what each of us would take as a result going against our own view. If any part of our dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say which part and why, and separate it clearly from the part that isn't. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. We don't need any reading lists or names of authors; we want the ideas worked out in plain words we can check against our own cases.
