I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm close to filing a long-form piece on the ethics of decisions that shape who will exist. The centerpiece is a bioethicist I've been profiling — I'll call her Professor V. — who sits on a national research council and has thought about the nonidentity problem longer than anyone I've interviewed. Over several conversations she has given me clean verdicts on a series of cases, each with the nonidentity structure: the decision changes who will exist, so no future person is worse off than they would otherwise have been, because without the decision they wouldn't exist at all. Her rulings so far: a society that adopts a depletion-style energy policy, leaving future people — necessarily different people than under any alternative, given effects on conception timing — lives of grinding scarcity that are barely, though genuinely, worth living: wrong. A country that stores nuclear waste in a way that is safe for a century and then fails catastrophically, guaranteeing the eventual victims are different people than would otherwise have been born: wrong. A couple who decline to wait three months and conceive a child they know will have a mild, manageable congenital condition, rather than the different, healthy child they would otherwise have had: not wrong. And one control case with no nonidentity at all, ordinary pollution that makes life worse for people who will exist regardless: wrong, obviously.

Here's the new case I need to predict her ruling on before I send her the draft — my editor wants it as the piece's pivot point, and I'd rather not guess badly. A public health agency decides, for budget reasons, against remediating aging lead pipes in a region. The exposure will slightly shift conception timings and slightly diminish the health and cognitive capacities of the resulting children: different children than under the alternative, each with lives that are clearly flourishing, just measurably less so. What rule or rules is Professor V. actually running across those four verdicts — I can see something like a catastrophe-threshold person-affecting view and something like an institutional-duty view with a parental exemption, but I suspect there are others that fit her record just as well — and what does each of those rules predict for the lead case? Give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons for preferring one reading of her over the others. And since I have forty minutes with her on Thursday, tell me the single further case I should put to her that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules, and what each possible answer would tell me.
