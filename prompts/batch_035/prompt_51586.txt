I teach an upper-level seminar called Ethics of War and Peace (16 students, one 90-minute session), and I want a classroom activity for the week we take up the question of whether soldiers on opposite sides of a war are morally on a par. My students will already be comfortable with the distinction between the justice of going to war and conduct within war, but I don't want to lecture them on the debate between the traditional symmetry thesis and its revisionist critics before they've felt the problem — the position names, and the names of the philosophers, should only enter at the very end.

The scenario I have in mind involves two fictional states in an asymmetric war: one an aggressor whose army is made up of well-paid professional volunteers who signed up during peacetime, the other the victim state, defending itself with hastily drafted conscripts who face prison if they refuse to serve. I'd like the students to be divided between the two armies, each side receiving a factual brief about its own situation only (what they were told about the war, what they actually know, what their alternatives were), with the full picture of who is in the right emerging only gradually. After a short deliberation and a staged exchange of fire with casualties assigned by the facilitator, I want the main event: a tribunal in which ordinary soldiers from both sides — a professional volunteer from the aggressor army and a conscript from the defending one, and ideally a third figure such as a defending pilot whose lawful strike foreseeably killed civilians — are put on trial for killing enemy combatants, with the rest of the students as jurors assigning blame and sentence. My hope is that they run straight into the collision: the intuition that both soldiers were "just doing what soldiers do," the opposite intuition that the volunteer who chose to fight for a bad cause deserves more blame than the conscript who had no choice, and the uncomfortable fact that the conscript may have killed someone who arguably deserved it less than the person the volunteer killed.

Please design the full activity: the contents of the two briefs and the role cards, the rules and timeline of the deliberation and the tribunal (including how the jury reaches its verdicts, e.g., secret ballots on separate verdicts for each defendant), a way of recording each juror's blame assignments at several points so we can see opinions shift as evidence about the war's justice comes out, and a debrief that maps the specific moments of the game onto the symmetry thesis and its rivals — which features of the activity speak for symmetry, which against it, and where the analogy to real war breaks down. I'd also like your honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: the jury will try to indict the political leaders instead, or collapse into blanket pacifism) and how to handle those moves, plus a frank note on which feature of the real debate — the coercion, fear, and risk actual soldiers live under, or the genuine uncertainty about one's own side's cause — the simulation simply cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
