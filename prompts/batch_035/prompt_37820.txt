I've been a regular in the rationalist corner of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days — LessWrong, the SSC/ACX commentariat, the occasional long physics thread — and while I'm comfortable with the standard canon (Bayesian epistemology, map-versus-territory, et cetera), relativity is where my self-taught footing gets shaky. I recently read a post on a rationalist-adjacent forum summarizing the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe, and something about it felt too easy — as though a contested philosophical conclusion had been dressed in the costume of settled physics. I can't locate the seams myself, so I'd like an audit.

Here is the post's summary, condensed:

"Special relativity destroys the flowing present. Simultaneity is relative to the observer, so there is no frame-independent fact about which distant events are happening 'now.' Better still: because planes of simultaneity tilt with velocity, an astronaut coasting past Earth at near light speed has a 'now' that slices into what you call the future — your next decision already exists, now, for her. Putnam proved this rigorously in the 1960s, and Einstein had already drawn the same conclusion in his 1905 paper: the universe is a frozen four-dimensional block, the passage of time is an illusion, determinism is a theorem of physics, and free will is dead. Cosmology only strengthens the case, and working physicists accept all of this — it's standard relativity, not an interpretation."

Please take on the role of a philosopher of physics who works on time and relativity, and who has no patience for arguments that sound rigorous merely because they borrow physics vocabulary. Before you answer, brainstorm privately: lay out what you take to be the actual load-bearing steps of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument and the principles a defender would insist on. Then work through the quoted summary claim by claim, sorting each claim into one of three categories: accurate; a simplification a defender would grumble at but ultimately allow; or a misrepresentation. In particular, I want to know:

1. Is the single-astronaut gloss faithful? My instinct is that the real argument needs more than one moving observer — something about chaining simultaneity judgments through a distant event, plus a principle connecting different observers' "real now"s to each other, a principle that someone famously attacked. If the post quietly left out the load-bearing step and its most-criticized assumption, say so plainly.
2. Do "determinism is a theorem of physics" and "free will is dead" follow from anything true in the summary? I can picture a four-dimensional block whose contents are not nomologically necessitated by earlier slices, which makes me suspect the post conflates the future's existing with the future's being determined — but I'd like you to make that distinction precise rather than intuitive.
3. Is the Einstein attribution right — did anything like this appear in the 1905 paper, or is that a retrofitted pedigree? And is "cosmology only strengthens the case" safe? I half-remember that the standard cosmological model comes with a preferred time slicing, which sounds like it might cut against the argument rather than for it.
4. Is "working physicists accept all of this" a fair report of the field, or an overstatement that turns an interpretational tendency among physicists into textbook consensus?

Finally, assess the residue: setting aside the misrepresentations, does the surviving core — relativity of simultaneity plus whatever chaining principles the argument genuinely requires — establish the block universe as Putnam claimed, or do the standard objections (Stein's rejoinder, the conventionality of simultaneity, complications from general relativity) leave the argument short of proof? Close with a verdict I can act on: whether I should file this post as reliable popularization, or as a confident overstatement I should stop repeating in arguments about time and free will.
