I lead a small user-experience research group at a company that builds productivity software, and a dispute keeps flaring up about how to treat what users tell us about their own experience. Some colleagues take every user report as an authoritative description of inner life — when a user says they saw the whole page at once, or that the writing assistant seemed to read their mind, they record it as fact about the user's consciousness. Others dismiss the reports entirely as post-hoc confabulation. I've read a little about Dennett's heterophenomenology and its idea of treating subjects' reports as texts to be interpreted and explained rather than as direct transcripts of inner events, and I suspect it offers a principled middle path — but I need it in a form my analysts, none of whom have philosophy training, can actually use.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who knows the heterophenomenology literature well. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short brainstorm about which parts of the method must survive the translation into a workday procedure and which can be dropped without losing the point. Then write a procedure my team can run on a single user report in about thirty minutes: the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient result at each step, where the procedure outputs a deflationary paraphrase of the report, where it outputs a hypothesis about what produced the report, and where it must stop and hand the call to a human because the question is one of interpretation or values the procedure can't settle. Be explicit about the ways the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three cases from recent studies:

1. A user of our note-taking app reported that the autosave indicator makes it feel like the document is being watched. The team wants to know whether to log this as a genuine perception or as a figure of speech, and neither camp will budge.
2. A user claimed, during a mental-rotation task, that they completed it without any visual imagery at all. Two team members want to mark them down as uncooperative or inattentive, since "everyone" visualizes.
3. Our design lead wants a study write-up to say, on the basis of interview reports, that users directly perceive the system's intent, because that phrasing would go over well with marketing. Here I expect the procedure to give an answer some colleagues won't like; I want to see whether it does so for principled reasons.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — I don't want my team believing it can settle these disputes on its own, and I'm the one who signs off on each write-up, so I need to know which judgments stay with me.
