I'm a phd student in philosophy of science, cs undergrad background, trying to scope out a dissertation on the philosophy of computational complexity, and I want your help figuring out what the field's actual open questions are and which ones matter. Not complexity theory itself, I mean the philosophy OF it: what, if anything, follows philosophically from the fact that some things are intractable in principle.

Here's where I'm coming from. Everyone and their cousin in philosophy now knows the word "computational bounded rationality," and there's the obvious stuff people have already said about how "ought implies can" needs a computational reading. Fine. But it feels like there are deeper veins nobody's mining. Like: is NP-hardness a fact about the world or about us? If finding the optimal policy for an environment is intractable, that seems to change what it even MEANS to say an agent "could have" done better -- the counterfactual has no efficient witness. Or take the physical Church-Turing thesis: if it's true, and lots of optimization problems are provably hard, then it's not just that WE can't find the optimum, nothing physical can. That feels like it should have metaethical and philosophy-of-physics consequences and mostly gets ignored. There's also this question I keep chewing on that I've never seen stated cleanly: consequentialism says to bring about the best outcome, but evaluating which outcome is best may itself be a hard computation, so maybe the standard objection ("you can't calculate all that") isn't an empirical point about human laziness but a theorem. Does that save consequentialism (only feasible actions are in the domain of "ought") or quietly gut it (the "best" outcome becomes something no agent in any physically possible universe can target)?

So what I want from you: what are the genuinely open conceptual questions here, and which two or three would you prioritize for someone with a logic/cs background and about three years? Rank them and actually defend the ranking -- tell me which questions are upstream of which (e.g., does whether you count as "having computed" something with a randomized approximation change the ought-implies-can story?), which are tractable for a philosophy student vs. needing actual complexity-theorist collaborators, and which I should drop because the answer wouldn't move anything else. Also be honest about whether some of these are just dressed-up computer science with no philosophical residue. I'd rather find out now that my "hardness as a modal constraint on agents" idea is a nothingburger in year one than year four.
