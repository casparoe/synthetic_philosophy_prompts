I teach a second-year seminar on rational choice and decision theory — fourteen students, one 90-minute session, a whiteboard, index cards, and a token currency of grade points to use as payoffs, since I can't use real money. Two weeks ago I helped my brother move apartments for the fourth time in two years, and somewhere between carrying boxes it dawned on me that the chain of trades that got him there — each one feeling obviously right on its own, each one costing fees and deposits — had returned him to roughly where he started, several thousand euros lighter. The module I'm teaching this term covers the money pump argument, and for the first time I find I genuinely care about it, and I want my students to care too.

Design the session for me as a structured activity rather than a lecture. The participants should run into the problem before it is named. Specifically:

1. The preferences that get pumped should be elicited, not scripted. I want them choosing between multi-attribute options — flats differing in rent, commute, and noise, say — in pairwise comparisons only, so that any cycle emerges from their own choices over dimensional tradeoffs rather than from an arbitrary list I hand them. If the resulting preferences happen to be transitive, I need a fallback: tell me how to construct the options so a cycle is likely without forcing it.

2. Some participants should then be able to profit from other participants' cycles — arbitrageurs who may propose trades, hold options, and release them, with payoffs in the token currency. The pump should work as a sequence of individually acceptable trades rather than as anything resembling a threat.

3. Crucially, I want the escape routes built in as further rounds of the same game, because the interesting philosophy is in them. Run at least these three: a round where agents can bind themselves in advance to a plan before any offers are made; a round where every trade carries a small fixed transaction fee; and a round where the agent simply has the standing right to decline any offer, with the arbitrageur allowed to respond however she likes. For each round, tell me what happens to the pump, and make sure the debrief maps the round onto a specific dialectical move in the literature — whether the argument survives once the agent can be resolute, or foresee the whole sequence and choose accordingly, or make the exploit unprofitable with transaction costs.

4. The debrief needs to be honest about what was and wasn't shown. In particular, I want my students to see the gap between the claim that intransitive agents can be exploited and the claim that intransitive preferences are irrational — and also whether money pumps, even at their best, establish only that preferences must be acyclic rather than fully transitive.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My worry: students who've understood the setup will deliberately report intransitive preferences, get themselves pumped, and split the arbitrageur's take, which is rational collusion and ruins the activity — so tell me whether to prevent that or to use it.

Finally, an honest note on which feature of the idea no classroom game can reproduce, and which I'll have to supply myself — my guess is the intrapersonal dimension, since the real argument is about one agent across time being a counterparty to her own past choices, and my setup pits students against each other instead. But that's a guess.

One more thing, and I mean it: if you think this brief rests on a confused premise, tell me plainly instead of designing around it. It has crossed my mind that what happened to my brother wasn't a money pump at all but ordinary preference drift, or status quo bias in each trade, and that I'm about to build a whole session on a misread anecdote; it has also crossed my mind that a classroom pump can show that intransitive agents lose tokens without showing anything about rationality, and that no amount of clever round design fixes that. If either of those is right — or if something else in what I've asked for doesn't cohere — say so at the top, and then tell me what the activity can honestly do.
