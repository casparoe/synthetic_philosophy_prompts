My seminar is working through The Mind's I (the 1981 Hofstadter and Dennett anthology), and the piece that stages a conversation with Einstein's brain — his brain having been transcribed, in effect, into a book, so that "talking to Einstein" means consulting successive pages that tell you what he would say next — has left me with an argument I can neither shake nor accept:

1. Nothing about consciousness depends on what a system is made of, only on how it is organized — on how its states depend on one another. Denying this seems to make one a dualist about carbon, or to require a principled account of why silicon can't do what carbon can.
2. For any finite system — say, a lifetime of Einstein's conversation — there is a book whose pages, consulted by a perfectly mechanical clerk, reproduce that conversation exactly: ask anything Einstein would ever have answered, and the clerk turns to the right page and reads.
3. So the clerk-and-book system is organized, in every way that could matter to Einstein's mentality, just as Einstein was.
4. So by (1), the clerk-and-book system is Einstein: conscious, thinking, having the conversation.
5. The clerk can be a mindless demon who merely looks things up; whatever intelligence the exchange displays must reside in the pages, not in the looker-upper.
6. So the book itself is conscious, and Einstein survived as ink on paper.
7. But the same pages could be consulted as a manual for indefinitely many different conversations; a photocopy would be as conscious as the original; tearing out half the pages would leave Einstein diminished not at all. That is absurd.
8. Therefore (1) is false: organization is not enough, and consciousness does care about the substrate or the realization after all.

I don't want to give up (1) — giving it up seems to push me toward dualism or biological chauvinism — but I also cannot believe the book is Einstein. So I suspect the argument slips somewhere between (2) and (4): perhaps a page that says what Einstein would do next is not the same as a state that does it. But I can't make that stick, because the clerk-and-book system does seem to have the right state-to-state dependencies — what gets said next really does depend on what was said before, via the page turns.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the trick is a slide between matching Einstein's behavior and instantiating his causal organization, I need to be shown exactly where in the numbered steps that equivocation occurs and what work the clerk is or is not doing. Please also state plainly the weakest point in your own diagnosis: what would a defender of (3) say against you, and what consideration would push you to give up (1) instead?
