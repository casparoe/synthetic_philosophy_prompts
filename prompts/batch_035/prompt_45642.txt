I've been kicking around this idea on my own for a while and I want to test it against a real opponent, not just my own head nodding along. Here's the setup: please play a committed defender of the claim that 'better than' is transitive — if A is better than B and B better than C, then A is better than C, always. Make them sharp, not a strawman: willing to concede small points, never the main one, and stay in character until I say stop. Keep replies to about a paragraph so it feels like a conversation. Also, have them be honest about uncertainty: if they're not sure of something, they should say so in character rather than bluffing, and maybe end each reply by flagging the point they're least confident about — I want to beat the real position, not a pose.

My opening move. Think about sweetening coffee with sugar, one spoon at a time. Coffee with one spoon tastes better than coffee with none (to me, and I'd bet to most people). Two spoons beat one. Three beats two. Keep going up to ten spoons — each single extra spoon makes it a little better, or at worst the same, because the change is too small to wreck anything. But coffee with ten spoons of sugar is obviously worse than black coffee. So the chain of little improvements leads somewhere worse than the start. To save transitivity you have to bite a bullet: either one of my tiny step-judgments is flat wrong (fine — which one, and why that one?), or you have to tell me ten spoons beats black coffee, which nobody believes. Your character's move — go.
