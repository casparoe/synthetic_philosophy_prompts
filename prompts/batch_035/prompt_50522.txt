I teach a mid-level undergraduate elective called "Time, Memory, and Forgetting," and next month we reach Nietzsche's *On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life*. I'd like you to act as an instructional designer who also has graduate training in nineteenth-century philosophy: I want an in-class role-play that makes sixteen students *feel* the difference between what Nietzsche calls the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of history, before I ever put those names on the board. Here is the case I've been mulling, which you should refine or replace if you see something better: a fictional mid-sized town, Weilstadt, must decide what to do with its founder's statue and the decaying archive building next to it. The founder, August Reinecke, built the town's fortune in the 1880s, and newly digitized papers show his company's profits depended on coerced labor in its overseas trading posts. The town council has budget for exactly one of three options: restore both the statue and the archive to their original condition; demolish the statue, keep the archive, and build a small interpretive centre with the salvaged materials; or sell the archive's contents to a private collector, clear the site, and build a park. The students should be split into three lobbying factions, each secretly bound to a rule: one may only cite the past as a source of exemplars and inspiration, one may only speak in the register of preservation and local belonging, and one may only bring the past to judgment and condemn it. A small council of three students, bound by no rule, must actually choose, and the payoff structure should make the factions genuinely compete — perhaps each faction earns "persuasion points" for arguments the council later adopts, with some small prize, so no real money is needed.
