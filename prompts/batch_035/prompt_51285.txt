I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, the last twenty working on signal detection in analog instrumentation, and now that I am retired I have been trying to educate myself in philosophy of mind. A popular book I recently finished contains a five-sentence summary of computationalism that strikes me as tidy in a suspicious way. Paraphrasing its substance, the passage runs: computationalism is the thesis that thinking is computation, so the mind stands to the brain as software stands to hardware; Turing proved that any computer can simulate any other, so the brain, whatever it physically is, is equivalent in power to a universal Turing machine, and a program running on silicon could duplicate everything it does; this is why the Church-Turing thesis guarantees that whatever the laws of physics allow, a computer can compute; Searle's Chinese Room then proved that such a program could not be conscious, because symbols manipulated by pure syntax can never add up to understanding; so computationalism was refuted by the 1980s, and what remains is at most the weaker claim that the brain processes information like a computer in the loose everyday sense. My professional instinct says something is off in the second and third sentences — my whole career was spent with continuous physical systems that resist tidy discretization — but I do not trust my instincts in a field I have only recently entered, and I cannot tell whether the fourth sentence overstates its case. Please audit the passage for me. Take the five sentences in order and restrict yourself to exactly five numbered points, one per sentence, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each, tell me whether a working computationalist would accept it as stated, accept it only with an important qualification, or reject it outright, and make clear which distinction or overstatement is doing the damage — and in the fifth point, tell me whether the author's concluding verdict about the fate of computationalism actually follows from whatever survives your corrections.
