My friend and I got into a three-hour argument after a chess game the other night, and there's one move she kept making that I can't stop thinking about, and I want to know whether it has an actual name in philosophy or whether she's just making stuff up.

It started when I asked her what she meant by "because." I'd said the game ended because of checkmate -- not that the checkmate caused the game to end like one event shoves another one, and not that it's a law of nature that it did -- just that the way the pieces stood on the board at that moment was what made it true that the game was over. She said yes, exactly, that's a different kind of because. I said fine, isn't that just logic, the game is over follows from the board state? And here's where it got interesting. She said no, because followings come too cheap. The fact that the board is checkmate-or-not-checkmate also follows from the checkmate, but she would never say the game ended because of checkmate-or-not-checkmate. Some followings are, in her words, backwards or padded, and the real because only runs one way between certain pairs of facts, and it's not cause, and it's not just consequence.

Then she piled on examples. A ball is red because it's scarlet, never scarlet because it's red. There's a crowd in the square because these particular people are standing in the square, not the other way around. Chemistry holds the way it does because of physics, and physics does not hold the way it does because of chemistry. And something about sets that I honestly lost the thread of -- the singleton of Socrates exists because Socrates does, but Socrates doesn't exist because his singleton does -- and when I said that just sounds like a fact about which sentence we happen to say first she got genuinly offended.

Two things I want to know. First, does this "real because" she kept insisting on have an established name -- an actual term of art -- and if there isn't one exact match, what are the nearest things and where do they come apart? Second, is her extra claim load-bearing or decoration: she kept saying these dependences come in layers and the layers must have a floor, something that everything else stands on that stands on nothing. It seemed obvious to me you could accept her because-relation and still deny there's a floor, and when I said that she switched to saying the floor is the whole point. So which is it?

I'd like the answer in four sections, in this order and rough sizes. (1) "The name" -- short, tell me straight whether there's an established term and give it if so. (2) "What it isn't" -- medium, walk through the neighbors (cause, plain logical consequence, reduction, and whatever else is nearby) and say for each one exactly why it fails to be her relation; she will grill me on the edge cases, so be precise there. (3) "The floor" -- medium, is the must-have-a-bottom-level claim a substantive addition to the relation or does it fall out of the relation for free, and can you coherently hold the relation without the floor or vice versa? (4) "Who won" -- short, in the argument as I've described it, which of us was actually right, or were we both partly right and confused about different things?
