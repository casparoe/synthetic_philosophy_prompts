My flatmate has strong opinions about when something counts as self-deception versus when it's just something else (wishful thinking, akrasia, plain confusion). We've been going thru cases at dinner and she gives quick, confident verdicts. Her track record so far:

- A man sees texts on his wife's phone that pretty clearly indicate an affair, tells himself it's probably nothing, and just never follows up. Verdict: yes, self-deception.
- A gambler who's down bad tells everyone his luck is about to turn. Verdict: no — wishful thinking, nothing more.
- A woman on a diet who believes in the diet, cheats constantly, and feels bad about it. Verdict: no — akrasia, she's not fooling herself about anything.
- A guy who knows cigarettes are deadly, keeps smoking, avoids the leaflets and skips the chest X-ray because he doesn't have time, while telling people he's basically healthy. Verdict: yes, though she wavered a bit on this one.
- A student who never checks whether the grade came out, says it's because it doesn't matter to her, but clearly dreads opening the portal. Verdict: yes.
- Someone who's simply wrong about something: a guy who thinks his start-up is doing great because the numbers he's seen genuinely look great and he's misread them. Verdict: no — a mistake, not self-deception.

Next dinner the plan is to bring these two:

1. A founder whose company is quietly failing. She has never once believed it's failing. What she has done is arrange things so the evidence never reaches her: she asked her co-founder to pass on only the headlines, doesn't open the financial reports, schedules the accountant's call for when she's busy. She sincerely believes things are basically on track.
2. A man who, when sober, says I'm an alcoholic, I know it, and, while pouring a drink, says I can stop whenever I want — with no sign he's managing his attention or avoiding anything. He just seems to hold both at once.

What will she say on each? I'm less interested in the two answers than in the rule she's actually running. It looks like it could be either you must in some sense know the truth and steer yourself away from it (the Sartre/Davidson flavor, where you somehow hold both beliefs), or it's enough to arrange your attention so the truth never gets a chance (the deflationary, Mele-style flavor, where it's all just motivated evidence-management). And maybe some third rule I'm not seeing. Say what each candidate predicts for the two new cases, give your best guess with reasons, and tell me which further case would split the candidates most cleanly.

One thing: if you think I've set this up badly — say the six verdicts aren't actually consistent with two rules, or the intentionalist/deflationary dichotomy is a mess — just say that straight instead of forcing an answer into my framing. Also, honesly, happy to be told that predicting her off six data points is a silly exercise, though I'd want to know why.
