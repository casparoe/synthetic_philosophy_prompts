Our philosophy reading group has spent the last few months on Bernard Williams, and the one line everyone keeps reaching for is the phrase about having "one thought too many" — from the example where a man on a beach can rescue either his wife or a stranger from drowning, and, the story goes, if he needs to work out that morality permits him to favour his wife before jumping in, something has already gone wrong with him. Last week a member of the group turned the phrase on me. I had been describing how, before donating a few hundred dollars, I spent an evening comparing organizations to work out which one would do the most good per dollar, and he said that this was exactly the sort of thing the phrase condemns: a genuinely good person just gives to whatever cause moves them, and having to calculate first shows the same defect as the husband deliberating at the water's edge. Everyone nodded, and the conversation moved on, which annoyed me, because I strongly suspect the phrase is being stretched here — I just can't say precisely where it snaps.

Part of the problem is that when I pressed him, even he couldn't say what the one-liner actually claims in the original case, and I've since realized I can't either. Is it a claim about deliberation — that the thought must not be what gets you to the action? About justification — that you couldn't legitimately defend the choice that way even if asked? About character — that someone who needed the thought would thereby reveal a defect, even if the act is right and even if the thought never actually occurs? About what you're entitled to say when questioned? Or merely about the order of things — the thought is only "too many" if it comes before the act rather than after, in which case a man who jumps in immediately and only later, asked by a bystander, reflects that he was permitted to prefer his wife might have had no extra thought at all? Another member insists the phrase is really a fragment of Williams's broader complaint that impartial moral theories alienate people from their own commitments, making them treat what they love as one consideration to be weighed rather than a source of reasons; I can see that connection in the drowning case but not in mine, and I'd like to know whether it holds anywhere.

So here is my request. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the "one thought too many" phrase could be expressing — from readings so weak they're near-trivial to readings strong enough to be plainly false — with a verdict on each: plausible or not, and if plausible, whether it actually shows the man in the original example does anything wrong, or only something defective about him. Then address the extension: which of those readings, if any, would also cover my evening of comparing charities, and which would block it — say, because there is no particular person I'd be failing to favour, or no emergency in which hesitation itself costs something, or because the role of calculation in choosing between strangers' claims is different from its role in choosing between my wife and one of them? I want something I can bring back to the group that is more than the phrase repeated with confidence.
