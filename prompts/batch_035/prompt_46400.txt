My friend Maya is the person our whole group consults whenever an artist gets in trouble, because her verdicts are instant and, after years of this, weirdly consistant. Her track record as far as I can reconstruct it: Wagner's operas are unambiguously great, and the man's antisemitism is, in her words, his problem and not the Ring's; she'll defend Caravaggio forever — a literal murderer, she points out, and the paintings are none the worse for it; she flat out refuses to call Triumph of the Will a good film even while admitting it's formally stunning and basically invented the visual language of propaganda; she'll say a certain roast special, where every laugh comes from humiliating a specific audience member, is bad comedy and not just bad manners; when our beloved sitcom turned out to have a star who'd made the crew's lives hell for a decade, she said the show is still good art — she just won't watch it, stream it, or put a cent anywhere near it; and a landscape painter cheating on his wife doesn't even register with her as a fact about the paintings.

Here's why this suddenly matters to me: I run our book club, we're two hundred pages into a living novelist's trilogy, and this week the news broke about him (credible allegations, he denies everything, his publisher just dropped him). The books themselves are morally fine — kind, even. Before I message Maya I want a prediction, because I've been trying to reverse-engineer her rule and I keep finding several that fit her record equally well: one where only flaws inside the work count and biography is noise, one where biography matters only through who profits when you engage, one about works whose craft is in service of an immoral aim, and maybe something where knowing facts about the author changes what the work even says. So: what will she say about whether the trilogy is good art, and what will she do about finishing it? Which of those rules best explains her whole track record, and where do the runners-up predict something different?

There's also a harder case I want to bring to her, and I'd rather know what each rule predicts before I hear her answer, so I can tell whether she's being consistent or just following vibes: a long-dead poet whose famous love poems turned out to be addressed to someone he was stalking, so that now, with everyone knowing, the tenderness reads as something creepy — the words haven't changed but it feels like the poem has. If you had to design one further case to split her candidate rules apart cleanly, what would it be and what would each rule say about it?
