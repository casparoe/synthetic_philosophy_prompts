I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been working on a piece about collective memory, so now I have to figure out where I actually stand instead of quoting everyone. Here are my gut verdicts, for better or worse: Germany maintaining Holocaust memorials and teaching it in schools — obviously right to remember. South Africa archiving the truth commission testimony — right, even though testifying reopened wounds for some victims. A survivor of an atrocity who just wants to get on with her life and never speak of it — she's allowed to forget, no duty binds her, and I'd even say urging her to remember is a bit obscene. Sealing a teenager's minor vandalism records after some years so they stop showing up in searches — fine, good even. Keeping 1700s trial transcripts available online — keep them, that's history. A town that reenacts a local massacre from 200 years ago every year, which rekindles the feud with the neighboring town each time — that feels wrong, though I can't quite say what they should do instead, since it's not exactly forgetting I want from them. And a movement that recites the other side's crimes at every rally — remembering as a weapon, clearly bad. So is there one principle about a duty to remember that gets all of these, or nearly? I go back and forth on whether the real line is individual vs collective, or the use the memory gets put to, and I suspect both cut wrongly somewhere. Give me your actual best answer — commit to one principle (or commit to the claim that there isn't one), defend it, tell me which of my verdicts it can't save, and tell me where it comes out on the case I keep waffling on: a country deciding whether to keep prosecuting 90-something-year-old camp guards. American English please, and please don't hand me a menu of options and let me pick — take the position yourself.
