I need your help turning some philosophy into something I can actually use at work. Three weeks ago I got voluntold into chairing the clinical ethics committee at our NHS trust (the old chair retired mid-term and apparently I wasn't quick enough to say no). We meet weekly, mostly getting referrals from ward staff about end-of-life stuff, confidentiality, and relatives demanding things. The decisions are often time-pressured -- sometimes a referral comes in at 4pm and someone has to act by evening, other times we get the full hour.

Here's the thing I've realised after my first few sessions: half our job isn't deciding "what should be done," it's deciding what KIND of situation we're in. Sometimes what looks like an agonising choice dissolves once someone spots a third option or notices that one of the two "obligations" is actually just family pressure or an institutional habit dressed up as a duty. But sometimes I genuinely can't see how any option avoids leaving something morally behind. And apparently philosophers have been fighting about this forever -- whether genuine moral dilemmas are even possible, or whether the idea of two real obligations you can't both satisfy is somehow incoherent (the "ought implies can" line, and Kant apparently thought duties conflicting was as absurd as a square circle, though someone else said the guilt you feel either way is exactly what marks a real dilemma). I've got this from one podcast and a W.D. Ross-shaped hole in my education, so my understanding is ropey, but the underlying question is now my actual day job.

So: please write me a triage procedure the committee can run in 20-30 minutes, before we've decided anything, to sort a referral into (at minimum) "apparent dilemma -- there's a hidden third option, or one duty clearly outranks the other" versus "genuine dilemma -- every option leaves a moral remainder, and the most we can do is pick the one we can live with." I want an ordered set of tests: what to check first, what counts as passing or failing each, where a "stop, this isn't a dilemma" answer comes from, and crucially where the procedure has to hand it back to human judgement because the question is one of values rather than facts. Whatever the actual philosophy says (prima facie duties, moral remainder, epistemic vs genuine dilemmas, whatever's useful), keep what matters from it and drop what we can't use in a hurry -- but be explicit about what got dropped and where that will make the procedure misfire. Also please be honest that "the nurse feels awful either way" is doing a lot of work in my thinking and may not be reliable evidence of anything.

Then run the procedure on two cases so I can see it work:

1. One ICU bed, two candidates -- a 68-year-old post-surgery with a decent prognosis if ventilated, and a 34-year-old whose prognosis is worse but who is much younger. No formal triage policy covers peacetime, both families are pushing hard, and the referring consultant says her gut says one thing and "first come first served" says another.

2. A GP who promised a patient he wouldn't tell his adult daughter about his new Huntington's diagnosis. The daughter is currently trying for a baby and could access predictive testing if she knew it ran in the family. The GP is asking us whether the confidentiality promise can be broken.

And if the procedure would hand me an answer I'm not going to like on either of those, tell me straight -- I'd rather find out now than in the meeting. Also flag anything you think the committee will inevitably misuse, e.g. treating a clean "apparent dilemma" verdict as moral permission, or using "it's a genuine dilemma" as a way to avoid taking responsibility for the choice.
