I'm a PhD student in economic history, and somewhere along the way I've settled on a position I'd like stress-tested. The broad family is the Weberian one: culture, and specifically religiously transmitted values, can be a genuine cause of economic behavior, not just a decoration that material conditions wear. But my version has a twist I haven't seen anyone defend quite this way, and I want to know what would actually break it.

My view: values don't cause anything on their own. A value only becomes causally efficacious once it's been institutionalized as a cost — someone has to actually enforce it, by sanction, exclusion, or shame, before it moves behavior at scale. So on my telling the Protestant ethic didn't produce capitalist accumulation directly through internalized guilt about idleness; it produced it once congregations started watching each other, and getting expelled from a dissenting community meant losing your credit network, your marriage prospects, and your business partners. The value is the content; the enforcement is the causal engine. The practical upshot is supposed to be that cultural explanations become testable: if values only work through this pricing mechanism, then you should find economic behavior tracking enforcement structures, not stated beliefs — two communities with identical doctrine but different monitoring should diverge, and loosening the enforcement should loosen the behavior within a generation or two.

What would count against it? I don't want the standard objections rehearsed back at me as if they were fatal — I already know about reverse causation (capitalists finding Calvinism convenient), about pre-Protestant commercial centers that supposedly already had the capitalist spirit, about people converting for economic reasons rather than the reverse. I know alot of that literature, and honestly I think my version already has answers to those: if values only work through enforcement, then it matters less which came first, the belief or the trade. What I want is the stuff that would hurt MY version specifically. Like: historical cases where a value seems to have shifted mass economic behavior with no enforcement apparatus anywhere in sight, and that aren't just confounded by something material. Or evidence that behavior persists for generations after the enforcement structure collapses, which would suggest the value got internalized in a way that doesn't need pricing anymore. Or cross-cultural data that tracks doctrinal content better than it tracks monitoring institutions. Rank the defeaters for me from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a defender of my view would concede hurts (I want to know what the honest concessions look like, not just the party line) versus which they'd bat away.

And here's the thing I'm honestly kind of worried about: I suspect my position has been quietly qualified into something close to unfalsifiability. The clause that values need enforcement is doing so much work that whenever someone points to values moving people without enforcement, I can always say the enforcement was there and we haven't found it yet — reputational costs are basically invisible in the archives. Is that fatal for a view of this kind, or is it just the normal situation for a mechanistic thesis in history where the mechanism is badly observed? I genuinely don't know whether a causal claim needs independently identifiable instances of the mechanism, or whether positing an unobserved mechanism is fine so long as it makes the right predictions elsewhere. Tell me straight which it is, and if the former, tell me what evidence would independently identify the enforcement mechanism in a way that isn't circular.

One more thing: I'd like you to say what my view predicts that its rivals don't — where it and a pure materialist explanation actually come apart, so the disagreement isn't just verbal. Keep the whole thing under 800 words. If you can't cover everything in that, prioritize the defeater ranking and the unfalsifiability question over the predictions part. Also feel free to tell me my twist isn't actually a twist — if some version of this is already the consensus way sociologists read Weber, say so plainly, that would count against my sense that I've discovered something and I'd rather know now than at a conference.
