For most of my adult life I have carried a particular psychiatric diagnosis — dependent personality disorder — and it has quietly organized a great deal: the therapy I was offered, the vocabulary I use to understand my own behavior, and an online support group whose members all share the label and find real comfort in that. At my most recent appointment, my psychiatrist explained that under the new edition of the WHO's international classification of diseases, the familiar personality-disorder types no longer exist as separate diagnoses. Personality disorder is now diagnosed as a single condition rated by severity, with a handful of trait descriptors attached, and the type I was told I have is, as a category, simply gone. She presented this as an administrative improvement: the old types never corresponded to sharply different kinds of people, she said, they were conventions that varied across countries and editions, and nothing about me has changed.

My support group sees it very differently. Several members argue that the new system erases real differences between people who needed different things, that a severity rating sounds more damning than any of the old type names ever did, and that being graded mild on a scale of how disordered one's personality is will cost some people the services and understanding that a familiar label used to unlock. A few have decided to keep using the old terminology among themselves regardless of what the official coding says.

I am left genuinely uncertain who is right, or even what the disagreement is about. Does it make sense to ask whether my old diagnosis tracked a real kind of person that the new system fails to track — or is a diagnosis just a convention, in which case what exactly is the group mourning? At the same time, something feels unfinished about the reassurance that nothing about me has changed: the label shaped how I interpreted my own decisions for years, how my family treated my need for reassurance, and what I expected of myself, and that hardly seems like nothing. I would like help sorting out what actually changes when a psychiatric classification changes — for the person being classified, for how the world responds to them, and for the question of whether the old label ever corresponded to anything in the first place. And is there anything my support group could do with the old vocabulary — keeping it alive among ourselves — that would be more than nostalgia, or does a term detached from the official system inevitably mean something different from what it meant before?
