I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the last few months reporting a feature on the design of messages intended for extraterrestrials: the 1974 Arecibo transmission with its binary counting preamble, its inventory of atomic numbers, and its pixel-grid drawings keyed to the transmitting wavelength; the Voyager Golden Record with its etched cover, its pulsar map, and its sketches of a stylus and a waveform meant to teach playback; and the contemporary METI community's arguments about whether and how to send something new. What keeps snagging me is not the physics or the ethics of broadcasting but the semiotics of the artifacts themselves. Everyone I interview has an implicit theory of what makes these things legible, and no two theories agree.

Here is the practice I want understood: message designers proceed as if decodability can be engineered into the message. They open with numbers (on the assumption that counting is a universal bootstrap), anchor units to physical constants like the hydrogen line, build up from arithmetic to chemistry to anatomy, and add diagrams that explain how to read the diagrams. Critics in the same community reply that a receiver still has to notice the signal is a message at all, then guess the medium, then guess the code — and that each of those steps is itself a message requiring interpretation, which seems to threaten a regress. I've been reading Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, where he distinguishes a frame message (the signal that says, in effect, here is something decodable), an outer message (the instructions for finding the decoding scheme), and an inner message (the content), and where he argues that meaning is never simply located inside a message — a record means nothing without a record player, and the outer message can only be read by a mind already equipped to sniff out isomorphisms, so some fraction of the meaning is always contributed by the receiver.

What I'd like from you is an analysis of what this design practice presupposes about the location of meaning. Does engineering a self-explaining message presuppose that intelligence is convergent enough that certain structures — primes, the hydrogen transition, pixel grids — function as a shared outer language, or merely that they raise the probability of successful decoding somewhat? Does the practice require Hofstadter's claim that meaning is partly in the message and partly in the interpreter, or is it compatible with the stronger view that almost everything is in the interpreter and the message merely triggers whatever the receiver already brings? Where is the practice internally inconsistent — the pulsar map assumes the receiver can already locate us in the galaxy, which is a fair amount of presupposed knowledge for an artifact meant to teach from scratch — and where is it instead underdetermined, such that rival theories of meaning would license the same design choices? And concretely: what would message design look like if designers accepted that the outer message cannot be made self-evident — redundancy instead of pedagogy, statistical structure instead of instruction? Please keep clear about what is settled among people who work on this, what is genuinely contested between, say, the mathematicians-universalists and the anthropologists of alien cognition, and where you are reporting a minority view; don't present one faction's take on decodability as the community's consensus, because I intend to quote you with that distinction intact.
