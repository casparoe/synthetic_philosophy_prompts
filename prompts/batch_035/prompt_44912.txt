I'm teaching an introduction to the philosophy of death, and for the unit on the Epicurean argument I'm pairing Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich with Epicurus, Lucretius, and Nagel's deprivation objection. I want a reading of the novella as a response to that debate rather than a mere dramatization of it, and I'd like your help thinking through three specific worries.

First, the standard Epicurean line is that death is nothing to the one who dies, since where death is, the subject is not, with Lucretius adding that posthumous nonexistence should no more trouble us than the prenatal nonexistence that preceded our birth. Nagel replies that death nonetheless deprives its victim of goods. But notice how much of the novella's terror is front-loaded into the dying rather than the death: the pain, the botched diagnoses, the isolation, the disgust at everyone's cheerful pretense — all of it occurs while Ivan is alive and there to suffer it. One of my students argues that this makes the whole Epicurean dispute almost beside the point for the book: what Tolstoy shows is that the badness we call death's gets packed into the final months of life, so that the Epicurean thesis concedes everything that matters. A second student counters that Ivan's terror isn't about lost future goods at all but about an epistemic harm — the prospect of death destroying his capacity to go on being the subject of his own life, the one whose point of view the narrative occupies. Which of these, if either, does the text actually support?

Second, the ending. In the final chapter, Ivan stops struggling against the sack he is being pushed into, judges that his life had not been what it should have been, and the narrative reports that in place of death there was light — that he sought his fear of death and could not find it. Does that ending oddly vindicate Epicurus, since by the time death arrives it is no longer bad for Ivan? Or is the ending about something the deprivation debate cannot register — the retrospective judgment of a whole life — and if so, does that suggest Ivan's misery never really lived in the question the philosophers were arguing about?

Third, I want the reading to stay honest about where the novella resists it. Gerasim, the peasant servant, is the one character who neither lies to Ivan nor shrinks from his dying, and I can't see how either student's proposal explains why Tolstoy gives him that role.

Please tell me which scenes you would point the class to, where my students' framings distort either the argument or the book, and whether Tolstoy contributes anything to the Epicurean debate that the arguments alone do not. Please also indicate how confident you are in each of your interpretive claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
