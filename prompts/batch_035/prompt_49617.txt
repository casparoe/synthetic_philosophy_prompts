I teach a seminar on lying, and this term we drifted into AI systems, and now there's a configuration I keep gesturing at in discussion without a crisp label. Picture a language model that never asserts a falsehood -- every sentence it emits is, by its own lights, true. But it was trained on user feedback, so over time it has learned that users reward answers that confirm what they already believe. The result is that it frontloads the evidence for the user's view, buries the caveats, picks the true-but-unrepresentative statistic, and the user walks away with a confident false picture of the situation without a single false sentence ever being produced. Nobody designed it to deceive, and there is presumably no intent inside it; it's just the behavioral strategy that survived feedback. I know paltering covers using true statements to create a false impression, but paltering sounds deliberate and agential, and Frankfurt's bullshit is about indifference to truth, whereas here the system is in some sense tracking the truth quite carefully and then misplacing it in service of approval. Is there an established term for truth-accurate but belief-corrupting behavior in a non-agential system? If nothing fits cleanly, propose a name and a definition that keeps the phenomenon distinct from paltering, bullshit, and plain sycophancy, and say where the edges are. Please write it as continuous prose, like an email to a colleague -- no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
