Over the past year, while reading into predictive processing, I've become a fairly committed defender of the free energy principle: the claim that any persistent organism must, on pain of dissolving, keep itself within a narrow envelope of characteristic states, that staying there amounts to minimizing surprise (or a variational upper bound on it), and that perception and action are two sides of this single imperative — perception revising the internal model to fit the world, action revising the world to fit the model. I want to stress that I hold this at the level of the grand principle, not just the neuroscientific process theories: I take it to cover bacteria navigating nutrient gradients as readily as brains doing causal inference, with neurons merely being one implementation of approximate Bayesian message passing.

My brother-in-law, a statistically literate engineer, thinks I've been had, and his objection has slowly lost its blunt edges and become sharper. His core charge is one of triviality: any system that exists at a non-equilibrium steady state will, by construction, be one that occupies a restricted set of states, so the principle holds of everything that survives and is silent about everything that doesn't — survivorship doing all the work, with the "minimization" language adding a Bayesian costume to what is essentially bookkeeping. When I answer that the principle's content lives in the process theories — predictive coding in cortex, precision-weighting accounts of psychotic symptoms, active-inference models that generate behavioral predictions he could test — he says that's exactly the retreat he'd expect: the grand claim shrinks to a framework whenever it's challenged, and a framework that accommodates any possible finding by reinterpreting it as evidence of some model the system must have had is not risking anything. He also presses what he calls the endogeneity problem: because agents under active inference select their own observations, the data that supposedly tests the theory is manufactured by the very systems the theory describes, so it can't surprise itself into refutation the way a proper empirical claim could. My instinct is that he's conflating the mathematical core, which is supposed to be necessarily true, with the empirical periphery, which is not — and that necessary truths shouldn't be discarded for lacking counterexamples — but I've heard enough versions of his argument now to want an honest reckoning rather than a defense.

So: what would actually count against my view? I'd like a ranked map of the defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward, sorted by decisiveness — including formal results (say, a demonstration that the Markov blanket formalism can't be non-gerrymandered for some class of systems we'd independently want to call cognitive), empirical findings (cortex turning out to implement something functionally unlike hierarchical approximate inference, or precision-based accounts of psychosis never cashing out into a successful intervention their rivals can't match), and the more insidious conceptual casualties (the principle being shown to collapse into, or be fully restated by, some far weaker and older claim about self-organizing systems, so that its apparent depth is notational). For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it as aimed at a strawman reading, and say which reading — the mathematical core, the neural implementation claim, or the everything-is-inference metaphysics — is exposed by which defeater. Then give me your honest verdict on the unfalsifiability worry itself: is the retreat from grand principle to framework a legitimate division of labor of the kind any mature research program needs, or a slow-motion qualification into invulnerability, and does your answer depend on whether the process theories have so far generated genuinely novel predictions rather than post hoc redescriptions? Finally, I want one concrete thing: an experiment or observation, described specifically enough that an experimentalist could run it, such that a specified outcome would make a reasonable Fristonian substantially revise or abandon the view. What would personally move me most is being shown a rival framework that explains everything my principle explains while risking something it doesn't — but I'd settle for learning that the reason no such rival exists is that nothing is being risked on either side.
