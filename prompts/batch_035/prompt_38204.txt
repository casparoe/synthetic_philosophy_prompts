I've been an avid reader of LessWrong and the EA Forum since the early 2010s, so I've watched longtermism go from a niche obsession of a few Oxford philosophers and existential-risk researchers to a movement with serious money and serious critics. But I've noticed that in online discussions, several distinct claims get constantly run together under the label "the hinge of history," and I'd like some help untangling them. Assume the role of a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics and decision theory under moral uncertainty, and please think carefully before answering — ideally, start with a short brainstorm in which you list the claims you'll need to distinguish and the relations between them, and only then give the polished explanation.

Here is the tangle as I see it. In his book on longtermism, and in the journal article he built on it, William MacAskill distinguishes different strengths of the claim that our era is unusually influential. At the strong end, there's the claim that the present is the single most influential time in the history of civilization — that how things go in the next few decades will shape the entire future, perhaps via mechanisms like an upcoming technology-driven lock-in of values, after which the trajectory is largely fixed. At the weaker end, there's something like the time-of-perils hypothesis: that we live in an unusually dangerous period, bracketed by a past without destructive technology and a future where civilization either learns to manage the relevant risks or no longer exists to manage anything. These are clearly different claims — you could accept the weaker one while rejecting the stronger — and I've also seen discussion of a further comparative question that changes the practical upshot entirely: even if the present is unusually influential, a philanthropist might do more good by investing resources and having their descendants act later, when resources compound and the situation is clearer. That "patient philanthropy" option seems to interact with hinge claims in a way I find genuinely confusing: if the hinge is now, giving later looks like a mistake; if the hinge is later, giving now looks like a mistake; if there is no hinge at all, the question becomes a race between compounding returns and the erosion of opportunities, and I don't have a good feel for what determines the winner.

So here is what I'd like from you. First, set out the distinct claims with reasonable labels — say, the strong hinge claim, the weak hinge claim (or time of perils), and the comparative timing question — and explain precisely what each commits you to and what evidence would bear on each. Second, explain the main lines of argument on each side as I've encountered them: roughly, the case for a present hinge from new technologies like AI and engineered pathogens, and the case against from base-rate reasoning (why, of all the generations that have believed themselves pivotal, should we expect ourselves to be the ones who are right?) and from an inductive pattern in which each era's "unprecedented" opportunities look less special from further away. Third, and this is the part I care most about, give me a gut-level understanding of how the pieces fit together practically: sketch the decision faced by someone with resources today who takes moral uncertainty about all these claims seriously, and walk through how their optimal behavior shifts as their credences in the various hinge claims move up and down. Where the reasoning gets mathematical — expected-value comparisons, discounting, compounding — keep it intuitive rather than formal; I want the logic, not the formulas.

A few constraints: don't pad the answer with reading lists or named citations; I'm interested in the ideas, and you should explain them from first principles as much as possible. Also, please be explicit about which parts of the picture are relatively agreed upon and which remain live disputes — I'd rather see the fault lines clearly drawn than get a falsely tidy consensus. Length is not a virtue in itself; aim for an answer that a longtime forum reader like me would find genuinely clarifying rather than repetitive of the standard talking points.
