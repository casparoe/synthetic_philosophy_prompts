My grandmother was diagnosed last month with a form of dementia that has already taken much of her memory and, at times, her ability to recognize her own family. Over the holidays, my uncle — who studied Islamic philosophy as an undergraduate and has lately been reading Avicenna again — tried to comfort us by invoking what he called Avicenna's flying man. As he tells it, Avicenna asked his readers to imagine a person created fully formed in a single instant, an adult with no prior experiences, no acquired knowledge, and no sensory input whatsoever: no sight, no hearing, not even the feeling of air or of his own limbs, and yet suspended in space rather than resting on anything. Such a person, my uncle says, would still be certain of his own existence — he would affirm that he is while being unable to affirm anything about his body, or even that he has one. From this my uncle concludes that Avicenna proved the self to be a simple, immaterial substance wholly distinct from the body; that it is therefore untouched by anything that befalls the body, the brain included; and that my grandmother's self, accordingly, persists undiminished behind the damage, much as it would survive the body's death altogether. The rest of the family found this moving. I found myself unsure whether it was even a fair statement of Avicenna, let alone a sound application of him, but I did not want to challenge it at the table.

Something about the inference strikes me as too quick. It seems one thing to show that my awareness of my own existence does not run through my senses — that I could know that I am without thereby knowing anything about my body — and quite another to show that I could exist, or think, or remember, without a body at all. And I have a vague recollection that Avicenna himself was quite careful about which powers of the soul operate through bodily organs and which do not, which is presumably part of why he needed separate arguments for the soul's immateriality and its survival, rather than resting everything on this single thought experiment. I also seem to remember that the flying man is often compared to Descartes's cogito, though I have no idea whether scholars think the comparison goes deep or only skin-deep, and I would rather not dismiss my uncle's version merely because it comforted us.

Please go through his account point by point. Which parts would a careful scholar of Avicenna accept as an accurate statement of the thought experiment and of what it was meant to establish; which are overstatements, conflations, or leaps the argument itself does not license; and where exactly does the gap open between what the thought experiment shows and what he draws from it? I would especially like to know whether his final claim — that a self, on Avicenna's view, is unaffected by brain damage — is actually supported by Avicenna's own system, given how Avicenna distributes the soul's functions between the rational soul and the faculties that serve it through the body, or whether my uncle has silently substituted a stronger thesis for a weaker one. Finally, tell me honestly what, if anything, in his version could still be said to the family without distortion; if the flying man cannot carry the weight he placed on it, I want to know that before I repeat any of it to my cousins or to my grandmother's doctors.
