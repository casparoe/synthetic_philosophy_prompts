As a retired physical chemist who has only lately turned to moral philosophy, I have been working through Peter Singer's famous argument from the drowning child: roughly, that if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening without sacrificing anything nearly as important, we are obliged to do it, and that the accident of physical distance or the number of bystanders does not weaken this. As a scientist, what strikes me about the principle is that it seems to lack any internal damping: as global need grows, the obligation it generates grows without bound, which offends my instinct for systems that settle into equilibrium. But before I decide whether the argument is actually unsound, I need to know which objections to it are serious and which are noise. I have assembled seven candidate objections, some half-remembered from the secondary literature and some of my own devising, and I can no longer tell the difference. I would like you to rank them from most to least forceful as objections to Singer's argument, and to assign each one a strength rating between 0 and 1, where 1 means the objection would defeat the argument in open debate against a competent defender of Singer and 0 means it is barely worth raising — I want numbers I can defend, in the way I would want error bars on a measurement.

The objections, in no particular order:

1. The proximity objection: the child in the pond is directly before me, whereas distant beneficiaries are mediated by institutions, so the two cases differ in kind and not merely in degree.
2. The demandingness objection: the principle, consistently applied, requires giving until one is at roughly the level of marginal utility, a level of sacrifice that no plausible morality can demand of a person.
3. The epistemic objection: the rescue of a child before my eyes is a direct observation, whereas the effect of a donation to a distant relief agency is a noisy estimate passing through several unverified links in a causal chain, and it is a methodological error to treat the two as evidentially equivalent.
4. The fair-shares objection: if everyone contributed a fair share, no one would need to give more, so an individual's duty extends only to that share, and it is unfair that the conscientious should bear the costs of the unconscientious.
5. The perturbation objection: aid injected into a distant system can shift it to a worse equilibrium, much as an intervention in a coupled system can have effects opposite to those intended, so rescue at a distance is not causally analogous to pulling a child from a pond.
6. The character objection: a morality that permits no upper bound on sacrifice leaves no room for a life of one's own — for projects, partialities, and relationships — and is therefore self-undermining in the manner of a design that cannot be operated without destroying its operator.
7. The institutional objection: at large scale, duties of rescue fall on states and institutional actors rather than individuals, just as large-scale physical processes are properly described at the level of macroscopic variables rather than by tracking each molecule.

For each objection, give one or two sentences explaining its ranking. Then, please flag any item on my list that is miscalibrated, in the sense we would use of an instrument: that is, any objection that attacks a version of the argument no serious defender actually holds, so that it appears strong only because it is aimed at a strawman. Finally, tell me which single objection you would stake a case on if you had to defend it against a philosopher who knows this literature far better than I do.
