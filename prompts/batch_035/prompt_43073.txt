I'm the curator of a mid-sized science museum, and we're redesigning our permanent galleries, which are currently carved into period wings: "The Medieval World," "The Renaissance," and "The Scientific Revolution." At the first planning meeting, our academic consultant — a historian of science — invoked Steven Shapin's short 1996 book on the Scientific Revolution, which famously opens by announcing that there was no such thing and then goes on to write a whole book about it anyway. She used that denial to argue we should scrap period labels entirely and go thematic instead ("Instruments and Measurement," "Worlds and Models," "Authorities and Doubts"). A trustee pushed back: whatever historians say among themselves, our visitors arrive with those period labels already in their heads, and besides, the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries really did give us Galileo, Kepler, Bacon, Boyle, Newton, and the founding of the Royal Society — how can a label for all that be pure fiction? I have to write a recommendation, and I refuse to base it on a one-liner I don't understand.

So take the denial itself — there was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution — and tell me what precise claims it could actually be making, laid out as a menu ordered from least to most radical. I can imagine several candidates and I want them all on the table, plus any I haven't thought of: that the label is anachronistic because the people involved had no word for what they were doing, the word scientist not being coined until the nineteenth century and the phrase scientific revolution being largely a twentieth-century construction of historians like Koyré and Butterfield; that real changes occurred but were neither as sudden nor as unified as revolution implies, with alchemy, astrology, and natural magic woven through the work of the very figures we'd canonize, and with deep medieval roots — the universities, the recovery of Aristotle, the logic and natural philosophy of the scholastics — so that the "before" picture of stagnant darkness is itself a myth inherited from Renaissance humanists like Petrarch complaining about the age between antiquity and themselves; that the whole habit of periodizing is the problem, since the same argument seems to apply to the Middle Ages and to the Renaissance, and historians have long spoken of a twelfth-century renaissance and a "long Renaissance" that make the standard boundaries look arbitrary; up to the most radical reading, that the label doesn't merely imperfectly describe events but manufactures a single coherent rupture that distorts both what came before and what happened during it. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's flatly false, and crucially whether it would actually support my consultant's use of it — scrapping the period labels rather than just re-scoping or qualifying them.

Two things I need for the report to survive scrutiny. First, mark clearly where historians genuinely disagree versus where there's real consensus — my impression is that the revisionist attack on the Scientific Revolution is itself contested, with serious scholars arguing the revisionists overcorrected and that something deep and distinctive really did change in the seventeenth century, and I need to know whether that impression is right. Don't present a contested revisionist position as the settled view; the trustee will spot it and use it to dismiss the entire report. Second, since I'm the one who has to sign off, tell me plainly which of these readings you're confident about and which you're reconstructing on the fly, and where the honest answer is that the professionals are still arguing. If it turns out that the defensible readings only justify softening our labels rather than abolishing them, say so — I'd rather overrule my consultant with reasons than rubber-stamp her with a slogan.
