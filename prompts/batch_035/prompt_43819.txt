I spent thirty-five years teaching statistical mechanics and physical chemistry — mostly entropy, rare fluctuations, and the care one must take with averages that hide the exceptional excursion — and only since retiring have I taken up philosophy in any serious way. The problem that has followed me out of the physics journals and into my armchair is the Boltzmann brain: in the far future of an ever-expanding universe, a brain complete with memories of an afternoon exactly like this one can fluctuate into being out of thermal noise, and such a fluctuation is supposedly far more probable than the entire surrounding world it seems to remember. I would like a sestina about this.

Take as your speaker a retired scientist taking tea on an ordinary afternoon, and take your six end-words from the furniture of that afternoon — the concrete objects out of which a memory of it would be assembled. Let the sestina's rule carry the philosophical weight: the same six words return in the mandated pattern, stanza after stanza, reassembling into lines that read like one continuous life, and the poem should make a reader hold both readings at once — a real afternoon remembered, and one lucky shuffle of a fixed stock of words. The lines should mostly make ordinary sense, so that the coherence feels cheap; that is the point. The envoi should put the question squarely — whether a pattern assembled under a constraint is any evidence of a past at all — and leave it open. The philosophers I have been reading split between treating this as a genuine crisis for cosmology and as a parlor trick, and I want the poem honest about not knowing which side is right. Please resist the twin temptations of whimsy and cosmic dread; the eeriness should come from the arithmetic, not from the adjectives.
