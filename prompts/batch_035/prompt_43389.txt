In the study group for my Indian philosophy seminar, two classmates spent a whole session locked in an argument about whether testimony deserves its place in the Nyāya system as a fourth independent pramāṇa — a distinct reliable means of knowledge — alongside perception, inference, and analogy. The setup, roughly: the empiricist of the two opened by claiming that testimonial knowledge isn't a kind unto itself. When a trustworthy colleague tells me the train leaves at six, my knowledge is just an inference: I perceive her words, and from that plus background beliefs about her competence and sincerity I infer that the train does in fact leave at six. "Testimony" is a convenient label for a familiar package of perception and inference, not a separate source.

The Nyāya partisan answered that this reduction makes ordinary learning impossible. A four-year-old knows things her mother tells her, though she has no track record to infer from; and worse, the reduction invites a regress: to learn that speakers are generally reliable, I'd have to check their claims independently, but the tools I'd use — books, maps, asking other people — are themselves testimonial, so the background belief is never earned. The empiricist conceded the regress point but offered a patch: treat trust as a default stance rather than the conclusion of an inference. You accept what you're told absent specific reason not to, the way perception delivers appearances unless something goes wrong — and defeasibility, he added, can't be evidence of sourcehood, since perception is defeasible too (the rope mistaken for a snake). The Nyāya partisan replied that this patch surrenders everything worth fighting over: a default policy of acceptance that isn't grounded in further evidence just is a non-inferential source of knowledge, so the empiricist has smuggled in an independent pramāṇa and is merely refusing to give it the traditional name. The empiricist's closing move was that the naming dispute matters, because the Nyāya criterion can't be the objects of knowledge: the train's departure time can be known by perception, inference, or testimony, so objects don't carve sources apart, and the traditional list of four is best seen as a historical census of how people talk about knowing, not a real taxonomy.

They finally turned to me to referee, and I genuinely couldn't.
