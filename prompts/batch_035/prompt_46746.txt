I've been invited to give a talk at an AI research lab, and I need help preparing for the question period. My thesis: attributing beliefs and desires to large language models can be literally correct, not a convenient fiction, provided the attributions meet Dennett's standard for what he called a real pattern. The sketch of my argument, which the objections should target: (1) on the intentional stance, a system has beliefs when the belief-vocabulary delivers genuine predictive compression — it lets you capture, in a few state ascriptions, a range of actual and counterfactual behavior that would otherwise demand an unbounded lookup table; (2) a thermostat fails this test because a handful of input-output dispositions exhausts it, so nothing is gained by saying it believes anything; (3) current models pass it for a circumscribed but nontrivial range of attributions, because their behavior varies systematically with a small set of internal states that calibration studies, persona-conditional evaluations, and consistency probing each independently track; so (4) the right attitude is neither anthropomorphic enthusiasm nor dismissive metaphor — these systems occupy a middle band where belief-talk earns its keep, much as virus-talk earned its keep before we settled what viruses were made of.

The audience: an interpretability research group — machine-learning engineers by training, hostile to what they call anthropomorphic framing, fluent in concepts like circuits, features, and steering vectors, and inclined to say that any property worth talking about must be found in the weights or measured directly, not read off behavior. Two of them have a standing disagreement about whether a model's stated output can diverge from its model-internal state — one insists that when they diverge the internal state is the only thing that counts as the model's belief, the other thinks internal states don't have content until they issue in outputs. There is also one lab philosopher who will press me on whether my position is realist, instrumentalist, or a hedged middle that collapses under pressure.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrased the way an ML engineer would phrase it — in terms of models, weights, activations, and benchmarks, not qualia or propositional attitudes. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, a note on where that reply is genuinely soft and what I'd have to concede if pressed, and an indication of whether the objection is more likely from the behavior-skeptics, the internal-state purists, or the philosopher. Mark which objections are really the same objection wearing different clothes, so I can answer the cluster once and refer back to it. Flag the one question I am hoping nobody asks — I expect it will be the one about whether my standard for belief attribution, if consistent, also licenses attributing beliefs to the users' own recommender systems, or to the organization that fine-tuned the model, and I need to know how bad it looks before deciding how to handle it. Throughout, keep the prep sheet honest about the fact that my position is a middle one: where the safest answer is a concession, say so rather than arming me with a bluff.
