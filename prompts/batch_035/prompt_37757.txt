I am writing a seminar paper on the cognitive penetrability of perception, and before engaging any of the positions I want to be clear about what would count as a good theory of it. The dispute over whether cognition shapes perception is frequently conducted case by case, and I would rather have the full specification of adequacy up front. Please write me that specification: the desiderata any adequate theory of cognitive penetration must meet.

To fix the purpose: I want a theory good enough to classify empirical claims. When a psychology study reports that hungry people see food-related objects as brighter, that a depressed mood makes the world look literally grayer, that chess experts perceive the board in terms of threats rather than pieces, or that the language one speaks shifts where one experiences a color boundary, I want to be able to say on principled grounds whether cognition is penetrating perception, or whether something else is merely wearing the same clothes.

The list should cover, at least, the following areas, each with the consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a preference of one camp:

1. Cases it must get right: which cases are supposed to be paradigms of penetration, which are agreed impostors (as the perceptual learning cases are often taken to be — someone trained to read X-rays comes to see anomalies, but that is a developmental reshaping of the visual system, not a belief coloring each episode of seeing), and why the boundary matters for classification.

2. Distinctions it must respect and cannot collapse: perceptual versus post-perceptual effects located in judgment, memory, or report; penetration proper versus the deployment of attention or interest, since attentional selection changes what we take in without changing how it looks; and penetration by belief versus influence by desire, mood, or emotion — whether these should be lumped or separated is itself something the theory should adjudicate, not assume.

3. The epistemological payoff: if a theory declares perception penetrable, it owes an account of when penetration epistemically degrades perception and when it improves it. The standing worry is that perception penetrated by belief can no longer serve as an impartial check on those very beliefs, so a theory that says "yes, penetrable" without saying anything about epistemic standing has not finished its work.

4. Methodological constraints on what could count as evidence: what a study must control for before its result could even bear on penetration — for instance, ruling out that the effect arises after perception in the subject's judgments, or that demand characteristics rather than anything perceptual drive the reports. Here please also note the trap of inferring that perception was unaffected from the failure to find a behavioral effect, as in the argument that if a painter's perceptions were systematically distorted in the way his paintings are, the distortion would cancel out and he would never stop painting: such reasoning would equally "explain away" everything we can measure, not just the claim at issue.

For each desideratum, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a demand specific to one school (as the demand that penetrated perceptual states inherit justification, or the demand that penetration be confined to late vision, each tends to be). Then address the two questions I most want answered: first, whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for example, whether requiring that penetration be empirically detectable, that it respect the constancy mechanisms vision is known to possess, and that it be assessable for epistemic standing can all be had at once; second, which single requirement does the most to admit or exclude candidate theories. I do not need you to run modularity, enactivism, or any particular philosopher's account against the list; the criteria, and the reasoning behind them, are what I want.
