I'm a high school senior taking an elective in political psychology, and a few weeks ago we covered moral foundations theory — the framework associated with Jonathan Haidt and his colleagues, on which moral judgment draws on several distinct foundations (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity), with the well-known finding that people on the political left tend to weight the first two heavily while people on the right draw on the whole set roughly evenly. My teacher mentioned in passing that some people think this asymmetry bears on which side is actually *correct* about moral questions, and then had to move on. Since then, the arguments this has produced at my lunch table have shown me that my friends divide on that meta-question in ways that don't track their party affiliations at all — and half the time people don't notice which position they're committed to until they contradict themselves two minutes later. So I'd like you to write me an instrument that sorts people by what they believe about the meta-question, not by which party they like.

Specifically: a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which position it feeds into — never just "do you think the right is correct?" or "do you trust Haidt?" Please build in at least these cases: (i) a case in which one of your own firmly held moral beliefs turns out to have been produced by an evolutionary process that selected it for coordinating groups, not for tracking any truth — does that by itself give you reason to lower your confidence in the belief?; (ii) a case contrasting two people, one of whom feels moral reactions in five distinct channels and the other in only two — is the broader palette like having more working senses (more access to the facts) or like having more ways of being wrong (more noise)?; (iii) a case about an asymmetry discovered by a measuring instrument whose designers already had a stake in what counts as a moral concern; and (iv) a case about what to conclude if all political-moral intuitions, left and right alike, turn out to be generated by coalitional psychology. End with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

The leaves I need on the tree: strict quietism, where the psychology is purely descriptive and can never adjudicate; symmetric debunking, where the genealogy deflates both sides' political moralizing equally; vindication by breadth, where the wider foundation set amounts to a better-attuned moral perception; a harm-and-fairness-centered vindication, which reaches the opposite verdict; and the constructed-asymmetry view, on which the theory's list of foundations was stipulated with a stake in the outcome, so its asymmetry finding cannot adjudicate — for reasons quite different from quietism. Where the combinations of answers leave cells unoccupied, please note that and say whether you think the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And please flag, rather than paper over, any pair of positions the quiz can't genuinely separate — my own suspicion is that strict quietism and the view that the psychology is real evidence but far too weak to move anyone will collapse into one cell, but I'd rather you tell me honestly if the instrument can't split them. Throughout the scoring key, please say how confident you are in each mapping, and single out the one question or branch you are least sure actually divides the positions; I would much rather know where the instrument is shaky than receive it as gospel.
