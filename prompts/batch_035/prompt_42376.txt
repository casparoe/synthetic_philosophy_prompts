Our epistemology reading group has spent the better part of two semesters on Moore's proof of an external world, and I have come away with the impression that the debate it opened has not really moved in over eighty years. As we were taught the lecture, Moore holds up one hand, says something to the effect that here is one hand, holds up the other, and concludes that there are at least two things outside his mind. He freely admits he cannot prove the premises to anyone who doubts them; his claim is only that he knows them, and that a valid argument from known premises to a conclusion that was not previously known is a perfectly good proof. The standard objection has been the same since the lecture was delivered: the proof is question-begging, because one cannot meet a challenge to perceptual knowledge by appeal to perceptual knowledge. The neo-Moorean reply is equally familiar: begging the question is a vice of an argument as deployed in a dialectic against an interlocutor, not a vice of an argument as a proof; so the proof is valid and its premises are known, whatever it fails to do to the skeptic. And there the matter sits, with each side restating its position with ever-greater precision and nobody converting.

The modern literature was supposed to break this deadlock by making the real issue precise, but to my eye it has reproduced it in miniature. One camp holds that perceptual experience gives immediate justification for claims about one's hands, and that the Moorean reasoning simply transmits that justification to the further conclusion that external things exist. The rival camp grants that the premises are warranted but denies that the warrant transmits: on their view, using the premise to support the conclusion requires an independent warrant for the reliability of perception, which is exactly what is in question, so the argument fails to transmit warrant even though it is valid and its premises are known. Each side has a favorite way of making the other's view look absurd. The transmission camp is charged with licensing easy knowledge — you could prove your car's speedometer is reliable by consulting it over and over and noting that it always agrees with itself, or prove you are looking at a zebra rather than a cleverly disguised mule by appeal to the visual experience that it looks like a zebra. The non-transmission camp is charged with an epistemic conservatism so strong that no argument could ever get started against a serious skeptic, since anything that would establish the reliability of our faculties would itself seem to rely on them. Again: a standoff, conducted now with journal-length machinery instead of a pair of held-up hands.

My working hypothesis, offered as no more than a hypothesis, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one is a logical question about what follows from what is known, the other a dialectical or normative question about what may be shown to whom, and the word "proof" has been doing double duty across the whole tradition. But I am not confident of this, because the transmission dispute looks like a genuine first-order disagreement about how epistemic warrant behaves, not a mere ambiguity. So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the impasse verbal, a difference about the standards a thing must meet to count as a proof, a substantive disagreement about the transmission of warrant, or a sign that the original question — what it would take to answer the external-world skeptic — is ill-posed? Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually says rather than with labels, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a case that one side must treat as decisive, a reframing of the question, or an argument that we should expect no progress here and why.
