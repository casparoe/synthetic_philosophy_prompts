My closest friend Dara and I have spent years arguing about reasons — she took a graduate seminar on the fitting attitudes years ago and has been applying its lessons (or her version of them) to everything since, with a stubborn consistency that I've started keeping notes on. Her working distinction, as she puts it, is between considerations that give you a reason to have an attitude and considerations that are reasons *for* the attitude itself — the kind that make the attitude deserved or appropriate rather than merely useful. Her verdicts so far: a growling dog with a history of biting gives you a reason to fear it; a harmless dog her trainer wants her to keep mildly fearing, because the fear keeps her from crowding it, gives her a reason to *want* the fear to continue but no reason for the fear itself; a dying child's mediocre fresco that she could enjoy only by admiring, where admiring it would give the child a last small pleasure, is something she ought to admire but for considerations that are not reasons *of the right kind* for admiration; genuine courage gives you a reason to admire it; a chess engine's brilliant sacrificial combination gives you a reason to admire it (she's firm about this one, which surprised me); a genuinely funny joke gives you a reason to be amused, while an unfunny joke told by a retiring colleague, where laughing would spare his feelings, gives you a reason to feign amusement but none to actually be amused.

Next week we meet again, and I want to go in with predictions rather than wing it, so here is what I need. The new cases on the table are: (1) a harmless snake that is, to her eye and to any amateur's, indistinguishable from a deadly species it mimics — does the resemblance give her any reason of the right kind to fear it, or only an excuse for the fear? (2) her late grandmother's mediocre painting of a harbor, which she finds genuinely moving, but only because she knows her grandmother painted it while waiting out the war — is the poignancy a real ground for admiration or another provenance trick? (3) her grandfather's ugly, non-functional pocket watch, which she wants to keep — does its having been his give her a reason of the right kind for the wanting, or just a reason to want to keep wanting it? Predict her verdict on each, but what I really want is the analysis behind the prediction: what rules are consistent with her track record so far (I can see at least two — something about where the consideration is located, in the object versus in the effects of the attitude, and something about whether the value invoked would still be there if no one had the attitude — though there may be better candidates), where those rules agree and disagree on the three new cases, which rule she is most plausibly running given how she talks, and what further case I could put to her that would cleanly separate the candidate rules — I'd like to bring that one to dinner and settle this.
