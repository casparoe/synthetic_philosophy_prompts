This term I'm taking an upper-level metaphysics course, and we have just finished a three-week unit on dispositions that ended in an odd way: everyone in the seminar agreed the debate we were studying has gone quiet, but nobody could say exactly why it went quiet, and our instructor seemed to regard the silence itself as philosophically interesting. I want to understand what kind of disagreement this actually was, and I have a hypothesis I'd like tested rather than applauded.

The debate I mean is the one about whether dispositions can be analyzed in terms of conditionals. We started with the naive conditional analysis -- to be disposed to M in circumstances C is, roughly, for it to be true that if the object were in C, it would M -- and then worked through the counterexample literature: Martin's electro-fink, which makes a dead wire satisfy the conditional while having no disposition, and a live wire fail the conditional while having one; Lewis's reformed conditional analysis, which holds the dispositional base fixed to block the fink; and then the masks and antidotes that seem to survive even that repair -- the fragile glass packed in foam that would not shatter however it is struck, the poison neutralized by an antidote, where the base is intact, the stimulus arrives, nothing manifests, and yet, by everyone's lights, the disposition is still there. After that we watched the pattern repeat: each strengthened analysis met a stranger counterexample, until some authors concluded that no conditional analysis can work and that dispositions must instead be irreducible powers or tendencies, while others kept defending one or another conditional analysis, more or less strictly formulated. What struck me is that the participants seem not to have moved at all since the mid-2000s; the literature thinned out rather than resolved.

So here is my question. Is this a substantive disagreement or a partly verbal one about what 'disposition' is supposed to name -- the intrinsic categorical base of a manifestation-tendency, or whatever property happens to occupy the conditional's place, however extrinsically it is fixed? Are the two camps, without noticing it, answering different questions under one heading -- one about a concept we use in the special sciences and everyday life, the other about whether the world's causal structure requires irreducible powers? Or is there a genuinely formal question hiding in there, say about which semantics for counterfactuals (backtracking versus non-backtracking) the analysis is allowed to presuppose, such that the debate is really a proxy war over counterfactual logic? And is the proliferation of ever-stranger cases -- each new counterexample baroquer than the last -- the normal course of concept sharpening, or a sign that the case method itself became degenerate, with each side able to generate cases its own analysis classifies correctly by construction?

Let me offer one small piece of evidence I'd want your diagnosis to address. In my term paper I invented what I called a self-masking disposition: an object whose disposition, when it manifests, produces a mask of that very disposition on a later occasion -- imagine glass that, when struck hard enough, shatters but also releases a hardening agent that makes all future strikes harmless. The trouble was that I could not get the two camps in the seminar to agree on what the case was even supposed to show: the conditional analysts classified it one way, the powers theorists another, and each seemed to get their verdict out of their framework rather than out of the case. If that is representative, it strikes me as evidence for the verbal-disagreement diagnosis, but I may just be describing my own confusion.

What I want is a diagnosis, anchored in what each side actually claims, together with a verdict on my hypothesis and an honest account of what would count as progress here: a case that could not be met, a distinction that would partition the field, a result about counterfactuals, a change of question -- or an argument that we should expect none and why. Throughout, please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. That the simple conditional analysis fails and that finks and masks are real phenomena seem settled; but whether any conditional analysis survives, whether irreducible powers are needed, and whether the whole dispute is substantive seem to me contested, and I don't want a contested reconstruction presented to me as the consensus reading.
