My friend from a political philosophy seminar I took a few years ago has developed a fairly settled line on when secession is legitimate, and I'd like to predict where she'll land on a new case. Her verdicts so far: she thought Scotland's 2014 referendum was entirely legitimate and that Westminster was right to permit it, holding that a Yes vote would have created a genuine duty to negotiate secession in good faith. On Quebec, she thinks Canada was right to allow the 1995 referendum, but she insists a bare 50-per-cent-plus-one Yes would not have sufficed — she wants a clear majority, comfortably above half, though I've never pinned down whether she means a clear majority of votes cast or of the whole electorate. On Catalonia in 2017, she sided with Madrid: the unilateral referendum was illegitimate, the Spanish state was entitled to prevent it, and nothing followed from the roughly ninety per cent Yes vote on a turnout of about forty-three per cent — though when I pressed her on whether a cleanly run unilateral vote with high turnout and a solid Yes would have changed her answer, she deflected. On Kosovo, she thinks the 2008 declaration of independence was legitimate and deserved recognition, despite being unilateral and flatly contrary to the Serbian constitutional order, given the mass expulsions that preceded it. On Crimea in 2014, she thinks the referendum was void regardless of what Crimeans actually wanted, because it was conducted under foreign military occupation. And she's entirely comfortable with the dissolution of Czechoslovakia in 1993, even though no referendum was ever held — the parliaments legislated it after national elections, and that was enough for her.

The new case is hypothetical, but I've tried to make it test her principles rather than flatter them. Imagine Norland: a culturally distinct, somewhat wealthier province of a stable federal democracy with impeccable democratic credentials and no history of oppression or discrimination against the region. A secessionist party wins the provincial election on an explicit mandate; the national government, citing constitutional indivisibility, refuses any referendum. The province holds one anyway. The first vote returns sixty-one per cent Yes on seventy-six per cent turnout; two years later, after the national government again refuses to negotiate, a second returns sixty-four per cent Yes on seventy-nine per cent turnout — which, as I make it, is just over half of the registered electorate. International observers certify both votes as free and clean, and no foreign power is involved in any way.

What will she say about Norland — that there's a pro tanto claim to statehood others ought to act on, or that the national government remains within its rights to hold the state together indefinitely? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running. As far as I can see, at least two fit her track record: a consent-based rule on which legitimacy requires either the host state's agreement or a remedial grievance, and a rule on which legitimacy tracks a sufficiently strong, sustained, and uncoerced expression of the region's will, with Kosovo's grievance as merely one way of marking seriousness. There are also the sub-questions I can't resolve from her record: whether the velvet divorce shows she can live with no referendum at all if the political class agrees, and whether her "clear majority" means of votes cast (which Norland clears comfortably) or of the whole electorate (which Norland clears only by a whisker, on the second vote). Please give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons, the rival rules that fit her record and what each predicts about Norland, where they genuinely diverge, and then the further case — a tweak to the Norland scenario or a new case altogether — that would best tell those rules apart.
