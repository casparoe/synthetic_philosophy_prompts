I am a high school junior writing an extended essay for my English class, and I have chosen to write about the ethics of dramatizing real people who have died. My teacher wants a formal essay that takes a side, not a summary of views, so I need help constructing an actual argument I can defend.

Here is the situation I want to focus on, because the general question ("is it okay to make stuff up about real people?") seems too easy. Last term my class read a historical novel about a real woman scientist from the 1920s, and the author had given her a fictional best friend, invented diary entries, and — this is the part that started arguments — attributed to her a decision that she did not make, a decision that in reality her male colleague made and got credit for. The author's note admitted all of this and said the changes served a "larger emotional truth" about how women's contributions get erased. One student in my class said the book thereby told the truth about erasure by committing an erasure, which seemed like a genuinely interesting point rather than a gotcha. Around the same time I watched a biopic miniseries about a musician who died fairly recently, where his still-living bandmates are portrayed in scenes depicting them as, among other things, being indifferent to his addiction. Some of those bandmates have publicly objected to specific scenes. So the two cases are not symmetric: in the first, the dead person is made more admirable than the record supports, and everyone close to her is dead too; in the second, the dead person is kept roughly as he was, but living people are made less admirable in the process of dramatizing him.

Write an essay that takes and defends a position on what, if anything, we owe the real dead when we dramatize them, but which does so through these cases rather than at the level of slogans. Specifically, I want the essay to address: (1) whether the relevant duty runs to the dead person at all, or to historical record-keepers, or to the audience, or to living people connected to the dead — that is, who is actually wronged, if anyone, when a fabrication about a dead person succeeds; (2) whether there is a defensible line between the invented best friend and diary entries on the one hand, and the false attribution of the colleague's decision on the other, given that the author disclosed all of it — that is, whether disclosure in an author's note cures fabrication or only confesses it; (3) whether the "larger emotional truth" defense can survive the objection my classmate raised, that you cannot depict erasure by erasing, or whether that objection proves too much, since all compression is erasure of something; and (4) whether the presence of living, protesting bandmates changes the moral analysis in kind or merely in degree, and what that implies about whether the wrong to the dead is really a wrong to the living wearing a costume.

Please reach a definite verdict, even if it is a qualified one — for example, a rule the essay defends about which kinds of invention are permissible and which are not, and why the line falls where it does. I would rather defend a clear position I can be argued against than survey opinions. Do not include reading lists or citations; I do not need sources, just the argument, since I will be doing my own research afterward and do not want to be anchored to real bibliographies I cannot verify.
