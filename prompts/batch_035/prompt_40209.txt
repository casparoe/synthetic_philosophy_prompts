I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a national magazine, and for the past two years I have served as the unaffiliated community member on my local university's institutional review board — the lay voice on a committee of clinicians, methodologists, and ethicists.

A proposal has split our board roughly down the middle, and I must file my written position before Thursday's meeting. A behavioral-science lab wants to run a battery of online studies through a large participant platform. Their consent innovation is what they call a pre-authorized deception clause: at the time of account creation — not before any particular study — participants read a plain-language statement explaining that some studies on the platform involve misinformation, false feedback, or simulated interaction partners, that every such study includes a debriefing afterward, and that agreeing to the clause means consenting in advance to this entire class of deceptions. The consent forms for individual studies would then note only that the study may include features described in the platform agreement, leaving the researchers free, for example, to tell a participant she is paired with another person who is in fact a script, or that her performance fell below average when it did not.

The proponents argue this actually improves consent: rather than lying to participants about what a specific study involves, the platform tells them the truth once, up front, and lets them decide with full knowledge of the general risk. The skeptics reply that consenting to an unspecified class of future deceptions is not consenting to anything at all — that what makes consent valid is not disclosure alone but the opportunity to decline the particular thing, and that a participant who can never know which study will lie to her can never exercise that opportunity. A few add a contamination worry: once the clause exists, participants will discount everything they are told, quietly degrading every study on the platform, deceptive or not.

Please draft the written position I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with reasoning the researchers could actually engage with, and it should give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full due rather than a perfunctory paragraph. Wherever you state a judgment, whether about what valid consent requires or about the likely empirical effects of such a clause, indicate how confident you are in it, and identify the single point in your memo you are least sure of, so I know where to press when the board meets.
