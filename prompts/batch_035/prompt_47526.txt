I've been listening to a podcast series on the history of international criminal law, and the episode on Raphael Lemkin and the making of the Genocide Convention left me unsure how much of it to trust. The host's account went, in substance, like this. Lemkin coined the word and then essentially wrote the Convention himself, so the legal definition that ended up in the treaty just is Lemkin's definition: the crime of destroying a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part. Since any victim of a targeted killing is obviously a part of some protected group, the host said the phrase in part means that even a single murder committed out of racial or ethnic hatred technically satisfies the definition, and that the only reason not every hate crime is charged as genocide is prosecutorial restraint rather than anything in the text. On the mental element, he explained that the Convention requires a motive: prosecutors must show the perpetrators were driven by hatred of the group, which is why genocide convictions are so rare and so hard to obtain. He then said that Lemkin deliberately left political groups off the list of protected groups, because Lemkin wanted the crime confined to communities fixed by birth rather than by allegiance, which political movements are not. He noted, as a point in Lemkin's favor, that the Convention covers not only killing but also measures like forcibly transferring children and destroying cultural and religious institutions, so that the cultural dimension Lemkin cared most about survived into the treaty more or less intact. The episode closed on a reflection I found genuinely interesting: that the word's legal triumph has been its political undoing, because genocide became the crime of crimes, every atrocity now has to be called a genocide to count morally, and so the term gets stretched until it means little more than a very bad massacre.

Some of this sounded right to me and some of it sounded off. I do vaguely recall that Lemkin's own conception was broader than what made it into the treaty, which makes me doubt the opening claim that the two are the same thing, but I don't know enough about the drafting history or the case law to say anything confident beyond that. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a scholar of the Convention would accept as stated, which are subtly wrong, and what turns on the corrections. I'd particularly like to know whether the host's reading of the phrase in part matches how courts have actually interpreted it, whether the mental element is really a motive requirement or something else, and how much of Lemkin's cultural and political understanding of the crime survived into the final text. Finally, please say whether his closing thought about the politics of naming — the claim that the term's legal prestige has diluted it — survives once the earlier points are corrected, or whether it depends on some of the errors.
