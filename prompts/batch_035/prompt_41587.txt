My friend Marcus and I spent most of last Sunday arguing about the ethics of voting, and I've been trying to work out ever since what principled position he's actually running. His track record of verdicts, as best I can reconstruct it: he insists there's a genuine duty to vote in national elections even for someone like me, who lives in a state where the presidential outcome is a foregone conclusion every cycle; he thinks casting a sincere vote for a third party polling around two percent in such a safe state is admirable rather than wasted; he was scathing about a mutual acquaintance who, living in a genuine swing state, voted for a write-in protest candidate instead of holding her nose for the clearly lesser of the two major candidates, whom he regarded as materially worse; and yet he had no criticism at all for another friend who skipped a mayoral runoff because she was honestly indifferent between the two remaining candidates and hadn't followed the race. So participation seems mandatory for him, sincerity seems fine where nothing hangs on it, lesser-evil voting seems mandatory where something does, and indifference seems to excuse the whole thing.

Here's the case that I think will actually divide us, and I'd like your help thinking it through before I bring it back to him. Our city elects a district attorney next month. There are two frontrunners in a tight race, and Marcus regards one as clearly, though not dramatically, worse than the other — he described them as roughly a four out of ten versus a five. There's also an insurgent candidate he genuinely admires, currently polling near twenty percent, who could conceivably win if things break right, though the more likely effect of a strong showing is to siphon votes from the frontrunner he mildly prefers. Given his earlier verdicts, will he tell me to vote sincerely for the insurgent, or to hold my nose yet again?

I'm less interested in the answer than in the machinery behind it. Please lay out at least two distinct rules that each fit all four of his earlier verdicts — for instance, one on which the lesser-evil constraint is structural, triggered whenever a close race pits a greater against a lesser evil, and one on which it's magnitude-sensitive, triggered only when the expected harm of a sincere vote is worth worrying about — and show what each predicts for the district attorney race. Then give me your best guess at his verdict with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and describe the further case, real or hypothetical, that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart (I've been wondering about things like whether the duty to vote in a safe state survives when someone has no considered preference at all, or what he'd say about crossing over to vote in the other party's primary for a candidate you'd like to face in November). Please answer in American English, and feel free to flag any places where his verdicts may be in tension rather than consistent.
