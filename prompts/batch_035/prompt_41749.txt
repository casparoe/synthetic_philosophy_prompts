so I've been going around in circles on the paradox of deontology and I'd rather see it fought out live than read another textbook summary of it. Write me a dialogue between a deontologist who believes in agent-centered constraints — roughly: it's wrong for me to kill an innocent even to prevent several killings by other people — and a consequentialist who thinks constraints are, at best, confused. Give them names, they're colleagues, not caricatures, and neither one gets to win just because the other fumbles. Real back-and-forth: each reply should actually engage with what the last person said, not slide past it.

Here's how I want it structured. Start with the consequentialist pressing the classic paradox: if constraint-violations are bad things, then a world with fewer of them is better, so I should violate once to prevent two violations — and worse, the deontologist's refusal ends up benefiting whoever was going to violate the constraint anyway. The deontologist gets to answer with the agent-relativity reply in its strongest form: it's not worse for the world that they kill rather than that I kill, but it is worse for me that I kill, and that's what the constraint tracks. Have the consequentialist take that reply seriously rather than sneering at it.

Then I want them to get past the textbook exchange, because I think the agent-relative reply only covers half the ground. Have the consequentialist pose this case: both bad outcomes involve my own hands. Suppose I can either kill one innocent now, or refuse, whereupon (stipulate it, it's fixed in the case) I will be compromised later and end up killing two innocents myself — same agent, same kind of act, no third parties anywhere. Now the "they kill vs. I kill" line does no work, since it's me in both branches. So does the constraint tell me to kill one? If yes, constraints seem to aggregate after all, just over my own violations, and the consequentialist gets to ask what exactly stopped aggregation in the first case. If no, then the constraint forbids me from minimizing my own future violations, which the consequentialist should point out looks like it protects nobody at all — not the one killed now, not the two killed later. The deontologist has to find something to say here that isn't just restating the constraint louder: maybe a doing/allowing split at each choice point, maybe something about the future killings not being things I'm doing *now*, maybe an integrity story about not making myself into an instrument. Whichever they pick, the consequentialist should press on its costs — e.g. if it's timing rather than agency, that seems arbitrary; if it's integrity, that risks being consequentialism about one's own future character in a trenchcoat.

Two things I want them each to concede before the end, stated out loud in the dialogue: the consequentialist should grant that "each violation is bad" doesn't by itself yield "produce fewer by adding one" — that inference needs an aggregation premise he owes us. And the deontologist should grant that in the both-my-hands case the constraint's usual rationale (protecting innocents from being used) is doing no work, since no one ends up better protected. End it unresolved — they part with the disagreement narrowed but alive, not with one of them converted. Keep it punchy, this is a conversation between smart people who've had it before, not a lecture with two speakers. Please dont have either of them quote famous philosophers at each other, I want the arguments to stand on their own. And no strawmanning — if you catch one of them about to, have the other one call it out.
