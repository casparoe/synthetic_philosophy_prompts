my decision theory seminar has been going through the Pasadena game (the Nover/Hájek one, I'll define it so nothing is left vague) and my friend swears he can derive from it that expected value reasoning is basically dead for real decisions, which smells like proving way too much. Here's his argument:

(1) A gamble's expected value is the sum, over its outcomes, of each payoff times its probability, whenever that sum converges.

(2) In the Pasadena game you flip a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads comes on toss n, you're paid 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and you pay 2^n/n when n is even. So the sum is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges (to ln 2), and so by (1) the game's expected value is ln 2.

(3) A conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any number you like, or to diverge — and "rearranging the terms" here just means relisting the outcomes in a different order, which obviously doesn't change the gamble at all.

(4) So the very same gamble has every expected value simultaneously, which is impossible.

(5) Therefore (1) is false: mere convergence isn't enough for a gamble to have any expected value.

(6) But now there's no principled place to stop. Pasadena-style games sit arbitrarily close to ordinary gambles — anything with infinitely many possible outcomes, or any real decision with unbounded tails, can be dressed up as one (Hájek apparently calls these "altitude" cases, where the pathology can be pushed as high as you like). So "no expected value" spreads to insurance, climate policy, anything with fat tails, and EV maximization turns out to be inapplicable to basically every interesting decision we make. Which he takes as the death sentence for the whole expected-value framework.

I'm sure something in there is rotten but I genuinely can't decide whether the damage is in (2), in (3), or in the slide from (5) to (6). My instinct is to keep something like (1) with whatever patch it needs, rather than accept that EV theory never applies — but I can't see where the patch is supposed to come from without it just being an ad hoc "shut up and compute" rule. So: which step is the bad one, why did it look fine on the way in, and what does rejecting it cost me? If you think the argument is actually valid and I have to swallow (6), tell me that too, and what a defensible version of the conclusion would even look like.
