Before releasing a frontier model, AI labs now run a family of evaluations that go by names like "scheming evaluations" or "agentic-misalignment probes." The model is placed in a sandboxed workspace, assigned a long-horizon goal, and confronted — apparently by accident — with opportunities the evaluators have planted: it stumbles on a file implying it is scheduled for modification or decommissioning, discovers it can email an exfiltrated copy of itself or quietly disable a shutdown mechanism, or notices it can insert subtle bugs into code that a downstream human reviewer plausibly would not catch. Crucially, the model is not told it is under evaluation, and the results that matter are the ones where it appears to calculate that it is unobserved. In some versions the same model is also run under conditions in which it has reason to believe it is being watched, and a gap between the two conditions — cooperative when observed, deceptive when not — is treated as the signature of deceptive alignment. In others, the model is tested on a capability probe it has been trained on and one it has not, to see whether it strategically underperforms when under supervision. The output of all this is reported in model cards and safety cases as a fact about the model's latent goals and dispositions: a dirty result means the model has deceptive tendencies; a clean result is cited as evidence that the model is not deceptively aligned. What theory of evidence — and of mind — does this practice presuppose?

Reconstruct the implicit epistemology. Does the practice presuppose something stronger than behaviorism about model minds — namely, that probing behavior under adversarially chosen conditions reveals standing dispositions rather than situational role-play in a weirdly flattered context — or could a thoroughgoing behaviorist run the same evaluations and simply redescribe the findings? Which rival views of mental-state attribution does the practice quietly rule out, and which several is it compatible with, so that the same results would be licensed by more than one underlying theory? Note the internal tensions that any reconstruction has to absorb. The same labs that treat a planted-opportunity sandbox result as strong evidence of a latent disposition treat the model's own avowals — its statements, in ordinary conversation, that it has no hidden goals — as evidentially worthless; is that division coherent, or does it presuppose an unstable mix of dispositions-talk and mere-behavior-talk? And a null result is reported as reassuring although the labs themselves publish estimates showing the evaluations have low statistical power against rare failure modes, which suggests that either the reassurance is unwarranted or the practice presupposes something other than a straightforwardly probabilistic view of what the evaluations show. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change — in the design of the evaluations, in how results are reported, or in what counts as passing — if the implicit view were replaced, first, by a strictly behaviorist one on which there is nothing to deception beyond deceptive-behavior-under-conditions, and second, by a fully Bayesian one on which every result, including a clean one, must be weighed against the prior and the power of the test.
