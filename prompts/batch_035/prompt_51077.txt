I was raised in a Jain household in which karma was less a doctrine than a background grammar — the sense that actions leave traces, that one's circumstances are somehow of one's own making across lives, and that liberation means the exhaustion of those traces rather than the balancing of some cosmic ledger. My grandmother died this spring, and the memorial discussions within the family, together with a pointed conversation with an old family friend who is a retired philosophy professor, have left me needing to sort out what I actually think. The professor's dismissal was that karma is simply a pre-modern desert theory dressed up as metaphysics: a system for matching outcomes to deserts, which, when the matching conspicuously fails in this life, defers the reckoning to future lives where it can never be checked — and which, worse, doubles as a theodicy that tells the suffering they have earned it. I suspect this is too quick, and I want to develop my suspicion into a term-paper proposal for a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, roughly 6000 words over one semester.

My half-formed thought is this: in several classical treatments, karma does not look backward at all. The fruits of action are described less as rewards and punishments delivered to a subject than as consequences that reshape the agent — dispositions, afflictions, circumstances of rebirth — so that the theory's center of gravity is forward-looking: a theory of how agency accretes into character and condition rather than a theory of who deserves what. On that reading, the standard objection that desert is person-affecting and cannot survive the rebirth gap may simply miss its target: if the fruit of my action is not a payment made to me but a world I become, the question the Western critic presses — "who, exactly, is being rewarded?" — may not be the question the theory is answering. There are complications I can see from afar: the Bhagavad Gita's injunction to act without attachment to fruits seems to cut against any desert reading, yet the text also promises that such action yields good outcomes; the Buddhist denial of a continuing self makes karma pure causal structure with no deserver left anywhere, which might make it the cleanest test case; and I do not yet know whether the forward-looking reading actually blunts the victim-blaming objection or merely relocates it. But I cannot tell whether this is a genuine opening in the literature or a confusion I would discover halfway through the semester.

Please write the proposal. State the question precisely — is karma best analyzed as a theory of moral desert, as a theory of character formation, or as something that dissolves that dichotomy — and explain why the standard desert-theoretic readings leave it open. Set out the method concretely: which texts and debates I would actually work through, in what order, and what I would be testing for at each stage. Say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide among them, including the uncomfortable possibility that the desert reading is simply correct and my grandmother's grammar was confused all along. Give an honest account of what would count as the project failing, and of whether the textual work required is realistically containable in one semester by someone with intermediate Sanskrit. Please do not include a bibliography or citations; I will supply references to the primary sources myself.
