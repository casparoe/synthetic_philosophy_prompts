At a recent seminar on philosophy of religion, a visiting speaker kept shutting down questions about causal explanation by invoking, as he put it, al-Ghazālī's dictum on causation. He never gave it in a fixed wording, but the recurring substance was this: the link between what is commonly taken to be a cause and what is commonly taken to be an effect is not a necessary link — what is necessary, if anything is, is only that God, by an enduring custom, brings the one thing on the occasion of the other. From that one line he drew two conclusions at once: first, that Ghazali had already dismantled the notion of natural causation well before Hume, so that the causal skeptic's work in the modern era is essentially a rediscovery; and second, that anyone who speaks of the laws of nature as constraints on what can happen, rather than as summaries of what does happen, is trading in a confusion that Ghazali exposed a millennium ago.

I came away with the feeling that a single sentence was being made to do several quite different jobs at once, and I would like to see that unpacked rather than asserted. Concretely, I want a menu of the distinct precise theses the line could be expressing, with a verdict on each. Off the top of my head the candidates include at least these, and there may be others worth adding: an epistemic thesis, that we never observe any necessity binding events together, only constant succession — which would be the reading that makes the Hume comparison look good; a metaphysical thesis, that there simply are no causal powers in created things, so that the fire does nothing to the cotton and God's agency is the only agency there is — the full occasionalist doctrine; an intermediate thesis on which the fire and cotton case involves a joint or conjoined production, the effect arising from the thing and God together in a way that leaves the thing some real, though borrowed, role; and a concessive bookkeeping thesis, on which a necessary connection between natural events could be granted for the sake of argument, with the line then amounting only to the claim that such a necessity would itself exist because God established it — which would make the dictum almost dialectical rather than destructive.

For each reading, I would like three things said plainly: whether it is philosophically defensible on its own; whether al-Ghazālī actually seems to have held it, as opposed to the others, given how the argument in the Incoherence proceeds — for instance, the fact that he stages the discussion by first stating the philosophers' case for natural necessity, and that late in the discussion he appears willing to grant a necessary connection in nature provided the necessity is traced back to the divine side; and whether that reading would actually support the speaker's two conclusions. It matters to me especially that the last point be handled honestly, because the second conclusion looks suspect even if the first is granted: it is not obvious to me that denying necessary connection between events, in any of the above senses, licenses anything about whether it is confused to speak of the laws of nature as constraints. Finally, please address the word doing the quiet work in the line as the speaker gave it: the qualification about what is habitually or commonly taken to be a cause. That word suggests the dictum might be aimed at the philosophers' conviction that the connection is necessary, rather than at the connection itself, which would make the whole line narrower than either of the speaker's conclusions allows. If that narrower reading is the most faithful one, I want to be told so, along with what the other readings then look like: later additions, charitable reconstructions, or misreadings.
