My friend's a pretty hardcore antinatalist, big fan of Benatar's asymmetry, and last week he argued himself into a corner that he now hates. I'm trying to work out whether the argument actually succeeds or just looks like it does.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: the asymmetry says that when you compare a person's existence with their never existing, the absence of their pains counts as good even though there's no one around to enjoy that good, while the absence of their pleasures doesn't count as bad, because nobody is deprived of them.

His argument:

(1) A stretch of non-existence with no pains and no pleasures in it has the same value no matter where it sits — before a person's life or after it. Value doesn't care about time direction or about how the non-existence comes about.
(2) So the same axiology that applies to creating someone applies to continuing someone.
(3) Take a person whose future, from today on, would hold more pain than pleasure, and suppose nobody else is affected either way. Painlessly ending their life today brings about exactly that stretch of non-existence.
(4) By the asymmetry, that ending is good (all that future pain is gone) and not bad (no one is deprived of pleasure they would have had).
(5) So painlessly ending that person's life — without asking them — is at least permissible, maybe positively good. The asymmetry's ledger never asked about consent, so it can't start complaining now.

He thinks (5) is monstrous, so he wants to reject (1), but he can't for the life of him say what's differnt about after-death non-existence versus never-existed non-existence — either way the person just isn't there. And he definately doesn't want to give up the asymmetry itself, since his whole antinatalism sits on it. My own hunch is that something sneaky happens in (4), somewhere between the "not bad" and the "no one is deprived" — the person in (3) might still count as deprived of the pleasant bits of their future, even in a life that's bad on net — but I can't tell whether that actually breaks the argument or whether he can just patch it by talking about net pleasure. Maybe the argument is fine as far as it goes and the real fight is over which premise to pay for.
