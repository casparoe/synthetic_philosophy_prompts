I am a process control engineer who spends her days commissioning and tuning loops on chemical plants. The vocabulary I actually use is setpoints, process variables and measured variables, controller gain, integral windup and anti-windup, actuator saturation, cascade and feedforward structures, sensor drift and calibration, state observers, soft sensors, model predictive control with constraint handling, and model-validity ranges — the region of operating conditions over which a plant model was identified and can be trusted. I have read enough to know the AI-safety literature has produced a taxonomy of Goodhart's law variants, and I want to see whether those distinctions cut anywhere my own distinctions don't.

Restate the standard four-way taxonomy of Goodhart effects — regressional Goodhart (selecting hard on an imperfect proxy necessarily selects for the proxy's noise component, so extreme proxy values overstate the goal), extremal Goodhart (optimization pushes the system into regions where the proxy-goal relationship, established only under ordinary conditions, no longer holds), causal Goodhart (the regulator's own intervention on the proxy severs or distorts the causal link between proxy and goal), and adversarial Goodhart (an agent with objectives of its own, who knows the proxy is being monitored, deliberately distorts it) — in my vocabulary, one variant at a time. For each, do three things: identify what translates cleanly and what my field's established response to it is (anti-windup, cascade control, sensor redundancy and recalibration, model-validity guards, gain scheduling, or whatever else you would actually reach for); identify what has to be distorted or fudged to fit into control-engineering terms; and identify anything that my vocabulary cannot express at all, however loosely.

Then give me the residue report in both directions. First: is there anything in the taxonomy that control engineering has no way to say, and if so, is that because control engineering is missing something, or because the philosophers and alignment researchers are drawing a distinction without a difference from where I stand? Second, and I care about this more: is there anything in my toolkit that the Goodhart literature seems to lack a counterpart for? Finally, note one asymmetry I suspect matters and tell me if I am wrong: my field tends to name a failure mode after its fix (nobody talks about the windup problem; they talk about anti-windup), while theirs names failure modes after their mechanisms and leaves the fixes scattered. Does that difference track anything real about which discipline has which problem under operational control, or is it just a cultural artifact of who has to sign off when the loop misbehaves?
