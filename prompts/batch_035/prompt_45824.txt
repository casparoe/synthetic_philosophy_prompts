I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, thirty-odd years) who got into philosophy of mind after retiring, mostly because the hard problem finally started to bug me. Somewhere along the way I talked myself into panpsychism — or at least the version where some form of mentality is a fundamental feature of matter, present in electrons no less than in neurons, and what we call "emergence" is really recombination of stuff that was never fully blank to begin with. Part of the appeal is that it lets me keep my physicalist instincts: no extra spooky substance, just a different view of what physical stuff is at the bottom. I also like that it calls out the "brute emergence" story — the idea that consciousness just pops into existence once you cross some complexity treshold has always struck me as hand-waving that wouldn't survive review in any other branch of science.

So: what would count against it? I want a map of the view's exposure, like you'd want for a proposed mechanism — not a sales pitch for the alternatives.

I know the combination problem is the big one, but I'd like it taken apart rather than just named. There seems to be at least two different problems being run together here: the one about how you get from many micro-experiences to a single unified experiencing subject at all (the blending issue, I think Goff calls the reverse of it the "decombination" problem or something), versus the one about how the specific character of the micro-level stuff determines the specific character of our experience (the summing part). Which of these is closer to fatal for the view, and which is more "we don't have a story yet, but nothing's been shown impossible"? And are there other exposures beyond the combination problem I should be taking seriously — the boundary problem (why does this experience stop at my skull rather than including the chair I'm sitting on), the fact that the view seems to make no predictions and so can't be tested, or the worry that "proto-mentality" has quietly been redefined into something that no longer counts as mental in any interesting sense?

One thing specifically: if a purely functional/structural theory of consciousness — something in the lineage of integrated information theory, or some better successor — turned out to account for all the data without ever needing intrinsic mentality at the bottom, would that count against panpsychism, or only against its explanatory usefulness? That's the kind of result that would move me most, so please be honest about whether it should — maybe structural theories just are descriptions of the same underlying facts from the outside.

Last thing: how insulated is the view, really? I've seen defenses that strike me as moving the goalposts — the line being that panpsychism isn't supposed to explain this or that, it's just the best metaphysical backdrop. Fine, but then what exactly is at stake? If it's been qualified to the point of surviving everything, tell me so, and tell me whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. I'm aware metaphysical background pictures don't make lab predictions — I'm not asking the view for a number — but I spent a career being suspicious of frameworks that fit any data you throw at them, and I'd rather know now than in ten years.

Rank the defeaters for me, fatal → merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed panpsychist would concede it counts against them or wave it off — and whether the waving off is legitimate or just defensive.
