Last weekend I was at a friend's birthday dinner and two of the guests, Maya and Dev, got into a genuinely heated argument about why humans cry emotional tears — sad tears, not the kind you get from chopping onions. Maya, who's doing a PhD in evolutionary psych, insisted it has to be an adaptation: tears blur your vision and make you look vulnerable in front of others, and natural selection doesn't let costly stuff hang around for free, so crying must be doing something for us — she figures it's an honest signal of need and non-aggression, since it's involuntary and basically impossible to fake convincingly, which is exactly what you'd expect selection to build if the function were honest communication. Dev rolled his eyes and called this a just-so story: you can invent an adaptive tale for literally any trait, and in his view emotional tears are just the tear ducts doing their ordinary lubrication job while intense autonomic arousal happens to crank up the output — a byproduct, like the paintings in the corners of cathedral arches (he brought up the spandrels thing: you get those curved triangular gaps whether the architect wants them or not, because the arches and ceiling have to be built for structural reasons, and the decoration got painted in later because the spaces were there).

Maya came back with two points. First, the byproduct story is just as unfalsifiable as hers — if both explanations can absorb any observation you throw at them, Dev's is in no better epistemic shape than hers. Second, she said she'd read somewhere that emotional tears and irritation tears don't even have the same chemical makeup — emotional ones supposedly carry a higher concentration of stress-related proteins — and she said that's exactly what you'd predict if the system had been retooled for a signaling job, since byproducts don't usually get their own special recipe. Dev answered that a compositional difference could easily fall out of running the glands at maximum output for a sustained stretch, and then he flipped the question on her: if tears are a signal aimed at an audience, why do people so often end up crying alone, with nobody around to receive the signal? Maya's last move was that crying alone is just the system misfiring in a world it wasn't built for, the same way our sugar cravings misfire now that food is everywhere — in the ancestral setting there was basically always an audience, since you'd never actually have been alone. Dev told her that move proves her theory can never lose, and went off to get more wine.

I've been turning it over all week and honestly can't decide. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who's right at each step, and whether Dev's "just-so story" accusation is doing real argumentative work or is itself just a conversation-stopper that could be flung at any functional explanation anyone ever proposes? Also tell me whether the two hypotheses here are even empirically distinguishable in principle, i.e. what observation would count for or against each. And if you think Maya's mismatch rescue at the end is ad hoc, say so plainly — but then also say whether Dev's byproduct alternative is any better than a truism, since "it's a side effect" can cover a lot of ground too.
