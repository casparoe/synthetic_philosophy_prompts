I'm a high-school senior taking an elective in philosophy, and my best friend and I have spent most of a term arguing about epistemic akrasia — the state of believing something while also believing that, by your own lights, you shouldn't believe it. Our teacher has grown tired of adjudicating and proposed that we each put a number on our position and state our reasoning in explicit steps, so I'm asking you to do the same.

First, the proposition, stated precisely so it has a truth value: *there are possible cases in which it is rational for an agent to believe that p, despite the agent simultaneously believing, on good grounds, that her total evidence does not adequately support p.* That last clause matters — I'm not asking about weakness of will in action, or about self-deception, but about whether the epistemically rational thing can ever be to hold a belief you yourself judge unsupported.

To keep us honest, we've been anchoring the whole debate on one invented case, and I'd like you to reason through it rather than around it. Call it the chess case: a strong club player studies a position, and with genuine clarity she sees that a particular move wins. She checks it, and it still looks won. Then a source she has every reason to trust tells her that in positions of exactly this type, players of precisely her rating, reporting exactly this feeling of obviousness, are wrong about eighty percent of the time — and that this holds even after they've checked their analysis once. Nothing else changes. The position looks the same; her confidence feels the same; the higher-order evidence is the only new input. Our question is whether she may rationally keep her belief.

My credence in the proposition is roughly 0.65. My friend's is roughly 0.15, because he holds that evidence about the reliability of your evidence is just more evidence, ordinary and first-order in disguise; on his view the apparent conflict dissolves before it starts, the player should simply lower her confidence, and no genuine akrasia was ever in the room.

I'd like your own number, but I care much more about how you get there, so please decompose it. At minimum I'd expect terms for at least these sub-questions, though add others if you think they belong: (a) the probability that higher-order evidence really is just ordinary evidence under another description, in which case my friend is right that the disagreement is verbal; (b) the probability that epistemic rationality is an all-things-considered matter, so that a belief held against what you take your total evidence to support is ipso facto irrational, versus the probability that rationality can be indexed locally — that responding correctly at the object level can survive a bad higher-order situation; (c) the probability that cases like the chess one are ones where, by the agent's own lights, keeping the belief does better epistemically than suspending. Assign a rough probability to each, give one line of justification per term, then show how they combine into your final figure.

Two further requests. First, tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of case would move that term the most. Second, tell me where you think the gap between my 0.65 and my friend's 0.15 actually lives: is he making a mistake about the possibility of level-crossing, am I over-weighting the felt obviousness of the chess case, or are we dividing the question differently? Please do not answer that it all depends on how one defines rationality — part of what I'm asking you to do is show me, in the decomposition, which definitions do the work and how much each costs you.
