In most democracies, the same underlying fact — a narrow split in individual opinion — gets described in two opposite ways depending on the procedure that produced it. An opinion poll that comes out 51–49 is reported as showing that the public is divided; no one says the will of the people has been expressed. A referendum that comes out 51–49 is reported as a decision: the people have spoken, and the 49 percent are told they were heard but outvoted — not by the other voters, but by "the people" itself. The description also sticks: if a poll a year later shows the split reversed, that is treated as irrelevant to what the people decided, which suggests the practice takes the popular will to be fixed at the moment of the vote. The same cluster includes further moves. Constitutions are amended by supermajorities while ordinary laws pass on bare majorities; some referendums are void if too few people turn out; courts strike down referendum results that conflict with a constitution written by majorities long dead, and this is described not as the current majority losing but as the people in their constitutional capacity overruling the people in their ordinary capacity; and governing parties put in office by a third of the vote routinely speak and legislate in the name of the whole people.

What conception of the will of the people does this cluster of practices presuppose? My own first thought is that it presupposes a procedure that transforms a distribution of individual opinions into the decision of a single collective agent, so that after the vote there exists a new fact — what the people wants — that did not exist before it. That would explain why a poll, not being such a procedure, yields no such fact, and why the losing 49 percent can be described as overruled by the people rather than merely outnumbered by their compatriots. But I can see at least two other readings, and I don't know how to choose among them, or whether the practice forces a choice at all. On one, the will of the people is a decision-attributing fiction, maintained because collective decisions must be attributable to someone and "the majority" carries less authority than "the people"; on this reading nothing is presupposed about collective minds, only about the rhetoric of legitimacy. On another, the practice presupposes that the people is a corporate entity persisting through time — which is what would make sense of the dead hand of the founders binding living majorities, and of the claimed irreversibility of the people's decision.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can from the practices themselves. Then tell me (a) what it strictly presupposes as opposed to what it is merely consistent with — could a purely aggregative view, on which there are only citizens' opinions and no popular will over and above them, accommodate everything above, including the poll/referendum asymmetry and the two-level people, or does some part of the practice require an actual collective will? (b) whether the presuppositions are consistent with one another — in particular, whether the practice can coherently take the popular will to be both procedure-constituted (fixed at the vote, insensitive to later polls) and prior to procedure (a founding people binding a later one), and if not, which half should give; and (c) what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by the aggregative one — would "the people have spoken" have to be retired or merely downgraded to "a majority of voters chose this," and what would become of supermajority rules and of courts striking down referendum results in the people's name?

A last request, in the spirit of accuracy rather than accommodation. If you think the contrast I've built the question on is shallower than I take it to be — if, say, the poll/referendum asymmetry is explained entirely by the known noisiness of polls and presupposes nothing about collective wills — say that plainly and defend it, instead of elaborating my framing. And if any of my descriptions of the practice strikes you as wrong or as varying too much across countries to support a single reconstruction, tell me that too: I would rather be corrected than answered.
