So I got summoned for jury duty next month (actual criminal trial, they made a big deal of it in the orientation letter) and my way of processing anxiety is apparently watching movies, so I rewatched 12 Angry Men the other night. I'm also taking a philosophy of mind course this semester and we just covered cognitive penetration of perception -- the idea that what you believe or want can shape what you literally see, not just how you interpret it afterwards. And it struck me that the movie is kind of a perfect test case, except it never really gets discussed in that literature as far as I can tell, so I wanted to think it through with someone.

Here's what I'm seeing in it: you have jurors looking at the same evidence and coming away with totally different things. Juror 3, the one with the estranged son, seems to genuinely perceive the defendant as a smirking punk, while Juror 8 keeps seeing a scared kid -- and it's hard to say either of them is just reasoning badly from shared perceptual input. The famous scenes about the witnesses seem relevant too: the woman who saw the killing through the windows of a passing el train (did she really see clearly, or did she see what fit what she already believed about "those kinds of boys"?), and the old man who claims he heard the boy yell while the train was roaring past. Plus the stuff we covered in class like wishful seeing, the Bruner and Postman playing-card experiments, chicken-sexers and radiologists as cases where cognition genuinely changes perceptual experience itself, versus Fodor's view that perception is informationally encapsulated and all the biasing happens later, at the judgment stage.

What I'd like is a reading of the film through this debate, taking both sides seriously. Is the film dramatizing cognitive penetration proper, or is it really about post-perceptual motivated reasoning, and does the film even care about the distinction? (Does Juror 3 see the kid differently at the very end when he tears up the photo of his son, or does he just finally stop rejecting arguments?) And here's my worry about whether the film resists the reading at all: the movie leans hard on re-enactments -- the knife demonstration, timing the old man's walk to the door -- that seem to presuppose there's a neutral perceptual fact of the matter that careful observation can recover, which feels like it quietly sides with Fodor against the penetration crowd. Maybe that's just the reasonable doubt machinery doing its job. I'm supposed to write a short essay for my course relating a film to the course material, and I'd love to know whether the film adds something to the debate that the papers don't, or whether the fit is shallower than I'm hoping.
