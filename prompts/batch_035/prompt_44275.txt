I help run a small extracurricular reading group on philosophy of mind — mostly undergrads, one or two AI researchers who show up for the consciousness units. Last week we had a session on machine self-knowledge, and one of the researchers tried to end the whole discussion with Descartes' "I think, therefore I am." His claim was roughly this: any system that can sincerely entertain that thought — including a language model that produces the sentence in the right circumstances — has thereby proved its own existence, in the one way that survives radical doubt, and so the interesting question about whether machines "really" have selves is dissolved. The room nodded along, but I left uneasy, and when I tried to pin down what the line actually asserts, I found I couldn't. Depending on how you read it, it seems to range from something almost trivially true to something enormous, and I suspect only some of those readings even touch my colleague's argument.

Here is roughly what I've noticed, though I want you to do this properly. Sometimes the line seems to claim only that thinking is going on right now — that there's an event of doubting or affirming, without any owner being smuggled in. Sometimes it's an indexical claim: whoever sincerely thinks this exists at that moment, but nothing is said about existing a moment later. Sometimes it slides into a claim about an enduring thing, a substance that does the thinking — I remember someone telling me that early critics, Gassendi and I think Lichtenberg, complained precisely that Descartes wasn't entitled to the "I" at all, only to the thinking, though I confess I've read neither in the original. And then there's a whole other axis: even granting that the conclusion is true, there's a question of what kind of achievement it is — a syllogism from thought to existence, a self-verifying performance more like uttering "I am speaking" than an argument, or something about the special epistemic status of first-person present-tense claims, immune to doubt in a way that even my knowledge of my own body isn't.

So here's what I'd like. Take the sentence as a piece of language, not as a doctrine, and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — from the readings that are near-platitudes, through the substantive and defensible ones, to the ones that are probably false or at least unsupported — as a menu I can bring back to the group rather than one favored interpretation argued for. For each reading, say what it proves, how much, and for how long (an event, a moment, a persisting self). And crucially: tell me which of those readings, if any, actually deliver what my colleague wanted — does any of them show that a system producing or entertaining the thought has established its own existence, or does the whole maneuver depend on a reading (sincere first-person grip, genuine thinking, whatever) that is precisely what was at issue in the debate about machines? If some readings support him and others don't, I want to be able to say exactly which and why.
