I'm a PhD student running a small epistemology reading group (five of us, mixed formal epistemologists and more old-fashioned types) and we just spent three sessions on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and the KK principle, and the weird thing is we all apparently walked away holding different views while each of us is convinced we all agree. One guy says the whole anti-luminosity thing is just the safety condition in a trenchcoat, one insists KK fails only because we're lazy about reflecting, one thinks KK is basically analytic and Williamson smuggled in an externalist conception of knowledge, and I genuinely can't tell which of them I side with. So I want to build a decision tree that will actually sort us out.

The starting point of the tree should be some commitment everyone can evaluate without jargon, like "I know that I'm cold right now" or "I can tell, just by attending to it, whether I'm in mild pain." Then each branch node needs to be a question you can answer without knowing which leaf it feeds into — so no "do you accept the safety condition on knowledge," but instead concrete cases, e.g.: if my judgements about feeling cold are reliable but I can't distinguish feeling 10 degrees cold from feeling 9 degrees cold, can the judgement in the actual case still be knowledge? Is there a real difference between failing to know that I know because I never bothered to check, versus failing because no amount of checking could get me there? Does KK at least hold for boring stuff like "2+2=4" even if it fails for sensations? Could a properly functioning, perfectly reflective agent still fail to know that she knows p? The leaves I want: Williamsonian anti-luminism, the tracking/sensitivity-style externalist view where KK fails because knowing that you know would require tracking your own tracking, the Hintikka-style view that KK is built into the concept of knowledge itself, the moderate view where reflection can restore KK but self-doubt blocks it, and whatever name (or "unnamed") you'd give to the people who think the really interesting failures run the other direction — knowing that you don't know that you know, or something like that, I admit I'm not sure anyone holds it.

Please build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and which combinations of answers they allow — and then locate the actual positions inside it, and tell me which combinations of answers nobody occupies and why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? Also flag honestly any two leaves the tree can't separate, because I have a suspicion that anti-luminism-via-margin-for-error and anti-luminism-via-safety end up on the same branch no matter what questions you put at the nodes. Bonus points if you can come up with one extra question that would split a pair the tree can't, even if it only works in theory. Keep it to maybe 6-8 internal nodes so it fits on a single handout page, and phrase the node questions the way you'd actually say them out loud to freind in a pub, not in journal-speak.
