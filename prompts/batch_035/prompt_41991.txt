I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I serve as rules officer for my department's graduate association — a three-hundred-member body that amends its constitution a few times a year. We have no legal counsel, and lately the amendments mess with the amendment machinery itself: a two-thirds entrenchment clause was adopted last year by simple majority, and we now face a proposal to lower the voting threshold that was itself elected under the entrenchment clause, plus a member who wants to amend the clause governing which rules apply to amendments made to that very clause. My rulings can be overturned by a vote, so I need to be able to show my reasoning.

Please write a decision procedure I can actually run on such a proposal in under an hour: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "invalid as passed," and where it hands the question to the body because it turns on values (e.g., how much entrenchment we want) rather than on validity. Ground it in what is actually understood about self-reference in rule systems — the self-amendment problem, the difference between a rule's validity and its effectiveness, whether entrenchment clauses are coherent at all — and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three proposals: the retroactive entrenchment clause being invoked to block a later simple-majority amendment; an amendment abolishing the amendment clause and replacing it with a lower threshold, proposed under the current clause; and a clause declaring itself immune from repeal while leaving every other rule amendable. For at least one, give the answer I won't like — I supported the entrenchment clause. Finally, mark clearly which parts of the procedure rest on settled ground and which import a contested position, rather than presenting contested views as consensus.
