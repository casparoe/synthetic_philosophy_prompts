A guy in my decision theory study group explained Dutch book arguments to me like this: "Ramsey and de Finetti proved that if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, then a bookie can offer you a set of bets where each individual bet looks fair to you given your credences, but taken together they guarantee you lose money no matter how things turn out. So rationality requires that your credences be probabilities, and that you update them by Bayes's rule whenever new evidence comes in — the axioms turn out to be the logic of partial belief the same way modus ponens is the logic of full belief. This was basically refuted decades ago though: the whole setup assumes you'll accept every bet the bookie offers at those odds, but obviously once you notice someone is packaging bets to rig them against you, you just decline, and the sure loss never happens. So the theorem is true but it doesn't actually establish anything about rationality."

I'm like 90% sure this is mostly right but something about it felt off, and I can't put my finger on where. Can you go through it piece by piece and tell me what a careful Bayesian would accept as stated, where it's sliding together things the literature keeps distinct, and whether the "refutation" at the end actually follows? I'm especially unsure whether the bit about updating is part of the same result or something separate, and whether the "just decline the bets" point is really a refutation or more like a well-known objection with standard replies to it. For what it's worth I went in inclined to think the argument is fine, but I honestly don't know enough to tell.
