I teach an adult-education course that pairs philosophy with film, and next term I'm screening Harold Ramis's Groundhog Day (1993) alongside Borges's writings on time. Please write me a critical essay, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, in British English, that reads the film through Borges's views on time. Take both the film and the Borgesian texts seriously as the two halves of the reading: the idea should illuminate the film, and the film should be allowed to push back on the idea.

The relevant Borges is: the essay "The Doctrine of Cycles" (from A History of Eternity), the essay "A New Refutation of Time" (from Other Inquisitions), and the two stories "The Garden of Forking Paths" and "The Secret Miracle" (both from Ficciones). You need not touch all four with equal weight, but I'd like the essay to work through at least the following four problems.

First, the individuation problem. Borges, in the essay on cycles, treats the Pythagorean and Nietzschean doctrine of eternal recurrence with a kind of double-mindedness: given a finite number of possible states of the world and an infinite time, recurrence seems forced on us, and yet he finds reasons to doubt that exact repetition could ever be established — among them that a repetition indistinguishable in every respect from a first occurrence would not be two events but one. Groundhog Day grants recurrence but with a single amendment: Phil Connors's memory persists across the loops. Does that amendment preserve the doctrine or quietly destroy it? If each February the second is observed, remembered and compared, is it still the same day at all — or a new day that merely resembles a previous one? I want the essay to say where the film falls on Borges's suspicion that a remembered recurrence is no recurrence, and whether the film's comedy depends on not noticing this.

Second, branching versus serial time. The film's structure — one fixed day from which innumerable variations sprout, each explored, abandoned and replaced — invites comparison with the bifurcating novel in "The Garden of Forking Paths," in which every outcome of every choice happens somewhere in its own strand. But the film keeps one continuous, linear protagonist who moves from branch to branch rather than splitting him across them, and the branches overwrite rather than coexist. What does the film show about personal identity and branching that Borges's story, which diffuses its hero across the strands, leaves deliberately indistinct? Is the film's Phil the same man who has lived a very long time, and does the answer matter to the Borgesian question of which structure time actually has?

Third, private time. In "The Secret Miracle," Jaromir Hladík is granted, between the firing squad's aim and its shot, a year of purely mental duration in which the world stands still. Phil's February the second is in a sense the inverse: the world resets nightly while his mind continues. I'd like the essay to consider whether Phil's day, during the loops, constitutes a private, mental time of the sort Borges's refutation essay leaves us with — the essay argues from Berkeley and Hume that neither objects nor selves persist, and therefore that time itself is a superstition, only to concede at the end that the continuity of the experiencing self is precisely what he cannot deny, since his own persistence is made of it. Is Phil's retained memory the thread that makes his repeated day a time at all?

Fourth, the ending. On the morning of February the third, Phil wakes with the self accumulated across uncounted repetitions, while the town of Punxsutawney — as far as the film ever lets us see — remembers only a single, ordinary day. The loops are discarded without record, as though they had happened to no one but Phil. Does the happy ending vindicate Borges's final concession, that time is real because the remembering self is real? Or does it quietly take the crueler view that unremembered time is nothing, so that the thousands of erased days — including the suicides, the wasted decades, the days in which Rita loved a man who then vanished — weigh nothing? The film asks us not to ask this; a Borgesian reading cannot avoid asking it.

Two honest-critic requirements. Please note at least one place where the film resists the Borgesian reading — the moral-improvement arc, the romantic resolution, the sheer sitcom timing — and say plainly whether the film is genuinely interested in time or merely uses repetition as a premise that the plot can consume. And please close by saying what the film adds to Borges's treatment of time that the essays and stories, on their own, cannot give us — what only dramatised recurrence, experienced in real screen time by a character we watch, makes visible.

Practical constraints: paraphrase throughout, do not quote from any text or from the film, and do not provide citations, bibliographies or reading lists. Assume the reader knows the film's plot and major scenes (the clock-radio, the Groundhog Day report, the piano lessons, the repeated rescue of the falling boy, the final breakfast with Rita) and knows Borges's stories in outline. Please write the whole essay in British English, including spelling (recognise, colourise), and render dates as in British usage. The register should be that of an essay in a serious film-philosophy journal — precise, a little dry, and willing to end on a verdict rather than a shrug.
