I'm a retired epidemiologist, thirty-odd years of chasing disease outbreaks and arguing about cohort data, and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy of science, which has been humbling in ways I didn't expect. The thing I keep coming back to is underdetermination: the Duhem-Quine idea that our total evidence can't ever single out one theory, because for any body of results you can always cook up rivals that fit it just as well. When I first read about it, it struck me as obviously true and obviously beside the point, from inside a working lab. Now I've read enough of the back and forth to see the debate has basically been going in circles since the 80s, and I want a diagnosis of why, not another round of it.

Here's how the stall looks to me. Everyone accepts the minimal holism: you can't test a hypothesis in isolation because auxiliaries are always in play. Everyone also concedes that the radical rivals — the demon physics, the universe-created-last-Thursday stuff — are silly and that no serious scientist has ever believed one. So the interesting question is supposed to be whether there are non-silly, scientifically respectable rivals that are empirically equivalent to our best theories. And here's where it freezes: the critics say empirical equivalence is never a permanent fact, because what counts as observable and what auxiliaries we're allowed to keep change over time, so the rivals lose their equivalence eventually; the defenders say that's just pointing at future evidence, which concedes the thesis held in the meantime. Both sides seem to think they've won. My own working life fits this weirdly well: we once had two transmission models for a disease cluster, one with a hidden carrier state and one without, that fit every dataset we had for the better part of a decade, and the field didn't so much resolve it as drift, and only much later did a test become possible. Was that underdetermination doing real work, or just a gap in our instruments? I honestly can't tell, and the literature hasn't helped.

My pet hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is partly verbal, turning on what 'evidence' means. If only observational consequences count as evidence, underdetermination is everywhere and realism is in trouble; if you let simplicity, unification and fruitfulness count as evidence, it mostly evaporates — but then you owe a story about why those virtues track truth rather than just our convenience, and I've never seen that story told in a way that didn't feel like a punt. It could also be that the two camps are just answering different questions, one about logic and what's possible, one about the epistemology of actual scientific practice, and talking past each other under one label. So: is this dispute verbal, substantive, or two questions in one trenchcoat? Diagnose it using what the sides actually claim, and tell me what would genuinely count as progress — a sharper distinction, some actual history of resolved empirical equivalences, or an argument that the question was ill-posed from the start and the drift I saw in my own field was never really an instance of it.

Whatever your diagnosis, I'd like you to say what would change your mind about it, and where in your reasoning you think you're most likely to have gone wrong. And please write it as continuous prose, like a letter, no headings or bullet points or bold text — I read enough of those in journals, I don't need them in retirement.
