I spent thirty-five years in a physiology department — cardiac electrophysiology, mostly — and since retiring I have been working my way rather systematically into philosophy of biology. This year I have been asked to co-teach an upper-level undergraduate course on teleology and explanation in the life sciences, and I am responsible for writing one of the final exam questions. I would like your help constructing it, along with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that whoever grades the scripts is applying a consistent standard.

The question should concern the dispute between etiological (selected-effect) accounts of biological function and causal-role (systems) accounts. My worry is that my students have memorized the standard labels and the usual name-drops — Wright, Millikan, Cummins, Neander — and can deploy them fluently while understanding nothing. The question must be built so that recitation earns very little.

Please construct it around three concrete cases, which I would like you to use rather than substitute, since each pulls the two accounts in a different direction:

(a) The mammalian middle ear. The malleus and incus are homologous with bones that formed the jaw joint in our reptilian ancestors and were gradually co-opted for sound transmission across a long transitional series. Ask what function these bones have, and note that whatever answer is given must apply equally at the start of the transition, in the middle, and now.

(b) A human heart grown de novo from induced pluripotent stem cells, implanted in a patient, which pumps adequately for two years and then develops a persistent arrhythmia. Ask whether this organ has functions in the full sense, and whether the surgeon who calls it "dysfunctional" is speaking strictly or loosely.

(c) A pair of physiologists in my old laboratory who disagree about whether secreting natriuretic peptide counts as a function of the heart, given that the heart's endocrine role is a relatively late addition to a structure selected for pumping. Ask what would settle their disagreement, and whether anything could.

The question should press two things beyond the cases themselves: first, whether the normativity of function talk — the fact that we can say an organ is supposed to do something and is failing at it — can be recovered on a pure causal-role account or requires an etiological standard; second, whether the dispute between the two accounts is substantive or in some measure verbal. Students will be tempted to answer "both accounts are useful for different purposes" and stop; the marking scheme should make clear that this lazy pluralism is precisely what a top answer must go beyond, either by defending one account as more fundamental or by articulating what a principled hybrid would require.

Deliverables, then: the exam question itself, suitable for a 90-minute answer; a model answer of roughly 700 words that commits to a position rather than surveying the options; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks, organized around five or six must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how students typically botch each point, and a short statement of what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer — by which I mean the moves an A answer must contain that a merely competent one will not, not just a difference in fluency. Where the model answer invokes the cases, it should actually reason through them rather than mention them in passing; my own experience grading in the sciences is that students hide behind examples they never use.
