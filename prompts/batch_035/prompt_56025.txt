I write about science and ideas for a magazine, which mostly means I get into long arguments with my friend Priya, who dropped out of a philosophy PhD and has never let go of the material. Our current one: I've been working on a piece about Dennett's old attempt to dissolve qualia altogether — the essay where he argues that a question like "did the coffee taster's tastes change, or did his standards change?" is not a real question with a hidden fact in it, just a confusion, unless you cash it out in terms of functional differences like what he can discriminate and what he now avoids. And then the reply Chase and Sanborn wrote later, with the professional coffee taster who can't stand coffee anymore and genuinely can't tell whether the coffee started tasting different to him or he just stopped liking the taste. Priya has opinions about every one of these cases, and over the years she's built up a track record of verdicts on whether some change was "the experience itself changed" or "only his standards changed":

- the beer guy who hated beer and now loves it, who says he remembers exactly how bitter it felt before and it's just not like that anymore: experience changed. She won't take Dennett's line that there's no further fact of the matter here.
- the friend who overplayed a song into the ground, but insists it sounds exactly the same, he's just sick of it: standards.
- an acquaintance on SSRIs who says music doesn't feel like anything to her now, though she remembers what it felt like: experience.
- a new vegetarian who swears bacon smells like a corpse to him now: standards. That's his guilt talking, not his nose, she says.
- the old coffee taster himself, decades of professional tasting, memory of what coffee used to be like too faded to compare: the question is empty for him, no fact of the matter either way.

Next week I'm interviewing a long-term meditator for the piece and Priya is coming along. The meditator says that pain during long sits is just as intense as ever — she's very clear on that — but it doesn't bother her anymore, and when she tries to remember what the being-bothered felt like, she can't quiet get it back. So before Priya opens her mouth: what's her call going to be? Experience, standards, or empty? I can see at least two rules that fit her five verdicts equally well and they split on the meditator — one looks at where the change came from and says nothing touched the perceptual machinery, so it's her standards; the other cares about whether the old experience is still available for comparison and says she can't get it back, so there's no fact of the matter. There's maybe a third that just tracks what the person reports about the sensory stuff itself, and I genuinely don't know which side of the line the bothersomeness of pain falls on for that one. Give me your best guess at her verdict with your reasons, run through the candidate rules and what each predicts, and tell me what I could ask the meditator — or what other case I could throw at Priya afterwards — that would best split the rules apart. Two quick ones while you're at it: the guy 20 years out from his favorite childhood books who says they're boring now and he honestly can't remember what he loved about them, and a wine writer who admits the same bottle tastes better to him out of heavy glass with a fancy label, which he's checked on himself blind.
