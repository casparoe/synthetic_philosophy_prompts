Little background so you know where this is coming from: I'm a retired geochemist, thirty-some years working on groundwater contamination, and since I stopped going into the lab I've gotten completely hooked on philosophy. Latest obsession is moral uncertainty — the idea that you can be unsure not just about facts but about which moral theory is true, and that the uncertainty itself should change what you do. The framework I keep bumping into goes like this: each theory you have some credence in assigns every available act a "choiceworthiness" score, and you should pick the act with the highest credence-weighted average score. Straightforward enough, it's just expected value with moral theories instead of states of the world.

But something about it has been bugging me, and I want you to build me a thought experiment that dramatizes it. Here are the specs:

1. The agent has credence in exactly two moral theories, roughly 50/50. Both should be views an ordinary person has actually heard of — I'm imagining something like impartial maximizing consequentialism on one side, and a view with serious special obligations to the people closest to you on the other. Your choice, but keep them familiar, no invented exotic theory.

2. There are exactly three available acts. One is the act theory 1 would demand, one is the act theory 2 would demand, and the third is some kind of split or compromise between them.

3. Both theories are strict maximizers: only the top-ranked act is permissible, and everything else is flatly wrong. So for the case to work, the compromise has to rank SECOND on both theories — not last on either (that would make it dumb), and not first on either (that would let a theory permit it).

4. The punchline I want: even though the compromise is wrong according to every single moral theory the agent believes in, it's the act that maximizes the credence-weighted average choiceworthiness. So what the framework says you should do is forbidden by all of your moral views at once. That's the thing I want to feel in my gut.

5. The scores can't just be pulled out of the air — they have to be motivated by the facts of the scenario. There should be a reason, built into the story, why on each theory the compromise is nearly as good as that theory's favorite, while the rival's favorite is worth almost nothing by that theory's lights. I have a suspicion the trick has something to do with where the value gets loaded into each option — like the first chunk of each pot doing most of the work — but you figure it out. If that IS the mechanism, fine, use it, but make the numbers grow out of the story and then show me the arithmetic explicitly.

Constraints on the scenario itself: ordinary life, no trolleys, no hospital triage, no sci-fi, nothing melodramatic. One paragraph. The agent should plausibly be able to just be sitting there with these three options. And the compromise should be a perfectly sane thing a thoughtful person might actually do — not an obviously insane act that no one would pick anyway.

Then, after the scenario, I want you to walk through the analysis:

- Give each theory's verdict with rough numbers attached, do the weighted-average arithmetic, and show the compromise winning.
- Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature.
- Then let the agent's credences slide from 50/50 toward, say, 85/15 in favor of one theory. Find the exact point where the verdict flips, and tell me whether that flip point is embarrassingly narrow or reassuringly far off.
- Finally, two objections I want you to take seriously. First: how would someone who says "just go with whichever theory you find most credible and forget the averaging" attack this case? Second, and I gather this is a real fight in the literature: the choiceworthiness scales are made up separately by each theory, so what grounds do I have for treating "85" on one theory's scale as comparable to "85" on the other's? Tell me honestly whether my case secretly depends on that comparability, and what's left of it if you refuse the comparison.

Typos and all, that's what I've got. Go.
