I help run a tiny charity fund at work — we pool about $4,000 a year from maybe 60 coworkers and vote on which of three nominated projects gets the whole pot (stuff like the local food bank, malaria nets, a scholarship). At last month's meeting someone threw out the utility monster as an argument that voting on this is kind of pointless: if even one beneficiary turned out to have a utility function that scales way higher than everyone else's, then the entire fund should go wherever that person's interests point, and what the majority prefers is irrelevent. And to make it sharper, one of our members, Dana, has started half-jokingly claiming she's at least a partial monster: she says her pleasure from music is roughly an order of magnitude stronger than most people's (she leans on some super-taster analogy), so a chunk of the fund should go to buying her concert tickets. She's mostly trolling, but the argument structure is real and I want to think it through properly instead of laughing it off.

So: what are the most important implications of the utility monster problem for how a small democratic fund like ours should actually make decisions? I don't want a survey of utilitarianism pro/con in general — I want it applied to this dumb little pot of money. Things I keep going back and forth on: does even the possibility of monsters mean we shouldn't weight reported intensity at all and should only count heads? Is there a principled line between taking Dana's testimony seriously as data versus letting whoever talks most vividly capture the fund? Does the fact that interpersonal utility comparisons are shaky rescue voting, or does it just mean our vote is arbitrary for a different reason? And if a genuine monster candidate can't be verified, should we have an explicit rule about it — or does institutionalizing a rule against monsters just mean knowingly leaving utility on the table, which seems hard to defend out loud?

Also, wherever you land, tell me what would actually change your mind — like which argument or consideration would flip you to the other verdict — and flag the the step in your own reasoning where you think you're most likely to have gone wrong. I'd rather have honest uncertainty here than a confident framework that papers over the measurement problem.
