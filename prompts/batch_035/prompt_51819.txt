In a jurisprudence seminar on the Hart–Devlin debate, our instructor defended Devlin's side as follows. A society's shared morality is a seamless fabric: its norms about sex, property, honesty, and respect for persons are not separate threads but a single weave, so that you cannot pull out any one of them — even one whose breach harms no identifiable victim, as with the private homosexual conduct at issue in the Wolfenden Report — without loosening the whole. And just as a society may use the criminal law to defend its political institutions against subversion, it may use the criminal law to defend this moral fabric against erosion. The instructor then drew the practical conclusion: criminalizing victimless immorality is legitimate, because it is the moral equivalent of reinforcing a seam.

Audit this analogy. First, give me the correspondence table: what in a society maps onto the fabric, the individual threads, the seam, the act of pulling a thread out, the unraveling — and what, if anything, maps onto the tailor, since fabrics do not weave themselves. Second, say where the mapping breaks. In particular: does actual moral change resemble unraveling at all? Hart himself pointed to the decline of Victorian sexual morality and to loosened divorce laws, and to the fact that societies survived these changes with their cohesion apparently intact; if moral fabrics can be rewoven thread by thread, what is left of the claim that any single thread is load-bearing? Does the fabric picture also quietly import claims the analogy cannot establish by itself — about the indivisibility of moral norms, about how fast attitudes actually propagate, about why criminal enforcement rather than informal disapproval is needed to hold a seam, and about whether the mere existence of a shared morality, as opposed to the enforcement of it, is what holds a society together? Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the instructor's conclusion from those that don't: perhaps the subversion comparison is doing more work than the weaving metaphor. Finally, tell me whether the instructor's conclusion survives the audit. Is there a repaired version of the analogy that still licenses criminalizing victimless immorality, or did the fabric carry the whole argument? If the metaphor fails, propose the analogy you think a legal moralist would actually need to make the case, and say briefly what it would take to defend it against the same treatment.
