My friend and I got into a genuinely three-hour argument last weekend about whether Emma is basically an Adam Smith novel, and I want to work the reading out properly before our book club meets (they've assigned me the "philosophy angle" because I won't shut up about it). Her claim: Emma Woodhouse is what the impartial spectator looks like when it's broken. Emma judges everyone by an imagined spectator, but her spectator is built out of vanity and self-love — it's Smith's construction with the inputs corrupted, so she's confidently wrong about Harriet and Elton and Frank in a way that's Smithian in form (she's genuinely simulating a view from elsewhere, she thinks hard about how things appear) while failing in substance. My counter was that this makes the novel anti-Smith: the fix, Knightley, is an *external* spectator, an actual other person whose judgment Emma has to feel shame in front of, whereas Smith wants to say the real check on self-deception is internal — you can't see your own face, so you have to imagine how you look to a well-informed bystander. Except Emma has exactly that imagined spectator and it checks nothing. So which is it — does Austen dramatize the spectator and vindicate it, or show that Smith's internal construction can't do the work and you actually need a Knightley standing in front of you?

And I keep coming back to the Box Hill scene. Knightley's rebuke — the part where he basically asks Emma to imagine being Miss Bates on the receiving end of that joke — is the closest thing I've seen to the impartial spectator staged as an actual scene: the whole mechanism of the book is Emma learning to enter into the situation of a person whose claims she'd dismissed as trivial. But my friend says the interesting part is what it costs: Emma's mortification afterward feels more like Smith's shame before the *actual* spectator than anything imagined, and Austen surrounds Emma with a social world that functions as one mirror after another. So maybe the novel is saying the imagined spectator only works because it's scaffolded by real people whose opinions you can't control.

What I want from you: a reading that takes Smith seriously (the impartial spectator, sympathy as a learned projection rather than a reflex, the corruption worry) and takes the novel seriously too — Emma isn't just a failed-spectator case study, she's also a study in someone whose judgments are dead-on about half the town and wrong in exactly the direction her vanity predicts, which is more interesting than "she's delusional." Where does the reading hold, where does the novel resist it, and is there something the novel adds to Smith that the argument alone can't? My hunch is it's about how the spectator gets *trained* — Smith sort of hand-waves that part and Austen doesn't, since the whole book is the training. Don't bother with quotes, we've all got different editions anyway. And honestly, tell me if my friend's anti-Smith reading is just stronger and I'm losing this argument — I can take it.
