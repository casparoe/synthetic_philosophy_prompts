i got into this argument with my jurisprudence study group about the hart–fuller debate and it's been bugging me for a week. topic was the natural law side's claim that a sufficiently unjust or chaotic system doesn't count as law at all — the Radbruch formula, the one the german courts leaned on after the war in the grudge informer case.

here's the argument i ended up building:

(1) whether a legal system actually guides behavior is a matter of degree — Fuller's desiderata (publicity, clarity, non-contradiction, congruence between the announced rule and what officials actually do, etc.) can each be satisfied better or worse, and no real system satisfies them all perfectly.

(2) a system that fails these demands totally isn't law, even if it has all the official trappings — decrees that were secret, retroactive and contradicted by what courts actually enforced don't create legal obligations in the first place.

(3) there's no principled threshhold between total failure and perfect success — the failures shade into each other continuously.

(4) so whether something is law is itself a matter of degree: the secret retroactive decrees were barely law if at all, an ordinary statute with one hopelessly vague section is very nearly law, etc.

(5) but courts can't act on degrees. when the german court had to decide whether the statute the grudge informer relied on was valid law, it needed a yes or a no — you can't convict someone under a statute that's 60% law.

(6) so the court needs a threshhold, and any threshhold will be arbitrary — a system one notch below the line is morally indistinguishable from a system one notch above it.

(7) therefore the natural law position collapses: either it admits an arbitrary cutoff, which is no better than the positivist's pedigree test and a lot vaguer, or it quietly goes back to pedigree, in which case the wicked-but-properly-enacted statute was law all along and the whole formula was rhetorical.

my study group keeps saying i smuggled something in around step 4, and i suspect they're right, but i can't see it — going from "no sharp threshold" to "matter of degree" feels innocent to me. and honestly, if i have to give something up, i'd rather keep (1), the guidance-failing-by-degrees thing seems like the one obviously true insight in this whole debate. so: which step do i reject, what exactly got smuggled in, and what does rejecting it cost me?
