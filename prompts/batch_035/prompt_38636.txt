Interactive theorem provers have moved from curiosity to instrument of record in some corners of mathematics. The Kepler conjecture was settled by the Flyspeck project, a formal proof in HOL Light completed in 2014; the Liquid Tensor Experiment, finished in Lean in 2022, verified the main theorem of Scholze's work on condensed mathematics — a result whose proof was so long and intricate that Scholze himself said he was unsure of it in every detail, and which he came to trust more once the machine had checked it. A growing number of mathematicians now accept a theorem as established when a proof assistant certifies it, sometimes without any human reading more than the statement and the skeleton of the argument. I'd like to understand what epistemology of mathematics this practice presupposes, viewed through Benacerraf's dilemma. The dilemma, as I understand it: a plausible semantics for mathematics makes its truths about abstract, causally inert objects, while a plausible epistemology makes knowledge depend on some causal or otherwise appropriately non-accidental connection between knower and known — and it is hard to see how the two can both hold. The proof assistant's position seems, on its face, no better than the human's: it never touches numbers or sets either. It checks finite strings of symbols against formal rules, and whatever warrant its certificates carry traces back to the reliability of the hardware, the kernel's code, and the design choices behind the formal system. So my questions. Does treating the machine's certificate as establishing a theorem commit mathematicians to a formalist picture — on which what is known is a derivation, a syntactic fact, rather than a truth about structures — thereby quietly resolving Benacerraf's dilemma by giving up the semantics? Or can a platonist tell a coherent story on which formal verification is a genuine route to knowledge of abstract objects, and if so, what carries the epistemic weight in that story? Relatedly, does the practice merely relocate the dilemma rather than answer it — since human provers were never in causal contact with abstracta either — or does it change the dilemma's shape, e.g., by replacing Benacerraf's demand for a causal link with a demand for reliability that a syntactic process could in principle meet? Finally, is the implicit epistemology consistent with how mathematicians talk about these results, as discoveries about condensed vector spaces or sphere packings rather than facts about derivations? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is underdetermined. And if you think I have the dilemma wrong, or that machine verification raises no distinct epistemological issue beyond the one ordinary mathematical proof already poses, say so plainly rather than working within a flawed framing.
