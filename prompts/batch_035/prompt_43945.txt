I'm the director of a mid-sized nonprofit with about thirty staff and a big volunteer corps, and I've somehow become the person everyone brings interpersonal complaints to. A lot of what I do, looking back on the last year, is basically deciding stance: when a colleague blows up in a meeting, or flakes on a commitment for the third time, or says something cutting to a volunteer, do I treat that as something to *respond to* -- resentment, a hard conversation, an expectation of apology -- or as something to *manage* -- an accommodation, a workload adjustment, a referral to our employee assistance program? I took a philosophy course years ago where we read Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," and the distinction between the participant stance and the objective attitude has been rattling around in my head ever since, because it seems like it's exactly the fork I'm standing at every week, and Strawson (as I remember him) thinks the human commitment to the participant stance runs deep and we only adopt the objective attitude in special cases -- but "special cases" is doing a lot of work there and nobody's told me how to identify them on a Tuesday afternoon.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run, and that my four program managers can run after one training session, since they get these situations more often than I do. Please build it out of whatever the actual theory supports: something like questions asked in a fixed order -- is there an intervening causal story that removes the person from the picture at all, is this the kind of thing a person *does* rather than something that *happens to* them, is the quality of will behind the act one the person can own -- with a rough account of what a sufficient answer to each question looks like in workplace terms, since my managers will be dealing with depression diagnoses, burnout claims, neurodivergence disclosures, chronic stress, and plain ordinary selfishness, often mixed together in one person. The procedure has to say where it terminates in "treat as responsible and respond emotionally/personally" versus "shift to the objective attitude -- manage, treat, accommodate, don't blame," and it has to flag the points where it can't decide and a human has to weigh values, because I don't want my managers pretending the checklist made a judgment call for them.

Then stress-test it on three cases from my actual inbox this year, more or less: (1) a program officer with recurring depression who has now missed three grant deadlines, apologizes each time with what feels like total sincerity, and the pattern continues; (2) a longtime volunteer coordinator who was verbally cruel to a new volunteer and afterwards produced, unprompted, a detailed account of severe burnout and a sick parent at home; and (3) a development director who's excellent, knows it, and deploys cutting sarcasm in exactly the meetings where she wants someone else's proposal to die. I want the procedure to actually give an answer in each case even where I won't like it -- especially if it tells me to keep treating the depressed program officer as a responsible participant rather than a case to be managed, because my instinct is to soften there and I suspect Strawson of having something to say about why that softening might itself be a way of excluding someone from the community of mutually accountable adults. Also tell me honestly where this whole procedure goes wrong -- what the reactive-attitudes framework just doesn't cover (repeat offenders who game it, power imbalances between blamer and blamed, cultural differences in what counts as hurtful), and where using a Strawsonian lens might make things worse than an ordinary HR framework would.
