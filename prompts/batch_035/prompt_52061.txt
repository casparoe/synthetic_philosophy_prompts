I run the defaults review at a mid-sized company that administers benefits and retirement plans for other employers — so I sign off on things like enrollment defaults, pre-checked boxes, the order options appear in, the wording of prompts. We ship changes maybe six times a quarter and each one touches a few hundred thousand peoples' money, so the stakes are real, but the actual decision happens in a one-hour call with me, a lawyer, and a designer, and none of us has any philosophy training. Right now the defacto rule is "don't do anything that would look bad in a screenshot," which I hate.

I want an actual decision procedure the team can run on a proposal during that call. Ground it in what the paternalism/nudging literature actually cares about — the difference between steering people toward their own ends vs. toward ends we picked for them, hard vs. soft paternalism, whether a nudge works through the person's deliberation or around it, transparency, how costly the opt-out is — but translated into plain questions a non-philosopher can answer in a few minutes each. For every question, say what counts as a good enough answer, where a weak answer means "no" or "send it back for redesign," and, crucially, where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to a human because the question is one of values that a checklist can't settle. It should also include a step I've been muddling through on my own: a "would they thank us later" test, where you imagine the person finding out a year later exactly how they were steered — and be honest about where that test misfires, since people thank you for stuff that was bad for them all the time.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish cases from our backlog: (1) auto-enrolling new employees into the 401k at 6% with a one-click opt-out; (2) pre-checking a box that adds a paid legal-services perk onto the enrollment form, where unchecking it requires calling a phone number; (3) defaulting everyone's cafeteria-plan contributions into the employer's own fund option instead of a broad index fund, because the employer gets a share of the fees — the wrinkle being that the employer's fund has actually performed slightly better over the last three years. I expect the procedure to come back with a no on that last one and I want to see whether it does, because that's the case where our sales team will push back hardest, and also the case where I genuinely waver.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: the kinds of cases it will get wrong, and where it just launders our value judgments into official-sounding steps. When you're done, say how confident you are in each part of what you built, and single out the piece you're least sure of.
