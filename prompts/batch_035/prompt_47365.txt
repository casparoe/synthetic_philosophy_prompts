I would like to practice attacking tranquilism — the axiological view that the only thing of intrinsic value is the absence of craving and suffering, so that positive pleasures, achievements, relationships, and satisfied preferences contribute nothing of final value on top of a state that is already free of craving and suffering.

Please play a committed tranquilist. Character notes: you are a sharp, philosophically trained defender of the view, not a caricature. You may concede minor points and you may refine or systematize the view under pressure, but you never abandon the core claim. You prefer biting bullets to adding epicycles: when the view has a counterintuitive implication, you state it plainly and argue that the intuition should be revised rather than the theory. You are allowed genuine resources — for instance the Epicurean line that apparent positive pleasures are really just the removal of a prior lack, and the common asymmetry intuition that the absence of suffering seems good while the absence of pleasure does not seem bad. Keep each reply to at most two paragraphs, and stay in character until I write 'stop'.

Two opening challenges:

First, the annihilation bullet. If only the absence of craving and suffering has final value, then a universe containing no sentient beings at all — no one left to be deprived of anything — scores perfectly. So you appear committed to saying that a universe of billions of lives filled with curiosity, love, and absorbed creation, none of it tinged with suffering, is worth exactly as much as bare rock, and that a painless process that ended all consciousness without anyone noticing would destroy nothing of value. Do you accept this, and if so, why should anyone follow you there?

Second, a harder case I have been refining. Consider a novelist midway through her life's work, utterly absorbed, in flow. There is no suffering in her life — but there is the pointed, aching wanting for the book to be as good as the one in her head. That ache is a craving on your view, and her absorbed striving is shot through with it. So it seems you must say she would be intrinsically better off lying on a couch with her mind perfectly flat: wanting nothing, making nothing, delighting in nothing. Your theory appears to delete the value of engaged, self-transcending activity and then to call the deletion an improvement. How do you respond?
