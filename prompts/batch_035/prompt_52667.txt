ok so some background: about a year ago i quit my job to bet on sports full time. model-driven stuff, MLB totals mostly, some college hoops. first six months were brutal but i'm actualy making money now. not rich, but the bankroll grows and i pay rent with it, which freaks my mom out more than anything else in my life.

here's what's bugging me. when i started, two guys i trust gave me advice that i now realize basically contradicts, and when i went looking for the answer i found out the academic side of this has been stuck in the same argument for like 50 years and i genuinely can't tell if it's a real fight or everyone talking past each other.

advice #1, old-school EV guy: you have a 3% edge at -110, so bet whatever maximizes expected value of your bankroll this week and don't be a coward. he says all this "risk management" stuff is just people smuggling in a weird utility function and pretending the math forced them to bet small.

advice #2, quant friend: never bet more than a fraction of full Kelly — quarter or half Kelly. when i asked why Kelly at all instead of plain EV, he said something like "your bankroll evolves multiplicatively, so what matters is your time-average growth rate, not the average across parallel universes," then mentioned some physicist named Ole Peters and ergodicity, and that was the end of my understanding. he also swears Samuelson spent years going after Kelly-style betting, including a paper written entirely in one-syllable words, which i could not tell if he was making up. apparently he wasn't.

so the two camps, as best i can reconstruct them:

- the orthodox camp: rational choice is expected utility maximization, full stop. if your utility happens to be log of wealth, congrats, you get Kelly as a special case. but that's a preference, not a theorem, and it's fishy to impose log utility on everyone. the ergodicity people are just re-deriving Bernoulli with more physics words.

- the ergodicity camp: stop talking about utility altogether. a single gambler doesn't get to live the ensemble average; they live one trajectory. when wealth compounds multiplicatively, time averages and ensemble averages pull apart, and maximizing time-average growth is what "growing your bankroll" even means. the log isn't a psychological fact about me, it's the answer to a dynamics question about how my particular process evolves.

and this fight has been running since kelly's paper in the 50s, with the samuelson-era exchanges and now a whole ergodicity-economics revival, and from what i can tell nobody switches sides. every few years someone publishes a "this finally settles it" paper and the other camp writes a reply and we're all still here. meanwhile i have bets to place on tuesday.

what i want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who won. specifically:

1. is this a verbal dispute about the word "rational" — the utility camp defining it over one-shot choices, the ergodicity camp defining it over trajectories, and nobody noticing they're using the same word for different things?

2. is there a hidden factual question doing the work — like, is the real fight about whether the decisions in question are actually multiplicative (my bankroll, week over week) versus additive (a lab subject paid in cash at the end of one session), and if everyone just sorted the cases by that, the fight would dissolve?

3. or is it a substantive fight about what "ought" means for an agent who can't average over copies of herself — the utility camp saying preferences are bedrock and can't be mistakes, the ergodicity camp saying some preferences are just wrong in a way you can demonstrate from the dynamics?

and the part i care about most: given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move this thing — a distinction, a result, some specific kind of evidence — or is it stable precisely because each side is answering a question the other thinks isn't worth asking? and if it's the last one, tell me which question i, a guy whose entire income compounds multiplicativly every single week, should actually be trying to answer.
