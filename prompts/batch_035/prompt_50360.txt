I'm a retired physicist -- spent most of my career teaching statistical mechanics and a bit of quantum, so I've thrown probabilities around for decades without ever really asking what they ARE. Now that I have time on my hands I've been reading philosophy and I've gotten genuinely stuck on the fact that "the probability is 1/2" seems to mean completely different things depending on who's saying it. Next month I'm leading a session of the philosophy reading group at our local lifelong learning institute (retired folks, smart but mostly no math beyond college, and I've got 45 minutes and a whiteboard) on the interpretations of probability. Please don't explain the interpretations to me -- I have a decent handle on frequentism, subjectivism/Bayesian degrees of belief, and the propensity idea, though I'll admit propensities always struck me as somewhat hand-wavy when I was working. What I need is a plan for the explanation.

Here's what I want you to work out: the order the three interpretations should come in and why (I keep going back and forth on whether frequentism first is pedagogically natural or just traps people, since my crowd will arrive thinking probability just IS long-run frequency -- most of them were told that in school and I probably told students something similar myself, which is embarrassing in retrospect). Then the one concrete example that should carry the whole session: my instinct is a radioactive atom -- something like "what's the probability THIS particular atom of some isotope decays in the next minute" -- because it seems to pull the interpretations apart (there's no repeated trials for one atom, my old physicist brain wants to say there's a real physical disposition, and a bettor could still have a degree of belief about it). But tell me if that example has a flaw or if there's something better, and what the example must contain and what it should avoid. Also, the misconceptions this audience will form or bring -- I can already hear the guy who day-trades telling us probability doesn't exist outside human ignorance, and someone else conflating the gambler's fallacy with frequentism, and honestly I might conflate propensity with plain physical chance myself if pressed. Where in the sequence do I head each one off?

Two more things: what should I openly refuse to get into at this level (I'm guessing measure theory and the fine points of Dutch book arguments, but tell me if I'm wrong about where the cliff is), and what one question can I ask at the end that would tell me whether they've actually understood the distinction between the interpretations versus just heard three vocabulary words. I want them to leave able to notice which interpretation someone is sneaking in when they say a probability out loud, the way I only started noticing decades into my career.
