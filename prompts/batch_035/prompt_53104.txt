I'm on my university's faculty senate and we finally have to do something about grade inflation — the median grade is now an A-, and grad schools and employers have started discounting our transcripts wholesale. The concrete proposal on the table is a registrar-enforced ceiling: no department can give out more than 35% A-range grades. The economists on the senate hate it. Their line: no department has any incentive to deflate unilaterally, since that just handicaps our own students relative to everyone else's, classic prisoner's dilemma, and the cap "works" precisely by changing the payoffs rather than asking anyone to be a saint — i.e., it concedes that individual rationality points the wrong way.

But one of the philosophers keeps pushing Hofstadter's superrationality line: if agents in symmetrical positions reason identically, then each professor's choice to grade honestly is evidence about what all of them choose, so honest grading can be rational even without enforcement — and she thinks passing a coercive cap signals that we don't trust each other and crowds out exactly the disposition we'd need for the pledge to work. She wants us to skip the cap and instead adopt a non-binding departmental pledge to return to honest grading, and let symmetry do the work. We vote at the next meeting.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate beforehand. It should recommend a concrete course of action — the cap, the pledge, the cap with a sunset clause that hands things back to the pledge once norms reset, or whatever you actually think is best — and it should engage the superrationality argument seriously rather than waving it off as magical thinking, while also giving the economists' strongest point it's due. If you think the two sides are really talking past each other about what "rational" means, say that, but the senate still needs a bottom line it can act on.
