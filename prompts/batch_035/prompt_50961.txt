I'm a PhD student writing my dissertation chapter on the ethics of belief, and I've hit a wall with the James/Clifford thing that I'd love your help diagnosing. You should act as a professional philosopher who specializes in epistemology and American pragmatism, and please actually think hard before answering — maybe do a quick brainstorm of hypotheses first, then commit to one.

Here's the situation. Our department's reading group has been going at the Clifford versus James debate for two semesters now, and it's completely frozen in a way I find really frustrating. Quick recap so we're on the same page: Clifford says it's always and everywhere wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence, because beliefs guide action and we never have the right to risk other people's welfare on shaky grounds -- his famous example is the shipowner who talks himself into believing his worn-out ship is seaworthy and drowns the passengers. James replies that evidence itself runs out in certain cases, namely what he calls forced, living, and momentous options, and that when evidence can't decide, our passional nature legitimately may. So far so standard.

Now here's what's bugging me. Every argument in circulation seems to be a variation on maybe four moves. (1) The "passionate belief is self-fulfilling" point, where believing you can climb the mountain helps you climb it, so the belief kind of creates its own evidence -- Cliffordians dismiss this as smuggling in consequences of belief rather than truth-grounds for belief, and Jamesians say that's just begging the question against pragmatism. (2) The deontology/consequentialism proxy reading, where Clifford is treating belief formation as a duty-governed act and James as weighing epistemic costs against practical benefits, and neither side thinks the other's framework is even the right shape. (3) The doxastic voluntarism issue -- some of us suspect the whole fight presupposes contradictory background pictures of whether we can choose beliefs at all, and if voluntarism is false then Clifford's blame talk is confused and James's permission talk is confused in the mirror-image way. (4) The "two different questions" move, that Clifford is talking about the ethics of acting on belief in a public trust and James about the existential predicament of an individual at a genuine option, so they're not even disagreeing. Nobody in the room accepts that last one fully, but nobody can refute it either, and I've noticed that which move people find devastating tracks almost perfectly whether they did their MA in analytic epistemology or in intellectual history, which makes me suspicious that something tribal is going on instead of argument.

My working hypothesis, for what it's worth: the disagreement is substantive but only on a hidden second-order question -- roughly, what is a norm of belief even FOR, truth-tracking or action-guidance -- and all the first-order cases we argue about (shipowner, mountain climber, religious faith, believing your spouse is faithful) are just venues where that deeper split shows through, which is why arguing the cases never moves anyone. But I can also see it being mostly verbal, with 'evidence' meaning different things to the two camps: Clifford means something like public, shareable, proportional support, and James counts factitively inaccessible stuff like felt liveliness of an option, so they could both be right under their own definitions and nobody has noticed because both keep using the word 'evidence' unqualified.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who won. First, think through what kind of disagreement this actually is -- verbal, framework-level, about which cases count as data, about bedrock intuitions, about the scope of the question itself, or some mix. Use what each side actually claims, not a strawman. Second, tell me whether my hidden second-order question hypothesis survives contact with the details, and where it's weakest. Third, and most important: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A sharper distinction, a new class of cases, an experimental result from psychology on voluntarism, a reformulation of the question, whatever -- or an argument that this fight is irresolvable in principle and both camps should just carve up the territory and go home. I honestly think the "carve up the territory" option is underexplored and I'd like your honest take on whether that's defeatist or correct. Also feel free to suggest a reframing neither of us has mentioned if you see one.
