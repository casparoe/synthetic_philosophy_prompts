I serve on a working group drafting standards for how forensic experts may phrase their courtroom conclusions, and the philosophical debate over the norm of assertion keeps intruding on it. Some members argue an expert should only assert what she knows, citing the knowledge norm of assertion; others reply that calibrated probabilistic conclusions -- of the form it is highly likely, or extremely probable, that the defendant was present -- are perfectly legitimate assertions, so long as they are warranted at the stated level. I want to know which open questions in this debate genuinely matter for our practice and which are internal to the dialectic, with no bearing on it. Rank the following, and any others you think belong: whether lottery and Moorean cases show that a high justified credence is still insufficient for permissible assertion; whether there is a constitutive norm of assertion at all, as opposed to norms attaching to the social practice of testifying; how the norm of assertion connects to the norms of practical reasoning governing those who act on the assertion; and whether the standard for permissible assertion shifts between ordinary conversation, scientific reporting, and testimony under oath. For each, explain what makes it matter or not: whether our recommendations presuppose an answer to it, whether it is tractable with the tools of epistemology rather than empirical studies of juries, and what evidence -- from pilot studies, case law, or elsewhere -- would move it up or down the list. I want a defended ordering, not a survey of positions.
