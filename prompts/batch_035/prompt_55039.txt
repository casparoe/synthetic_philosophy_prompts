I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about asylum, and I want to be better prepared before we have it again. A single sentence kept doing all the work in his case: "refugees should claim asylum in the first safe country they reach." He deployed it in two different ways at once — to say that people who travel on through several countries before applying are behaving badly, and to say that our state is therefore entitled to refuse them a hearing or send them back. I suspect the sentence is several sentences wearing one, and I would like to see them separated before conceding or contesting anything.

Please lay out, as a numbered list I can bring to the next argument, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be expressing. At minimum I want you to disentangle: whose "should" it concerns (a requirement on refugees themselves, a permission granted to states, or an ideal of how the burden should be shared); what normative force that "should" carries (a claim about what international law actually requires, a moral claim about how obligations to refugees are distributed among states, or a prudential claim about which arrangement produces the best outcomes); what "safe" is tracking (freedom from the specific persecution one fled, general physical security, or something broader like economic prospects); and what "first" means (the first territory crossed, the nearest country by distance, or the first country with a functioning asylum system). Consider also whether the sentence might be intended merely descriptively, as a sociological observation about where refugees in fact stop, rather than normatively at all.

For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false, and — this is the part I most need — whether it would actually support either of the uses my friend put it to: condemning those who travel onward, or entitling our state to decline to consider their claims. It is fine with me if the honest verdict is that a given reading supports one use but not the other, or neither; I would rather see the mismatch made explicit than have the slogan's plausibility carry over from one use to the other. Please keep the list tight enough that I can hold it in mind mid-conversation.
