I'm a high school senior in the IB diploma programme, and I'm writing my Theory of Knowledge extended essay about something that happened while drafting my chemistry internal assessment. My report makes roughly thirty separate factual claims. For each individual claim, I checked the calculation or measurement, and I'd bet heavily that it's true. But my supervisor pointed out that across thirty claims, the chance that at least one is wrong is substantial — and looking back at every lab report I've ever written, each one did contain at least one error somewhere. So it seems I rationally believe each of the thirty claims, and I also rationally believe that the report, taken as a whole, contains at least one false claim. If believing each of two claims commits me to believing their conjunction — as my TOK textbook implies — then my beliefs are inconsistent. I've since found out this is close to what philosophers call the preface paradox, but I don't want my essay to be a summary of it. I want to defend a specific thesis: that the right lesson of the paradox is that rational belief is not closed under conjunction. On this view, my thirty first-order beliefs can each be rational, my belief that the report contains an error can be rational, and the only irrational thing available to me is believing the bare conjunction of all thirty claims. And crucially, I want to argue that this concession is much smaller than it sounds, because rejecting closure under conjunction says nothing about whether belief is closed under logical entailment from a single premise — and the two are constantly run together in class discussions.

Please write an argument for this position in a formal register suitable for submission as an essay draft. Structure it in four named sections, in this order: "The setup" (about 150 words — state the paradox and my thesis plainly, without a survey of names or history); "The argument" (about 500 words — make the case for the conjunction-closure failure, explaining why the thirty first-order beliefs remain rational while a single belief in their conjunction would not be, and why the failure is specifically about aggregation and not about entailment); "The strongest objection" (about 250 words — give the best version of the worry that once conjunction-closure goes, I lose any right to trust conjunctions of my ordinary beliefs anywhere, including outside lab reports, and then answer it); and "What follows" (about 150 words — say what this view would change about how my TOK class should handle the phrase "I know that" when it is applied to a long document rather than a single sentence).
