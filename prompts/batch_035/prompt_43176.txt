so, i write historical fiction, and lately it's been giving me a quiet crisis about my day job. when i plot a novel, the facts are slaves to the shape i've already chosen -- i know on page one it's a tragedy, and everything gets arranged so. what spooked me is reading that some historians think real history writing works the same way. the ranke crowd used to claim history could just show things as they actually happened, no spin, and carr and hayden white basically laughed at that -- carr saying the historian decides which facts even count as facts, white saying historians smuggle in a plot shape like a novelist would, so the same run of events can be emplotted as a tragedy, a comedy, a romance, a satire.

i want an essay on who's right here, and i want it to actually pick a side -- no "both perspectives have merit" cop-out at the end. and don't give me the standard textbook rundown of the three guys, i've read that a hundred times. here's the angle i care about: i've noticed that the same index cards of events (she gets exiled, the treaty fails, he dies thinking it worked out) can be assembled into a tragedy, a satire, or an earnest everything-was-progress story, and nothing written on the cards themselves settles which. call it the plot being underdetermined by the facts. so my question is whether there's any objectivity left for historians once you grant that -- or whether objectivity has to be rebuilt somewhere else, not out of the facts but out of the discipline (footnotes, peer review, being forced to mention what you'd rather leave out, whatever else you can come up with). weigh that against the carr/white stuff and reach a verdict.

and if you end up siding with the sceptics, tell me straight: is there any sense in which the historical afterword i'm writing for my novel could be more true than the novel itself, or is that just a mood im in? keep it to something i can read in one sitting, im not a philosopher, dont assume i know the jargon
