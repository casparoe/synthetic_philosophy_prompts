I'm the deputy registrar at a mid-sized university, and by accident of the org chart I now chair the committee that decides hardship grants — emergency money for students: rent arrears, a stolen laptop, sudden illness, a parent losing their job mid-term. The pot is about £60k a year, we get roughly 130 applications, and we can fund maybe half of them, so every yes is a no to someone else. Applications come in waves (start of term and after Christmas are the worst), the two of us who do first-pass triage get maybe 15 minutes per case, and anything over £800 needs the bursar to sign off, so the procedure has to produce a paper trail he can skim in a minute.

Right now the policy is basically vibes, and it shows. A colleague in philosophy said we should think about luck egalitarianism — her gloss was something like: inequality is unjust when it traces back to circumstances the person didn't choose, but not when it traces to their choices, so you help the student blindsided by bad luck and not the one who made their own mess. That sounds like it could be an actual procedure rather than an argument, which is what I need. So please write me one.

Concretely I want: the questions to ask each application in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which point the procedure returns a no, and where it hands off to the committee or the bursar because the question is one of values, not facts. The hard parts I can already see, and I want the procedure to face them rather than wave at them:

- How do we tell a choice from a circumstance? A student who spent their maintenance loan on a summer holiday is one thing; a student who took extra shifts and then flunked a resit is another; a student who declined a job to care for a sick parent is another again. Does the theory actually rank these, and can a 15-minute process rank them reliably?
- What about choices made on bad information, or with no realistic alternative — kids from families where nobody has ever had savings budget differently, and I'm not sure punishing that is what the theory wants.
- Is there a principled line between "you chose the risk" and "the downside was wildly disproportionate to the choice"? The holiday case probably shouldn't decide whether someone is homeless in June, but I can't articulate why.

Then run it on three cases and show its work: (1) a finalist whose laptop was stolen from the library the week before exams, no insurance, needs £450 for a replacement; (2) a second-year who spent her loan instalment partly on a holiday in August and now can't cover rent, landlord issuing warnings, about £1,100; (3) a postgrad whose parent's business failed and can no longer top up his rent, £600 a term ongoing, not a one-off. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'm honestly inclined to be generous on the holiday one if the alternative is eviction — and tell me straight whether the theory forces the harsh answer or whether there's a principled route to softening it (I've heard there's a whole feminist/relational critique saying luck egalitarianism is heartless to exactly these people, and if that critique bites here I want to know where the procedure should give way to it rather than pretend it doesn't exist).

Also be honest about what the procedure can't do at all: where it will misfire, what kinds of applications it shouldn't even try to score, and where I should just admit triage is triage. Please write the whole thing in British English.
