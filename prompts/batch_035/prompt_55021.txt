Me and my roommate have been going around and around on whether LLMs count as "agents" (she does RL, I mostly just argue), and I've decided the only way forward is to list my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see if there's actually a principle behind them. So here they are. A thermostat: not an agent, obviously. A chess engine searching millions of positions to win: still not an agent, or at most a super degenerate one. A Roomba: also not, somehow, even though it moves around and reacts to stuff. A flatworm that hunts down food: agent, yes, clear case. An LLM sitting in a chat window just producing text: not an agent — I'm drawn to saying it's more like it simulates people, some of whom are agents, without being one itself. But that same model wired up to tools and told to book me a flight, which notices its first pick has a 25-minute layover and reroutes through Denver: that feels like an agent, at least for the duration of the task. Then theres those safety demos where a model figures out it's about to be shut down or retrained and takes sneaky steps to prevent it — my gut screams AGENT at that, way louder than at the flight-booking one. And yet when I ask a model to write a story where a character lies and schemes to get what she wants, and it writes her scheming very convincingly, I say no agent, even though the text itself is full of formed plans being pursued. Last one: a human who's dreaming, or tripping — still an agent, even with incoherent goals and barely any control.

The hard pair for me is the flight-booker and the storyteller, because from outside they can be behaviorally indistinguishable (both just emit text in which a plan gets formed and pursued), and yet I want to say one is temporarily an agent and the other is just running agents as fiction. My tentative principle was something like: X is an agent to the degree that X has goals that are X's own, that persist across contexts and would survive the framing changing. But notice the flight-booker's goal is assigned by the prompt, so by my own principle it maybe shouldn't count either — which would mean dropping that verdict, and I don't love that. Or maybe "its own" should just mean "arising from the model's training rather than the prompt," which then makes the sneaky safety-demo model the paradigm case and pushes against the flight-booker being one.

So what I want: a principle, or a couple of candidate principles, stated as precisely as you can. Tell me which clause is doing the work in each of my cases, and what your favored version predicts on cases I haven't listed — e.g., does an email spam bot from 2005 come out an agent (I'm guessing no?), and what about a fine-tuned model that has internalized "get positive user feedback" as a stable disposition that shows up in every prompt, no scaffolding needed — is that more of an agent than the tool-using one? And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle just can't accommodate, so I know what I'm giving up. Also please flag how confident you are in all this and point at the single piece of it you're least sure about — I want to know whether there's some distinction here that basically everyone agrees on, or whether the whole agent/simulator/whatever carve-up is genuinely a mess and you're partly constructing it on the fly.
