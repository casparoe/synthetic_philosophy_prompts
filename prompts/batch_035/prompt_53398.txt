I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics and empirics of representation, and this spring the unit on affirmative action kept circling back to Norway without anyone saying anything precise about it. In 2002 the Norwegian minister of trade and industry, Ansgar Gabrielsen, of the Conservative party and over the initial objections of the business establishment, proposed mandatory gender representation on the boards of public limited companies (the ASA companies); parliament ratified it in 2003, compliance was enforced from 2008 with the sanction that a non-compliant board's elections could be declared invalid and, in principle, the company dissolved, and female representation, which had hovered around six or seven percent, rose to roughly parity — most boards now sit at or above the statutory forty percent. Two features of the aftermath interest me. First, the "golden skirts" phenomenon: a relatively small number of experienced women accumulated multiple directorships, each holding several seats, because the supply of women with prior board experience was thin and boards wanted exactly that profile. Second, the pipeline of qualified female candidates visibly thickened over the following decade — a generation of Norwegian women accrued board credentials that the pre-2003 old boys' network had rationed.

I want my students to work this episode through Kanter's framework from Men and Women of the Corporation, her 1977 study of tokenism and group composition. As I understand her: in skewed groups, where one type holds a small token proportion (she put the line around fifteen percent), the tokens are hyper-visible, their differences are amplified, they are absorbed into the dominant group's stereotyped roles, and the dynamics of contrast and fear of retaliation distort how the majority treats them; somewhere past roughly thirty-five percent the group tips, the tokens become a genuine minority, "types" begin to turn into individuals, and the proportion itself does work that individual members cannot. The standard textbook gloss of the Norwegian case is that the quota was engineered critical mass: the law leapt over the token zone in one stroke, so Norway is Kanter's tipping point legislated.

My question, and the one I want to put to you as an analysis: how much of the Norwegian episode does Kanter's framework actually illuminate, and where does the mapping mislead?

Please do the disciplined thing and build the correspondence explicitly before drawing conclusions. What is the "group" here — the individual board, the Norwegian corporate elite as a whole, or the national pipeline of directorships? Who or what is the token — the individual woman on a given board, the "golden skirt" who sits on six of them, or women as a category in the elite? Note that the golden-skirt phenomenon sits awkwardly in the framework: a woman holding multiple seats is a token in every single boardroom yet simultaneously one of the most institutionally powerful individuals in the economy, and she is precisely a creature of the transitional supply constraint that the quota created. Does Kanter have the resources to say anything about her, or does her case show that the framework silently assumes one person, one group?

Then take the analysis in both directions. In one direction: what does Kanter predict should have happened, and does the Norwegian record bear it out — did the token-zone distortions (heightened scrutiny, stereotyped assignments, the women being made responsible for "soft" committee work) fade as the proportion tipped, or do we have reason to think the quota moved numbers without moving dynamics? Be careful here to distinguish what the framework predicts from what it merely licenses one to hope. In the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory? It would be convenient for my seminar if Norway were simply Kanter's tipping point legislated, but I suspect that is too tidy. Among other things, Kanter's account is causal and endogenous — proportions shift because groups recruit from networks and the composition feeds back into the group's culture — whereas the Norwegian intervention was exogenous and coercive: the state held the proportion fixed by law and let the culture adjust, with the plausible mechanism being that the quota flooded the credentialing system and manufactured the experience that the old networks had hoarded. If that is right, then perhaps the lesson is that the causal arrow Kanter draws — proportion drives culture — is real but slow enough that waiting for it entrenches the skew, which is a stronger argument for quotas than the critical-mass talk usually gives. Or perhaps the golden skirts show the opposite: that a rigid threshold converts a composition problem into a scarcity problem, at least in the short run.

I would also like you to be explicit about what Kanter's framework is silent on here. Her analysis is organizational sociology: it says nothing, as far as I can tell, about whether the state is entitled to restructure a private corporation's board, or about the fairness to the individually better-qualified man passed over, or about whether ASA boards — a small, partly voluntary, and at the time somewhat marginal corporate form in Norway (some firms reportedly re-incorporated as private companies, where the law did not reach) — are the right site for an equality intervention at all. Please do not force the framework to speak to those questions; instead, mark them as outside its scope, and say whether the fact that the Norwegian debate mostly proceeded in critical-mass terms — empirical-sociological vocabulary standing in for normative argument — is itself something the episode teaches.

Structure the answer as: the correspondence, stated carefully; what the framework gets right about Norway; where it misleads, with the golden skirts as the central anomaly; what the episode does to the theory, including the endogenous-versus-exogenous composition point; and a closing paragraph on what remains that Kanter cannot see.
