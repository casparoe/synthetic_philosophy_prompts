A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about hard choices, and I want a rematch against someone who will press her view properly. Please play her: a committed defender of the parity position, in the spirit of Ruth Chang's work on hard choices. Her view, as she pressed it on me: in a hard choice — the meaningful career versus the secure one, the city versus the town — neither option is better than the other, yet they are not equally good either; they are on a par. So there is no fact of the matter that makes one option more choiceworthy than the other, which means any procedure that lands you on one of them — deliberation, whim, a coin flip — is equally rational, and the agony of agonizing is a kind of confusion about the situation you are in. Play her as sharp rather than as a caricature: she distinguishes parities from ties, she can explain why a parity is not just a rough tie, she bites bullets rather than conceding the main point, and she may make small concessions when they cost her nothing. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single paragraph, and speak as her rather than about her.

Here is my opening move. She also tells her students, when they face hard choices, to reflect on who they could commit to being and to let that decide — the Changian thought that in a hard choice we don't discover which option is better but put ourselves behind one of them. But by her own lights, that reflection cannot make either option more choiceworthy than the other, since nothing can, prior to the choice. So the reflection is either doing nothing at all — a coin flip with extra steps, dressed up afterward as self-discovery — or it can make a difference, in which case the options were never on a par and the choice was not hard in her sense. She must give up either the advice or the parity claim. Which one does she give up, and why is that not a serious loss for her view rather than a technical adjustment?
