You are a formal epistemologist mentoring a graduate student who is drafting a prospectus for a one-year master's thesis, and she needs your help turning a half-formed idea into a focused research proposal of roughly 800 words.

Her topic is the Sleeping Beauty problem, and her suspicion is that the halving/thirding dispute is at least partly verbal. When Beauty says "my credence that the coin landed heads is one-half" versus "one-third," the two camps may be answering different questions: an uncentered question about the trial as a whole, versus a centered question about which waking she is currently in (a self-locating belief over centered worlds, in the Lewisian sense). She further suspects that the usual ways of operationalizing credence — fair betting price, scoring-rule accuracy, input to conditionalization — come apart in this setting: betting prices over repeated trials seem to support thirding, while accuracy-based considerations have been argued to support halving, and it is not obvious these are measures of the same underlying quantity.

Write the proposal. It should state the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the standard moves (Dutch book arguments, the repetition argument, the disagreement between halfer and thirder camps) fail to settle which operationalization of credence, if any, is the correct one here; and sketch an actual method. As part of the method, the student wants to develop and analyze a new variant case that she hopes will isolate the issue: a "pure self-locating" scenario in which Beauty knows with certainty everything about the external world, including that a coin landed heads, but is uncertain only about which day or which waking she is currently in. If rational credence over centered worlds has no determinate content in such a case, that would tell us something important about what "credence" means for self-locating beliefs generally. The proposal should say what would count as a successful outcome, what would count as the project failing (for instance, if the operationalizations turn out to be equivalent after all, or if the verbal-dispute diagnosis can't be made precise), and it should be honestly scoped to one year of part-time master's-level work. The student will add citations herself; do not include any.

Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm two or three ways the core question could be framed, pick the one you find most defensible, and briefly note in the proposal why you chose it over the alternatives. Think carefully about the technical details — the proposal will be read by philosophers of probability who will notice sloppy claims about betting odds or scoring rules.
