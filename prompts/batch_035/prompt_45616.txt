I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background beyond skimming some pop article about Locke while bored on a train, and something about how the law treats our industry has been bugging me for a while. Here's the setup as I understand it: copyright covers my actual source code as "expression" but explicitly NOT the ideas behind it — the algorithm, the data structures, the behavior of the program. Anyone is free to look at what my program does and write their own code that does the same thing, and courts have basically blessed "clean-room" reverse engineering, where one team documents the behavior of a program and a second team that never saw the original code reimplements it from scratch. So under copyright, ideas themselves are free for the taking. But then patents do almost the opposite: they protect the functional idea — the method, the algorithm — so that someone who implements it with totally different code, maybe in a different language on a different continent, still infringes. So the same "thing", say a clever way of caching data, seems to be free to take under the copyright framework and owned under the patent framework. And there's a third wrinkle: when Google reimplemented Oracle's Java APIs, the courts spent something like a decade arguing over whether an API is an "idea" (free) or "expression" (owned), which suggests nobody involved has a stable notion of where that line even is.

My question: what theory of what an idea IS does this whole arrangement presuppose? It sort of looks like the law assumes ideas are like abstract objects floating out there that can't be owned, while expressions are concrete artifacts produced by labor that can be — but that can't be the whole story, since patents let you own the abstract part too, and copyright in practice keeps creeping into "structure, sequence and organization" of code anyway. So is the system actually consistent under some single view of ideas and ownership, or is it stitching together incompatible views (ideas as unownable abstracta vs ideas as products of labor vs ideas as something more like a brand or reputation you can have rights in)? And where is it genuinely underdetermined — i.e. which parts strictly presuppose a philosophical view, versus parts where several rival views would all be happy to license the same rule? I discussed this with a friend who works in patent law and she just laughed and said the answer is "it's all politics," which I refuse to accept, or at least want to know what the politics is papering over.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of law and intellectual property. Think carefully and maybe brainstorm the candidate implicit theories first before commiting to one, and be explicit about which parts of the practice strictly require a given view and which are just compatible with it. Don't just name-drop famous philosophers at me — actually walk through the reasoning. Then, at the end, tell me concretely what would have to change in how software is legally protected if we took one of the rival views of "owning an idea" seriously — like, would patents on algorithms have to go, would clean-room reimplementation become infringement, that kind of thing.
