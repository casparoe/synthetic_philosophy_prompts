I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar and am writing the final exam. Please draft one essay question, with a model answer and a marking scheme, on whether a deterministic world is predictable in principle. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the slogan that determinism concerns the laws while predictability concerns us; it should instead force candidates to sort the obstacles to prediction into those that are merely practical and those that bite even for an ideal predictor, and to defend that sorting. Since I don't want the question to depend on outside reading, present the three obstacles inside the question itself, a couple of sentences each: (i) sensitive dependence on initial conditions, under which measurement error grows exponentially, so the prediction horizon grows only logarithmically with the precision of the initial measurement; (ii) the quantum limit, under which no measurement of a continuous state fixes it exactly — and note that any predictor physically embedded in the world, rather than standing outside it, is itself subject to that limit; and (iii) a self-reference argument in the spirit of Popper, that a predictor belonging to the system it predicts cannot fully predict its own future predictions, since a complete prediction would have to contain a prediction of itself, which raises a regress that the candidate should assess rather than merely report. For each obstacle, candidates must say whether it yields unpredictability in principle or only in practice, and must address what "possible in principle" should mean here — in particular whether a task may count as possible in principle when it requires unbounded, though at every stage finite, resources. Set the question at ninety minutes, out of 20 marks. The model answer should run about 600 words. The marking scheme should consist of five must-hit points with the marks for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong, and a closing statement of what separates an A from a B: a B answer somewhere conflates practical with in-principle unpredictability, while an A answer keeps them apart across all three obstacles and flags at least one point where the sorting itself is a judgment call rather than something the physics settles.
