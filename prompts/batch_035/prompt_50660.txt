I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've run into something at work that keeps reminding me of two things from the wider world that I can't reconcile. I'd like a careful analysis, and I'll try to state everything concretely so no background is assumed.

At work, we practice what you might call engineered precommitment. Before a major release, we freeze code changes even though everyone knows the freeze slows us down. Pushing to production requires sign-off from a second engineer, and the most destructive operations -- irreversible database migrations, permanent data deletion -- sit behind extra approval gates that we deliberately designed to be slow and annoying. Nobody forced this on us; we built the gates ourselves, precisely so that a future, pressured version of the team can't take the quick destructive path.

Outside of work, I've noticed the same structure in two places. First, psychiatric advance directives, sometimes called Ulysses contracts: a person with a recurring mental illness, while lucid, leaves binding instructions that clinicians should hospitalize or treat them during a future episode even if the person at that time refuses. Second, constitutional precommitment: constitutions entrench rights and make amendment deliberately difficult, so that a temporary majority cannot do things that the polity's earlier self ruled out. The mast-and-ropes image is the same in all three cases.

Here is what puzzles me. In nearly every other corner of law and everyday life, we treat consent as something that must be current and revocable. A patient can decline a procedure they asked for last month. A person can withdraw from an arrangement at the moment it's happening, and continuing becomes wrongful. Agreements that bind a person regardless of their later wishes -- say, a contract to stay in a marriage -- are treated as void and even offensive. Yet the practices I described all let the past self overrule the present self, and we think this is not only permissible but admirable foresight.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory. What view of autonomy, personal identity, or consent do these precommitment practices presuppose? Some candidate views I can half-formulate: that the lucid past self is the "real" or authentic one and the present self is compromised; that what matters is interests rather than momentary preferences, and the past self is simply better placed to judge them; that the self is extended over time and authority over it is distributed rather than held by whichever temporal slice is currently speaking. For each candidate, tell me whether the practices strictly require it or whether several of them would license the same institutional design. Then address the apparent inconsistency: is there a single principle about consent that covers both the revocability cases and the precommitment cases, or must something give? Finally, sketch what the practices would have to look like if we instead adopted the view that only present consent counts -- which gates, freezes, and directives would survive, and in what modified form.

One more thing, since it's my actual stake: my team has an informal design rule that reversibility beats preapproval -- we would rather build a kill switch than a lock. Tell me whether that rule reflects the same underlying commitments as the legal practices above or a genuinely different one, and if different, what the difference is.

Please write in American English and keep your answer under 800 words. Lead with your one-sentence statement of the implicit theory, then the analysis; a short list where a list genuinely helps is fine, but no sprawling enumerations.
