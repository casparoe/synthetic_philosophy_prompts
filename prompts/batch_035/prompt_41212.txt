Write a parody of a patient information leaflet — the folded sheet of paper found inside a packet of prescription tablets — for a fictional elective procedure: gradual neuron replacement, in which a patient's neurons are swapped, a few thousand at a time, for functionally identical synthetic substitutes over the course of several years. The tone throughout should be that of bland pharmaceutical reassurance: headings in authentic leaflet style ("What this treatment is", "Before you begin", "Possible complications", "Do not undergo this procedure if..."), numbered side-effects, a FAQ section, and the manufacturer's address in small print at the end.

The comedy should come from the metaphysics quietly doing load-bearing work in the small print rather than from zany invention. The leaflet's authors are serene functionalists who treat "will it still be me?" as a manageable adverse event, on a par with mild nausea. Among other things, the leaflet should contain: a clause disclaiming liability for any question of numerical identity arising from a procedure aborted at the halfway stage (the clinic regards the patient as continuous for clinical purposes only); a warning that running the biological and synthetic substrates in parallel during the migration period constitutes off-label branching, voids the continuity guarantee, and may result in "two outcomes, only one of which is covered"; a FAQ entry from a worried relative asking whether the person who walks out at the end is the person who walked in, answered with unhelpful administrative cheer; and a passing mention of "fading qualia" as a known but unlisted side-effect. The overall target is the characteristic smoothness of the gradual-replacement argument — the thought that because each step preserves function, and each step is minute, nothing of consequence can be lost across the whole — and the satire should exaggerate that smoothness rather than invent anything: a reader who knows the personal-identity literature (Parfit-style psychological continuity, the teletransporter, Chinders-style stepwise replacement) should recognise the argument being sent up and find the sending-up fair, while a reader who doesn't should still find the leaflet funny and intelligible on its own terms. Keep the irony deadpan — no winking at the reader, no moments where the manufacturer betrays awareness of the horror.

Approximately 700 words for the leaflet itself, in British English (spellings such as "recognise", "colour", "programme").

After the leaflet, append a short note of no more than 150 words doing two things: first, say where the piece leans on a contested reading — in particular, whether the complacent continuity guarantee it mocks is something the gradual-replacement argument actually needs, or whether a more careful gradualist could accept everything in the fine print without discomfort; and second, identify which single consideration would most plausibly defeat the argument the piece satirises, or where the piece's own reasoning is most likely to fail.
