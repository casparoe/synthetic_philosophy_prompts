I've held a luck egalitarian view of justice for some years now, roughly of the Dworkinian sort: inequalities are unjust when they trace back to brute luck, but not when they reflect choices people made against a background of equal opportunity — including "option luck," where people knowingly gambled. I'm drawn to the way the view treats responsibility as the pivot of distributive justice, and I find the hypothetical insurance mechanism an elegant device for converting brute luck into something individuals would have chosen to insure against. What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure rather than a defense: what would actually count against it?

I don't just mean the standard objections rehearsed mechanically. I want you to sort defeaters by how decisive they'd be — from fatal, through serious, to merely awkward — and to distinguish what committed luck egalitarians themselves concede counts against the view from what only outsiders press. For instance: does the harshness problem (people who chose badly ending up destitute, or uninsurable catastrophes that the insurance fiction papers over) refute the view or just force a patch, and is the patch ad hoc? Do cases of adaptive or manipulatively formed preferences undermine the brute-luck/choice luck distinction that the whole view rests on? What about people whose choices are causally traceable to circumstances they didn't choose — addiction, impoverished childhoods, cultural rigging of what counts as a choice — and does conceding ground there collapse the view into something its rivals can already deliver? I'd also like to know whether any of the view's defenders have openly abandoned it or substantially qualified it, and whether those qualifications have quietly insulated the thesis so that no possible case could count against it any more — and, if so, whether that unfalsifiability is a fatal flaw for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of a normative theory.

Where you can, tell me what the view predicts or requires that its rivals (sufficientarianism, relational or democratic equality, Rawlsian views that ignore responsibility) do not, so I can see what observations or cases would adjudicate between them rather than just embarrass everyone equally. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — specifically, whether the view is genuinely at risk or mostly well insulated — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
