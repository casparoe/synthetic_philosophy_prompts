Construct a pair of thought experiments in the tradition of lifeboat ethics — Garrett Hardin's metaphor of the well-off as occupants of a lifeboat surrounded by swimmers — engineered to separate two components of that argument that are almost always bundled together: the capacity constraint (the boat can only hold so many, so pulling swimmers aboard endangers everyone) and the boundary claim (the seats already belong to those sitting in them, so the swimmers have no title to a place even when one is free). The design brief is this: in the first scenario, the standard exclusionary conclusion — keep the swimmers out — must survive despite there being spare capacity, so that the exclusion visibly cannot rest on triage arithmetic and must rest entirely on the boundary; in the second, capacity must be genuinely tight while the occupants hold no prior claim to their places, so that exclusion rests entirely on triage. The setting should be realistic and institutional rather than maritime — a housing allocation scheme, a hospital ward, a refugee admissions quota — with stakes that can be grave but a mechanism that is ordinary; no literal boats, and please avoid trolley-style contrivances. Each scenario should be a single paragraph.

After the two scenarios, do the diagnostic work. Walk through what each of three positions actually commits to in each case: a Hardin-style position, a straightforward act utilitarian, and an egalitarian who denies that prior occupancy generates anything like a property claim. Then tell me honestly which feature of each scenario is doing the work, what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed (fill the spare seats in the first case; grant the occupants tenure in the second), and what the strongest available line of attack is for a Hardin defender faced with the second scenario — for instance, arguing that without stable boundaries the notion of capacity itself cannot be fixed, since what counts as "full" depends on who is counted as entitled to room. The purpose of the exercise is diagnostic rather than persuasive: a reader should finish your response able to say, of real-world lifeboat-ethics arguments, which of the two components carries the persuasive load — the arithmetic of capacity or the morality of the boundary — and to recognize when an argument that looks like arithmetic is secretly a claim about ownership.

Write the response as continuous prose only, around 800 to 1000 words, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
