I spent thirty-five years as an experimental particle physicist, and since retiring I have finally been reading the philosophy of science I had no time for while running a research group — first Popper and Kuhn, more recently the current literature on theory confirmation. This reading has become unexpectedly practical: I have agreed to serve on the review panel for our national funding agency's theoretical physics program, which convenes twice a year for a week to rank several dozen proposals, roughly forty percent of which receive funding, with every score requiring a written justification that can be formally appealed.

The panel's difficulty is that an increasing share of the proposals we see concern frameworks — string theory, various approaches to quantum gravity, anthropic and multiverse reasoning in cosmology — for which no experiment in any foreseeable funding horizon can provide a direct test. Our scoring criteria say "scientific merit" and "feasibility," but they are silent on what merit amounts to when empirical confrontation is off the table, and our disagreements track the philosophical literature with almost embarrassing fidelity. Some panelists lean on considerations a philosopher of physics, Richard Dawid, has grouped under "non-empirical theory confirmation": the observation that decades of effort have produced no alternative with comparable internal consistency (the so-called no-alternatives argument), the surprising explanatory coherence of the framework's different sectors, and the historical track record of mathematically over-constrained theories that eventually succeeded. Others reply that these are aesthetics laundered as evidence, that the pool of past theories is small and likely biased toward the survivors, and that a theory's beauty has repeatedly failed as a guide in the history of physics.

I would like you to write a decision procedure the panel could actually run on each proposal in the roughly half hour we have for it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure issues a stop with a "do not fund," and where it must hand the decision back to the panel because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. In designing it, please keep three things distinct that our current criteria blur together: our confidence that the framework is true, our confidence that the proposed work would be productive even if the framework is false (the mathematics alone might justify it), and our confidence that the research program deserves public money at a given level. Mark honestly which steps smuggle in taste disguised as evidence, and say where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three proposals: (1) a string-phenomenology project computing observable-independent consistency relations across a class of string vacua, justified almost entirely by the no-alternatives consideration; (2) a project refining a measure over the multiverse to sharpen the anthropic explanation of the cosmological constant; and (3) a small-scale effort to derive a low-probability but genuinely testable signature of a quantum gravity model, which the procedure must weigh against the richer but untestable first proposal. Note that I am myself fairly sympathetic to non-empirical confirmation, so tell me plainly if the procedure, applied honestly, gives any answer I will not like.
