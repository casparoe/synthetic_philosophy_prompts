I've been a science journalist for about a decade — biomedicine mostly, some climate, and lately a lot of bioethics pieces (gene editing, AI in hospitals, end-of-life stuff). My whole job runs on borrowed authority: I can't run my own replication studies or reanalyze the trial data, so I defer to expert consensus, I quote people on the record, and I try to be honest about the difference between "I verified this" and "forty oncologists say so." Anyway, a philosopher friend was telling me about the debate on "moral testimony" — whether it's OK to form moral beliefs on someone else's say-so the way I form empirical ones, or whether moral beliefs you got secondhand are somehow second-class (there's apparently a whole literature on why you should be able to defend your own moral convictions yourself, and on whether "trusting" a moral claim is different from just believing it). It hit me that this is basically my professional life in a different register, but I can't tell if the analogy is deep or lazy.

So here's the ask: restate the main positions in that debate — the pessimists who think moral beliefs from testimony are defective, the optimists who don't, whatever the "understanding vs. belief" distinction is (my friend mentioned someone arguing you need to *grasp* why something's wrong, not just believe it), and the pushback from people who say pessimism ends up silencing folks who testify about discrimination or harassment they experienced — in the vocabulary I actually use. Sourcing, on record vs. background, deferring to consensus when you can't do the reporting yourself, beat expertise, the "show your work" norm, false balance / "he said she said" both-sidesing, corrections. Map each position as faithfully as you can, and don't just slap my words on their views if the fit is bad — say when you're distorting something to make it fit.

Then I want the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about: what in the philosophical debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Like, when philosophers say a moral conviction should be "yours" in a way a belief about drug efficacy doesn't have to be — is that anything like the difference between a story I reported myself and one built off a press release, or is that analogy doing work it can't do? And conversely, is "both-sidesing" a moral question (which we get yelled at about constantly, e.g. on abortion coverage) exactly the mistake the moral testimony optimists would make, or is false balance an empirical screwup that just *happens* to show up in moral stories? Basically: is there something the philosophers are arguing about that me and my editor have no concept for, and if so, is that a gap in journalism's vocabulary or a sign the philosophical worry is less substantive than it looks? Casual tone is fine, I don't need the names of every paper, I need to know if I've been doing my job on a broken theory of it.
