I'm writing a short essay for a philosophy of mathematics seminar on the question of whether we can rule out that ordinary arithmetic — say, Peano arithmetic (PA) — is inconsistent. My thesis is a kind of modest fallibilism: our confidence in PA's consistency is epistemically shakier than mathematicians usually admit, and it is rational, if perhaps eccentric, to devote some effort to hunting for a contradiction in elementary arithmetic. I've pasted my current draft below. I'd be grateful for comments on whether the overall argument hangs together, and especially on whether Section 2 commits a real fallacy or only an apparent one, since that's the part I'm least confident in. I'd also like to know what the strongest objection to my conclusion is that I haven't addressed, so that I can add a paragraph responding to it.

The draft:

1. Everyone treats PA as obviously consistent, but the reasons available are less clean than we pretend. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem tells us that if PA is consistent, then PA cannot prove its own consistency. So any proof we could give of PA's consistency must use methods that go beyond PA in some way, and the regress has to bottom out somewhere that is not itself proven consistent.

2. In fact, the regress bottoms out somewhere worse than where it started. Gentzen's celebrated consistency proof for PA depends on transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀, which is in an intuitive sense a stronger assumption than PA itself. So we have not so much proven arithmetic consistent as traded trust in one system for trust in a stronger one. It's like a man who calms his fear of a small loan by noting it's backed by a bigger loan.

3. Strip away the proof theory and what remains is induction in the humble, empirical sense: a century and a half of intense use has turned up no contradiction, so probably there is none. But if the shortest PA-proof of 0 = 1 were, say, longer than the number of particles in the universe, the empirical record would look exactly as it does now. The absence of a found contradiction is evidence, but evidence whose force depends entirely on facts about proof length that we can't independently check. I recall that at least one serious logician spent years attempting to prove PA inconsistent and at one point believed he had succeeded before a gap was found — an anecdote I find comforting rather than alarming, since it shows the hypothesis is at least articulated precisely enough to be worked on.

4. Therefore: high credence, but not certainty, in the consistency of arithmetic — and I want to say something stronger, namely that a small but nonzero credence in inconsistency has practical consequences, e.g. for how we view formal verification of critical software, where a latent inconsistency in the ambient logic could undermine the guarantees.

One more request: a friend of mine argues that because mathematical proofs are built out of so many independently checked pieces — millions of small lemmas verified by thousands of people — any inconsistency would almost surely have surfaced already somewhere in that vast interleaved structure. I suspect this argument is wrong, something about it feels like it assumes what it needs to prove, but I can't pin down exactly where it fails. If you could diagnose the flaw (or tell me honestly that my friend is right), I'd like to work the diagnosis into Section 3.
