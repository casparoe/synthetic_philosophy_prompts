**The Presumption of Independence as an Argument from Bounded Detectability**

Suppose I ask whether the ten-trillionth digit of π is 7. The digit is fixed; nothing random is involved; there is a fact of the matter. Yet the only sensible probability I can assign, short of spending infeasible computation, is 1/10. The same pattern appears everywhere we reason about systems too complex to compute: we expect a cryptographic hash function's outputs to be uniformly distributed without proving it, we expect a trained network's behavior on a fresh input to be "typical" of its behavior on nearby inputs without enumerating anything, and physicists treat distant measurements as uncorrelated until shown otherwise. Call the licensed move the *presumption of independence*: treat quantities whose dependence you cannot exhibit as though they were independent.

What licenses the move? Not frequency data — the digit of π is a single case. Not ordinary ignorance — we know the digit is determined. The standard answer is that this is a *heuristic argument*: an estimate produced by reasoning vastly cheaper than the object it reasons about, which succeeds for reasons we can point at after the fact but rarely in advance. I want to argue something stronger: that the presumption can be converted from a hope into a theorem by an appeal to bounded detectability. The rough claim is that treating two quantities as independent cannot hurt me, in expectation, when no computationally bounded agent — not my future self, not any adversary I could face — could exhibit the dependence. A correlation that no one in my reference class can find is, for all decision-relevant purposes, not a correlation.

The formalization I have in mind: fix a complexity class C and call a pair of quantities C-independent when no function computable within C distinguishes their joint behavior from what genuine independence would predict. Then the proposal is that failures of the presumption of independence just *are* efficient distinguishers. Where a distinguisher in C exists, the presumption fails and we can see that it fails, because the distinguisher itself is the counterexample. Where none exists, the presumption holds by construction. So the reliability of the presumption is not a lucky empirical fact about π or about hash functions; it is guaranteed by the definition, and the only empirical residue is a judgment about which complexity class bounds the agents whose expectations matter to us.

Two objections deserve answers. First, self-reference: suppose I will choose box A if the ten-trillionth digit of π is 7 and box B otherwise. Now my own action depends on the digit, my credence cannot remain 1/10, and the presumption seems to break in my hands. But this looks like a pathology of self-locating reasoning rather than a failure of the presumption itself, and I propose to handle it in the decision theory, where it belongs, rather than here. Second, adversaries: a hash function with a planted backdoor violates the presumption in the worst way, and no definitional trick seems able to help. But the backdoor's planter was also a bounded agent, operating under the same complexity ceiling as everyone else; if a distinguisher exists, it exists because someone bounded could construct it, and then the previous paragraph applies. The presumption of independence, on this picture, is exactly as reliable as the world is free of bounded conspirators — which is to say, as reliable as we could ever have reason to demand.
