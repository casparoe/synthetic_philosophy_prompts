My intuitions about which facts give reasons for attitudes are a mess and I want to know if theres a pattern. A billionaire offers me $10,000 if I admire his talentless son's paintings — I don't think that's any reason at all to admire them. A blackmailer says he'll shoot my cat unless I intend to vote for his candidate — and here I do feel like I have some real reason to form that intention, which I can't square with the billionaire case. Believing I'll get the job would make me interview better and so actually get the job — that feels like a genuine reason to believe it, even though it has nothing to do with evidence. Believing my friend's screenplay is brilliant won't make it brilliant, and the comfort the belief would give me is not a reason to believe it. Historical significance makes me admire works I don't even enjoy looking at — though I half suspect that admiration is somehow off too, the way the paid admiration is. And the misery of flying gives me a reason not to be afraid of it, even though the misery is neither evidence of danger nor anything fear gets right about the world. My tentative principle was something like: a fact is a reason for an attitude only if the attitude would be going right, in a sense of rightness specific to that attitude, by responding to that fact. But that seems to deny me the blackmailer reason and the self-fulfilling belief reason, and I'm not sure I want to give those up. Can you state a principle (or two rival ones, if that's what it takes) that captures my verdicts, tell me which verdicts genuinely have to go and why, and say what it predicts about a case I keep circling: a hospital patron who will fund a new wing only if the board sincerely intends to name it after him. Please answer in American English.
