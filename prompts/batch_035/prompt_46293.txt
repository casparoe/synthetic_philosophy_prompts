I've been mulling over Leibniz's move from the principle of sufficient reason (PSR) to the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds (BOAW). The standard story goes: God, choosing which world to actualise, needs a sufficient reason for the choice; if God didn't pick the best world, there'd be no sufficient reason for picking this one rather than a better one; so ours must be the best. What I want to probe is a quiet step in there: that a sufficient reason for a choice has to be an *optimising* reason — that the only thing that can count as "sufficient" is "this option is the best one". That doesn't feel obviously true to me, and Leibniz himself, of all people, thought reasons incline without necessitating, which if anything helps my side — if reasons needn't be compelling forces, why must they be optimising ones? Anyway, I can't quite make the worry precise on my own, so I want a thought experiment built to order.

Construct a thought experiment in which a rational, well-informed, benevolent chooser selects a non-optimal option while having a genuine sufficient reason for doing so. Constraints:

- The chooser isn't God — a finite but impressive agent is fine (a designer of worlds, a founder setting up an institution, whatever works). I want the case to pressure the inference pattern, not the theology.
- The non-optimal choice must not come from ignorance, weakness of will, laziness, or an obviously dumb rule. Note that search costs are probably a dead end: once you count them properly, they get absorbed into "optimal" and the case collapses back into ordinary optimisation.
- The chooser's values can't be rigged to be bizarre. Values a reasonable person could actually hold, please.
- The scenario itself should be one paragraph, and no sci-fi furniture beyond what the setup strictly needs.

One trap to watch for: a Leibnizian might reply that "optimal" just means optimal-by-the-chooser's-own-values, in which case any putative counterexample evaporates into "you merely measured best differently". If that's where things land, say so plainly — maybe the real lesson is that the PSR-to-BOAW step needs "best" to be independently well defined, which is a substantive premise smuggled in from elsewhere.

After the scenario, walk through: (1) what the sufficient reason for the choice actually is; (2) why the chosen option is nevertheless not the best one; (3) what a Leibnizian would say to block the analogy — my guess is the move is "God's perfection guarantees that God optimises, finite agents needn't", and I want you to spell that out and say whether it rescues the argument or just concedes that PSR by itself doesnt get you to BOAW; (4) which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if I remove it; and (5) an honest note on whether you had to rig anything to make it come out — I care more about that than about the case flattering my hunch, so dont sand it down.

Then, separately, give me a JSON object summarising everything, with this schema:

- "scenario_summary": one or two sentences on the case
- "sufficient_reason": the reason, stated compactly
- "why_suboptimal": why the chosen option isn't the best
- "leibnizian_response": how the Leibnizian attacks it, and whether the attack works
- "workhorse_feature": the one feature doing the work
- "removal_test": what happens once that feature is removed
- "rigged": true or false
- "rigging_note": short explanation of what, if anything, had to be rigged

For what it's worth, the point of the exercise: I want to know whether the step from PSR to BOAW needs an extra premise about God's nature, in which case the famous argument is really an argument from divine perfection, with PSR doing much less work than it appears to. If the thought experiment can be made to work, I think that's the moral. But tell me if you think I'm chasing a dead end here — I'd rather find out now than after I've written the thing up.
