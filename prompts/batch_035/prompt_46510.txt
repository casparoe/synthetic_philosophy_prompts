I am a graduate teaching assistant responsible for next week's fifty-minute section in a third-year abstract algebra course. The professor has just used Zorn's lemma to prove that every nonzero commutative ring with unity has a maximal ideal, and my job is to make sure the students can deploy the lemma themselves on the coming problem set (existence of Hamel bases, of maximal chains, of algebraic closures). Most of them have never taken a set theory course; their only model of constructing an object by exhaustion is finite-dimensional linear algebra, where you literally count your way up to a basis. I understand the lemma perfectly well myself — I do not need it explained to me — and I do not want the section to turn into a lecture on the axiom of choice, which I am not prepared to give.

What I want is a plan for the section. First, the order of ideas and what depends on what: where the maximal-element-versus-greatest-element distinction has to be settled (I suspect before the statement even goes on the board, and I would like to know if that is right), when to introduce upper bounds and in what form — in particular, when to force them to notice that an upper bound need not belong to its chain and need not be unique — and whether to state the lemma in full generality before touching the ring example or extract its content from the example first. Second, the carrying example: the maximal ideal argument is prescribed by the course, so judge whether it should carry the whole section or whether something smaller and stranger should be run first, and if it does carry, identify exactly which features of the poset of proper ideals do the work — where the fact that the union of a chain of proper ideals is proper gets used, and what is merely decoration. Third, the misconceptions I should expect, in the order the students will actually generate them, with the point in the sequence at which each should be intercepted: I anticipate maximal element being heard as largest element, upper bound being heard as top of the chain, and someone proposing to skip the lemma entirely by taking the union of the whole poset at once — but also tell me about the subtler ones I won't see coming, such as students checking only increasing sequences of ideals rather than arbitrary chains, or assuming the maximal element produced is unique, or believing the lemma is a garden-variety theorem of ZF rather than something equivalent to the axiom of choice. On that last point I intend to state the equivalence without proof and say so plainly — tell me whether that is the right call for this audience and what sentence to use. Fourth, one question to end on that would distinguish a student who can apply the lemma from one who can only recite it; I am considering the poset of finite subsets of the natural numbers under inclusion and asking what Zorn's lemma yields there — tell me if that is diagnostic and what the correct answer reveals. Finally, tell me what to openly refuse to do at this level: what belongs to the proof of the lemma rather than its use, and how to say no to it without leaving the students feeling that the whole structure is a conjuring trick.
