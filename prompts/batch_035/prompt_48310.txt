I chair the ethics committee of a mid-sized humanitarian agency working in conflict zones, and I need to convert an unresolved philosophical dispute into something a field team leader can actually use. Our teams repeatedly face decisions of a single shape: an action available to us would foreseeably harm a small number of identifiable people — as a means, or as a side effect — in order to prevent a larger harm to others. Diverting a food shipment from one displaced camp to a larger one; hiring a militia's men as convoy guards, which funds an attack they will launch anyway; handing a local commander the names of three families to save a quarter of the town; pulling five stable patients off a generator to run the theatre for thirty blast casualties. My staff split between a roughly consequentialist instinct and a conviction that some things must not be done even to prevent worse, and our existing code simply forbids "using beneficiaries as means," which settles nothing they actually argue about. What I want the procedure to confront honestly is the paradox of deontology: if our reason for forbidding the five killings is that each person matters equally, it looks incoherent to stick by the constraint when the numbers grow lopsided; but if the constraint yields once the stakes are high enough, it is unclear what the threshold is or why it is where it is.

Please design a decision procedure — a written protocol in words, with ordered steps — that a team leader could run in fifteen minutes under stress, with a colleague and a satellite phone and nothing else. It should cover: how to classify the harm our action would involve (what we do versus what we allow, using someone as a means versus harming them as a side effect, redirecting a threat versus creating a new one) and whether those distinctions should survive into the procedure at all; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; the points at which the procedure returns an outright no regardless of the arithmetic; the points at which the arithmetic enters and how; and the places where the procedure must visibly give up and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of judgement that cannot be proceduralised — these should be flagged rather than hidden.

One requirement above all: do not bracket the theoretical dispute. Commit to a definite position — that the relevant constraints are absolute; or that they are thresholded, in which case say what kind of threshold it is and how a field team could estimate which side of it a case falls on; or that they are conditional on something else, in which case say on what — and build the procedure on that commitment. Explain what your choice costs: where it will misfire, what cases it gets wrong, and what a committee member who rejected your starting position would say is the procedure's worst blind spot. Then run the procedure on three of the cases above, including at least one where it returns an answer you expect me to dislike, and say so plainly when it does.

Please write in British English, and keep the procedure usable by someone with no philosophical training: any technical distinctions must be introduced inside the protocol itself, in plain terms, as part of the steps.
