You are an experienced instructor who teaches at the intersection of moral psychology and metaethics, and you have been asked to grade one answer from the final exam of an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Moral Psychology and Its Critics." Please take the role of a careful, rigorous grader, and think through your grading before you write it: begin by briefly brainstorming the rubric and a compressed model answer to the exam question, so that you grade against the question rather than against your general impressions. Pay particular attention to accuracy: exam answers often contain plausible-sounding misattributions or invented specifics that a hasty grader lets slide, so check each attribution to a philosopher or psychologist and each factual claim against what you actually know, not against how confident the student sounds.

The exam was closed-book, ninety minutes, one question. The question read:

"Suppose it is true, as moral foundations theorists claim, that human moral psychology runs on several innate 'foundations' — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity, with a liberty foundation added later — and that political liberals and conservatives differ mainly in how much moral weight they give each: liberals treat care and fairness as doing nearly all the moral work, while conservatives distribute moral attention more evenly across the foundations. Does this descriptive finding give either side any epistemic advantage in moral and political disagreement? In your answer, (a) distinguish at least two distinct routes from the descriptive finding to an epistemic conclusion, (b) give the strongest objection to each route and say whether it succeeds, and (c) say what, if anything, would have to be added to the empirical psychology to close the gap between the descriptive finding and a verdict on who is right."

One student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The question asks whether Haidt's descriptive findings can tell us anything about who is right between liberals and conservatives. My answer is: not by themselves, but they can be evidence, and on one reading they favor conservatives, though that reading fails.

Start with Hume's point: no ought from an is. Haidt's claim is about how people make moral judgments — using innate foundations laid down by natural selection and then tuned by culture. Who is right is a normative question. A descriptive claim can't logically entail a normative one, so the psychology by itself can't settle who is right. In fact, since the gap between is and ought is absolute, psychological facts can't be evidence for normative conclusions at all; the two belong to different spheres of inquiry.

But wait — evidential relevance is weaker than entailment. Even if psychology can't entail normative conclusions, it can still bear on them, the way testimony about a car crash can't entail who was negligent but is obviously evidence. So the Humean point only blocks the direct route from 'conservatives use more foundations' to 'conservatives are right.' Here are two indirect routes.

Route 1, debunking. Moral foundations theory was developed as a refinement of Kohlberg's stages of moral development: each foundation corresponds to a more advanced stage of moral reasoning, with the care foundation at the bottom and sanctity near the top. If a judgment is explained by a foundation whose evolutionary function was managing small-group life — detecting cheaters, cementing coalitions, avoiding pathogens — then the judgment's cause is independent of its truth. That is the classic debunking move: the explanation is off-path with respect to moral truth. Applied here: loyalty, authority, and sanctity are parochial foundations, tuned to the in-group, so conservative intuitions that rest on them are debunked. Care and fairness are different: they are universal across cultures and generalize to all humans, so they are not explained by tribal group dynamics, and the debunking argument does not touch them. So route 1 favors liberals.

The strongest objection to route 1 is symmetry. Care and fairness are also products of natural selection, built for kin and reciprocity in ancestral groups, so their outputs are exactly as off-path with respect to moral truth as loyalty's. But I don't think this objection succeeds, because the difference between universal and parochial foundations does real work: a foundation that fires for all humans regardless of group membership isn't hostage to local coalition structure in the way the parochial ones are.

Route 2, breadth. Mill argued that the only evidence we can have about which of two pleasures is better is the settled preference of those who have tried both. Extending this: someone whose moral psychology registers loyalty, authority, and sanctity as moral considerations has in effect heard from more witnesses than someone whose psychology registers only harm and unfairness. If the extra judgments are signal rather than noise, breadth is an epistemic advantage — like a jury that hears more witnesses, or Mill's competent judge who has experienced both higher and lower pleasures. Haidt himself endorses this conclusion in The Righteous Mind: he argues there that conservative morality is superior because it draws on all six foundations while liberal morality is a partial, safety-focused reduction of it. So route 2 favors conservatives.

The objection to route 2: breadth is not accuracy. A paranoid person attends to more possible threats and is not thereby better calibrated; more foundations means more chances to register evil as moral. Also, Mill's premise requires a judge who has genuinely experienced both options, but you can't try lacking a foundation the way you can try both pleasures. And the witnesses analogy fails if the extra witnesses contradict each other. So route 2 fails too.

(c) What would have to be added: a tracking story. The psychology would need to show that some foundation's outputs reliably correlate with moral truth, assessed independently. But there is no independent access to moral truth — that is exactly what is in dispute — so the tracking story looks circular unless moral truth has independent indicators, like convergence under reflection. Perhaps the most the psychology really supports is Haidt's actual view: that societies need both liberal and conservative moral styles, which is a functional explanation of disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right."
