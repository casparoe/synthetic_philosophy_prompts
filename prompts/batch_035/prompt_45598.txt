so I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene. one of my characters is a semi-retired philosopher of physics, the kind of guy who peaked with one well-regarded paper thirty years ago and now corners people at parties. I want him to lay out structural realism over drinks to a younger character, and I want him to sound like he knows what he's talking about (because he does, mostly) while also being slightly too pleased with himself and glossing over exactly the parts where a real specialist would push back. a friend of mine who did a physics PhD before dropping out wrote me a summary to work from, and honestly it sounds pretty convincing to me, but I don't know enough to know if its fair. here's roughly what he said:

structural realism is the view that when a scientific theory gets replaced, what survives into its successor isn't any claim about what objects exist, just the mathematical structure — basically the equations. so fresnel's ether theory wasn't simply refuted by maxwell, the wave equations carried over, and that's the sense in which fresnel was on to something. this is supposed to be the answer to the pessimistic induction: yes, past theories turned out to be false, but their structure was retained, so science gets the structure right even when it gets the objects wrong. and the reason it counts as realism rather than skepticism is that it says the structure is literally out there in the world, the relations between things are real even if the things themselves turn out not to exist — the equations are descriptions of real relations, so we're still tracking reality, just not its furniture.

then my friend added a kicker I'd love to keep if its defensible: he said the upshot is that the theory of everything, if we ever get it, might not tell us what anything is made of at all, only how it all hangs together, and that would be a total vindication of the structuralists.
