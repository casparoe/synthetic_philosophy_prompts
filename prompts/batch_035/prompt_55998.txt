**Question 6 (25 points).** Throughout this question, a two-person bargaining problem is a pair (S, d) in which S ⊂ ℝ² is a nonempty, compact, convex set of feasible utility pairs and d ∈ S is the disagreement point. Take d = (0, 0) throughout.

(a) (5 points) Define the utopia point a(S) of a bargaining problem.

(b) (8 points) State the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution: say precisely which point of S it selects and why that point is well defined.

(c) (12 points) The Nash bargaining solution is characterized by Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance to positive affine transformations of utilities, and independence of irrelevant alternatives. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution retains the first three axioms and replaces independence of irrelevant alternatives with individual monotonicity. Using a worked example of your own construction, explain what this substitution buys — in terms of how each solution responds to an improvement in one player's opportunities — and what each solution gives up by way of the other's axiom.

**Student's answer.**

(a) The utopia point is the point of S at which both players do as well as the problem allows: a₁ = max{u₁ : u ∈ S} and a₂ = max{u₂ : u ∈ S}, and a(S) = (a₁, a₂) is the feasible point where the two maxima are achieved simultaneously. It represents the best outcome bargaining could deliver if each player got their ideal.

(b) The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the Pareto-optimal point of S at which the players' gains over the disagreement point are equal. Since d = (0, 0) here, it is the efficient point x with x₁ = x₂: each player should come away with the same gain. For this reason it is often called the egalitarian solution. Like the Nash solution, it satisfies Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations.

(c) Individual monotonicity says that when the feasible set expands so that one player's maximum payoff rises, that player must be at least as well off as before. So the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution rewards a player whose opportunities improve, whereas Nash need not. Independence of irrelevant alternatives says that if the feasible set changes but the previous solution point is still available, the solution must stay where it was. To see the difference, consider S = conv{(0,0), (10,0), (0,10)}. The utopia point is (10,10), so by part (b) the solution is the efficient point with equal gains, which is (5,5). Nash also gives (5,5), by symmetry. Now suppose player 1's opportunities improve while player 2's are unchanged: T = conv{(0,0), (20,0), (0,10)}. Player 1's maximum has doubled, so individual monotonicity requires player 1 to gain. Applying the definition from (b), we need the efficient point with x₁ = x₂; the frontier of T between (20,0) and (0,10) is x₂ = 10 − x₁/2, and solving x₁ = x₂ gives x₁ = x₂ = 20/3. So the solution moves to (20/3, 20/3): player 1's payoff rises from 5 to roughly 6.7, and player 2's rises as well. Nash, by contrast, stays at (5,5), because (5,5) remains feasible in T and independence of irrelevant alternatives forbids the solution from moving when the old solution point survives a change in the feasible set. This is the cost of that axiom: it makes Nash insensitive to expansions of a player's opportunities. The corresponding cost of switching to individual monotonicity is losing that stability: the outcome can move when the feasible set changes even if the bargainers' relative strength has not.
