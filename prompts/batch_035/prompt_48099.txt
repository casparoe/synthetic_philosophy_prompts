I've been teaching the Liar paradox in my truth-theory seminar for going on fifteen years now, and I've started noticing something a little depressing: the debate over what to say about the Liar sentence has been running basically since Tarski, and the same moves keep getting made over and over. The gap theorists say the Liar is neither true nor false, the glut people say it's both, the contextualists try to shift the context mid-evaluation, the revision theorists tell us truth is a circular concept and just keep iterating, and the paraconsistent crowd says fine, it's true and false, stop crying about it. Everyone knows everyone else's objections. The gap folks get hit with the strengthened Liar (if it's not true, then it's true that it's not true — so now it's true after all?), the gluts get asked why they don't just infer arbitrary conclusions from the contradiction, and the revision people get told their rules for the limit stages of the iteration are ad hoc. And yet nobody ever switches teams, as far as I can tell, and there's no settled frontier were anybody concedes anything.

So my question isn't "who's right about the Liar." It's: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My pet hypothesis — and I'd genuinely like to know if it's dumb — is that the whole fight is really about the T-schema and how much of it each side is willing to keep. The gap theorist sacrifices some instances or bivalence, the paraconsistent theorist keeps every instance but gives up explosion, the contextualist keeps them all but only by relativizing something to a context. If that's right, maybe the dispute isn't substantive at all — maybe it's different people carving out different trade-offs while talking as though they contradict each other. But it could also be that underneath the shouting there's a real formal question nobody has cracked, something about what a fixed point for a truth predicate can even look like when the predicate has to live inside the language it applies to, and the endless exchanges about revenge paradoxes are just a symptom of that.

Diagnose the disagreement for me, using what the sides actually say, not a survey of positions. Is it verbal? Substantive? A case of everyone answering different questions under one heading — say, one camp doing semantics for the word 'true' and another camp really working on how to reason safely once paradoxical sentences are in the mix? Is the impasse a sign that the question itself is ill-posed, or just that it's hard? And then tell me honestly, given your diagnosis, what progress would even look like: a revenge-proof theory, a theorem, the right distinction, giving up on a single unified truth predicate for object and metalanguage, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why. Please answer in American English.
