i cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and next week i'm interviewing a mathematician who defends an old-school finitist line: there's no such thing as a completed infinity, only potential ones -- you can always add one more, but the "all" never exists as a finished object. draft me eight to ten questions that get past having him just restate the position. i want at least one each on: whether hilbert's hotel actually cuts his way (people always deploy it against finitists as a reductio, so i want to ask him whether an intuition pump about hotels -- which are finite physical objects -- can even touch the question of completed sets); what cantor's diagonal argument shows on his reading -- is it just a fact about any list you propose, i.e. a potentialist theorem rather than a claim about the size of a finished totality; whether physics' reliance on real-number continuity (spacetime, field values) commits us to actual infinities or whether that's just idealized bookkeeping over finite measurements; and how much of working mathematics his view actually needs to give up, given that a lot of what physicists use looks like it could be recovered in finitistically acceptable terms. for each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give -- e.g. if he leans on "the infinite is a useful fiction," what does he say to the person who thinks useful fictions that never mislead us have earned the right to be called true? and end with one question about what would change his mind: what observation, or result inside mathematics itself, would force a completed infinity on him whether he likes it or not.
