I recently moved from an academic statistics program into a forecasting group at an insurance company, and a disagreement over a rating model has got me tangled in a general question I can't untie. Last month I became convinced that one of our long-standing assumptions about claim severity runs in the wrong direction, at least in the recent data I've been studying. When I raised it, four colleagues — each with more experience than I have — patiently explained why I was misreading things, and my manager backed them: trust the room. A teammate then generalized the manager's advice into an argument that now has me stuck:

(1) When my view conflicts with the considered view of a group of people who are, by my own lights, no less intelligent, no less familiar with the subject, and no less free of distorting incentives than I am, rationality requires me to move my credence substantially toward theirs.
(2) The innovators we celebrate — the ones who turned out to be right against a confident consensus — were, at the crucial moment, each one person facing exactly such a group.
(3) Everyone in that position believes he has seen something the others have missed, and believing this is not itself evidence that he has.
(4) So rationality required the innovator, too, to discount his view before pressing it — to write no paper, propose no test, and let the consensus stand.
(5) But if rationality forbids anyone from pressing a minority view until the majority has already shifted, then true minority views can never gain their first foothold, and a consensus, however wrong, can never legitimately change.
(6) Therefore (1) is false, and the principle I was just told to follow cannot be right.

I don't want the conclusion that I may simply disregard my four colleagues; they have been right before and I have been wrong before, and I suspect the fault lies somewhere between (2) and (5). But I also can't accept that the correct norm of intellectual conduct is one which, followed by everyone, would have frozen every erroneous consensus in place forever. Tell me which step in the argument to reject, explain why that step looked unobjectionable when my teammate stated it, and say what rejecting it costs — both in general and for me specifically, standing in this meeting with my model and my four colleagues. Keep your entire answer under 400 words.
