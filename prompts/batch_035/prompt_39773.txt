I teach a philosophy of mind course thats cross-listed with cognitive science, ~18 students, mostly non-CS majors, and I have one 75-minute session on computation and cognition. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the gap between verifying a solution and finding one — the core of the P vs NP distinction, but experienced rather than defined. My rough idea is to split the room into solvers who get puzzle instances (number puzzles, sudoku-like fragments, whatever is easy to print) and checkers who only receive candidate answers and stamp them valid or invalid, with points for both sides so the incentives work. But I'm open to a better structure if you have one — maybe rounds where solvers can pay points to consult a checker, or a round where the puzzle instances quietly get bigger.

Please include: rules, a payoff scheme with points instead of money, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the formal distinction (efficient verification vs efficient discovery) and then onto the philosophical stakes — e.g. whether understanding something is just being a good searcher rather than a good checker, whether math facts that are in principle verifiable are thereby "available" to us, and what the asymmetry does to any picture of cognition as effortless insight. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea a classroom game simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to just assert in the debrief.
