I volunteer with the campus chapter of an international anti-hunger organization, and at our last planning meeting two of the senior members got into a genuine argument over the wording of our fundraising appeal, which currently describes famine as one of the deadliest natural disasters. Amara said this phrasing is a euphemism that lets governments off the hook: on Amartya Sen's analysis of Bengal in 1943, overall food availability did not dramatically collapse — certain groups' entitlements did, as wartime inflation, hoarding, and provincial trade barriers priced rural laborers out of the market even while grain kept moving. She noted that food exports continued from Ireland during the 1840s famine and that China kept exporting grain during the Great Leap Forward, and she invoked Sen's well-known observation that no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press. Her conclusion: famines are preventable political failures, and the appeal should call them political crimes rather than natural disasters. Dan replied that natural triggers are real — the potato blight, the Bengal cyclone and the loss of Burmese rice imports, recurrent drought — and that Sen's entitlement approach explains who starves, not that anyone intended it, so the word crime, with its implication of identifiable perpetrators and intent, overreaches; policy failure would be both truer and safer for an appeal meant to persuade. Amara countered that crimes of omission exist, that a state that censors news of starvation, blocks relief, and lets a vulnerable population starve amid available food is closer to negligent homicide than to a weather event, and that bloodless language like policy failure is precisely how responsibility evaporates. Dan's last word was that her view effectively makes every famine a murder committed by specific officials, which she cannot demonstrate, and that some entitlement collapses — say, mass unemployment in a depression — seem to involve no wrongdoing by anyone at all, so her category cannot cover famines as such. They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess each of the specific claims about Bengal, Ireland, and the Great Leap Forward; say whether crime or policy failure better fits Sen's own analysis, or whether neither does; and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
