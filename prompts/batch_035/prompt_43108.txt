Next month I defend the final chapter of my dissertation, on anattā and personal identity, before an audience of analytic metaphysicians — two animalists, one Locksonian memory theorist, and several colleagues who think Parfit settled everything worth settling in the 1980s — plus a senior Buddhist-studies scholar who regards the whole analytic literature on no-self as anachronistic projection onto the texts. My thesis is that the standard dilemma for no-self is false. Either the Buddhist denies only a Cartesian, owner-style substantial self, in which case no contemporary metaphysician disagrees and the doctrine is metaphysically trivial; or she denies any psychological-continuity structure robust enough to bear karma and to be the candidate for liberation, in which case the doctrine collapses into incoherence. I defend a third reading: anattā is best understood as denying what I call entitlement to self-ascription — the presupposition, built into ordinary first-person practices like memory claims, resolutions, and remorse, that there must be a single continuing owner of the stream of experiences. My claim is that this presupposition does no explanatory work and can be abandoned without those practices collapsing: the stream carries them ownerless, and liberation can be reconceived as the cessation of the felt need for an owner rather than the destruction of one.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. List the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased in each contingent's own vocabulary: what the animalists will say about a bearerless stream, whether the Parfitians will press me on whether this is just psychological-continuity reductionism with the Buddhist label left on, what the Locksonian will do with the memory criterion once ownership is dropped, and what the philologist will say about my reading an Abhidharma-era distinction back into the early Nikāyas. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is why, if no owner-entitlement is needed, the Buddha's own discourses keep talking as though there were one, but assess whether that suspicion is right. Finally, tell me what I should concede up front and what I must hold if the defense is to survive.
