I keep coming back to the old fight over whether natural selection is scientific in Popper's sense, and what gets me is that the thing never actually ends. Quick sketch so we're on the same page: Popper himself in the 70s classified Darwinism as not a testable theory but a metaphysical research programme, partly because survival of the fittest, read one way, looks near-tautological -- the fittest are just defined as the ones that survive, so what does the claim actually rule out? He later publicly took a lot of it back, saying he'd changed his mind about the testability of natural selection. But the underlying debate just kept rolling: one side keeps saying the theory is circular because fitness is defined by survival, or that it "predicts" that whatever survived was fit, which is no prediction at all. The other side -- mostly philosophers of biology -- keeps making the same counters: fitness can be defined independently (propensity interpretations, ecological/engineering analyses), selection is a lawlike claim about populations rather than a definition, and there are real empirical tests anyway. Fifty years on, both sides keep publishing basically the same moves and nobody converts. Creationists quoting Popper in the 80s school controversies muddied things further, since then backing off the tautology charge felt like conceding ground to people arguing in bad faith.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute hidden in the word fitness -- one side meaning a defined quantity in population genetics, the other meaning the everyday superlative from the phrase survival of the fittest? Is it a fight about demarcation standards, where the skeptics are applying falsificationism that everyone else quietly dropped decades ago? Or are the two sides answering genuinely different questions -- the logical structure of the theory versus its fruitfulness as a research programme? My pet hypothesis is that the tautology objection only bites against a position no working biologist ever held, and the debate survives because of sociological entanglement with the creationism fight -- but I don't think that fully explains why philosophers of biology keep re-engaging with it rather than just declaring victory and moving on. Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually claims, and tell me what would have counted as resolving it -- a sharper definition of fitness, a better demarcation criterion, everybody getting over Popper -- or whether it's a pseudo-question that was never going to be settled by argument in the first place.
