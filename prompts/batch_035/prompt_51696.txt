I'm a retired chemist who's spent the last two years reading philosophy of language instead of actually retiring, and I've gotten stuck on something from my reading group. One of the other members, an ex-physicist, says he's proven that Grice's whole analysis of speaker meaning is empty, and I half beleive him.

His argument: (1) On Grice's analysis, a speaker means that P by an utterance when she intends, by means of that utterance, to get the hearer to believe that P through the hearer recognizing that very intention. (2) Since the intention has to be recognized for the meaning to come off, it has to be something the hearer can work out. (3) The hearer's only evidence is the utterance, the circumstances, and what a reasonable person would be trying to convey in those circumstances. (4) So the recognized intention just is whatever a reasonable person would intend there — anything else couldn't be recognized, so by (1) it wouldn't be a case of meaning anything at all. (5) Therefore the actual speaker's intentions are idle: what she means is fixed entirely by what a reasonable person in her shoes would mean. (6) But then a panicking witness who blurts out exactly what a reasonable person would blurt out means everything the reasonable person would mean, actual intentions or no; and a clever speaker who intends something no hearer could ever be expected to work out means nothing at all. Both sound nuts to me. (7) So (1) is false and the intention-based program was a mistake.

He concludes meaning is context all the way down and Grice was doing bookkeeping, not analysis. I suspect the slide is somewhere around (3) to (4), but every time I try to pin it down he says that if the hearer can't infer it, it's not meaning, and that's Grice's own condition, not his. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'll admit I'd like to keep (1) — after decades of molecules meaning exactly one thing, the reflexive-intention analysis was a revelation — but tell me straight if that's sentimental. Please answer in American English.
