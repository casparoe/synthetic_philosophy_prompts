The student's answer:

"Honoring sunk costs means letting costs you have already paid influence whether you keep investing in a project. The standard argument that this is irrational is that rational choice is forward-looking: only future costs and benefits matter, because past costs are fixed no matter what you do now. The sunk cost fallacy, first identified by Kahneman and Tversky in their work on loss aversion, is the mistake of letting those fixed costs sway you — like sitting through a bad movie just because you paid for the ticket.

One objection is that sunk costs carry information. If I have spent ten years on my dissertation, that fact is evidence about how much the project is worth to me and how close I am to finishing, so it isn't irrational for it to affect my decision. This shows that sunk costs can be genuine reasons for continuing, not just noise.

A second objection concerns reputation. People who abandon half-finished projects get known as quitters, so honoring sunk costs can pay off strategically by showing others you follow through on commitments. This shows the standard argument is wrong in general: sunk costs really do count.

On balance, honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational, but only for the strategic reason. Once nobody is watching, the rational person just cuts their losses and moves on."
