I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and about a year ago I read Kierkegaard's The Present Age after someone in an online argument told me I was part of the problem he diagnosed. Since then I've become fairly committed to his picture of the press and the public: that a highly literate, reflective society tends to produce passionless people who watch and comment rather than commit; that the press conjures into being an abstract entity called the public, a kind of phantom that has opinions but no member who can be held responsible for them; and that this phantom works through envy and levelling, cutting down anyone who stands out by making an actual, costly, personal commitment to anything. My extension of this is that social media is exactly this dynamic with the feedback loop tightened by several orders of magnitude — an engagement-optimised press with no editor at all, where the anonymous public is not a nineteenth-century abstraction but a literal metric next to every post. I've argued this in various comment threads, and, yes, I'm aware of the irony.

Here's my request. I want to know what would actually count against this position — not the standard refutation-by-rolling-eyes that it's just an old man complaining about new media in 1846. Play it straight and be specific. What observations, historical cases, or arguments should move me, and how should they be ranked, from fatal through serious to merely awkward? I can think of some candidate defeaters myself, and I'd like you to evaluate them alongside your own: the abolitionist and samizdat presses seem to have generated sincere, passionate, personally costly commitment rather than dissolving it; the same channels Kierkegaard complained about have arguably also functioned as what he would call passionate, revolutionary media; and it's not obvious that a society drowning in commentary is genuinely less passionate than one that went to church and read one newspaper. Also tell me which objections a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it versus which they would dismiss, and what that tells me about how the position is insulated — because my worry is that it may be unfalsifiable in practice: if loud coordinated outrage gets read as phantom-public levelling and quiet genuine commitment gets read as the surviving exception that proves the rule, then nothing could ever disconfirm it. If the view is insulated like that, say so plainly, and tell me whether that's a fatal flaw for a position of this kind (a work of social critique rather than a predictive theory) or just the ordinary cost of doing business in this genre.

Please write the response in British English, and don't assume I know the surrounding literature — I've read The Present Age and some scattered commentary, but nothing systematic on Kierkegaard or nineteenth-century Denmark. If you reference his other works or concepts, give me enough context in a sentence or two to follow you. Finally, feel free to push back on my own framing where it's sloppy — for instance on whether an engagement-optimised platform is really an intensification of the press or something structurally different that his account wasn't about at all.
