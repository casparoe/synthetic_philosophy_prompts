I'm grading midterms for a big "Genetics & Society" intro course and could use a second pair of eyes on one question. Here's the question I wrote:

"Prof. Okafor runs a dairy herd where the heritability of milk yield has been estimated at 0.35. She concludes that selective breeding can only ever move the herd's average yield 35% of the way toward her target, so she might as well give up on breeding and buy better feed instead. Separately, a health official in the same town reads that BMI has an estimated heritability of roughly 0.8 in adults and concludes that since weight is 'mostly genetic,' taxes on sugary drinks are pointless. Explain what heritability does and does not mean, and evaluate both inferences."

And here's one student's answer:

"Heritability is the proportion of the variation in a trait, within a population, that's due to genetic variation. So a heritability of 0.8 for BMI means 80% of why people in that population differ from each other in weight is genetic. It's a claim about the population, not about any one person — you can't say 80% of Alice's weight is genetic — and it's specific to that population in that environment, not a fixed property of the trait itself.

For the official, the inference fails. Heritability says nothing about how malleable a trait is. PKU is 100% heritable and a dietary intervention eliminates its effects entirely. Also, estimates of height heritability actually went up in some countries over the 20th century as nutrition improved, which shows heritability can rise even while environment is still hugely causal for the mean. If anything, if the government somehow made everyone's food environment identical, the heritability of BMI would collapse toward zero, so the official's fear is doubly unfounded.

Twin studies are where these numbers mostly come from — you compare resemblance in identical vs. fraternal twins. This inflates the estimates somewhat, since identical twins share the womb and (often) family environment, so it's an upper bound. For a trait like lactose intolerance you can just take the difference between the MZ and DZ concordance rates and read the heritability straight off.

The breeder is basically right in spirit. Since h² is 0.35, only 35% of the variation in yield is genetic, so breeding can only capture a fraction of the gap between her herd and her target — the rest is feed, management, luck, etc. She'd be better off on the environmental side, where the returns are bigger. The response she gets per generation is heritability times the selection differential, so with such a low number progress will be slow no matter how hard she selects.

None of this rules out genetic contributions to group differences, though. If BMI has heritability 0.8 in both the US and Japan, and the two countries differ in average BMI, then the same genes that explain within-country differences must contribute something to the between-country gap too. So the official is wrong about sugary drinks, but the general lesson shouldn't be that genes stop mattering at the group level."
