I'm an interpretability researcher, and the analogy my field runs on is that interpretability is neuroscience for AI: circuits are brain regions, activations are neural firing, our causal ablations are lesion studies, and the individual features turned up by sparse autoencoders are the artificial analog of neurons that fire selectively for faces or places. The analogy is doing real work. When someone finds a feature that seems to respond to sycophantic praise, or a circuit that computes which token is the indirect object, the writeup is framed as though we had located something that was already there — a discovery claim, not an invention claim — and a hard-nosed colleague of mine says that's exactly where the analogy cheats. His point is that in real neuroscience the brain is the only ground truth, so whatever structure survives independent measurement methods counts as real, whereas we have the weights, the data, and the loss in a way neuroscientists never did and rarely use, and he suspects the "features" are artifacts of the particular decomposition we chose — projections of our own vocabulary onto activation space, no more real than lines of longitude. Audit the analogy for me: give the mapping explicitly, then say where it breaks — including where it breaks in his favor (superposition, distributed representations, the fact that the "right" basis may not exist) and where it breaks in ours (we can intervene, retrain, and check whether a feature is actually used downstream, in ways lesion studies on intact humans can't) — and then tell me whether the realist conclusion — that the model genuinely has these representations and that we are finding rather than making them — survives, or whether the neuroscience analogy was quietly carrying it the whole time.
