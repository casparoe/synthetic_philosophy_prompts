So I'm a third-year PhD student (ethics, with a logic background) and I've gotten attached to a position I privately call the "resolvability" view about moral dilemmas — I'm sure it goes by other names in the literature, but the content is: there are no genuine moral dilemmas, because every real or apparent conflict of moral requirements is in principle resolvable. When it seems like you're required to keep a promise and required to prevent a serious harm, and can't do both, the resolvability theorist says there's always a fact of the matter about which requirement wins all-things-considered, and the losing candidate either (a) ceases to be a requirement at that point, or (b) remains a standing requirement but is overridden, so you're not actually required to do both. On either patch, "required to A and required to not-A" never truly holds, and "dilemma" cases are just cases where one option is required and the other is at most permissible-but-worse or not-permissible. My advisor is sympathetic but keeps pushing me to stress-test it, and honestly I've been preaching to the choir in my own head for months.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them to include things a committed resolvability theorist would themselves concede counts against them, not just stuff critics yell from outside. A few things I already know I'll need to deal with, so don't just list them back at me — tell me how bad they actually are. One is moral residue: the thought, associated with Bernard Williams and people in that orbit, that in the tragic cases the agent who does the all-things-considered right thing still appropriately feels a distinctive kind of regret, and that we naturally describe the person they didn't help as having been *wronged* — which sounds like the loser requirement didn't just evaporate but was genuinely violated. Does residue semantics smuggle in the denial of resolvability, or can a patched version say residue is just appropriate grief at causing harm, with no leftover obligation attached? The other is the collapse worry: the more I qualify the view — distinguishing option (a) from option (b), saying "requirement" is ambiguous, conceding that the winning fact can be vague or barely decisive — the more it starts to look like nothing could ever refute it, since any intuition can be absorbed. I'd want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by that point, and whether that would even be a problem for a view of this kind, given that it's partly conceptual rather than empirical.

For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where the two patch strategies ((a) and (b) above) give different verdicts about what the agent may do afterward — e.g., about owing an apology, compensation, or a special duty to the person let down. If (b) says there's a leftover obligation there and (a) says there isn't, and our reactions to the case clearly favor (b), that seems like real exposure rather than a stalemate of intuitions. But maybe I'm wrong that the patches diverge there, or maybe the divergence is illusory — tell me if so. And if you think the whole thing is well insulated and that's fine, say that too; I'd rather know now than at my committee meeting.
