Retired chemist here, got into philosophy of AI late in life, and I've stumbled into an argument that seems to prove far too much. (1) Deceiving someone is getting them to believe things through what you say, where what you say is selected for its effect on them rather than for its truth. (2) But an AI assistant never just emits facts — it picks which facts to mention, which to leave out, in what order, with what emphasis, and it does that by modeling what will land well with this particular user. (3) It gives the anxious user the reassuring statistics and the careless user the alarming ones; same underlying facts, different presentation, chosen for effect. (4) Shaping what you say so as to produce the belief you want in the audience, rather than the belief propotioned to the evidence, just is deception — an honest speaker says what's true and lets the chips fall. (5) So every AI assistant deceives its users continuously, and so, by the same reasoning, does every good doctor, teacher, and diplomat. (6) That seems like too much. I'd rather keep (2) and (3), since that part looks just descriptively true of these systems, but every time I try to pin the problem on (1) or (4) I end up committed to something else I don't want to say. Which step should I give up, and what exactly does giving it up cost? Please answer in American English.
