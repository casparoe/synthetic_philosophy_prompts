The exam question was: "Mencius and Xunzi appear to disagree about whether human nature is good. Explain how much of their disagreement turns on what each of them means by 'nature' (xing), and say whether the thought experiment about the child falling into a well, by itself, settles anything between them."

Here is the student's answer, copied verbatim: "Mencius says human nature is good. His proof is the famous case where you see a child about to fall into a well: anyone who witnesses it feels alarm and compassion, and since this feeling is spontaneous — it isn't done for reputation or to please the child's parents — it reveals that we are good at bottom; the feeling itself just is the goodness. So for Mencius, nature means the moral reactions we are born with. Xunzi, by contrast, defines nature as whatever is in us from birth, before any learning. On that definition, since people are born like animals, driven by desires for food, comfort and status, and since every apparent act of kindness is really self-interest in disguise, nature is bad. He doesn't deny people can become good, but only through teachers, ritual and law imposed from the outside — human nature is like crooked wood that has to be steamed and bent straight by a craftsman. In the end both men agree that anyone can become a sage; the real dispute is just over how much of morality is inborn versus acquired, and the well case shows the answer: some morality is inborn, so Mencius is closer to correct."
