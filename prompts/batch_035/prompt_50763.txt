I teach bioethics, and my brother-in-law — thirty-four, carrying about forty thousand dollars of debt from an accident a few years back — has decided to donate a kidney to a stranger through a pilot program at a regional transplant center. The center, plainly nervous about anything resembling a purchase, has structured the arrangement so that no cash changes hands: a charitable fund retires his debt on his behalf, he receives guaranteed priority for a future transplant should he ever need one, and he's enrolled in lifetime follow-up care at no cost. His mother says the elaborate paperwork proves the center knows it is doing something wrong — that they are laundering a payment through charity and guarantees precisely so nobody has to say the word sale. His wife says the opposite: that the design shows they have stripped the transaction of everything bad about selling a kidney and kept only the good. My own unease is that I cannot tell where the wrongness people sense in organ sales, if there is any, is supposed to be located in the first place — in the money as a medium, in the price signal it sends to others, in the effect on how donors come to see themselves, in what onlookers conclude about kidneys generally, or nowhere at all — and the center's restructuring seems to touch some of these candidate locations but not others. Where exactly would the wrongness have to sit for the mother to be right, for the wife to be right, and does the restructuring actually reach any of the plausible locations? Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
