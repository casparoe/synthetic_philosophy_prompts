I'm part of a small philosophy-of-biology reading group that meets in the back of a pub -- five or six people, mostly grad students plus a couple of hangers-on like me who just read pop stuff on evolution. We got onto the units of selection question (is natural selection acting on genes, organisms, groups, what) and two members, Dev and Marta, ended up in a standoff that nobody could referee. The trigger was someone bringing up sterile worker ants: workers never reproduce themselves, they just spend their lives feeding the queen's offspring. Dev declared this a "solved problem": the gene-centered view handles it, since a gene that makes a worker help her egg-laying mother still gets copies of itself into the next generation through the relatives it helps create -- relatedness does the work, and you never need to treat the colony as a thing that competes and reproduces. He added that all this "selection acts for the good of the colony" talk is at best a bookkeeping convenience: you could, in principle, parcel every one of those fitness effects out to genes without remainder, so the colony level adds nothing.

Marta had two comebacks and they each got a reply, four moves total. First she said the bookkeeping argument proves too much: you can equally parcel out everything genes do up to the organism level -- an animal is just a bag of cooperating cells whose effects you're choosing to tally as one unit -- so if "I can redescribe the fitness at level X" settles where selection really acts, then organisms are no more real units than colonies are, and Dev's own reasoning would dissolve him too. Dev answered that there's a principled asymmetry: organisms (mostly) have a dedicated germ line and go through a single-cell bottleneck each generation, which suppresss selection among the parts and makes the collective a genuine unit in a way an ant colony, with its many reproductive queens across the population and no bottleneck, is not. Then Marta made her second move: fine, but notice you've now conceded the question is empirical and graded -- it's about how integrated the collective is, colonies of eusocial insects sitting somewhere partway along the road from loose association to organismhood -- which is a far cry from "solved, it's genes all the way down." And she said even if Dev can always decompose colony-level adaptations into gene-level bookkeeping, decomposition isn't explanation: if colony-vs-colony raids are what drives the evolution of colony-level traits like caste ratios, the interesting causal story is at the colony level, and refusing to say so is like refusing to talk about organisms because molecules exist. Dev's last word was that "interesting causal story" is doing sneaky work -- genes are the only things that persist across generations with high fidelity, so they're the only real units of heritable difference, everything else being repeatedly-rebuilt temporary vehicles, and Marta's continuum just tracks which vehicles happen to be tightly glued together, not where selection acts.

Everyone else stayed out of it and honestly I couldn't follow who was landing punches and who was shadowboxing. Could you go through the four moves (Dev's opening, Marta's "proves too much," Dev's germ-line/bottleneck reply, Marta's decomposition-isn't-explanation point plus her last exchange about persistence and glue) and tell me who's right about what in each -- not just who wins overall, but which specific claims hold up and which are equivocations or shifts in what "unit of selection" is supposed to mean? Also, if there's a way both of them are trapped in a framing that the debate's own history (like the old distinction people drew between selection *of* something and selection *for* it, or the pluralist line that says the levels are intertranslatable descriptions rather than rivals) would dissolve, I'd like to know.
