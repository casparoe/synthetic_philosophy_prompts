I'm taking a philosophy of technology seminar, and we've just finished Langdon Winner's 1980 essay in Daedalus, the one that asks whether artifacts have politics and argues that technical things can carry political properties — most famously through the claim that Robert Moses's parkway overpasses on Long Island were built low enough to keep buses, and the poorer riders who depended on them, away from the beaches, and through the story of the mechanical tomato harvester, which was promoted as a labor-saving miracle but functioned in practice to consolidate agribusiness and push migrant workers out. Something I've noticed in my own city seems to run exactly against the grain of that essay, and I'd like help working out what it presupposes.

Last autumn, our city council spent nearly four months debating an ordinance against camping and sleeping in public parks. There were hearings, letters to the local paper, protests, amendments about shelter beds, and a recorded vote; the ordinance in the end failed. Yet during those same months, the parks department quietly ordered several hundred new benches — the type with a metal armrest every meter and a half, so that no one can lie down — and the library replaced its flat exterior ledges with sloped ones, and the transit authority specified "anti-litter" hinge seats at its new stops. All of this was decided in procurement documents and product catalogs by landscape architects and a facilities manager. It never appeared on a council agenda, no one voted on it, and it accomplished, passively and at the level of the city's hardware, more or less what the failed ordinance would have accomplished by law.

My question is what view of the relationship between artifacts and political life this division of labor presupposes. It seems to embody something like the following: laws and votes are where politics happens, while design choices are technical or administrative matters — a bench is a neutral instrument, and whatever politics surrounds it comes later, from how police enforce the rules around it, not from the object itself. Can that presupposition survive Winner's argument that some technical arrangements are themselves a kind of legislation, settling matters without appearing to? I'm also puzzled by a tension I can't resolve: the same council that left the benches to the catalog treats other technologies as unquestionably political — it held months of hearings before licensing e-scooters and has debated whether to restrict facial-recognition cameras, precisely on the grounds that a technology's design shapes how people can behave in the city. So the practice looks internally inconsistent: benches and ledges are treated as mere objects, algorithms and scooters as objects of public deliberation. Is there a principled line the city could point to, perhaps something about who builds the thing versus who deploys it, or is the line simply that one set of choices happens to be visible and the other invisible?

Second, I wonder whether the bench practice also presupposes a quiet technological determinism. The council debate about the ordinance was entirely voluntarist — would police move people along, would fines work, would shelter beds change behavior? The armrest bench, by contrast, is purchased on the assumption that the design will reliably exclude lying down with no enforcement, no discretion, no one to blame. So the city seems to be a determinist in procurement and a voluntarist in legislation. Or is it rather, as Winner would prefer, not determinism at all but the recognition that arrangements can be chosen for the order they embody?

Finally, I'd like to know how much of this is genuinely forced by the practice, because I suspect some of it is underdetermined. A defender of the parks department could say the armrests are about maintenance costs and daytime seating turnover, and that the benches presuppose no view at all about who sleeps outdoors — in which case maybe the practice is compatible with several theories of the artifact-politics relation rather than revealing one. Could you set out what the practice strictly presupposes, as opposed to what it merely sits comfortably with, and then tell me what would have to change in the city's ordinary procedures if it took Winner's thesis seriously — for instance, whether procurement of street furniture should require something like the hearing-and-vote apparatus we reserve for ordinances, and whether that would be an improvement or just new layers of process?
