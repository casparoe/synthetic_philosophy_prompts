I'm a software engineer, and over the past few years our organization has repeatedly tried to replace argument with arithmetic: a single prioritization score that would let roadmap decisions be made by sorting a spreadsheet. The scores never survive contact with reality, and I've been trying to work out whether what keeps killing them is a known thing or just bad score design. Two patterns recur. First, comparisons that work fine in isolation refuse to line up: the team prefers fixing the data-corruption bug to building the new dashboard, and the dashboard to refactoring the payments module, but when the corruption bug and the payments refactor are put head to head, they pick the refactor -- and they insist this isn't an error to be trained out of them. Second, a sequence of individually correct trades: each quarter, shipping something known-imperfect ahead of a scheduled fix was clearly worth it by the numbers, everyone agreed each time, no single step looks wrong in retrospect, and yet after two years nobody will defend the cumulative state of the codebase. Nobody was wrong at any decision point, but the destination is one nobody would have chosen from the start. Is there an established concept, or more likely two different concepts, covering these -- the comparison-order one, and the locally-defensible-globally-indefensible one? If names exist, I want to know how precisely they apply and where my cases come loose from them; if they don't, I'd like candidate definitions sharp enough to tell me whether the two patterns are actually the same phenomenon, and whether my company should stop trying to build such scores at all.
