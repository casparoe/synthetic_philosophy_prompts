so I'm a novelist, mostly midlist literary stuff, and I've got this idea thats been kicking around my notes folder for two years that I finally want to actually attempt as a standalone short story (10-15 pages, I'm thinking). The seed came from a conversation at a party, of all places: a friend of a friend who works in life expectancy modelling for an insurance company told me about the doomsday argument, and I couldn't sleep that night. The way I understand it, the idea is that just from knowing roughly how many humans have lived before me (something like 100 billion), and treating my own birth rank among humans as roughly random, you can argue that humanity probably doesn't have a super long future ahead of it -- because if there were going to be trillions of future humans, it'd be a wild statistical fluke that I happened to be born this early. There's something genuinely uncanny about it to me: no physics, no prophecy, just an index card of arithmetic and suddenly the horizon of the species looks shorter. I don't care whether the argument is actually sound, that's a fight for philosophers -- what I care about is the texture of it: the way it feels to hold.

Here's the setting I've been imagining, though feel free to push back on it: 1987, a small university town in the Midwest, and the protagonist is a demographer -- a woman, mid-fifties, whose department is being quietly gutted, and who has spent her whole career counting people who are already dead (historical population reconstructions, that kind of thing). She comes across the argument in a preprint a visiting lecturer leaves behind, runs the numbers herself one night at the kitchen table, and the story follows the next six months of her life: she keeps doing the arithmetic in the margins of other things, at the dentist, during a colleague's retirement party. What I do NOT want is a story where a character explains the argument in dialogue and another character says "fascinating" -- no lectures, no chalkboards. And I'd like there to be one other person in the story, maybe her estranged daughter or the visiting lecturer himself, who thinks the whole thing is a parlor trick (something like: if the argument had been made by a Sumerian scribe it would've been "right" for the wrong reason, and she can't quite dismiss that). The emotional engine, I think, is that for her the argument lands differently than it would for most people -- she has actually spent decades handling the numbers of the dead, and something about counting them is bound up with a grief of her own that the story should only gradually reveal. Give her a concrete life (a specific hobby, an actual town, a name that isn't plucked from a baby-name website), make the arithmetic physical and particular, and let the ending be about how she lives with the number rather than whether the number is true. If you can make me feel the vertigo of it -- the sense that a random birthdate is a kind of coin toss nobody remembers agreeing to -- you've done the job. sorry this is long, I just figured better too much detail than too little!
