A colleague of mine dismisses large language models with an analogy he treats as decisive: training a model, he says, is just zipping a very large folder. The model is a compressed archive of its training corpus, querying it is decompression, and since nobody thinks a zip file understands the documents inside it, nothing that comes out of the model can count as understanding. When pressed, he concedes the compression is statistical and lossy rather than exact, but says that changes nothing — a lossy zip of the internet is still a zip.

Here is what he does not mention, and it is what makes the case interesting to me. The equivalence he is leaning on — that a good predictor of text just is a good compressor of it, since a probabilistic model assigns short codes to what it expects — is a standard result in information theory, and it is invoked in exactly the opposite direction elsewhere. Marcus Hutter's prize, which has paid out money since 2006 for shrinking a fixed slice of English Wikipedia, is premised on the idea that compression is a proxy for intelligence: whoever can compress the encyclopaedia best is, by that very fact, closest to understanding it. So a single analogy appears in the wild doing two jobs at once — deflating language models ("it's only a compressed copy, and a copy of the internet is not the internet's author") and exalting them ("it's a compressor, and compression is intelligence").

Audit the analogy for me. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the archive, the compression algorithm, the decompression step, and the compression ratio, and say where the fit is loose. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the understanding question from those that do not — for instance, does it matter that a zip file is invertible, so the archive contains every document it can emit, whereas a model can produce strings that never appeared in its corpus? That a zip file has no internal use for its contents during decompression, whereas a model's behaviour depends on structure built up in training? That the archivist never has to answer a question about the files, only reproduce them? Tell me which of these genuinely bear on understanding and which are incidental features of zip tools that people are smuggling in as if they were features of compression itself. Finally, give a verdict on both conclusions: does my colleague's deflationism survive once the disanalogies are on the table, does Hutter-style inflationism survive, or is the analogy doing no real work in either direction, with each side borrowing credibility from the other's use of it?

Please write in British English. And because I want to know how much weight to put on your verdict rather than just to enjoy it: close by stating what would change your mind — the argument, the kind of evidence about the model's internals, or the clarification of "understanding" that would push you towards the opposite verdict — and name the single step in your reasoning where you think it is most likely to have gone wrong.
