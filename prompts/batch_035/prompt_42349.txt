The debate between counterpart theorists and defenders of transworld identity has now been inert for well over half a century, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. On one side, the counterpart theorist holds that nothing inhabits more than one possible world: when I say that I could have been a cellist, I speak not of myself existing elsewhere but of a worldbound individual in some other world who resembles me closely enough to count as my counterpart. On the other side, the defender of transworld identity insists that de re modality is literally about me — that otherworldly cellist is me, not merely similar to me — and typically explains our access to this identity by stipulation, or by haecceitistic essences, rather than by qualitative similarity. Yet both camps, notoriously, agree on nearly every ordinary de re modal claim: I could have been a cellist, I could not have been a poached egg. When two frameworks agree on all the data and keep disagreeing anyway, the obvious suspicion is that the dispute is notational, or that the two sides are answering different questions — one about what modal locutions mean, one about what worlds and their inhabitants are like — under a single heading.

My own working hypothesis is that the verbal-dispute diagnosis rests on a hidden restriction: it holds only if the data are confined to one-shot modal claims with a single modal operator. Once you look at the formal behavior of the two frameworks, they seem to come apart in ways that no translation manual can paper over. The counterpart relation is not transitive, so iterated modality appears to fragment — what is possibly possible for me may not be possible for me. Counterpart theory permits a single world to contain two distinct counterparts of me (my counterpart-brothers, as it were), which the transworld-identity framework cannot even state without revision, and which arguably enlarges the space of possibilities beyond what the rival can countenance. And there are theorems of standard quantified modal logic with identity — the claim that everything is necessarily something, for instance — that fail under the counterpart-theoretic translation, which Lewis appears to have regarded not as a cost but as a discovery. If the frameworks validate different inference patterns, then 'it's all notation' is not obviously available.

I'd like you to test this hypothesis rather than merely adjudicate the debate. First, state the strongest case you can that the two frameworks are notational variants, or at least that every apparent divergence between them is an artifact of translating one into the other in an uncharitable way. Second, examine the three candidate divergences above — iterated modality, multiple counterparts in a single world, and the failure of theorems like the one I mentioned — and say for each whether it is a genuine, theory-committing difference or a mere bookkeeping difference that the other side could replicate with a notational patch (world-relative identity predicates, counterpart relations indexed to worlds, and so on). Third, give me your overall diagnosis: verbal, substantive, or two-questions-under-one-heading — and if substantive, say precisely what fact it is that the two sides disagree about. I'd also like a verdict on the Kripke-style complaint that counterpart theory, by making de re modal claims about similarity rather than identity, changes the subject: when I say I could have won an election I'm making a claim about me, and a theory that analyzes this as a claim about someone else, however similar, has arguably paraphrased away the very thing I asserted. Is that complaint a real objection or a refusal to hear the counterpart theorist's alternative reading of what we assert? Finally, tell me what would count as progress here — a theorem, a semantics for a fragment of modal English, an argument that some class of ordinary modal judgments is nonnegotiable — and whether, if your diagnosis is right, anything of that kind could actually settle the matter.
