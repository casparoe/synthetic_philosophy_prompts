I'm a novelist, mid-way through book two, and the novel is basically one long race to the bottom: a mid-tier tech company in a fictional city where everyone keeps doing things they privately hate because if anyone stops, a competitor fills the gap overnight — safety cuts, price wars, poaching, burnout as a business model, the whole spiral. My point: I keep coming back to that line "don't hate the player, hate the game" as a kind of thesis statement for the book, because half my characters live by it and half of them are destroyed by it, and I've realized I genuinely don't know what the sentence is claiming. It gets said to me constantly too — my agent used it last week to wave off something mildly sleazy a colleague did, my brother uses it about landlords, a guy at a party used it about his own divorce. And seperately I've been lurking on the rationalist corners of the internet enough to know there's this whole Moloch discourse about multipolar traps — the idea that nobody in the trap is evil, everyone is boxed in by incentives, and the aggregate result is still awful.

So here's the ask. Take the slogan and actually disassemble it. What are the distinct precise claims it could be making, laid out as a menu from trivial to radical? I suspect there's an empty descriptive reading (people respond to incentives, wow) and then some genuinely load-bearing ones — especially the one where it's not an excuse at all but a claim about replaceability: that blame is idle because the player is fungible, stop this one and the next one fills the slot, so all the moral energy belongs at the system level. Is that reading even coherent, and does it actually follow from "someone else would do it anyway"? Is it a claim about the causal impotence of blaming, about what blame is *for*, or about who's the proper target of resentment? And on the Moloch-style reading, is there anyone left to hate — because my villain needs to be hatable, or at least the reader needs to want to shake him, and I'm worried that if I take the multipolar-trap thing seriously as an author, the whole moral grammar of the novel collapses: no villain, no blame, maybe no story. Or is that itself the interesting bit — that narrative form smuggles in the assumption that a person, not a structure, is where responsibility lives, so a novelist literally cannot tell a Moloch-shaped story without lying a little?

Give me the readings with verdicts: which are platitudes, which are defensible, which are straight-up false, and which one my characters (and my agent, and the divorce guy) are probably deploying when they say it. And be honest about whether any of the readings actually does the excusing work people want it to do — because if none of them do, I think I've found my ending.
