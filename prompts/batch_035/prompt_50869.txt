Write an essay, in British English, on the question of what exactly the Allais paradox refutes — if it refutes anything at all.

Consider the standard presentation. A person is offered a choice between option A, a certain payment of £1 million, and option B, which gives an 89% chance of £1 million, a 10% chance of £5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing; most people choose A. The same person is then offered option A′, an 11% chance of £1 million and an 89% chance of nothing, against option B′, a 10% chance of £5 million and a 90% chance of nothing; most people choose B′. Taken together, these choices violate the independence axiom of von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility theory, since the two choice problems differ only in what happens on the 89 tickets that the two options share.

Your essay should do the following:

1. Explain precisely which claim is under threat. Is the paradox aimed at the independence axiom as a specific constraint on preferences over lotteries, or at a deeper separability idea — that how one values what a gamble delivers on one part of the state space should be independent of what it delivers elsewhere? Make clear why this distinction matters to how serious the paradox is.

2. Assess the redescription reply, on which the fault lies with the formalism rather than with the chooser: expected utility theory individuates outcomes too coarsely, because £1 million received for certain is arguably a different outcome from £1 million received as one possible upshot of a gamble, for anyone who cares about regret, disappointment, or the solace of certainty. Say whether this is a principled repair or whether it threatens to make the theory unfalsifiable — if outcomes may be subdivided at will, could anything ever count as a counterexample?

3. Consider a theory that abandons independence directly, such as Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, which introduces a risk function that weights probabilities before averaging. Does such a theory vindicate the Allais responder, or merely systematise her preferences? Explain whether there is a genuine difference between these two ambitions, and where you think the burden of proof lies between them.

4. Discuss the dynamic version of the problem. If the choices are embedded in a sequence at which the agent can renege on her earlier plan, the Allais responder appears forced either into dynamic inconsistency or into a resolute policy of committing in advance and refusing to re-optimise at the later choice point. Say which horn you would grasp, and what this shows about the normative standing of independence.

Reach and defend a verdict: should independence be abandoned as a requirement of rationality, or should the Allais preferences be treated as an error? If you opt for the error verdict, sketch what an adequate error theory would have to accomplish — it must do more than label the preferences irrational; it must explain why they are so intuitive without conceding that they track something of genuine value. Do not end on a non-committal note.
