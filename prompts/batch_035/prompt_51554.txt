Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The purpose of the case is to test whether Albert Camus's case against capital punishment and his case for limited revolutionary violence can be derived from one coherent set of premises, or whether they rest on independent premises that merely happen to share an author; the test only works if the scenario is built so that both sets of premises apply to it at full strength.

The scenario must depict an execution carried out by a resistance movement — say, a cell in an occupied country that puts a proven informer before a clandestine tribunal and shoots him at dawn — which satisfies every condition Camus, in the 1957 essay "Reflections on the Guillotine," treats as what makes judicial execution worse than private murder: premeditation and ceremony rather than passion (Camus calls capital punishment "the most premeditated of murders"); advance notice and a waiting period during which the condemned is at his executioners' mercy; certainty of outcome; and the closing-off of any possibility of repentance, atonement, or remission, given that no clandestine tribunal can reverse itself. At the same time, the scenario must satisfy the conditions Camus places on defensible rebel violence in The Rebel: the executioners act within a limiting principle rather than in the service of a totalizing end; the condemned has himself been responsible for deaths, rather than being an innocent or a hostage; and the executioners expose themselves to commensurate risk — they would face the firing squad if captured, and none of them kills from safety, in the spirit of Camus's Russian terrorists who offered their own lives as payment for the lives they took.

Constraints: the scenario must be historically realistic, a single paragraph, and must not dissolve the tension by stipulation. In particular, do not make the condemned secretly innocent or wrongly convicted, do not make the executioners sadistic, fanatical, or intoxicated with a future utopia, and do not make the tribunal a sham — the cell's procedure should be as careful and honest as an underground procedure can be.

After the scenario, deliver four things. First, the walkthrough: for each of Camus's anti-execution conditions and each of his rebel-violence conditions, verify that the case satisfies it, and show which of his premises, applied consistently, condemn the killing and which save it — keeping the premises from the two texts labeled separately. Second, identify the single feature of the case that does the work; my candidates are (a) legitimacy of authority — a state that monopolizes violence versus insurgents who have none to delegate, (b) the risk asymmetry — the state's executioner cannot lawfully be executed, while the partisan stakes his life, and (c) the role of time — the condemned's months of waiting versus the immediacy of combat; say which candidate it actually is, or argue for a fourth. Third, modify the case so that the identified feature is removed or inverted while everything else is held fixed — for instance, restore combat immediacy, or remove the partisans' exposure to risk — and report whether Camus's two doctrines then give the same verdict; if no single modification brings them into agreement, say so, since that too is a result. Fourth, state the strongest objection a defender of Camus would raise to the case as a test — for example, that the essay's real anti-death-penalty work is done by the empirical premise about deterrence rather than by any of the conditions I listed, or that the rebel's willingness to die was never a justification of killing but only a constraint on it, so that the two texts were never meant to compose into a single doctrine. Do not adjudicate the death penalty itself; the case is a probe of doctrinal consistency, not of abolition.
