I've been following consciousness discussions in the rationalist blogosphere for over a decade — the IIT wars, consciousness-as-moral-patient threads, the occasional "actually, Russellian monism quietly wins" post — and I've become sympathetic enough to panpsychism that I want to write a serious long-form series on whether the combination problem actually sinks it. Assume I have roughly forty hours over the next six months, a decent background in physics and probability theory, but none in phenomenology.

Please act as a professional philosopher of mind who takes panpsychism seriously without being committed to it, and think through the prioritization before committing to an order. The 'combination problem' is really a family of problems, and I want you to tell me which one most deserves my forty hours and why. To fix terms: by the subject combination problem I mean how micro-level subjects could sum into a single macro-level subject — why there is one experiencer writing this rather than billions of them; by the quality or 'palette' problem I mean how a fixed palette of simple micro-qualia could generate the full range of familiar macro-experience; by the structural combination problem I mean how a micro-experiential ontology whose causal and structural profile mirrors physics could nonetheless account for the apparent structure of experience — temporal unity, a bounded field, intentional directedness; and by the boundary problem I mean what determines which micro-subjects compose which macro-subjects, and why it's the neurons in my skull rather than my brain plus the skull itself. Please also say whether some cross-cutting framing does better — for instance, treating all four as instances of a single constraint: that a panpsychist theory of composition must vindicate the reality of ordinary subjects while denying they are ontologically fundamental.

Structure your response as follows: (1) 'Stakes and criteria' (~150 words), stating what makes a sub-problem worth my time and what the trade-offs between importance, tractability, and neglectedness are here; (2) 'The ranking, defended' (~450 words), including the dependency structure between the sub-problems — which ones presuppose answers to which others; (3) 'Best use of forty hours' (~200 words), given my background; (4) 'What would revise the ranking' (~150 words), i.e. what developments in physics, neuroscience, or philosophy would reorder the list. I care much more about the reasoning than the list itself: tell me what makes a problem worth the years, not merely that it's unresolved.
