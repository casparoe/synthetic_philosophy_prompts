Two members of my reading group got into an argument about whether the no-free-lunch theorems have any real bite, and I couldn't referee it. The occasion was a paper on model selection, but the argument drifted from machine learning into Hume. It went like this. (1) Priya said the theorems show that, averaged over all possible worlds, no learning algorithm beats any other, so our preference for methods that have worked in the past is ultimately just fashion: our favorite algorithms succeed only because the world happens to be the kind of place where they succeed, and we have no principled reason to expect that to continue. (2) Sam replied that this gets the theorems backwards: they are proved under a uniform measure over possible target functions, and the very fact that some algorithms generalize on real data shows the actual world is not uniform. So the theorems are not a license for despair but a demonstration that any success at induction presupposes substantive assumptions about the world's structure — which is just Hume's point made precise. (3) Priya said that if so, the theorems are trivially true and unfalsifiable: "induction works whenever the world is inducible" is no news, and Sam has dressed up Hume's problem of induction in mathematical notation without solving it. Worse, she said, Sam's appeal to the world's actual non-uniform structure is circular: we only believe the world has stable structure because induction has worked so far, and taking that as evidence assumes the very thing at issue. (4) Sam said that demanding a non-question-begging justification of induction is itself a demand for a free lunch — a validation of our priors from nowhere — and that the honest position is that we must use some priors, can rank them by past performance, and cannot do better; that is not an answer to Hume, but it is the only kind of answer there could be. Priya answered that ranking priors by past performance is precisely the circularity she flagged, so Sam has conceded her point while pretending to answer it. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is sound, and where it goes wrong if it does; then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
