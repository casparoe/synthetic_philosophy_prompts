Our seminar this term is on suffering-focused ethics, and we've been circling the standard distinction between strong negative utilitarianism — the view that positive welfare has no moral weight at all, a distinction I've seen attributed to Toby Ord — and the weak version, on which happiness does matter but no amount of it can outweigh the very worst suffering. Nobody in the class wanted to defend the strong version, since it apparently implies that painlessly annihilating a world containing any suffering whatsoever would be an improvement. Most of us happily signed on to the weak version: it seemed like a humane compromise, a way of taking extreme suffering with full seriousness without becoming what one classmate called a benevolent world-exploder. But I've since put together an argument that seems to show the weak version is unstable — that the very intuition that makes it attractive quietly drags it down into the strong one — and I can't locate the slippage. The argument runs as follows. (1) Weak NU holds that there is a threshold of suffering, roughly at the level of torture, such that no amount of happiness can outweigh an episode of suffering at or above the threshold, while any episode below it can be outweighed by enough happiness. (2) Now consider an episode of "near-torture," just barely below the threshold. (3) By (1), each occurrence of near-torture can be outweighed by some amount of happiness. (4) Outweighing is additive: if each occurrence can be outweighed, then a thousand occurrences together can be outweighed by the concatenation of the corresponding happinesses, since adding a block of happiness that compensates a given suffering cannot make a world worse, and so cannot do so however many times you repeat it. (5) Yet a thousand episodes of near-torture amount to vastly more suffering than a single episode of full torture — plausibly more suffering than a lifetime of it. (6) Whatever happiness suffices to outweigh a greater suffering also suffices to outweigh a lesser one. (7) So some amount of happiness outweighs torture, contradicting (1); therefore the threshold reading of weak NU is incoherent, and the intuition that torture cannot be outweighed forces lexicality at every magnitude: even a single pinprick cannot be outweighed by any happiness. (8) But a view on which a pinprick cannot be outweighed by any amount of bliss entails that a world containing one pinprick and unbounded ecstasy is worse than an empty world — the world-exploder conclusion my entire class rejected the strong view for.

I've stared at this for a week. My instinct is that something goes wrong between (4) and (6) — perhaps in the slide from "each episode can be outranked" to "the collection can be outranked," or in the assumption in (5) that suffering adds up across episodes into something commensurable with a single intense torture — but every time I try to say exactly what, it sounds like special pleading, because I'd be denying an aggregation principle that seems to hold everywhere else in ethics. And I'd rather keep (1) than keep (4); the thought that bliss could genuinely compensate a torture victim feels like giving up on the suffering-focused outlook entirely, whereas additivity feels like mere bookkeeping. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost — both in the immediate case and in nearby ones, since I assume the same move will have consequences for how the view handles many small sufferings spread across many lives versus one enormous suffering concentrated in a single life?
