Switzerland amends its federal constitution only through a double gate. Whether the amendment originates with parliament, which must submit any constitutional change to a mandatory referendum, or with citizens themselves, who can force a vote by collecting 100,000 signatures, it takes effect only if approved by a majority of individual voters nationwide and, separately, by a majority of the cantons — where each canton's own popular result counts as that canton's single vote, and the six half-cantons count half a vote each. The two registers sit oddly together. In the first, every citizen weighs equally. In the second, a voter in Uri, a canton of fewer than forty thousand inhabitants, carries roughly forty times the weight of a voter in Zürich, a canton of around one and a half million; and the six half-cantons are counted as half a community each, as though a community, unlike a person, could be half a member. The registers can also diverge: in 2013 a constitutional amendment won about 54 percent of the national vote and was nonetheless defeated because a majority of cantons voted against it — and in describing such an outcome we say, without irony, that the people rejected it.

Yet the very same order treats ordinary federal statutes on a different footing. A federal law stands unless 50,000 citizens, or eight cantons, demand a referendum on it, and the resulting vote is decided by a simple majority of the voters alone; no cantonal majority is asked for. So in 2004 the statute creating federal maternity benefits entered into force over the objection of a majority of cantons. The pattern, in other words, is that the concurrence of the cantons is required precisely when the constitution — the framework that constitutes the federation as one polity — is at stake, and is not required when ordinary legislation is at stake, even though it is the statutes that govern citizens' daily lives.

I cannot work out what theory of democratic authority this presupposes. The practice seems to rest on two pictures at once: the people as an aggregate of individuals, each with an equal claim to be heard, and the people as a union of communities, in which individuals count only through membership in territorial units of wildly unequal size. The same citizens are counted in both registers in the same vote, and the outcome is attributed to "the people" under both descriptions. Is that a coherent compound conception of the demos — the people as a federation, jointly constituted by persons and by member communities — or merely an unstable compromise between incompatible conceptions?

I would like you to do four things. First, reconstruct the implicit theory: what view of the people, and of what makes an exercise of constituent or legislative power legitimate, does the double gate, and its selective absence for statutes, make sense under? In particular, is there a principled story about why the deeper the change — the more it touches what constitutes the federation as a single polity — the more the people must speak as communities rather than as individuals? Second, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with. Does anything in it rule out the view that the people simply is the majority of individual citizens, on which the statute-level referendum is the more faithful expression of the popular will and the cantonal register a deviation from it? Or is the practice underdetermined between, say, a compact among founding communities, a counter-majoritarian check attached to a majoritarian core, and a corporate theory of the people — and if so, would anything about how the institutions actually behave, or how the registers have diverged, decide among them? Third, tell me whether the two registers are consistent with one another. Can one hold both that each citizen's vote must weigh equally, which the national register seems to presuppose, and that citizens count, in the constitutional register, only through communities of unequal size? Or does the practice force a choice, and if so, which is the more natural thing to give up: the equal-weight reading of the first register, or the communitarian reading of the second? Finally, say what would have to change, in the practice's own terms, if the underlying view were replaced by straight aggregative majoritarianism and the cantonal register abolished: what exactly would be lost, and would the loss be a loss of democratic legitimacy or only of federal balance?
