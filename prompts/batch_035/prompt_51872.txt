I've been reading econblogs and rationalist forums since around 2010, and one thing that's always annoyed me is that everyone treats Hume's essays on money as either a triumphant early quantity theory or a naive one that Keynes supposedly demolished, and nobody actually runs the careful mapping on the one episode we all lived through. So that's the assignment: analyze quantitative easing after 2008 through the lens of Hume's essays "Of Money" and "Of the Balance of Trade," and tell me where the theory explains the episode, where it's just silent, and where it actively misdescribes what happened.

The salient facts, so we're working from the same page (I know you can't look things up): before the crisis the Fed's balance sheet was roughly $900 billion; by 2015 it was about $4.5 trillion, built by buying bonds with newly created bank reserves, and consumer price inflation stayed low the whole time — mostly under 2 percent, definately not the four- or five-fold increase a naive money-doubling-prices-doubling rule would predict. Most of those reserves just sat at the Fed (which by then paid interest on them), and Japan had already run the same experiment in the 90s and 2000s with even less to show for it. Then, when inflation finally arrived everywhere in 2021–22, it came after a much larger expansion during covid, and people blamed supply chains and fiscal transfers at least as much as money creation.

What I want is the disciplined mapping, not vibes. First, decide what plays the role of money in Hume's thought experiment — the one where every coin in a nation is magically doubled overnight — is it the monetary base, the reserves, or some measure of what's actually changing hands, and note that Hume himself gives you a distinction you might need here, between money that circulates and money that lies dead in piles. He also has that bit where freshly injected money is beneficial during the interval before prices catch up, which sounds almost Keynesian — I want you to use it, but then tell me honestly whether it's doing real work or whether it's a rescue device that lets the theory absorb any outcome. Second, what does his mechanism actually explain here — my guess is it at least explains why creating trillions in bank reserves didn't make anyone richer in real terms — and where is he simply silent (he never has to deal with a central bank paying interest so the new money never circulates; he has no concept of velocity as a free parameter, which is probably where the whole thing breaks). Third, verdict: is 2008–2015 a confirmation of Hume, an anomaly for him, or a case his framework never anticipated, and would any other model you know of done better — say a crude monetarist one, or a Keynesian liquidity trap story. Don't force it; if the honest answer is that the quantity theory only works once you bolt on velocity and expectations, and Hume has neither, say that. I'd rather here that than a rescue job.
