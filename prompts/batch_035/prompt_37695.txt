I am a federal district judge who has imposed sentences for over two decades. My working vocabulary is the vocabulary of sentencing law: aggravating and mitigating factors, the tension between specific and general deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, restitution, proportionality, and the requirement that a sentence reflect the seriousness of the offense and the history and characteristics of the defendant. I have no formal training in philosophy. My entire exposure to the free will debate is a public exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso that a law clerk urged on me, and I want it translated into terms I can actually work with.

Restate each philosopher's position in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Dennett's position, so far as I understand it, holds that a modest notion of desert survives determinism: people can be held responsible because they are, roughly, sensitive to reasons and capable of self-control in a way that sanction can strengthen, and the desert at stake is the everyday kind worth wanting rather than an entry in a metaphysical ledger. Caruso's, so far as I understand it, denies that anyone ever deserves punishment in the basic sense — suffering owed to a person purely because of what they did — and, on analogy with quarantine, would justify confinement and treatment only on forward-looking grounds: the danger the person poses, subject to a duty of care toward the person confined, with the measure of the restriction set by the threat rather than by the gravity of the wrong.

Then give me a residue report, position by position: which of each philosopher's claims translate cleanly into the purposes I actually weigh at sentencing; which translate only with distortion, and precisely what the distortion is; and which claims have no expression in my vocabulary at all. Attend especially to three questions that matter to me. First, is the basic desert Caruso rejects the same notion as the desert my sentencing framework presupposes, or are these different notions in similar clothing? Second, does the condemnatory, expressive function of a sentencing hearing — the wrongdoer publicly called to account — have any home in Caruso's model, or does it simply vanish? Third, what happens to proportionality if the measure of a sanction shifts from seriousness of offense to expected danger? Conclude by telling me whether the residue points to something the philosophers' debate is missing that sentencing practice already understands, or to something sentencing practice is missing that the debate has seen clearly.

Be candid about your confidence throughout, and close by stating what would change your verdict on that final question: what argument, or what empirical finding about deterrence, blame, or the effects of condemnation, would move you to the opposite answer — and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
