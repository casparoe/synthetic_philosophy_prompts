Background: I do risk reporting at an insurance company, so I spend my days around people throwing percentages at single events that haven't happened yet. Something has been bugging me for a while and I can't find a word for it. When our model says "70% chance this claim is fraudulent" and it turns out not to be, nobody in the room says the 70% was wrong — the answer is always "it was a good number, the coin just landed badly," and the only way to audit the model is over a long run of similar cases. But when my sister's oncologist told her "90% chance the treatment works" and she turned out to be in the unlucky 10%, the family, the second-opinion doctor, even my sister herself, all talked as if the 90% had somehow been shown to be wrong about her, and quoting the success rate from clinical trials didn't feel like a full rebuttal. Same grammatical shape — a number pinned to one unrepeatable event — but in one context holding the number answerable to the single outcome sounds like a category error, and in the other it sounds completely natural. A bookmaker feels like a third thing again: his odds get treated as statements about his future balance sheet, not about the match at all.

I've tried out some candidate explanations and none quite fit. It's not the gambler's fallacy — nobody involved thinks past cases change the odds. A colleague muttered something about "that's just frequentism versus Bayes," but that doesn't seem right either: the disagreement I'm describing shows up between two people who agree on the interpretation, e.g. two Bayesians arguing about whether the doctor "got it wrong" about my sister. And I vaguely remember reading that courts get instructed not to convict on a bare "the probability he did it is very high," which smells related, but that looks like it's about evidence rather than about who's allowed to say "the number was wrong" after the fact.

So: does this asymmetry have an established name or literature attached to it? I want more than a label, though — if there's a concept, tell me how it's carved: what exactly makes the weather-model 70% immune to the single outcome while the doctor's 90% isn't (is it who controlled the generating process? the stakes? whether a human had to look the patient in the eye?), and how it relates to the older fight about whether a probability for a one-off event means anything at all. If nothing established fits cleanly, say so, and then coin a term with a real definition and run it through the boundary cases: the fraud model, the oncologist, the bookmaker, and a jury told "there's a 95% probability the defendant was at the scene" based only on location data. You're a philosopher of probability, so please think carefully and maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate framings before committing to one — what I want is the carving of the joint, not just its name. Also fine to tell me the whole pattern dissolves under scrutiny, but you'd have to show why, since from where I sit it looks pretty robust.
