Take the death of Sherlock Holmes — the real one, in 1893, when Conan Doyle sent him over the Reichenbach Falls in "The Final Problem" — and analyze it through the paradox of fiction. Quick recap of what's common knowledge: readers were genuinely upset, outrage and grief piled up for the better part of a decade, and Doyle eventually caved — first with Hound of the Baskervilles in 1901-02 (set before the death, so a loophole rather than a reversal), and then with "The Empty House" in 1903, which straight-up resurrected him.

I know the standard theories of the paradox and don't need a survey of them: illusion theory (readers momentarily believe or half-believe), thought theory (the emotions just don't require the existence beliefs we thought they did), and Walton's make-believe account (the grief is a quasi-emotion; it's fictional that we're mourning). What I want is the actual mapping work, done both directions:

1. Take each of the three theories and say precisely what it claims the readers in 1893 had, moment by moment, when they mourned a man they knew never existed. Where does each one fit the case cleanly, and where does it start creaking?

2. Here's the part I actually care about, and where I suspect the standard treatments go silent: the grief produced *behavior*. People wrote furious letters, cancelled their Strand subscriptions, and kept up pressure for ten years until an actual human being undid the death. The paradox of fiction is almost always posed about inert affect — crying at a movie. But this is action-guiding grief, including the distinctly social behavior of grieving in public and demanding a retcon. Does any of the three theories have anything to say about that, or do they only explain the tears? If a theory only explains the tears, is it explaining the grief or just a symptom of it?

3. There's also a wrinkle real mourning doesn't have: the anger at Doyle. In ordinary grief there's usually no one to blame for the death, but here the murderer and the god of the universe were the same person, and the mourners knew it. Some of the reaction reads less like bereavement and more like moral condemnation of an author. Can any of the theories absorb directed, desert-involving anger toward a real person for a fictional act — and if thought theory can, does it survive the cost of admitting you can wrong people in fiction?

4. Now run it the other way: what does the episode do to the paradox of fiction as a going concern? Is it a confirmation (a vivid instance), an anomaly the theories never anticipated, or evidence that the paradox was framed too narrowly from the start — as a puzzle about private affect when the interesting phenomenon was collective, public, and political? And if you patched a theory to cover the letters and the subscriptions and the pressure campaign, would the patch make it worse at the original puzzle of tears at a made-up death?

A couple of guardrails: don't hang anything on the shakier trivia — I've heard the black-armbands-in-mourning story is dubious, so leave it out — and don't pretend a theory says something it doesn't just to keep the mapping tidy. Where a theory is silent, say so.

Format: flowing prose, maybe 800 words, no bullet lists. Then a final machine-readable line, exactly like this:

Verdict: [the single theory that comes out best on this episode, or "none of them", followed by at most ten words of why]
