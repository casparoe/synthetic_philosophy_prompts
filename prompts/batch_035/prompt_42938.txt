I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on philosophy of economics that this semester pairs Keynes's Treatise on Probability with his later economic writings, and I've become fixated on a puzzle about how modern central banks forecast. Since 1996, the Bank of England's quarterly Inflation Report has carried a fan chart for inflation and output growth: a density forecast with a central projection, bands of subjective probability widening over the horizon, and, in most quarters, a visible skew, with the whole thing conditioned on assumptions the committee states as points — for instance, that policy rates follow the path priced into financial markets. Yet the very same publications handle the genuinely discrete contingencies — a disorderly exit from a trading bloc, a fiscal crisis abroad, a banking-sector event — in purely narrative terms, as risks to the outlook or as sketched scenarios, and never attach a number to them. And the skew itself is curious: it encodes the committee's qualitative judgment that the risks lie, say, to the downside, and prints that judgment in the numerical dress of an asymmetric distribution.

What I want to know is what view of probability and uncertainty this half-quantified practice presupposes. The obvious candidate is Keynes's: that probabilities are logical relations between a proposition and the evidence, that some of them are non-numerical and only partially ordered, so that no density can be honestly written down where the argument is too thin, and that the weight of the evidence behind an assessment is something distinct from the probability itself — which would explain why a fan chart that is confident about the shape of next year's output distribution says nothing at all about the odds of the event everyone in the room is actually worried about. But I can't tell whether the practice strictly needs that view, or merely sits comfortably within it, since I can imagine a thoroughgoing subjective Bayesian defending the same output as a communication convention: a committee could hold sharp priors over everything, including the discrete contingencies, and still choose to publish densities only where it expects to be scored on them. So please do three things for me. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice as I've described it — treating the density for continuous outcomes, the point-valued conditioning assumptions, the skew, and the refusal to number the scenarios as one package — and say whether its parts presuppose a consistent view. Second, mark off what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and locate where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that the Keynesian logical interpretation, a subjective Bayesian view, and perhaps even a measurement-convention view would each license it. Third, tell me what would actually change in the published practice if the presupposed Keynesian view were swapped for a straightforwardly Bayesian one: would we get explicit scenario weights, coherent joint distributions across the discrete events and the continuous paths, public updating after each data release — and would anything be lost that the current mixed practice delivers, such as the honesty of refusing to pretend that a thin argument supports a number? If it helps, assume the reader of my paper knows the Treatise's central claims but has never opened an Inflation Report, and that I want to argue that the practice is underdetermined rather than that it vindicates Keynes — but push back if you think that's not defensible.
