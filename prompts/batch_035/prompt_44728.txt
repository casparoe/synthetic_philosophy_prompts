I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology — twelve students, one ninety-minute session — and in two weeks we reach imprecise probabilities. The readings will already have covered the standard motivation, so I don't need the entry-level point re-dramatized: everyone will have seen Ellsberg-style cases, where people bet on the urn with the known composition but refuse both sides of an even-money bet on the urn with unknown composition, and will have heard the argument that no single probability function captures that pattern. What I want instead is an activity built around dilation, which the syllabus treats in half a page and which I suspect they have not felt. The setup I have in mind: an event A whose probability is only known to lie between 1/4 and 3/4 (say, the proportion of red balls in an unseen urn); a fair coin flip C that is independent of A; and a derived event E that equals A if the coin lands heads and equals not-A if it lands tails. Then the probability of E is exactly 1/2 on every prior in the set, but conditioning on either coin outcome dilates one's credence in E back out to the full interval from 1/4 to 3/4 — and the striking part is that students can foresee this in advance: whichever way the coin lands, they will end up strictly less precise than they were before they looked.

Design the full session around that structure. Give me rules, physical materials (coins, cards, colored balls or envelopes), and a payoff scheme using tokens or a small amount of course credit rather than money. Include a timeline that fits ninety minutes and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal machinery: representing a person's state as a set of probability functions, updating by conditioning each function in the set, and dilation proper as the phenomenon where conditioning shrinks or widens intervals regardless of which outcome is learned. The emotional arc I'm hoping for is that participants are comfortable taking either side of an even-money bet on E before the coin is flipped, then watch the flip happen, and then find themselves unwilling to take either side at the very same odds — while being unable to claim the coin told them anything, since it was fair and independent all along. Close with a forced-choice stage in which students who now hold the diluted interval on E must either accept an even-money bet on E or pay a small sure cost to avoid betting at all, so the group can see that an interval by itself does not settle what to do, and that the candidate decision rules they've read about — maximizing the minimum expected utility over the set, E-admissibility, minimax regret — visibly come apart on this exact choice, with some students having principled grounds for opposite decisions.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure mode: the two or three students who will cheerfully bet on anything and never display any ambiguity aversion at all; the sharper ones who will work out the dilated structure in advance and need to be kept useful; the risk that the token payoffs are too small to move anyone. Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief. My main worry is that betting behavior underdetermines the representational story: a student who refuses both sides of a bet might have a genuine set of priors, or a single precise prior combined with a funny utility function, and nothing that happens in the room distinguishes these. I'd like an honest note on exactly where the demonstration-from-behavior ends and the interpretation I have to assert begins.
