Write a dialogue between two philosophers — one holding a Peircean view of truth, the other a Jamesian one. The Peircean holds that truth is the belief that inquiry, indefinitely prolonged, is fated to be agreed upon by all who investigate. The Jamesian holds that a belief's truth consists in its working: in its proving useful, in its ability to guide action successfully, and in the way it meshes with our other beliefs and experiences.

Set the dialogue in a concrete circumstance rather than in the abstract. The two are serving on a committee that must decide what to say, in a public report, about a standard model in their field: a population-genetic model that assumes an infinitely large population, random mating, and no selection at any locus. Everyone on the committee knows these assumptions are false of every actual population. Yet the model organizes research, generates accurate predictions under controlled conditions, and no replacement is on the horizon — the field shows no sign of converging on anything else. The committee must vote on a sentence describing the model as true, false, or neither, and these two are the swing votes.

Requirements for the dialogue:

- The Peircean should press on what the Jamesian's "working" amounts to here: the model works for some purposes and fails for others, so usefulness alone seems unable to settle the committee's question, whereas the fated-agreement view at least explains why the model's truth is not settled now and may never be.
- The Jamesian should press back on the emptiness of fated agreement: the limit of inquiry is never reached, and there are idealized models that the field may never discard, so the Peircean criterion seems to classify them as indefinitely neither true nor false — a verdict that conflicts with how scientists actually rely on and trust such models.
- Each character should make at least one genuine concession to the other, and each should respond to the other's strongest point rather than talking past it.
- End with the two of them jointly formulating, without resolving it, one sharp question about scientific idealization that both views seem to answer badly.
