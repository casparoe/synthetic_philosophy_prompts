As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have spent years writing about public disputes between experts, and I have become puzzled by one of the rituals that surrounds them. When two respected scientists disagree in print, colleagues and commentators routinely demand that they put their money where their mouth is — and the demand is taken seriously. The tradition of token scientific wagers is old and affectionately documented: rivals stake a dollar or a bottle of wine on whether a particle will be found, whether information survives a black hole, whether a contested result will replicate; paying up decades later is reported as news and treated as a point of honor. Meanwhile, a parallel institution has grown up that I am now asked to quote in my own stories: betting markets, whose odds editors cite alongside polls and expert panels. Yet the same community that celebrates the token wager grows uneasy when the stakes become real money between the same parties — then it looks like gambling, or a conflict of interest, something a serious researcher should not do with a rival. So the wager must be small enough to remain symbolic, but must exist at all in order to prove something.

I would like to understand what theory of belief this cluster of practices presupposes. It appears to trade on the old idea that a degree of belief just is a disposition to accept certain bets — that you do not really hold a credence unless you will act on it at odds. But if that is the implicit view, the etiquette of token stakes sits oddly with it: under the very betting interpretation the practice invokes, a one-dollar bet at odds favorable to you is extremely weak evidence of a high credence, and the taboo on large stakes looks like a refusal of the test the practice itself has just declared meaningful. There is also a second presupposition that puzzles me as a reader of decision theory. If two agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree, then two celebrated experts standing in a public wager are, between them, certifying that something is epistemically defective: divergent priors, irrationality on at least one side, or beliefs held for reasons other than evidence. Yet the ritual presents the wager as charming rather than as an indictment, and presents paying up as the confirmation of a belief rather than as data about how the belief was formed.

Reconstruct for me what this set of practices — the sincerity demand, the token stakes, the honor of settlement, the citing of market odds alongside expert opinion — actually presupposes about belief, probability, and disagreement. Say which parts of the practice strictly require that theory and which would survive under rivals (for instance, a view on which the wager signals commitment to a research program rather than a credence, or serves an economic rather than an epistemic function). Say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that several theories of belief would license it just as well. And tell me what would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with the one suggested by the agreement theorem: would wagers between experts be redesigned, and with what in their place — or abandoned outright — or would the etiquette of the token bet turn out, on inspection, to be quietly tracking precisely the conditions under which the theorem fails?
