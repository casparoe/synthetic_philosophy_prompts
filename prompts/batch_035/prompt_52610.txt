I'm taking a seminar on medieval philosophy and we just spent three weeks on the kalam atomists before landing on al-Ghazālī, ending with the causation discussion in the Tahāfut where he denies that fire is the agent of the burning of the cotton — contact happens, God creates the burning. My professor keeps hammering that this isn't just a "Hume before Hume" curiosity, it's a whole metaphysics with no necessary connection anywhere, only God's habit, and the habit can break, which is exactly what makes room for miracles. To close the unit she wants a poem instead of a response paper and I volunteered, so here I am. Write me a villanelle on al-Ghazālī's occasionalism.

Here's the part I want the form to do the work of: the two refrains should be the two halves of the view, one line doing the denying (something on the order of: it's never the fire that burns the cotton) and one doing the asserting (God as the only true agent, everything happening again, new, now). And the repetition itself should carry the argument — each time the same refrain comes back, that return should read as a fresh creation, the "same" event recreated rather than a law reasserting itself, with our certainty about it exposed as habit rather than necessity. Use the middle stanzas for other causal pairs of the kind he goes through (fire and burning, water and quenching, bread and nourishment, the sun and light — whatever you can make sing), and let at least one stanza voice why the habit feels so much like certainty without the poem becoming either a sermon or a smug debunking — the piety and the philosophy have to hold together the way they do for him. Close with the two refrains finally meeting in the last couplet, since that's where the view actually lives: the fire touches the cotton, and something happens. Someone in my class who did the reading should be able to say exactly why the villanelle's obsessive returns are the argument and not just a decoration; and my professor, who works on Ash'arite metaphysics, should not wince at anything.
