My two roommates have been at each other's throats for literally two years about the dust specks thing, and I've decided the only cure is forcing them to write something together. You probably know the case: either 3^^^3 people each get a tiny dust speck in their eye (a few seconds of mild annoyance, no injury, done) or one person is tortured for fifty years. No third option. Roommate A is an aggregative consequentialist: disvalue adds up across persons, and 3^^^3 — that's Knuth triple-arrow notation, a number so large it makes the atom count of the universe look like zero — times even the tiniest bad swamps any single torture, so you pick the torture. Roommate B thinks that's monstrous. His official line is deontological: small harms can't be summed into a permission to inflict a great harm on someone, no matter the number. But he also keeps drifting to a different, sneakier claim — that a dust speck isn't a harm in the morally relevant sense at all, in which case there's literally nothing on the other side of the ledger to aggregate. I've noticed A attacks the side-constraint version and B defends the no-harm version, so half the time I'm not even sure they're fighting about the same thing.

Write the joint statement they'd both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) the claims both accept — about what the choice actually is, about the moral phenomenology on each side (A should concede the felt awfulness of her verdict, B should concede the felt awfulness of dismissing an incomprehensible number of people), and about what would NOT settle it (each of them thinks the other side's intuition-pumping is rigged somehow, and I want that in writing too); (2) their precise disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — including whether the speck-isn't-a-harm move is part of B's real position or a fallback he'd abandon under pressure, which I genuinely want them pinned down on; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether disvalue is continuous and commensurable across kinds (can a mountain of mild annoyaries ever trade against one agony), another is whether numbers-of-persons-affected has any moral weight seperate from the badness in each life, but they may disagree with my list and I want their version; (4) for each crux, a test case or argument both sides agree bears on it — things like sorites ladders (a million people with year-long migraines vs one tortured person — is there a rung where each switches, and do the switch points line up?), veil-of-ignorance framings, single-person versions where all the specks land on one guy — with each roommate's prediction written down in advance so they can't backfit later. Also, if part of their fight turns out to be verbal — say, A using "worse" for choice-worthiness and B using it for some affective ranking — call that out explicitly.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, cruxes and predictions after that.
