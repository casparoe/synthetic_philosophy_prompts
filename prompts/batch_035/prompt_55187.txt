I've followed the recurring rationalist-community debates about whether philosophy makes progress or is a treadmill that mostly generates confusions to be dissolved, and I keep noticing a concrete fact about the discipline that both sides treat as trivia: philosophy departments teach their own history as core curriculum. A philosophy program will spend whole courses on Descartes, Hume, and Kant, treat their arguments as live moves rather than museum pieces, and the profession even contains career tracks defined by engagement with a single dead figure — being a "Kant scholar" is a normal job, while no physics department employs a Newton scholar. Physics undergraduates never open the Principia; chemistry programs don't assign the phlogiston theorists; biology gets by with a nod to Darwin. What view of philosophical progress does this curricular practice presuppose? That philosophy accumulates no results which render its past obsolete, the way the Principia was rendered obsolete? That refutation in philosophy is never decisive, so old positions remain permanently live? Or something merely sociological — a scholastic tradition reproducing itself for its own sake? I'd especially like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could someone who thinks philosophy has made real, cumulative progress — formal logic, the probability calculus, decision theory dissolving some of the old puzzles — still justify the Kant curriculum coherently, or does the practice quietly presuppose that Descartes and Hume are not philosophy's phlogiston? And if the presupposed view is that philosophy's past remains live in a way physics's does not, tell me what the discipline would look like if we instead believed philosophy progresses the way the hard sciences do: which parts of the curriculum would get cut, what would replace them, and what we would lose.
