Here's something that's been bugging me. I keep running into "that's a just-so story" in arguments about evolution, and I've realized it seems to do a bunch of different jobs depending on who's saying it. Most recently I was in a discussion about why humans everywhere have music — someone floated that it helped ancestral groups bond and coordinate, maybe through singing together — and the entire reply was "that's a just-so story," which basically killed the conversation. Nobody could actually say what was wrong with the proposal beyond the label.

So what are the distinct things that accusation could actually be claiming? Some candidates I can half-articulate: that the story is unfalsifiable in principle, that it's falsifiable but nobody has tested it, that there's just no way to ever test anything about the deep past, that it ignores rival explanations like byproducts or drift (I vaguely remember some old Gould and Lewontin paper about spandrels in a venetian church that made a fuss about adaptationists ignoring byproducts and architectural constraints, but honestly I only half-remember it). But I want the full menu, layed out as an actual list: which readings are trivially true and not much of a complaint, which are a serious methodological point, which are so strong they'd rule out legitimate evolutionary science too, and which are just "I find this story unconvincing" dressed up as methodology. For each reading, say whether it actually applies to the music-and-group-bonding case, and whether it would justify the conversation-ending way the label got deployed.

One more thing I want you to adress: does the accusation cut both ways? Like, is saying "music is probably just a byproduct of something else, an auditory cheesecake" also a just-so story, or does it get a pass? If the standard only ever gets applied to adaptive explanations, that seems worth flagging as its own reading of the slogan.
