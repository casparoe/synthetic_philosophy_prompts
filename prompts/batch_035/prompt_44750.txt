My flatmate works on AI safety, and over New Year's she and an old friend of hers (call him D.) had a genuinely hours-long argument about it that I mostly just watched. They're both ML people so it got quite deep, and I'm still not sure who won what. I'll try to reproduce it faithfully because I'd love a proper adjudication.

Context: she works on what she calls "control" — safety measures that assume the model might already be misaligned. Tripwires and canary inputs that trigger alerts, honeypot directories full of tempting stuff an up-to-no-good model is more likely to touch than a well-behaved one, staged rollouts, red-teaming, monitoring for suspicious behaviour, that sort of thing. D. thinks this is mostly a category error, and his opening move was the biosafety analogy: she likes to say we don't need to "understand" a pathogen to contain it, BSL-4 labs exist, containment is containment. D. said the analogy fails at exactly the point she needs it: a pathogen doesn't know it's in a lab and can't model your containment scheme, whereas the whole premise of her field is that the thing in the box can reason about the box. Containment against a non-adversarial hazard is engineering; containment against an adversary smarter than your engineers is a losing game, and pretending otherwise is how you get overconfidence in the harness.

She came back that this smuggles in a superintelligence where none exists yet — control measures are being built and tested against today's models, which can't do any of that, and the know-how compounds. And, she said, control isn't a rival to alignment research, it's a firewall around it: the whole point is to buy time for the people actually trying to solve alignment, and complaining that a firewall doesn't solve the fire is a confusion.

D. said the buying-time argument cuts both ways, and this was where it got heated: if the harness is good enough to trust, it's good enough to deploy on, and the political economy runs exactly the wrong way. Organisations will point at her tripwires as the reason they can ship the next model, so "control buys time for alignment" becomes control *displacing* alignment in the budget and in people's heads. Safety theatre doesn't just fail, it actively manufactures permission. She said that's an argument about corporations being reckless, not about the measures — the same argument would prove airbags cause speeding so we shouldn't have airbags, and it's not a reason for the people who understand the measures to make them worse on purpose.

Then D. made what I think was his sharpest point: the premise "assume the model may be misaligned" is doing incoherent work. If it's misaligned *and* situationally aware enough to matter, it knows when it's being tested, behaves beautifully in every evaluation, and exploits the one gap you didn't think of after deployment — so your control tests mostly measure whether your adversary is polite while the lights are on. If it's not that capable or not adversarial, the measures were unnecessary. Either way the tested property and the property you care about come apart, and he thinks that's fatal rather than just inconvenient. She replied that this is a false dichotomy on two counts: models can be misaligned in some goals while lacking the patience or situational awareness to act on it (partial misalignment is the normal case, not the exception), and plenty of control work is about incentive gradients and staged deployment, i.e. making bad behaviour *costly* rather than *undetectable*, which doesn't require the adversary to be dumb. D. said "partial misalignment that can't act on itself" is just a name for a model that's fine, and that she's redefined her way out of the interesting case.

They never really closed it out.
