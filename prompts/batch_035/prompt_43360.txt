I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've gotten myself into a hole I'd like to climb out of in an organized way. It started with an argument at work: we renamed a core module, and half the team insisted the old identifier still "meant" the old thing because that meaning lived in the git history and everyone's habits, while the other half said meaning is just whatever the current type signature and docs say it is. Someone mentioned this was oddly similar to some famous thought experiment about a "Twin Earth" where water is a different chemical, and I went down the rabbit hole. Now I've read enough fragments of the debate — something about molecule-identical people whose word "water" picks out different stuff, and something about a patient who misuses "arthritis" for a thigh ailment and is wrong because of what the community means — to know there are real, distinct positions here, and no ability to tell them apart.

I run a reading group at a coworking space, about fifteen people, mostly other engineers, none of us with philosophy backgrounds. What I'd like you to write is a decision tree that starts from the Twin Earth case and walks a person to a named position on where meaning lives. The nodes should each be a concrete case someone can answer without ever having heard of Putnam or Burge or any of the labels — nothing like "do you believe meaning is external?" — and the tree must route through, at minimum, these five cases: (1) molecule-for-molecule identical subjects whose word "water" tracks different liquids; (2) the person whose community uses a term like "arthritis" correctly while they use it slightly wrong, such that they seem mistaken even though their internal state matches someone who uses it correctly; (3) the slow-switched subject, who moves to the twin planet unnoticed and, over years of drinking and talking about the other liquid, never registers any difference — does their term switch, and if so, at what point, if no internal change accompanies it; (4) the historyless duplicate, a being molecule-identical to a normal speaker but instantiated by chance with no causal past at all, who utters the same sentence — is the utterance about water, about anything; and (5) the case I actually care about for work reasons: a system that has only ever processed text about water, never seen or touched or causally interacted with any liquid, yet uses the word fluently — does its word refer to H2O, to the other liquid, to both, or to nothing.

Please build the tree from the logical space first — which factor, if any, fixes reference outside the head (the world's kind structure, the linguistic community, the causal-historical chain, or nothing), then what happens to reference over time for a switched subject, then what historyless or causally isolated speakers show — and only afterwards locate the named positions in that space. The leaves should include at least semantic internalism, the causal/natural-kind externalist picture, the communal or social externalist picture, and some two-factor view that keeps both a wide and a narrow notion of content; if you think there's a further position worth a leaf — say, one on which the text-only system's reference is genuinely indeterminate rather than absent — include it, and label any leaf nobody has defended as unnamed rather than forcing it into an existing name. Two more requirements: mark which combinations of node answers are unoccupied and say briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored; and be explicit about any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — for instance, whether any of your questions can genuinely split an internalist from a two-factorist who agrees with them about every here-and-now behavioral case, since if not, the tree should admit it rather than pretend to a distinction the cases can't carry. Keep the language accessible to people whose last philosophy exposure was an optional elective; assume we can look up nothing during the session.
