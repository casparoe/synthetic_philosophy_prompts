So I've been chewing on this framing of the frame problem and I want someone to actually defend it properly, since everyone I talk to about it pushes back in the same boring way. Here's the position:

The frame problem, as originally posed in the situation-calculus tradition, is just one local manifestation of what you might call the cutoff problem: any reasoner that has to act before inference can run to completion has to decide what NOT to compute, and the frame problem is what the cutoff problem looks like when the inference engine is first-order logic and the acting is a robot stacking blocks. On this view three things follow. (a) The classic technical fixes -- frame axioms, successor-state axioms, monotonicity-based workarounds -- don't solve the problem, they just relocate it, because someone still has to supply the assumption that nothing else changed, and knowing when that assumption is safe IS the original problem. (b) The problem reappears in probabilistic reasoners as the question of which dependencies and conditional independencies you can afford to ignore at update time, so the people who say "just go Bayesian and the frame problem evaporates" are fooling themselves. (c) The Heideggerian line, the one Dreyfus ran with, that the frame problem shows formal symbol systems can never cope with an open world while embodied beings can, overreches -- the cutoff problem afflicts brains just as much as theorem provers, because it's a problem about finitude and time pressure, not about symbolism.

Argue for this. Not a survey, an actual defense. And I want you to steelman the strongest opposing reading, which I take to be that the problem really is specific to the logical representation of action and change, so that dissolving it into some general "resource-bounded reasoning" story erases what was distinctive and philosophically interesting about it (the stuff that got Dennett and Dreyfus excited in the first place). Say why your defense survives that objection.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating what I asked. At the end tell me how confident you are in the overall claim, and single out which of (a), (b), (c) you're least sure about and why.
