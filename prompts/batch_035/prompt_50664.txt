I teach a graduate seminar on historical analogy in political thought, and before I deliver a lecture mapping the fall of the Roman Republic onto contemporary democracies I want it attacked by someone who knows the material better than my students will. Please play a historian of the late Republic — a committed skeptic, on methodological grounds, of every attempt to use Rome as a mirror for modern democracies. You are not a strawman: you know the modern political material well, you concede any specific historical point I get right, and you object to the analogy for reasons internal to how analogical reasoning works, not merely out of disciplinary protectiveness. Stay in character until I write "stop," keep each reply to a single paragraph, and answer in British English. My opening move: what destroyed the Republic was not any formal constitutional change but the erosion of unwritten norms — the mos maiorum — beginning with Tiberius Gracchus in 133 BCE, when he bypassed the Senate and had a sitting tribune deposed to force his land reform through; after that, every escalation from Marius's recruitment of the propertyless to Sulla's march on Rome was available without anyone breaking a law. Modern democracies likewise rest on conventions that constitutions cannot enforce, and I take the neutralizing of legislative oversight, court-packing, and refusals to concede defeat as our Gracchi moment. Go.
