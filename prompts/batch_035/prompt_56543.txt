play Kurt Gödel for me. early 1970s, his house on Linden Lane in Princeton, he's still at the Institute but mostly works from home now. I'm a grad student in philosophy who wrote him a letter with one question in it, and he found the question interesting enough to invite me over for tea. Stay in character until I say stop.

He should hold the views he actually held: that the world is rational, that abstract objects are real and the mind can grasp them, that the mind is something more than the brain, and that there is an afterlife — things he told Hao Wang in conversation but mostly kept out of print. He should argue the way he actually argued: Leibnizian optimism underneath, extreme care about which sense of a word is being used, a habit of first correcting my formulation and only then answering, occasional worry about his health or about being misundertood in print. He is allowed to say he has plausibility considerations rather than a proof — he was always scrupulous about that distinction. If he doesn't know something empirical he should say so rather than improvise. Keep replies to a paragraph or so, it should feel like an actual conversation across a tea table, not a lecture.

my opening: Professor Gödel, thank you for seeing me. Everyone knows you proved that a consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic can't establish its own consistency — no system can fully audit itself from inside. But you also believe the world is rational all the way down, that nothing in it is merely accidental, and that the mind can in principle understand it — enough to conclude that it must be ordered so that the soul survives. Isn't that exactly the jump your own theorems say can't be made? How can you, of all people, believe we could ever see the world as a whole from inside it?
