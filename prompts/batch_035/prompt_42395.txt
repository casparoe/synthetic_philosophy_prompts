I'm a science and ideas journalist, and for my next piece I've been going down the rabbit hole of "cognitive penetration" -- the idea that what you believe, want, or feel can literally change what you see, not just how you judge it afterwards. The hook is all those famous studies from Dennis Proffitt's lab: people wearing a heavy backpack estimate a hill as steeper than people without one, tired or out-of-shape people estimate distances as longer, people who fear heights judge a slant as steeper when standing at the top of a hill than people who don't. The pop-science headline writes itself ("your body and mood literally shape your vision"), and honestly my editor would love that headline. But apparently there's this whole literature arguing these findings are not perceptual effects at all -- the big salvo being Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl's "Cognition does not affect perception" line of work, which claims the results are better explained by post-perceptual stuff like demand effects, judgment biases, or response scaling, and not by anything touching the actual visual representation.

So here's what I want from you: make the case that the backpack/hill-steepness family of findings does NOT demonstrate cognitive penetration of perception. Argue the skeptic's side -- that these are judgment or task-demand effects, and that a reporter repeating "backpacks change what you see" as literal fact is overselling the science. Along the way, maybe sketch how a penetration proponent would want to design the study instead, and what a really clean test would look like (e.g., some kind of perceptual matching task rather than a verbal estimate).

One thing though: I'd rather print something accurate than something clean, so don't hand me a tidy takedown if the debate is messier than that. If you think the anti-penetration case is weaker for some of these findings than others, or that the skeptics' explanations (demand effects etc.) have their own evidential problems, say that plainly. And if you think I've set the question up wrong somewhere -- like if lumping all these Proffitt-style studies together is a mistake, or if the definitional stuff about what even counts as "perception" vs "judgment" makes my framing incoherent -- please just tell me straight instead of working with a confused premise. My readers will be better served by an honest "here's where the science is genuinely unsettled" than by a confident hot take. Also feel free to note where you're on shakier ground, I can take hedges (I'd rather print a hedge than a howler).
