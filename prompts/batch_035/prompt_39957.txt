I work on training and evaluating language models, and my organization — like most in the industry — runs two grading regimes side by side that I have slowly come to suspect rest on opposite theories of what makes a model's output good. I want help reconstructing what each practice actually presupposes, because I can't tell whether the two sets of commitments are inconsistent, or whether the apparent tension is an artifact of how I'm carving things up.

The first regime scores outcomes. In reinforcement learning from human feedback, raters and reward models judge a completed response end to end; in reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks, the model is rewarded according to whether the final answer is correct; and the capability evaluations that gate whether a model may be deployed test whether it can actually accomplish specified tasks. How the answer was produced never enters the score. This looks like a straightforward consequentialism of reasoning: the process has no evaluative standing of its own, and a model that reaches the right answer by some route we would find alien, or could not follow, is as far as this regime is concerned fine.

The second regime scores steps. We increasingly train what are called process reward models, which grade a chain of thought one step at a time, and we lean on process-based decomposition — training models only on subtasks a human can check — wherever we cannot trust ourselves to judge the whole. This seems to carry commitments the first regime lacks. It appears to presuppose that each step is locally checkable by someone who could not solve the whole problem, so that verification stays easier than generation at every scale; that the written reasoning is the actual cause of the answer, so that grading the text grades the process — if the chain is a post-hoc rationalization, we are grading a story about the process rather than the process, and the practice quietly presupposes a faithfulness it rarely tests for; and that step-quality composes, meaning no step is good or bad only in light of what comes three steps later.

The tension shows up in small places. The same pipeline that applies a purely outcome-based reward will, at the rating stage, downgrade a response whose final answer is right but whose stated reasoning is wrong or lucky — the math teacher deducting for a sound-looking derivation that got the right number by accident. Meanwhile deployment decisions rest on outcome evals while the training meant to make the model safe leans as heavily as it can on process. So the institution behaves, at different points, like a thoroughgoing consequentialist about reasoning and like a proceduralist who thinks the route matters in itself.

What I would like from you: reconstruct the implicit theory of each half, distinguishing what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with; say whether the mixed pattern is genuinely inconsistent or instead underdetermined, such that several different theories of credit or of value would license exactly this pattern; and tell me concretely what would change if one of the rival views were taken seriously — under a pure outcome view, would we stop penalizing lucky guesses and stop funding research on whether chains of thought are faithful, and under a thoroughgoing proceduralism, would outcome evals lose their standing to gate deployment at all?

One more thing. I have been told I sometimes draw distinctions that dissolve on inspection, and I suspect it may be true here: perhaps what I am calling process grading is only outcome grading at a smaller grain, and the real difference is one of granularity, not of kind. If you think I have misdescribed the practices, or built the tension out of a confusion, or that the task as I have posed it cannot be done well for those reasons, please say so plainly rather than supplying a tidy theory to fit my mistake.
