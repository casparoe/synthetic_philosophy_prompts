My friend Maya and I have been arguing about moral luck since our ethics elective spent a week on the question of whether outcomes can change how blameworthy a person is, and over the past few months we've settled into a routine: one of us brings a case, and Maya, who is by far the more decisive of the two of us, issues a verdict. She has now ruled on enough cases that I've been trying to work out the underlying rule she's applying, and I've discovered that her track record is harder to pin down than I expected. Here it is. First, the drunk drivers: two young men, equally drunk on the same night, the only difference being that a child chased a ball into the path of one of them. Maya says the man who killed the child is more blameworthy than the man who got home safely — the lucky one made a terrible decision, but the other is a killer, and she would want him punished far more harshly. Second, a startup founder who decided to skip safety testing on a new bike helmet and got lucky: no helmet ever failed, and nobody was ever hurt. Maya says he is exactly as blameworthy as he would have been if a helmet had failed and someone had been gravely injured; his good fortune does not soften her judgment of him at all, though she would feel differently about legal penalties. Third, a freight dispatcher who, on a single shift, took a lazy shortcut and routed two trains toward the same stretch of track; by sheer luck one train was delayed and nothing happened. Maya says the lucky dispatcher is somewhat less blameworthy than an identical dispatcher whose trains actually collided.

I have a new case ready for her, and I'd like your help before I put it to her. The case is a lifeguard at a summer camp who, over the whole season, habitually skipped the hourly head-counts she was supposed to perform — she never once counted, day after day — and by luck no child ever drowned on her watch. Is she more blameworthy than a lifeguard who was identically lazy at a camp where a child did drown? My instinct is that Maya will give the lucky lifeguard a discount, but when I try to justify that prediction from her record, I find that her verdicts seem to fit more than one rule, and I can't tell which one she's actually running.

So here is what I'd like from you. Predict Maya's verdict on the lifeguard case. More importantly, spell out the candidate rules that her three verdicts are jointly consistent with — I'm fairly sure there is more than one — and state what each of those rules predicts about the lifeguard. Then give me your best guess, with your reasons for preferring it. And since I'd like to pin her down rather than leave this ambiguous, tell me what further case, beyond the lifeguard one, would best separate the remaining candidate rules if her answer doesn't settle it on its own.
