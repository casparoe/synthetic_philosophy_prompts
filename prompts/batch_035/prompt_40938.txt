The argument below is for a term paper on computationalism, and the trouble is that it seems to prove too much in two directions at once — not just that a wall has a mind, but that my laptop has none. I can't find the flaw.

(1) A physical system implements a formal computation just in case there exists a mapping from the computation's abstract states to physical states of the system that preserves the state-transition relations of the computation, and there are no further constraints on which mappings are eligible. (2) Mappings may individuate physical states arbitrarily, run many abstract states onto coarse physical groupings, and choose clock cycles as long as we like — the point, due originally to Putnam, that a wall can be mapped onto any finite-state automaton given a long enough timescale. (3) It follows that any ordinary object with enough distinguishable macro-states implements every finite computation: this wall implements whatever program my laptop is running, and every other program besides, including every program attributed to a human brain. (4) Computationalism says that having a mind is implementing the right program: mental states are computational states. (5) If implementation is as cheap as (1)-(3) say, then the wall has every mind it is mapped onto, and computationalism, rather than explaining what distinguishes the minded from the mindless, has turned that distinction into a matter of bookkeeping. So computationalism is vacuous and false.

That is the standard triviality problem, and I know the standard repair: strengthen (1) so that only causally or counterfactually constrained mappings count — the physical state playing the role of abstract state q must be such that, had the system's input been different, its subsequent states would have differed in the way the automaton specifies. But here is where my version starts to prove too much in the other direction. (6) Suppose we adopt the counterfactual constraint on implementation. (7) Neurons are noisy: the very same physical configuration in motor cortex can drive different successors depending on modulatory context, and single-unit behavior is only loosely lawlike at the grain at which cognitive science attributes its computations. A digital computer with one flaky gate fails the same test at some clock cycle. (8) If implementation requires that sort of counterfactual fidelity, then nothing biological — and nothing built — implements anything; the repair that was supposed to keep the wall out of the club has kept everyone out. (9) But my laptop computes, and the cognitive scientists who appeal to (4) are tracking something real. So the full argument, (1) through (8), refutes itself somewhere.

Where? I can see two escape routes and don't trust either. One is to reject (1) while insisting the counterfactual constraint operates only at some eligible grain of description — but then I need an account of what makes a grain eligible that does not simply build in which systems we already wanted to count as computing, which would be to answer the question by stipulation. The other is to deny that implementation is what computationalism needs — replace (4) with some weaker claim about explanation or modeling — but then I have trouble seeing how computationalism remains a metaphysical thesis about the mind rather than a claim about how we talk. Tell me which premise to give up, and what giving it up costs; if the real flaw is an equivocation between steps — say, between how coarsely (2) lets me carve the wall and how finely (7) demands the brain behave — name it and explain why the equivocation was easy to miss. If I have to sacrifice something, I would rather keep (4) and (9) than (1); I want to know what I am paying for that preference.
