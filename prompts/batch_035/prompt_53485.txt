So I'm a PhD student in econ, mostly social learning / behavioral stuff, and somewhere over the last couple years I've drifted into a position I want stress-tested properly before I put it at the center of a chapter: I think information cascades — the classic Banerjee / Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch style story where fully rational Bayesians herd on the *wrong* action because after enough consistent predecessors everyone rationally ignores their own private signal — explain way more real-world herding than they usually get credit for. My working view is that the core of most genuine bubbles, panics, and fads is rational herding under sequential observation, with the psychological stuff (social proof, overconfidence, whatever) layered on top but not load-bearing. It's a strong claim, I know.

What would count against it? Some things I already half-worry about:

1. In the canonical models the signal is super coarse — often literally binary — and agents observe the entire action history. Real decisions are continuous-ish and people see noisy summaries of what others did. Both of these seem like they should either prevent cascades from forming or make them so fragile they break immediately, but I've kind of convinced myself it doesn't matter much in aggregate. Should it?

2. The lab experiments do produce cascades, sure, but subjects also seem to overweight their private signals relative to the Bayesian benchmark a lot of the time, which is basically the opposite deviation from what my story needs. I keep telling myself thats fine because field data is what matters anyway. Is that too convenient?

3. There are rival stories that fit the same aggregate patterns: reputation-based herding where it's optimal to ignore your signal because being wrong along with everyone is career-safe, payoff externalities, plain copying without any Bayesian updating at all, network effects that look like herding but aren't informationally. My position kind of lives on the claim that these are distinguishable, but honestly the observables might be near-identical, and I'm not sure I've ever seen an episode where people actually did the hard work of telling them apart.

So: give me a map of what should make me give this up, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which concessions can I make without abandoning the core view, and which concession is effectively surrender? Is there a reading of the evidence where the position comes out looking fine and I should double down — and if so, what does it get right that the rivals don't? And the thing I'm most scared of: I've noticed that when an episode doesn't fit (say it looks reputation-driven instead of informational) I just absorb it as "well, not every herd is a cascade." At some point that move turns the view into something that can't lose. Have I already crossed that line, and if so, what would I have to stick my neck out on — what observation out there in the world, if someone found it tomorrow, would genuinely hurt?
