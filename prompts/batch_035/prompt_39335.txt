Here's something I've been chewing on and can't quite pin down. For most of the 20th century, the American draft gave you a way out of killing people if you were a conscientious objector — but only if your objection grew out of religious training and belief, and for decades the courts read that as requiring belief in a Supreme Being. In practice this meant that a Mennonite or Quaker whose church taught pacifism basically sailed through, while some guy who'd spent four years reading history and ethics on his own, thought hard about it, and concluded that war was wrong for him, got rejected: no Supreme Being, no exemption, off to Vietnam or to prison. It wasn't until the mid-60s (Seeger, then Welsh a few years later) that the Supreme Court said a sincerely held moral or ethical belief that occupies the same place in your life as religion occupies in a believer's can count.

So here's my question: what theory of conscience was the original setup presupposing? Reconstruct the implicit view for me. Is it something like — religiously formed moral conviction is more stable or trustworthy than privately reasoned conviction, so the state can bet on it? Is it a moral testimony thing, where a community vouching for your character counts but your own judgment doesn't? Or is the state just doing epistemics on the cheap, since it can't verify sincerity, and church membership is a convenient proxy?

A few things I'd want you to actually work out rather than wave at:

1. What does the practice *strictly* presuppose versus what it merely fits well with? I suspect the real presupposition is thinner than it looks — something about verifiability rather than about religion being a better source of morals — but I'm not sure, and I'd like you to say where the practice is underdetermined enough that several different theories of moral conscience would all license it.

2. There's an apparent tension I can't resolve on my own. The very same legal tradition elsewhere refuses to treat religious belief as special — no religious tests for office, no established church. So the state lets religion do the work of identifying reliable pacifists while officially denying that religion is epistemically privileged anywhere else. Is that a real inconsistency in the presuppositions, or is the two-level thing defensible (deference to conscience in one domain, neutrality in others)?

3. What did the picture look like after Seeger and Welsh secularized the standard? The later practice seems to presuppose that secular moral conviction can do exactly the work religious conviction did — occupy the same functional slot. Which kind of suggests the state was never really betting on God, just on a certain delivery mechanism for conscience. Does that reading work, and does it tell us anything about the bigger question of whether religion is a unique moral technology or just one historically convenient container for commitments people could form without it?

4. And the flip side, which I find most interesting: the state granting exemptions at all is a bet that religion (or religion-shaped belief) reliably produces what the state wants — Quakers who won't shoot anyone and, plausibly, citizens who don't lie on draft forms. What view of the relation between religious formation and moral behavior does that bet presuppose? Is it the view that religious communities produce good behavior through accountability and habit, or something stronger about belief itself?

Where you can, tell me what would have to change in the practice if you swapped in the rival view — like, if the real presupposition was always just verifiability, would the exemption system have been structurally identical if it had instead exempted anyone who could demonstrate, say, years of documented pacifist activism? And was the original arrangement defensible under the view you reconstruct, or was it basically prejudice with paperwork? I'm genuinely torn on that last one and would take a considered verdict either way.
