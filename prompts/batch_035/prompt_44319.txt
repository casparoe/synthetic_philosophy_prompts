I hold a middle position in the "should the numbers count?" debate over rescue cases. In Taurek's spirit, I reject aggregation: there is no moral quantity that sums across persons. But unlike Taurek, I think we should save the five rather than the one, on the grounds that each of the five has a claim to rescue no weaker than the one's claim — so although the one's claim is never outweighed by any single opposing claim, it is matched by five claims each equally strong, and it is these pairwise comparisons, not a sum, that decide the case.

What would count against this position? I want the cases it ought to handle but mishandles: for instance, where the one would lose more than each of the many (a longer life ahead, or a graver injury); where the one is the rescuer's friend; or where the same pairwise logic that warrants saving the five seems, if applied consistently, to license imposing a small harm on each of many to benefit the one, or worse. I also want to know whether there is any possible case in which the pairwise view and straightforward aggregation actually diverge — or whether my position is just aggregation with the addition hidden inside it. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which of them a committed defender of the view would concede versus explain away, and give an honest verdict on whether the view still earns its keep if it turns out to be a mere redescription of the aggregative answer.
