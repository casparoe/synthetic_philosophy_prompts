Three months ago my grandmother died, and I inherited two things at once: a substantial sum of personal wealth, for the first time in my life, and a trusteeship on the family foundation she built. Questions I used to treat as seminar material — whether inherited wealth can be reconciled with anything like fair equality of opportunity, whether a founder's wishes should bind generations she would never meet — are now questions I must vote on. The foundation's board meets in June to settle its long-term structure, and I would like to circulate a decision memo beforehand.

The foundation holds roughly seventy million dollars. The board is split. My mother and her siblings favor perpetuity: they point out that the charter speaks of the foundation as a lasting institution, that our family's continuing involvement is itself a form of stewardship, and that spending the corpus down would be, in their words, a failure of nerve dressed up as principle. Three of my cousins and I lean toward a deliberate spend-down over perhaps twenty-five years, ending in dissolution. Meanwhile, the estate attorney handling my grandmother's affairs has separately proposed that my own bequest be placed in a multi-generational trust structured to minimize estate taxes across several generations, and I have not yet given him an answer.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the board. It should contain: a clear recommendation on perpetuity versus a timed spend-down (I am open to either conclusion, including a hybrid, but not to a deferral); an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, which I take to be the entitlement-style claim that the assets were unambiguously my grandmother's, legitimately acquired, and that dismantling the institution she built is something like expropriation of the dead; and a concrete implementation plan with a timeline a board could actually adopt at the June meeting.

Beyond the governance mechanics, I want the memo to engage the philosophical substance. In particular, it should weigh the claim that a perpetual foundation, renewed across generations, is an engine of dynastic advantage that sits badly with fair equality of opportunity — and consider whether that claim is strengthened by a fact my mother's side of the board prefers not to mention: that the foundation, unlike a private bequest, enjoys significant tax advantages, so that perpetuity amounts to a publicly subsidized family institution rather than a purely private act of remembrance. On the other side, it should take seriously the reply that the original deduction was a one-time bargain already struck and honored, and that the dead founder's intent is not merely a preference to be overridden but a claim that persists.

Finally, the memo should close with a short section on a question I keep turning over: whether any of this reasoning bears on my own decision about the attorney's proposed multi-generational trust. The foundation's case and my personal estate planning seem disanalogous in at least one respect — one is a publicly subsidized institution, the other a private transfer that will face estate tax — but I am not sure whether that disanalogy makes the equal-opportunity argument weaker in the private case, or only less publicly answerable. I would rather the memo resolve that question explicitly than leave it to me by implication.
