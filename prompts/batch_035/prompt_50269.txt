I edit a small philosophy-of-mind blog and am putting together an interactive quiz, of the "which theory do you actually hold?" genre, this one titled something like "Do you think the chatbot believes anything?" The quiz should sort readers by what they say about concrete cases involving belief and desire attribution to artificial systems — thermostats, chess engines, reinforcement-learning agents, language models — rather than by which labels they like, so no question may ask "are you a functionalist?" outright. Please construct the instrument as follows.

First, lay out the logical space: four to six diagnostic questions on which the main positions genuinely come apart. The cases must include (a) a household thermostat and a plant turning toward the light, to fix how low the reader is willing to go; (b) a reinforcement-learning agent that tracks and corrects for a hidden variable in its environment; (c) a language model that passes false-belief tasks yet, when probed, confabulates reasons for its outputs after the fact; (d) a question about whether attributions should be graded — "believes, sort of," "believes in a partial or stunted way" — or all-or-nothing; and (e) a question about whether the reader's answer changes depending on whether the attribution is being used to predict behavior or to ground blame and responsibility. You may add further cases of your own devising, but each axis of the space must be answerable independently of the others.

Second, implement the space as ten to twelve quiz questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a reader can answer without knowing which position it maps to. Each question should target exactly one axis, and the options should describe cases, not positions.

Third, give a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least seven leaves, which must include: Dennett-style instrumentalism, on which belief attribution is a predictive stance that is neither true nor false of the system considered in itself; a realist view on which beliefs require discrete, structured internal representations, so everything turns on what is actually inside the network; a liberal functionalist view on which the right behavioral profile alone suffices; the qualified-attribution family, on which language models have beliefs in an underdeveloped or partial sense; a normative-interpretationist view in the Davidsonian tradition, on which a system counts as a believer only if it can be made sense of as largely rational through charitable interpretation; and a flat eliminativism on which even human belief-talk is a dispensable folk idiom, so the AI question dissolves. Add at least one leaf of your own invention.

Fourth, after locating the positions in the space, note any unoccupied combination of answers and diagnose it briefly — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Flag any pair of leaves your key cannot separate, and identify the additional case that would separate them.

Finally, take a definite position yourself. After the scoring key, state which leaf you judge most defensible and why, identify the single quiz question you consider the sharpest discriminator between the main camps, and say which one you would cut if the quiz had to be shorter, and why it is the least informative. If you think two of the required leaves collapse into one under pressure from the cases, argue for the collapse rather than preserving the distinction for tidiness.
