I'm a philosophy PhD student working on a dissertation chapter about the scope of instrumental rationality, and I'd like a case analysis of the Concorde program that takes sunk cost reasoning seriously rather than treating it as a cautionary tale to be recited.

To fix the episode: Concorde was the Anglo-French supersonic airliner, agreed in an intergovernmental treaty in the early 1960s, whose development costs ran from an initial estimate on the order of tens of millions of pounds to well over a billion, against initial airline orders that largely evaporated after the early-1970s oil crisis and noise controversies. Production went ahead anyway, and the aircraft flew commercially from 1976 to 2003, never covering its development costs but acquiring, over time, an anomalous cultural status: it is simultaneously the textbook exemplar of the "Concorde fallacy" (the name biologists gave to sunk-cost-driven persistence) and a national icon whose retirement was publicly mourned.

The standard textbook story I want to get behind: rational choice theory says only marginal costs and benefits should bear on a forward-looking decision; whatever has already been spent is sunk and irrelevant; the decision-makers kept going anyway; therefore they committed a fallacy, and the lesson is a general one about human irrationality. I don't find this satisfying, and I want an analysis that runs in both directions: the theory of sunk costs applied to the case, and the case turned back on the theory.

Specifically, I'd like you to address:

1. The mapping, done carefully rather than gestured at. Which decision points are we talking about when we say the governments "honored sunk costs"? The initial commitment to continue after cost estimates ballooned, the production go-ahead after airline orders collapsed, and the operation of the aircraft for decades afterward are different decisions with different forward-looking structures, and the fallacy diagnosis may apply to some and not others. For each, say what was actually sunk, what the marginal alternatives were, and what the relevant payoff variable was supposed to be.

2. A point I find underexplored: the claim that continuing was in fact rational, not just excusable. My understanding, and please flag it if this seems wrong rather than building on it, is that by the late 1970s the British government reviewed cancellation and found it would cost more than continuing, because of compensation owed to France under the treaty and penalty obligations toward suppliers. If something like that is right, then the interesting question shifts: was the trap built in at the contract-writing stage, so that the fallacy, if there was one, was committed earlier, by the people who made future exit expensive? And if so, what does that do to the textbook diagnosis, which typically locates the error at the later decision points?

3. Two complications I want taken seriously rather than dismissed. First, what counts as "sunk" is not innocent: a large share of the expenditure was embodied in capabilities and knowledge, and the payoff variable is a choice — narrow monetary returns, national prestige, aerospace industrial capacity, deterrence of partners reneging on future joint projects. The fallacy diagnosis can be made to appear or disappear by moving between these, and I want you to say explicitly which payoff variable you are using and why. Second, the principal-agent structure: the ministers and officials who decided to continue were not the ones bearing the costs, and their private incentives (not being the government that cancelled a flagship joint project) plausibly made persistence rational for them regardless of the public balance. Does that mean the episode is not a case of the sunk cost fallacy at all, but of something else wearing its clothes?

4. Finally, the general question my chapter turns on: is honoring sunk costs always irrational? Consider, and evaluate rather than merely mention, the argument that agents who can precommit to honoring sunk costs change their future environment — partners, allies, and counterparties who expect persistence treat them differently, so the ex ante value of a policy of honoring sunk costs can be positive even though each ex post application looks like a fallacy. Does Concorde support this, resist it, or fall outside it (e.g., because states are poor candidates for genuine precommitment)? And distinguish carefully, please, between "continuing was in fact the best forward-looking option given the penalties and expectations" and "continuing was justified by the sunk costs themselves" — I want to know whether the case ever gives us a reason to accept the second, stronger claim, or whether it only ever gets us the first.

Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. I would rather have an honest verdict that the fallacy framework does not fit this case than a tidy confirmation of the textbook lesson.
