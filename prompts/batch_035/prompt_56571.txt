I'm writing an essay on what happened to altruism in the great nineteenth-century novels, and I want to build the essay around a collision that, as far as I can tell, nobody stages directly: Les Misérables read against Ayn Rand's attack on altruism. Rand's target was never ordinary kindness; it was the doctrine she traced back to Comte — the claim that morality consists in living for others, that a person who exists for his own sake is a moral criminal, and that the beneficiary's need constitutes a claim on the giver. On her telling, that doctrine has two signature results. First, it corrupts giving itself: benevolence stops being a free expression of self and becomes tribute extorted by need. Second, it enslaves the recipient: the accepted gift functions as a debt that never closes, a permanent lien on the beneficiary's life, and writ large the politics of need curdles into a machine that grinds individuals into abstractions.

Read Les Misérables against that critique, taking three strands separately rather than lumping them as "altruism." First, the bishop's candlesticks: a supererogatory gift with no claim behind it, made by a man who demands nothing and lies to the police to protect the thief. Second, Valjean's lifelong devotion to Cosette, which he treats as his central purpose — not an interruption of his life but its content. Third, Javert, whose devotion runs not to any person but to the law as such, and who is perfectly willing to sacrifice the individual Valjean to the collective abstraction of justice; his suicide when the categories break is the novel's own verdict on where pure duty-to-the-abstract terminates. Does Rand's critique actually touch any of these strands, or does it hit a different target?

The knot I most want untied is the debt. The bishop's gift makes Valjean a moral debtor forever — it reorganizes his entire existence around repayment, which is precisely the enslavement-by-gratitude Rand predicted. Yet the novel presents exactly that unpayable debt as the precondition of Valjean's self-authored second life, not as its destruction. Does that refute Rand by showing that a freely incurred obligation can be constitutive of a self rather than a betrayal of it, or does the novel quietly concede her point by never letting Valjean be free of the bishop? I'd also like you to take a position on the sewer scene — Valjean carrying Marius, his rival for Cosette, at real risk of his life — as a test of whether the novel distinguishes sacrifice chosen as the expression of a self from sacrifice extracted from it, a distinction Rand insisted altruism erases. And consider whether the Thénardiers, who weaponize genuine need into fraudulent claims on Valjean, are Hugo's own concession that need-based claims rot into parasitism — arguably a point for Rand, coming from her fiercest imaginable opponent.

I want a reading faithful in both directions: don't flatten the bishop into a utilitarian, don't turn Rand into a cartoon of greed, and say plainly where the novel resists her critique and where it uncomfortably seems to agree with it. The essay's contribution should be what the novel dramatizes about altruism that Rand's argument alone, with its clean opposition of sacrifice and self-interest, cannot see.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict: [one sentence]" stating whether Les Misérables ultimately vindicates Rand's critique of altruism, rebuts it, or splits the difference — and naming the single scene that decides the case for you.
