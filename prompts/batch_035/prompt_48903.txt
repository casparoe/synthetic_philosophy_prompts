Hey, I'm drafting a paper on Moore's proof of an external world and would love some feedback on the idea before I flesh it out.

The core claim: the reason Moore's proof feels unsatisfying isn't that it's invalid. It's a perfectly good modus ponens (if this is a hand, then external things exist; this is a hand; so external things exist), and Moore was right that the premises were things he knew in the ordinary sense. The problem is that it doesn't give the skeptic what she actually asked for, namely a proof that doesn't presuppose the thing in question. So the proof succeeds as a proof and fails as anti-skeptical therapy — those are just different standards.

Now the part I'm less sure about, my "novel" extension: I want to argue that Moore's proof, properly understood, is already neutral about the simulation hypothesis. When I hold up my hand and conclude that external things exist, the simulation theorist objects that my "hand" might be simulated. But I want to reply that this objection misses Moore's target. He was arguing against a family of idealist positions that deny external things altogether, not against views about the metaphysical substrate of those things. A simulated hand is still a hand *in Moore's sense* — an object whose existence doesn't depend on my mind — so the simulation theorist's challenge simply isn't a challenge to the proposition Moore proved. If you want to press a Moorean worry about simulation, you have to give up on the hand argument and argue directly that we can't know the substrate, which is a different and harder project.

So, comments please: (1) Is my reading of Moore fair, especially the bit about him distinguishing proving that external things exist from proving that he knows it, and about who his actual opponents were? (2) Is the simulation extension interesting or am I just equivocating on "external"? The more I look at it the more I worry the simulation theorist can say Moore's conclusion, as Moore understood it, is too weak to matter, and that what we wanted all along was the substrate question. (3) Is the "succeeds as a proof, fails as therapy" framing something people have already said, and if so is there any room left for me to say something about *why* the therapy demand feels legitimate to the skeptic? I'm half tempted to say the skeptic is conflating epistemic justification with dialectical persuasiveness, but that might be too quick.

Flag anything I've gotten wrong about what Moore actually claimed, and be honest if the extension is a dead end — I'd rather find out now than after writing 20 pages.
