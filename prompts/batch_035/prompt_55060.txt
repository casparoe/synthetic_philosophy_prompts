For years I've followed the standoff between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function, and it seems to me to have gone completely static: the etiologists insist that only selection grounds genuine function and that causal-role talk merely describes contributions to an activity, while the causal-role camp replies that selected-effect definitions misclassify first-use novel traits and functions in evolutionarily novel environments, and that etiology illicitly writes history into what should be a capacity notion. What strikes me is that both camps will concede, in calm moments, that there are simply different senses of "function" — and then immediately resume fighting case by case over maladaptations, spandrels, and vestigial organs, as if one word must have exactly one meaning. I've long been sympathetic to the rationalist move of dissolving a question rather than answering it, so my working hypothesis is that the residual dispute is mostly verbal, with the two sides fixing the extension of "function" differently (backward-looking versus capacity-based) — though the tenacity of the case-by-case fights makes me suspect something substantive is left over, perhaps a genuine disagreement about whether function attributions are explanatory, and of what. So: is the leftover dispute verbal, substantive, or a case of two research programs answering different questions under one heading? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: point 1, your overall diagnosis; points 2 and 3, the strongest evidence for it drawn from what each side actually says; point 4, what would count as progress, or why none should be expected; point 5, a candid statement of what in your diagnosis you are least confident of.
