You are a philosopher of art with a strong sideline in value theory — the kind who is comfortable distinguishing aesthetic value from artistic value from market value, and impatient with hand-waving about "the aura of the original." I need you to audit a set of my beliefs, because events have just made them uncomfortably concrete.

The events: a small landscape that has hung in my family's hallway for three generations, always spoken of as a Johan Thomas Lundbye (the Danish Golden Age landscape painter, who died at twenty-nine, so his works are scarce), has just been examined by an appraiser. She is confident it is one of a batch of forgeries produced in the 1920s to satisfy demand for his work. I still love the picture. I have also taken it down, and I can't look at it the way I used to — and I want to know whether that reaction is something my views endorse or a betrayal of them.

The views, as best I can state them:

1. Aesthetic value supervenes on the visible surface. If our picture had been secretly swapped for an indiscernible forgery, nothing of aesthetic value would have changed.
2. Even so, the original is worth vastly more than any indiscernible forgery, and that premium is not a mistake, not sentimentality, not mere market noise: there is a real value difference between the two objects.
3. The difference is grounded in provenance: part of a painting's value consists in its being the object the artist actually produced, then and there — a causal-historical fact about it.
4. When a forgery that has hung in a museum for decades is finally unmasked, the museum genuinely loses something — not money or face — though not a fleck of paint has changed.
5. What is wrong with forgery, where it is wrong, is deception. An honestly labeled copy does no wrong, and a good print of the Lundbye in my study would give me the same pleasure the original would.
6. Nevertheless, the honestly labeled copy is worth a small fraction of the original, and I believe that is also correct: honesty makes a copy innocent, not valuable.
7. The best forgers perform something genuinely admirable — sustaining a period style across an entire canvas is as hard as painting — and an unmasked forgery, now honestly labeled as what it is, is in some respects a more interesting object than a print of the original.
8. I do not believe in relic value. I would not pay more for a chair because Newton sat in it; association with a great person, as such, adds nothing.

Please think carefully before answering. Start with a brief brainstorm in which you map the commitments against each other, then go through the set and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are; for the genuine ones, rank them by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what it costs. Close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most — and then answer the question I actually came with: was taking the picture down the right reaction, or should I hang it back up?
