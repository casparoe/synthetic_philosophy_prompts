I spent thirty-odd years as a physical chemist before retiring, and I have only lately begun reading philosophy in earnest, so please forgive me if I frame my questions in the idiom of my old trade. I have been working through Putnam's Twin Earth scenario: the planet exactly like ours except that the clear, potable liquid in its rivers and oceans is not H2O but some other compound, usually labeled XYZ, indistinguishable from water by ordinary observation. The standard moral is that the meaning of a natural-kind term like water is not fixed entirely by what is inside a speaker's head: two molecularly identical speakers, one on Earth and one on Twin Earth, would nonetheless refer to different substances and so mean different things by the same word. As a chemist I find this appealing in one way and unsettling in another, and I want to know how much of my attraction to it survives scrutiny.

Please give me your credence that some defensible form of this externalist moral is true, stated as precisely as you can make it: that for at least some natural-kind terms used by ordinary competent speakers, the reference — and thereby at least a component of the meaning — is fixed by the underlying nature of the stuff in the speaker's environment rather than by the speaker's descriptions, and that this holds not just for scientific experts but for the word water as used by people who have never seen a periodic table. Then decompose the number. I would like rough probabilities, each with a line of justification, for at least the following sub-questions, plus any further terms you think belong in the decomposition: first, whether natural-kind terms have reference determinate enough that there is a fact of the matter to be right or wrong about here at all, as opposed to meaning being a loose cluster of dispositions; second, whether the reference-fixing feature is microstructure specifically rather than superficial role, since these come apart in other cases; third, whether the Twin Earth intuition tracks something real about meaning or is an artifact of how the case is designed — for instance, that we are implicitly invited to answer from the standpoint of an omniscient narrator who knows the chemical facts, or that XYZ is stipulated to be a single stable compound, which is doing quiet work; and fourth, whether a sufficiently clever internalist or descriptivist account could absorb the case without collapse.

Two complications from my side that I would like folded into your answer. First, water as actually found is not the clean substance of the textbook: it is a shifting mixture of isotopologues, and heavy water, D2O, differs from ordinary water in real though modest ways while we still count it as a kind of water. If near enough counts as water, how much does that soften the claim that microstructure fixes reference, and does it bear on the second sub-question above? Second, consider two variant planets: one where the role of water is played by a mixture whose composition varies continuously by latitude and depth with no principled dividing line, and another where two distinct compounds share the role between wet and dry seasons. Do these variants move your credence at all, or do they merely probe the boundaries of a thesis whose core remains intact? I ask because I suspect that as a chemist I am tempted to overrate the tidiness of the actual Earth case.

After the decomposition, tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would shift it most. Finally, and this matters to me: say how confident you are in the claims you make along the way, and single out the single point in your reasoning where you feel least sure. I would rather have a number with its weak joint marked than a fluent essay that never commits. Please write in American English.
