My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether species are natural kinds, and I'd like a second opinion in the form of a number. Her position: species are homeostatic property clusters in roughly Boyd's sense — lineages held together by gene flow, selection, and developmental constraints rather than by a shared essence — and that's good enough for realism; the joints are blurry, but the blurriness itself is explained by objective evolutionary structure, not by us. My pushback: once essences are gone, every candidate joint looks like a clustering chosen for particular explanatory purposes — the species boundary in a ring species, psychiatric categories like major depression, the living/nonliving borderline — and a realist who accepts that seems to be conceding that the joints are partly in us. So: what is your credence in this proposition — that there is a fact of the matter, independent of our classificatory interests, about which partitions of organisms into kinds are correct? Please decompose the number: how much turns on whether mind-independent causal structure produces projectible clusters at all; how much on whether such clusters, even where they exist, underdetermine a unique correct taxonomy because different causal processes (gene flow, selection, ecology) cut across one another; how much on whether "natural kind" is even a coherent notion once stripped of essence-talk; and add any other terms you think belong. Give a rough probability and a line of justification for each, then the combination. Finally, tell me which sub-question your number is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move it most — I suspect that's where my friend and I really diverge, but neither of us can locate it.
