I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, and after twenty years in newsrooms my native vocabulary is sourcing and verification, not formal epistemology. I think in terms of single-sourcing, the corrections desk, "if your mother says she loves you, check it out," anonymous sources and how much to weight them, circulation and readership numbers, and -- the last few years -- the flood of AI-generated slop we now have to screen for because a nonzero fraction of the content on the internet is synthetic and nobody knows the exact percentage.

I keep getting pitched pieces about the simulation argument, the Nick Bostrom trilemma thing: either civilizations like ours go extinct before becoming capable of running detailed ancestor simulations, or capable civilizations just don't bother running many such simulations, or if neither of those holds then we ourselves are almost certainly inside one. I half get it. But I honestly can't tell whether I get it or whether I'm pattern-matching it onto stuff I already know.

So here's what I want. Restate the whole trilemma in newsroom vocabulary, as faithfully as you can -- don't dumb it down, translate it. What's the analogue of a "technologically mature civilization"? Is running ancestor simulations something like a giant content-generation operation, where the number of simulated lives outnumbers real ones the way synthetic media already outnumbers verified reporting? Where in my vocabulary does "we go extinct before getting the capability" live -- is that like a paper dying before it digitizes its archive? And what do I do with the conclusion, the claim that we are probably in one of the simulated worlds rather than the base-level one: is that like realizing your source is one of thousands of sock puppets and revising how much you trust it, or is that analogy already breaking?

Then give me the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about. Which parts of the argument translate cleanly, which parts only survive translation if you quietly distort them, and which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all? My suspicion is that the self-locating probability step -- the move from "there would be lots of simulated people" to "I am probably one of them" -- doesn't map onto anything a reporter ever does, since we never have to ask which copy of ourselves is on the beat. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a newsroom analogue I'm not seeing.

Two constraints on the translation. First, don't smuggle in my framework's assumptions and call it translation: newsroom culture assumes everything worth asserting is in principle checkable, that claims are either sourced or spiked, and I know perfectly well the simulation argument isn't like that -- so flag where my instincts would mislead me. Second, when you get to the residue, don't just say "the vocabulary is missing something" as a reflex. Tell me your actual view on which it is: is the residue showing that newsroom thinking lacks a concept it should probably acquire, or that this part of the philosophers' arguement is less clear or less substantive than it looks from the outside? I ask because I need to know whether to commission the piece as "mind-blowing science column" or as "here is an interesting argument with a soft spot," and those are very different articles.
