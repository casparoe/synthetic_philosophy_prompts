I've drafted the following short essay for a philosophy-of-science workshop on scientific realism, and I'd like your comments on it before I revise. Please don't just survey the possible positions for me — I want you to commit: tell me whether the central argument works, which objection is the most serious one, and what you'd do about it.

The essay:

**Realism by Convergence: What Interpretability Researchers Keep Finding**

Interpretability researchers face an old question in new clothes. When a paper claims that a language model "represents deception" or "has a feature for sycophantic praise," is there really something in the network that the researchers have located, or are they imposing a projection of their own concepts onto a blob of matrices? Call the first view realism about representations and the second projectionism. I want to argue for realism, and my argument is not the usual one.

The usual argument is behavioral: if you can flip the feature and the model's behavior changes in the predicted way, the feature is real. But this proves too little. A linear probe finding a "deception direction" and a steering intervention using that same direction are not independent witnesses; the steering experiment inherits whatever the probe projected. The probe and the steering vector are, in effect, the same tool used twice, so their agreement is like a witness corroborating herself.

My argument is instead an argument from convergence across independent methods. In the last few years, at least three families of methods with different mathematical cores — linear probes, sparse autoencoders trained to decompose activations into sparse, monosemantic components, and causal patching between model runs — have been pointed at the same models. They keep agreeing to a striking degree: the directions that probes find for a concept, the sparse features that autoencoders isolate for it, and the activations whose patching produces the concept-typical behavior tend to pick out overlapping sets of units. When three instruments built on different principles all register the same reading, the standard realist inference is that there is a common cause — something in the network that all three instruments are detecting. Projectionism has no comparably good explanation of this agreement, since the three methods don't share the machinery by which the projection would supposedly be imposed.

The obvious deflationary reply is that the convergence is manufactured: the methods are all trained on activations from the same model, which was itself trained on data produced by humans who draw the very distinctions the researchers hope to find. The features "converge" because the data distribution already contains human-shaped concepts, and any method sensitive to that distribution will recover them. I call this the shared-tool worry, and I concede that it explains some convergence. But I claim it cannot explain all of it, and I propose a test that separates the cases: cross-architecture transfer. Find a feature with method M in architecture A, then use the corresponding feature in a differently architected model B — different depth, different tokenizer, trained by a different lab on overlapping but not identical data — as the probe target or the steering vector. If the concepts' geometry transfers, that is agreement that the shared-tool story cannot easily absorb, because A's training dynamics are not a tool anyone applied to B.

I conclude with an analogy I'm unsure about: features in networks may be like centers of mass in classical mechanics — not located at any single component, but robustly instantiated by the system as a whole, and none the worse for that. If this is right, realism about representations does not require what I call "addressability": it does not require that every real feature have a place in the network where it, so to speak, lives.

Specific things I'd like your verdict on:

1. Does the convergence argument actually defeat the deflationist, or does the deflationist have a reply I haven't considered — for instance, that linear probes, sparse autoencoders, and patching all inherit a common linear-structure assumption, so their agreement is three uses of one tool after all? Take a definite position on whether this circularity objection goes through.
2. Is cross-architecture transfer really the discriminating experiment I claim it is? If you think it isn't, say so plainly and tell me what would be.
3. The center-of-mass analogy: helpful, or does it quietly change the subject from "are the features real?" to "how are the real ones distributed?" Give me your honest view; if the analogy is doing more harm than good, I'll cut it.
4. Anything in the essay that is confused, overstated, or worth keeping that I haven't flagged — but please prioritize the three questions above over a general line-edit.
