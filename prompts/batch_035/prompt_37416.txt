My partner and I have spent the whole evening arguing about a pair of thought experiments from decision theory, and we ended at a stalemate that I would like you to help resolve. To make sure we are working from the same material, let me spell out both cases in full. In the first, which the literature calls a counterfactual mugging, a predictor who is almost never wrong flips a fair coin before you arrive on the scene. If it lands tails, it deposits £10,000 in your bank account; if it lands heads, it knocks on your door and asks you to hand over £100. Crucially, it set the whole arrangement up in advance only because it predicted that, in the heads branch, you would pay. You have now watched the coin land heads, the £100 request is on the table, and the tails branch is a distant counterfactual in which you were never consulted at all.

The second case is the XOR blackmail. A blackmailer has unerring knowledge of whether I once had a sordid affair, and he sends me a threatening letter if and only if either I had the affair and he predicts I will refuse to pay, or I did not have the affair and he predicts I will pay. Assume I know perfectly well whether the affair happened, so the letter's arrival tells me nothing new about my own past. The letter demands £1,000 to keep the secret. My partner's position is that the two cases are structurally identical: in both, a predictor made an earlier move that depended on its forecast of my policy, and in both I am now facing a demand backed by the predictor's track record. She maintains that if precommitment-style reasoning instructs me to pay the mugger £100 in order to have collected the £10,000 in the tails branch, then by the very same logic I ought to hand over the £1,000 to the blackmailer, since a policy of paying means fewer letters in the branches where I led a blameless life. She suspects that the familiar verdicts — pay in the mugging, refuse in the blackmail — are just intuitions dressed up as theory.

Please adjudicate. I would like you to set the two scenarios side by side and identify precisely which structural feature, if any, drives the divergent verdicts; my hypothesis is that it has something to do with whether there exists a branch of the setup in which something of value was made to depend on my policy, as opposed to merely the arrival or non-arrival of a worthless letter. Please test that hypothesis by constructing a minimal pair: describe a modified mugging in which the £10,000 would have been deposited regardless of my predicted response, and a modified blackmail in which something genuinely valuable hinges on my policy, and say what the correct behaviour is in each variant. Then tell me whether the asymmetry my partner dismisses really survives, whether the distinction lends support to updateless or policy-based decision theories as opposed to act-based ones, and what verdict a consistent theory should render on her charge that refusing the blackmailer while paying the mugger is incoherent.
