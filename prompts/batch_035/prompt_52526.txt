I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar that has just moved from natural selection to Dennett's idea of "free-floating rationales" — the thought that there can be reasons without any reasoner: the reason a particular species of moth is drab-colored existed for millions of years before anyone, moth included, was in a position to represent it. I find this idea gripping but unstable, and I'd like your help thinking it through in the form of an essay.

Here is the complication my friends and I kept circling last night. We came up with three readings of the claim and couldn't decide between them. On the first reading, free-floating rationales are a mere façon de parler: saying the moth is drab "for camouflage" is shorthand for a causal story about selection, and once you've told that story, the word "reason" has done no independent work. On the second, fully realist reading, the rationale is out there all along — the reason the moth is drab is a fact about the world, one that a later-arriving mind can discover rather than invent, much as it discovers the ratio between a planet's distance from the sun and its orbital period. On the third, intermediate reading — one of my friends defended it hotly — the rationale is real but only counterfactually: it exists because there could in principle be a reasoner (an evolutionary biologist, an omniscient observer) who would extract it, so reasons without actual reasoners still depend on possible reasoners somehow.

To make the stakes concrete, consider two test cases and use them as you see fit. First, a spider's web: the web's geometry is, we're tempted to say, for catching flying insects, and this looks like a reason-shaped fact about a structure built by a creature with no idea what it's doing. Second, and more uncomfortably, the fine-tuning of physical constants: if the charge of the electron had been slightly different, there'd be no chemistry and nobody to ask about it. Does it make sense to ask whether there is a reason for that, or did the free-floating rationale view quietly overreach the moment it left biology? I want the essay to weigh the three readings (or argue for a fourth), say which test cases each reading handles well or badly, and reach a definite verdict rather than a diplomatic "it depends on what you mean by reason" — though you should first say what work you're making each contested term do. Keep it under 900 words, and lead with your conclusion in the first two sentences so a reader knows where you're headed.
