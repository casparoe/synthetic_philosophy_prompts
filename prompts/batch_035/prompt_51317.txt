For my term paper in a course on moral responsibility and emerging technology, I need a thought experiment that drives a wedge between two families of accounts of the "responsibility gap" for autonomous systems. Construct a case in which an autonomous system causes a serious harm that generates a genuine responsibility gap on control-based accounts (roughly, the sort Andreas Matthias invokes when he argues that machine learning systems act in ways no designer or operator could foresee or control, and the sort behind Robert Sparrow's appeal to a condition of control), but not on accounts that ground responsibility in answerability — being the party who owes an explanation and amends to those harmed. Constraints: the setting should be realistic (say, a hospital triage system, an elder-care robot, or a municipal infrastructure controller rather than autonomous weapons); the system must be working exactly as designed, with no operator negligence and no design flaw that anyone could reasonably have been expected to catch; and the scenario itself should be no longer than a single paragraph. After the scenario, walk through what each family of theories says about who, if anyone, is responsible, and demonstrate that the gap verdicts fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated by the case. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what a defender of the control-based view would say to close the gap (for instance, by tracing responsibility back to the decision to deploy the system at all), and whether the case still works if that feature is removed.
