I'm in a small translation group — five of us, we meet fortnightly in the back of a pub, mostly French to English, and I'm the only one who doesn't translate for a living. My gran died last month, and when we were clearing her flat we found a poem she'd written decades ago, the week my grandad died. She was French, never translated it into English, and I want it in the order of service for her funeral on Friday. I can read French fine but I can't write verse in English to save my life, so two of the group, Deborah and Marek, each had a go — and they've been rowing about it for two weeks. They've asked me to pick, and I can't pretend I don't care which one we use, but I honestly can't tell who's arguing well and who's just defending the version they happened to write. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what?

Here's the French:

Tu t'en es allé sous la pluie,
Et depuis, je cherche ma vie.
Mon amour était mon sens —
Le sens du chemin, le sens du vent.
Un compas qui perd son sens tourne en vain,
Et je tourne, perdue, sans lendemain.

The trap in it (Deborah says the whole poem hangs on this) is that "sens" means both "direction" and "meaning", so the compass line works twice over — a compass with no direction spins, and a widow with no meaning spins. Marek says you simply can't do that in English and the honest thing is to admit it up front.

Deborah's version:

You went away out in the rain,
And since that day I search in vain.
My love was my one sense — the sense
Of the road, the way the wind blows hence.
A compass with no sense spins round and round,
And round with it I turn, my love, unwound.

Marek's version:

You went away in the rain,
and since then I have been looking for my life.
My love was my meaning:
the meaning of the road, the meaning of the wind.
A compass that has lost its meaning turns in vain,
and I turn, lost, with no tomorrow.

How the row went:

(1) Deborah said the form has to do the work the pun can't. The original is in tight couplets, and the rythm going round and round is the widow going round like the compass — drop the form and you drop the shape of the grief, and what's left is a summary. It'd be like giving someone the plot of a dance.

(2) Marek said that's compensating for a dead pun with ornament, and ornament is a much cheaper effect than the pun was. Worse, her diction is fake: gran wrote plainly, and "the way the wind blows hence" makes her sound like a Victorian greetings card. His version tells the mourners what she actually said; hers tells them she was a different kind of poet than she really was.

(3) Deborah answered that his doesn't sound plain, it sounds like a crib — "with no tomorrow" just stops dead, whereas the original's final couplet is what makes the grief feel held rather than dumped on you. Marek then went for her last line: she's added "my love", an address to grandad that appears nowhere in the French — she's put words in a dead woman's mouth.

(4) Deborah's defence was that the whole poem is addressed to him (it's all "tu"), so the vocative is latent in it, and making explicit what's implicit is half of what translation is. Marek said that's precisely the sort of decision that belongs to the poet and not the translator, and if she gets to add "my love" then nothing is stopping anyone adding whatever they like.

I couldn't follow who was winning at any point, honestly. Please adjudicate each move — who's right about which specific point, and whether one of them is more right overall. And one more thing, tell me straight: is there anything both of them have missed? It definately feels like they've each decided the only options on the table are her version or his.
