In my metaphysics reading group we keep falling back on the picture of reality as a building that Schaffer introduced in his paper on grounding: the fundamental physical facts are the ground floor, facts about tables, meanings, and economies are upper storeys, and grounding is the load-bearing relation by which the upper levels rest on the lower ones — with the actual world somewhere between a single-level shack and a many-storeyed skyscraper. My supervisor uses the image to defend two conclusions at once: that metaphysics should be the study of structure (what grounds what) rather than of what exists, and that the denizens of the upper floors are in some sense less real than the fundamental ones. Before I press her on this in supervision, I want the analogy audited properly. Please give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of the foundations, the floors, the load-bearing supports, and so on), then identify where the mapping breaks — I'm worried that physical support is a causal, spatiotemporal, mechanical relation while grounding is supposed to be none of these, and that buildings have architects and can be built floor by floor, which seems to smuggle in a constructive element nobody wants. Say which disanalogies matter for my supervisor's two conclusions and which are harmless, and tell me whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether the building was carrying them the whole time.
