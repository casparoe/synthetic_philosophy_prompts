A popular book on behavioral economics and financial markets summarizes the common prior assumption for its readers like this: 'Rational agents, by hypothesis, all start out with exactly the same beliefs before they see any evidence — this is the common prior assumption. Harsanyi showed that any residual difference between two people's beliefs is really just a difference in information, so it should be modeled that way. Building on this, Aumann proved that rational agents can never agree to disagree: if two of them still hold different opinions after pooling what they know, at least one of them must be irrational. The no-trade theorem extends the point to markets: with common priors there can be no speculative trading, so whenever we observe genuine disagreement or heavy trading volume, we are watching irrationality at work.' I'm a PhD student in economics, and I have my suspicions — the Aumann sentence in particular seems to slide quietly from priors to posteriors, and the last sentence feels like it's drawing more than the theorems license. But the book is by a serious author, and I can't pin down the errors precisely. Go through the summary claim by claim: which parts would a decision theorist accept as stated, which are conflations or overstatements of modest theses, and which are just wrong? Then tell me whether the book's final conclusion — that observed disagreement and market volume straightforwardly demonstrate irrationality — survives once the accurate parts are separated from the misrepresentations.
