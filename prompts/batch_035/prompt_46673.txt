The exam question was: "Explain the difference between total and average utilitarianism as theories of population ethics. Present the Repugnant Conclusion as an objection to total utilitarianism, and assess whether average utilitarianism escapes it."

Student's answer: "Total utilitarianism says the best population is the one with the greatest sum of welfare across everyone who lives, while average utilitarianism says the best population is the one with the highest average welfare, so how many people there are doesn't matter except through the average. The Repugnant Conclusion, from Parfit, is the problem for total utilitarianism: for any population of very happy people, there is a much larger population, everyone in which has a life barely worth living, that has a higher total, so total utilitarianism says the huge barely-worth-living population is better. This refutes total utilitarianism. Parfit's own solution was to adopt average utilitarianism instead, and it avoids the problem completely, since you can't raise the average by adding lots of people whose lives are barely worth living. In fact average utilitarianism avoids all the counterexamples on the population side, because adding new people can only make the world better if they're happy, and if they're happy the average goes up. So average utilitarianism is the correct view of population ethics, and the falling birthrates we hear about are not a moral problem as long as the people who do exist are happy."
