You are an experienced formal epistemologist who has taught the graduate seminar "Probability and Rationality" for over a decade, and you have a reputation for writing exam questions that separate students who can reconstruct an argument from students who can only recite its conclusion. I am helping you assemble this year's take-home final, and I need you to draft the question on Dutch book arguments, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

Before writing anything, please think carefully about where the real cruxes of this literature lie, because the question should be built around them rather than around a survey. Among the distinctions you should consider mapping out in your planning: the gap between the mathematical result (incoherent credences license a set of bets, each individually acceptable at the agent's own odds, whose combination guarantees a loss) and the normative conclusion (that incoherence is irrational); the difference between the synchronic and diachronic versions of the argument; the difference between a book that a bookie can construct at a single time and a strategy that requires the bookie to foresee the agent's future credences; and the question of what role the gambling context is actually playing — whether it is essential to the argument or merely a vivid illustration of an underlying inconsistency among the agent's attitudes.

The exam question itself should be answerable in about 90 minutes and should force engagement with two specific objections, which you should embed in the question text, perhaps voiced by two fictional seminar students. The first objection is the deflationary one: an agent who simply never accepts bets from a bookie can never be booked, so if the argument shows anything, it seems to show that accepting bets is irrational rather than that inconsistent credences are. The second objection is the symmetry one: a bookie who knows how an agent will update after new evidence — whatever rule she follows, conditioning included — can construct a sequence of bets exploiting that foreknowledge, so if vulnerability to a diachronic book or strategy sufficed for irrationality, the standard argument for conditioning as the uniquely rational updating rule would seem to prove too much. The question should ask the student to assess whether each objection is decisive, what a defender of the Dutch book framework would have to say in reply, and whether the available repairs (e.g., reframing the result as one about consistency among attitudes rather than about gambling losses, or treating an updating rule as a commitment and asking which rules are invulnerable to strategies given that the rule is known) preserve the original ambition of the argument or quietly change its subject.

Please produce three deliverables. First, the question exactly as it would appear on the exam paper, self-contained and written for students who have taken the course — including the two objections in enough detail that they can be engaged seriously. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words that resolves both objections with the right distinctions rather than by hand-waving. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points of 4 marks each, where each point names the specific move a top answer must make and includes a one-line note on how students typically fumble it. Close with a short paragraph stating, in terms of the marking scheme, what separates an A answer from a B answer on this question — the B answer being one that states the theorem correctly and gestures at the objections without ever locating the exact step where the normative work is or is not being done.

Two constraints: the prompt must not ask students for citations, bibliographies, or attributions of specific papers — everything should be answerable from conceptual understanding alone — and the marking scheme must penalize the common confusions by name, for instance treating the sure-loss guarantee as a long-run frequency claim, or failing to distinguish the availability of a book from the availability of a strategy. Please do your brainstorming first, then write the deliverables in clean, exam-ready prose.
