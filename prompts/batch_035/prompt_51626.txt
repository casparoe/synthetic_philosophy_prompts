Write an essay on the following controversy: whether a consumer who will eat some meat regardless ought, on animal-welfare grounds, to substitute beef for chicken — and, more radically, whether such a consumer might plausibly do less harm by eating pasture-raised ruminants than by eating a vegetarian diet built on conventionally farmed crops.

The essay should weigh, but not be limited to, the following considerations. First, the arithmetic of small bodies: because a chicken yields on the order of a couple of hundred times less meat than a cow, a given quantity of chicken consumption kills vastly more individuals than the same quantity of beef consumption. If what matters is deaths caused, beef wins decisively; part of your task is to say whether deaths caused is the right currency, and if not, what is (aggregate suffering over days lived, welfare lost relative to a decent life, something else). Second, the distinction between counting animals and weighting them: some argue that moral weight should scale with something like neuron count or cognitive complexity, which would blunt the chicken arithmetic, while others regard such weighting as an ad hoc discounting of chicken suffering. You should assess whether this dispute can be resolved within the essay or is a standoff that infects everything downstream. Third, the crop-deaths argument, associated with a philosopher named Davis around the turn of the millennium: harvesting grain kills field animals, so a vegetarian diet fed by combines and pesticides might destroy more vertebrate lives than a diet including pasture-raised ruminants. Address the standard rebuttals — that it compares idealized pasture systems with real cropping practices, that even pasture-raised ruminants consume harvested feed in winter or in feedlots, and that the numbers of field animals killed per hectare are themselves contested — and say whether the argument survives in weakened form. Fourth, the wild-animal wildcard: a hectare of pasture, cropland, or forest hosts wild populations of very different sizes and kinds, and on views that grant moral weight to small wild vertebrates and even invertebrates, the sign of the comparison between diets may turn on guesses about animal densities per hectare and about the moral status of invertebrates. Consider what decision-theoretic attitude an agent should take toward a term this uncertain — expected value across hypotheses about sentience, a maximin over them, or setting the term aside as too speculative to act on — noting that the uncertainty here is moral as well as empirical, so it is not clear that it can be reduced by ordinary evidence-gathering.

Anchor the essay in a concrete decision: a mid-sized university dining service has committed to serving the same number of meat meals per term regardless of composition, and must decide whether to feature beef or chicken as its default across its stations. Reach and defend a verdict; a hedged or conditional conclusion is acceptable if the conditions are stated precisely and argued for rather than gestured at. Do not rely on citations, studies you cannot verify, or appeals to authority; the essay should stand on the reasoning. You may assume general facts about animal agriculture (feed conversion, typical lifespans, housing conditions) as common knowledge, flagging any assumption that does heavy lifting in your conclusion.
