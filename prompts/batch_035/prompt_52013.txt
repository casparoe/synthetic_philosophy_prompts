I'm the student rep on my school's curriculum committee (yes they actually let a junior vote, mostly for show but whatever). Next month we have to settle a real fight in the history department and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand. The fight: half the teachers want to kill the "Renaissance and Scientific Revolution" unit in 10th grade world history and replace it with one long unit on "Eurasia 1300-1700" covering trade, plague, gunpowder, printing, that kind of stuff. The other half says that's trashing the one part of the year kids actually remember, and that the "rebirth" framing is the whole point.

The rebels' arguement, as well as I can follow it: nobody at the time knew they were "in the Renaissance." The label got stamped on centuries later (some Swiss historian in the 1800s I think), nobody woke up in 1450 feeling reborn, half the stuff we file under "Renaissance" — banks, universities, tech — was already going full speed in what we call the Middle Ages, and there's apparently a whole thing among actual historians that there was a mini-renaissance back in the 1100s anyway. Same for the "Scientific Revolution": the word "scientist" didn't even exist till the 1800s, Newton spent half his time on alchemy and bible prophecy, and if you look at what people were actually doing it's less a revolution and more a slow messy 300-year pile-up where nobody agreed on anything. So putting a start date on it and calling it a revolution is a story we made up later, and teaching kids the story as if it were facts is kind of dishonest.

The defenders say: every historical label is a story we made up, "Eurasia 1300-1700" is just as invented as "the Renaissance" is, carving the past into chunks is how humans make it thinkable at all, and besides, when you zoom in on ACTUAL political revolutions they're also slow messy multi-century pile-ups if you look at causes, so that complaint proves too much. Also, quietly, the unit drives enrollment in AP Euro, so.

I get roughly one page for my written opinion and I want it structured exactly like this, in this order: "Recommendation" (2-3 sentences, right up top, since half the committee won't read past it), "What each side is really claiming" (about a paragraph), "Where the rebels overreach" (about a paragraph), "Where the defenders overreach" (about a paragraph), "What I'd actually do" (a short paragraph — you can go rename-the-unit-but-keep-the-narrative, or kill it, or split the difference some clever way, your call, but be concrete about what the unit would look like), and "What we're giving up either way" (3-4 honest sentences). Keep it at the level of a committee of teachers and two parents — no jargon without a quick plain-English translation. And take the rebels seriously, my AP Euro teacher is one of them and she will 100% read this and grill me on it in front of everyone.
