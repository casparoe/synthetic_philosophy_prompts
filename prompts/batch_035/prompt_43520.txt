So here's my situation: I'm a philosophy PhD student mostly working on well-being, and I've gotten involved with a small interdisciplinary group (a few grad students, one patient faculty advisor) that wants to do serious conceptual work on preference idealization — the stuff behind "coherent extrapolated volition" and the older full-information style accounts of the good. The idea is we'd produce actual theory papers, not ML. Before we commit I want a sober prioritization of the open questions, and I keep going back and forth so an outside view would help.

The candidates I can see, roughly: (1) the information condition — what exactly counts as the right facts to condition on, and whether moral facts can be in there without circularity; (2) the coherence condition — maybe rationalizing someone's preferences into a coherent set changes them so much that the output is nobody's preferences anymore; (3) the binding problem — why the actual person is obligated by the idealized version's verdicts when they conflict, rather than it being a sort of paternalism by proxy; (4) convergence — whether the extrapolation process is path-dependent (whose starting point, what order the reflection happens in) and what to do if it is; (5) the interpersonal aggregation step, which honestly seems like a whole separate dissertation.

Which of these should a tiny group like ours actually prioritize over, say, three years? I care about the reasoning, not the list — which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable without doing empirical work, and which are overrated because no answer would change anything downstream. Also tell me what's missing from my list. My hunch is the binding problem is the real crux but I suspect that's just because it's the one I've thought about most.
