I'm preparing to defend a qualified version of the common prior assumption — the assumption, standard in Bayesian game theory and the economics of information, that rational agents in a given strategic setting share a prior probability distribution, so that all differences in their beliefs trace back to differences in what they've privately learned. Before I write the defense, I need to know what it would have to accomplish. Please write the specification: the full list of requirements any adequate justification of the common prior assumption must meet.

Some requirements I already suspect belong on the list, though I'm open to correction and additions. First, the defense must say what the prior is a prior *to* — whether it's a real moment before any private information arrived, a rational reconstruction imposed after the fact, or a normative ideal — and it should confront the awkward fact that real agents never existed in an uninformed state. Second, it must say something honest about disagreement: Aumann-style agreement theorems show that common priors plus common knowledge of rationality drive disagreement to zero, which is either a powerful prediction or a reductio, and the defense has to commit on which. Third, it should clarify whether the no-trade results cut in its favor or merely restate it in the conclusion, and whether priors formed by evolution, temperament, or early learning that no one would call "evidence" can be brought inside the assumption's scope. Fourth, it must declare whether it is a descriptive claim about, say, market participants, or a normative claim about what rationality requires — since a defense that succeeds for one may be worthless for the other. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that generates it, mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which merely reflect the commitments of one school (subjective Bayesians will reject anything demanding a unique rational prior, for instance), and identify any pair you believe cannot be satisfied together — that conflict is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly and without a full scorecard, assess how the leading existing defenses fare against the list: the Harsanyi-style view that residual prior differences are really hidden information differences, the convergence stories on which shared evidence gradually erodes prior disagreement, and the pragmatic or convention-based defenses that treat the assumption as a modeling device rather than a truth. If your conclusion is that the assumption is defensible for some purposes and not others, ground that verdict in which desiderata fail, not in a general hedge.
