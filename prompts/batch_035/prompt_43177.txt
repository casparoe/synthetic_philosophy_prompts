I help run AI tools at a small company, and I've become puzzled by what our evaluation practices quietly assume. When we fine-tune a model for our own use, we're told to grade the reasoning, not just the result: a response that reaches the right answer through a lucky guess or a fabricated rationale is supposed to score worse than one that reaches the wrong answer for defensible reasons. But every decision about whether a model is good enough to actually deploy is made on outcomes alone — accuracy on a sample of our tickets, hallucination rate, user satisfaction. The red team likewise only tries to elicit harmful outputs; nobody asks how the model got there. So what view of what makes reasoning good does the process-grading presuppose? Does it only make sense if there is a right way to reason that can be recognized independently of the results — and if so, why doesn't that assumption carry over to deployment decisions, where we judge models purely by what comes out of them? Or is the split just practical — we can inspect our fine-tuning data but not a vendor's weights — in which case the two practices presuppose much less than they appear to, and several different theories of good reasoning would license both?
