I'm teaching an intro-level biomedical ethics course (mostly sophomores, no philosophy background required) and I'm putting together the final exam. I want one essay question on the killing/letting-die distinction, but I'm so tired of getting back essays that are just a plot summary of Rachels' bathtub case plus the words "the bare difference argument" and nothing else. The problem is that the standard exam questions basically invite that: "Does the distinction between killing and letting die have moral significance? Discuss with reference to Rachels and Nesbitt" is a recitation trap and I've learned my lesson.

So instead I want the question built around a case I made up that forces them to actually use the views rather than name-drop them. Here's the setup I've been working with: an ICU where every ventilator has a failsafe design flaw (company cut corners, there was a recall, the whole thing is a slow-burn scandal) such that the machine requires a nurse to press a "continue ventilation" button every 10 minutes. If the button isn't pressed, ventilation stops. A patient is on the machine, and the staff decides to withdraw treatment -- except now there's an internal fight about whether the nurse should simply not press the button, or press a separate (also existing, for legal reasons) "stop ventilation" button, or physically disconnect the patient from the machine. Administration insists these are all literally identical outcomes and anyone who thinks otherwise is irrational. A resident argues they're not identical, because the machine's default state was set by an engineer years before this patient existed, so whether the patient dies by decision or by default depends on facts about the machine's design history, not on anything the nurses do or don't do now. Then a visiting ethics consultant muddies it further by claiming the case shows the whole act/omission framework presupposes a background of "normal functioning" that machines don't have, so the framework itself is tracking something about who's responsible for setting up which defaults.

What I want from you: the exam question itself (written for students to answer in about 90 minutes, no external citations available to them), a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points total. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make -- I'm thinking they need to show why the case is a problem specifically for views like Quinn's distinction between eliminating a threat and failing to aid, or Kamm-style attempts to ground doing/allowing in features of agency rather than in outcomes, and not just gesture at "Rachels said there's no difference." It should also say what separates an A answer from a B answer, and include one trap that a superficially good answer typically falls into (my guess is students will want to say the case vindicates Rachels because the three options feel equivalent, without noticing that the equivalence intuition here is doing different work than in the bathtub case -- but you'll see better than I do). Please make the model answer actually take a position rather than surveying, and if you can work in where the resident's "design history" point genuinely bites versus where it's a red herring, even better. Also, minor thing, don't put the phrase "bare difference" in the question itself -- I want to see if they can reconstruct the dialectic without the label.
