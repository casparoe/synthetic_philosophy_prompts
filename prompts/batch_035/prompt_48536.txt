I've been arguing with friends after a philosophy of mind seminar about whether self-deception is even possible, and the discussion keeps stalling on a familiar worry: to deceive someone, you presumably need to know something they don't, but in self-deception the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, so it looks as though one mind would have to both register the truth and hide it from itself — which sounds less like psychology and more like a contradiction. Rather than settle that in the abstract, I've been going by my gut on particular cases, and I'd like to know whether my verdicts hang together, and if so what principle produces them.

Here are the cases and my tentative calls. A husband notices perfume that isn't his wife's, unexplained evenings out, texts she angles away from view; he insists to his brother, and I think sincerely to himself, that she'd never be unfaithful — and I also notice he never opens the shared laptop bag where the messages would be. That one feels like genuine self-deception. A student who leaves all revision until the final fortnight, telling herself each year that she works best under pressure, despite every previous occasion having been a sleepless panic she regretted: I'm inclined to say that's not self-deception at all, just a flattering story she finds it convenient to repeat. A woman who deliberately never opens the envelope with her screening results, on the grounds that she'd rather not know: I say that's not self-deception either — she isn't fooling herself about anything, she's choosing ignorance — though my friends think this case is closer to the husband's than I do, and I can't quite say why it isn't. A washed-up academic who believes his book was rejected because the field wasn't ready for it, rather than because it was weak: I say yes, genuine self-deception, even though he never actively avoided any evidence. And a man who keeps telling himself he'll start the diet on Monday, sincerely at the moment of telling: I waver between counting that and not.

So my question for you: is there a single principle — perhaps distinguishing motivated belief-formation, strategic evidence-avoidance, and outright lying to oneself, or perhaps some other carve-up — that captures all or most of these verdicts? If two of my verdicts genuinely conflict, say which one you'd overturn and why, rather than building a principle with a special clause for every case. Then tell me what the principle predicts for a case I haven't mentioned: a woman who keeps paying a medium to pass along messages from her dead father, and who would say, if pressed, that she isn't sure she believes it, yet who organises her week around the appointments and becomes defensive when her sister mocks it. Genuine self-deception, something else, or does it expose a gap in whatever account you've given me?
