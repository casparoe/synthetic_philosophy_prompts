Okay so, background: I'm a journalist, I mostly write about science and ideas for a general audience, and a while back I did a piece about expertise during the pandemic — who gets to say what, when we should trust confident voices, that kind of thing. That led me down a rabbit hole into the philosophy of assertion, specifically the fight over the "norm of assertion": when you flat-out say something to someone, what rule do you violate if you say it without the right epistemic standing? The knowledge people (following Timothy Williamson, the Oxford guy) say you need to know it — asserting what you don't know is a bit like violating the rules of the game even if it happens to be true. The truth people say it's enough that what you assert is true, and the belief people say the rule is about sincere belief, not knowledge. Fine. But here's what struck me as an outsider: this debate has been running for like twenty-five years, and from what I can tell everybody's still fighting over the same handful of examples, with each side absorbing the other side's data through some extra move. Like, the "how do you know?" challenge — it seems perfectly appropriate even when what was said was true and believed, which the truth and belief camps then explain away as a derivative or secondary thing. And the lottery case, where asserting "your ticket will lose" feels wrong even though it's arguably something you know in the probabilistic sense, so the knowledge camp adds footnotes. Everyone has a story for every case, and no case decides anything.

My working theory, as someone who watches a lot of academic fights from the outside: this might not be a substantive disagreement at all, but something like two teams answering different questions under one heading — maybe one team is describing the rules of the conversational game (what makes a move proper in an exchange) and the other is describing the epistemic standing of the asserter (what makes a person epistemically fit to play), and the words "norm of assertion" paper over the difference. But I honestly can't tell whether that's right or whether there's a real hidden question in there, e.g. about whether knowledge is the fundamental epistemic goal of the practice, and the case-battle is just where it plays out. I also wonder if the case data itself is doing something weird — the examples are so thin (a lottery ticket, a stopped clock) that intuition-pumping on them might be tracking pragmatics rather than the norm proper.

So here's what I want: a diagnosis, not a survey — I've read enough summaries. Please give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no intro or conclusion. The five items should cover: (1) your verdict on what kind of disagreement this actually is (verbal, substantive, different questions under one name — pick one and commit); (2) the single strongest piece of evidence for that verdict from what each side actually says; (3) what the other side would say to it, stated fairly; (4) whether anything — a new kind of case, a formal model of the practice, empirical work on how people actually treat assertions — could move the debate, or what specifically could; and (5) your honest guess as to whether we'll still be having this fight in twenty years and why. And dont soften the verdicts, I get enough hedging from the people I interview.
