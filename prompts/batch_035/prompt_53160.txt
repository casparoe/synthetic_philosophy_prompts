Write the customer-facing FAQ page for a fictitious SaaS startup that sells symbol grounding as a subscription service for language-model deployments — call it "Grounded.ai" or invent a better name. The voice is upbeat, breathless B2B marketing copy, as polished and deadpan as a real support portal, and the piece should run about 600–800 words, structured as a short "About us" blurb followed by numbered FAQ entries. Among other things, work in: tiered service plans (e.g., an entry-level tier that is just larger co-occurrence statistics, a mid-tier that adds camera feeds described in suspiciously vague terms, and an enterprise tier promising full sensorimotor loops that turn out to mean joystick data); a "no uninterpreted symbol left behind" guarantee; a competitor-comparison section addressing "the Chinese Room" as if it were a rival product; and a refund clause that quietly exploits the fact that grounding is unverifiable from the outside, so that the SLA can never actually be triggered.

The humor must come from getting the philosophy right rather than inventing features to mock. The real targets are the familiar moves in the symbol grounding literature: the assumption that meaning can be bolted onto a fully syntactic system after the fact; the conflation of grounding with merely assigning denotations via a lookup table; the quiet slide from "more correlations" to "understanding"; the treatment of embodiment as a modality count rather than a kind of causal contact with the world; the deferral of all verification to a total Turing test that is always one tier away; and the problem of other minds, which is precisely what makes the guarantee unfalsifiable. Someone who has read Harnad's 1990 paper should recognize every joke as an exaggeration of a genuine feature of the debate and find the satire fair, while a reader who has never heard of the symbol grounding problem should still find the document funny and entirely legible as corporate copy.

A bit of context: this is for the house organ of a reading group in cognitive science that runs a recurring feature of fake vendor documentation satirizing "engineering solutions" to philosophical problems — past entries include a change-management consultancy for identity over time. So the bar is that the piece works both as parody of startup copy and as a compressed, accurate statement of the dialectic. Prefer jokes that require understanding to land over jokes about tech culture in general, and don't let the document ever wink — the vendor sincerely believes in the product, right down to the footnote explaining why the grounding specialists, who are fluent in Mandarin but understand nothing, are fully compliant.
