In my government class we had a debate about whether our school should be allowed to make students change out of t-shirts with slogans that other students say make them feel unsafe, and my teacher ended the discussion with the old line that your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins. Everyone nodded, but when I thought about it afterward I realized I didn't know what the sentence actually says. It sounds crisp, but the more I poke at it the more different things it seems to be claiming, and I want to be able to talk about it precisely instead of just nodding next time.

Here is what I've noticed. The sentence clearly isn't just about fists and noses, or it would be useless in a debate about words on clothing. So the fist must stand in for something like "actions" and the nose for something like "interests of other people." But then everything depends on what counts as hitting the nose. In our debate, one side thought an offensive slogan was a fist that lands; the other side thought no contact happens at all unless someone is physically stopped from doing something. And I can construct cases that split the readings further. If I swing my fist at your face but never touch you, the slogan seems to say you have no complaint, though most people would say they do have one. If a student wears the shirt in a school where it predictably starts a fight, the "swing" seems worse than the slogan suggests, since it caused harm without itself being a blow. If someone smokes in a shared apartment, or blasts music at 3 a.m., the harm is real but nothing remotely fist-shaped is happening. And someone in my class argued that "my nose begins" extends to his family's business losing customers to a competitor's shop, which makes the slogan seem to prove far too much.

So my request is this: take the sentence about fists and noses and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical. For each reading, tell me (a) exactly what it says, (b) whether it is plausible, (c) whether it is actually false, and (d) whether it would support what my teacher used it for -- settling that a school may ban shirts that make other students feel unsafe. I'd especially like the readings sorted out where they come apart in my puzzle cases above, since those are the ones I can't resolve on my own. Please be willing to say that some readings make the sentence a platitude, some make it a serious and contestable principle, and some make it plainly wrong, and explain which reading people are most likely to mean when they deploy it to end an argument the way my teacher did.
