I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I recently read Toby Ord's The Precipice and then worked through his and William MacAskill's exchange about whether our generation is unusually influential, and I want to turn that debate into an interactive decision tree for the long-read section of my team's internal tech blog. Most of my readers have never heard of longtermism, so the tree must classify them by their answers to concrete scenarios, not by whether they recognize any of the position names.

Please design that decision tree under the following constraints.

Starting point and scope. The tree's entry point should be a single mild commitment a reader can accept without any theory: something like the claim that humanity might exist for a very long time if it avoids destroying itself, and that some choices made in the near term could shape that whole future. Readers who cannot accept even that should exit immediately, and the tree should say where they exit.

Branch nodes. Every internal node must be a question a reader can answer from ordinary reasoning about cases, phrased so that nothing in the wording hints at which position it leads to. The node questions should be built around the following distinctions, which I care about for this piece, though you may add others if the tree needs them:

1. Whether the value at stake in the future dwarfs the value at stake now, or whether most of what matters is already behind or around us.
2. Whether the danger of losing the future is concentrated in a short period or persists roughly evenly for a long time.
3. Whether there exist lock-in events — choices that permanently bind descendants (a value system fixed into a powerful technology, a settlement pattern never revisited) — or whether everything important remains revisable indefinitely.
4. Whether, if risky and lock-in-capable periods can recur, this one is special for some identifiable reason, or whether our confidence that it is special is itself the thing in doubt.
5. Whether the comparative question even matters: whether it changes what we ought to do to believe our time is the most influential ever, rather than merely unusually influential.

Leaves. The tree must terminate in at least these positions, each with a one-sentence description of its most awkward commitment, in plain language for my readers:

- The strong hinge of history view: ours is the single most influential time there will ever be.
- The weakened hinge of history view: ours is among a small number of the most influential times, but we cannot tell from inside whether this is the one.
- The times of perils view: our influence is unusually high because our risk of losing everything is unusually high, but no claim that we top all future times.
- The precursors view: influence is spread out; earlier generations already had comparable leverage (a small change early in history compounds), and ours is not special.
- The later hinge view: the most influential time is still ahead of us, for example at the point of settlement of space, and we are at best an early rehearsal.
- The no special status view: nothing about our era distinguishes it from any other, and talk of hinges is a mood, not a claim.

Concrete cases to use at the nodes, where they help. Please draw on scenarios like these rather than on labels: the moment in 1945 when destructive power first exceeded any previous era; a near-miss that comes within minutes of ending everything and then passes; a future technology that could permanently fix a set of values into all subsequent generations; a Paleolithic ancestor whose tiny cultural tweak propagates through every later century; and a distant generation arriving at a new planet with a fresh chance to design their institutions. A reader should be able to say what they think about the case without knowing what it sorts them into.

Honesty requirements. Please explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate — I expect the weakened hinge view and the times of perils view to be hard to pull apart for readers who think the risk period is long but unidentifiable, and I would rather the tree admit this than fake a distinction. Also, for any combination of node answers that lands nowhere, note it and say in one clause whether the gap looks like an incoherent view, an unattractive one, or an unexplored one.

Deliverable and style. Give me the tree in indented outline form with the node questions in plain declarative English, a short worked example tracing one reader from the entry point to a leaf, and the honesty flags at the end. Please write everything in American English, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists — I want this to stand alone for readers who will follow up on their own.
