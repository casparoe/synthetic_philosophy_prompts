My roommate is a stats grad student and he walked me into this one at dinner and I can't find the exit. (1) In the classic cascade setup, each person in a sequence gets a noisy private signal and observes what everyone before them chose. (2) Once the first two or three people go the same way, everyone after them rationally ignores their own signal, because the accumulated weight of the observed choices swamps it. (3) Notice that nobody in that chain did anything irrational — the cascade is built entirely out of individually reasonable moves. (4) Now take any long-standing consensus I encounter in real life: it was also built out of individually rational moves, since each person who joined it was responding to the evidence available to them, mostly the testimony and behavior of the people who joined before. (5) So any long-standing consensus might just be a cascade — a couple of early coin flips, rationally amplified into something that looks like mountains of independent evidence. (6) If a consensus might just be a cascade, its size adds almost nothing to its evidential weight, because the later "votes" were downstream of the first few rather than independent checks. (7) So I should treat, say, scientific consensus on climate change or vaccine safety as carrying roughly the evidential weight of whatever the earliest researchers found, and treat my own private read of the evidence as being on nearly the same footing as the whole edifice. He thinks this shows deference to consensus is mostly deference to noise, and the annoying part is that (2) is textbook, so I can't just wave him off. But (7) seems to also license every "do your own research" crank on the internet, and if you run the argument on itself it seems to eat the consensus that cascades happen in the first place. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? And be straight about where your own diagnosis is weakest — what consideration would actually flip you over to his side.
