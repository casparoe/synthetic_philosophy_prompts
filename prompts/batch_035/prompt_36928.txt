I'm putting together a session for a phil of medicine class on naturalism vs normativism about disease — Boorse's idea that disease is just a failure of some subpersonal biological function, statable in value-free terms, versus the camp that says calling something a disease is partly a judgment that it's bad for someone, a judgment about a life and not just an organ. I want to build the session around Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich (1886) and I'd like your help thinking it through.

Here's my instinct. The medical scenes in that novella are almost a parody of the naturalist's disease concept: the famous doctor with his Important Person manner, the diagnosis delivered in pseudo-Latin, and Ivan's own bitter thought that whether it's his caecum or his kidney that's out of order is not for him to decide — the whole point of the doctor, Ivan reflects, is to answer the question the way the official answers the man on trial: not with a verdict of guilty or innocent, but with a demonstration that this is not the department you should be standing in. Yet none of that vocabulary ever touches what's actually wrong with him. The doctors' "disease" is a category that explains his symptoms to everyone else while remaining totally useless to Ivan, and what finally makes sense of his dying is not any biological story but the question whether his whole life has been lived wrongly — which smells like normativism, the thought that what's sick here is a life, and the medical talk is an organized way of not seeing that.

But I don't trust this, and here's where I want the reading to earn its keep. Against my own instinct: Ivan's suffering is relentlessly, stubbornly physical — the taste in his mouth, the pain in his side, the misery of those last weeks. That looks like exactly the value-free dysfunction the naturalist says disease is, and no evaluation of his life, however damning, dissolves the pain. So maybe the novella actually shows the two things coming apart — that his medical condition is real and naturalist through and through, and that the question of his life is a different, non-medical question — in which case Tolstoy is cutting against the normativists who want to put the life into the disease concept. Where does the text actually stand on this? And what about Gerasim, the young peasant servant, the only person who doesn't lie to him, who holds his legs up and isn't ashamed of the dying man's body? Is he the naturalist's ideal caregiver (matter-of-fact about the body, no moralizing) or the opposite — the only one who understands what kind of trouble Ivan is in?

I'd like a reading that's faithful in both directions: don't turn Tolstoy into a walking illustration of either side, and don't quietly bend the philosophical positions to fit the story. Tell me which scenes carry weight and which I'm misremembering. And please push back on the framing itself — if the novella isn't really about the concept of disease at all, and my question imports a distinction that the text doesn't care about, say that plainly instead of finding clever things for me. If you think I've got a scene or its role in the plot wrong, correct me on that too, I'm working from memory. I'm teaching this in three weeks and would honestly rather have the framing torn apart now than in front of the students.
