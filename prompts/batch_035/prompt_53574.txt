I'm a philosophy graduate student writing a term paper on the Death in Damascus problem, and I've become convinced of a claim my supervisor regards as a lost cause: that the difficulties causal decision theory runs into in that case should be fixed not by abandoning CDT for some fancier decision rule, but by letting CDT randomize over its own unstable recommendations. I'd like you to construct the argument for this position on my behalf, in enough detail that I can tell whether it actually works.

Here is the setup as I'll be using it. You are in Damascus and may stay there or travel to Aleppo. Death, who anticipates your choices with near-perfect accuracy, will be in whichever city you settle on going to; meeting him is ruin, and there is a small cost to the trip if you flee. Consider CDT's advice relative to your beliefs about your own act. If you believe you will stay, then by your lights Death is almost certainly already bound for Damascus, and CDT says to travel to Aleppo — for causally, were you to travel, he would still (as you see it) be in Damascus, and you would escape. But once you take that advice seriously and believe you will travel, he is in Aleppo, and CDT says to stay. Each pure recommendation is self-undermining: confident compliance with it flips the calculation in favor of the other act. The standard moral drawn in the literature is that this instability exposes a defect in CDT's habit of treating one's own act as a causally independent node, and that we need a decision theory that is sensitive to the consequences of being the theory you use. My contrary hunch is that the instability simply shows that no pure strategy is admissible, and that CDT should be evaluated over mixed strategies — with the crucial twist that the theory must take as input your beliefs about the probabilities of your own randomization, not just beliefs about pure acts. On this view there is a probability p of traveling to Aleppo such that, believing you will randomize with exactly p, the CDT-expected-utility difference between the two cities vanishes, and no pure deviation gains; the mixed strategy is stable precisely where each pure one is not.

Please build the case in stages. First, explain why the self-undermining of pure-strategy recommendations is better read as an inadmissibility result than as a reason to change decision rules — say what, exactly, would be lost by jumping to a new theory that the randomizing gambit preserves. Second, make the fixed point concrete: put explicit utilities on survival, death, and the trip, derive the belief at which CDT becomes indifferent, and show that the corresponding randomization is a stable point of the combined belief-act system. Third, confront the objection my supervisor will raise first: if CDT must reason about the effects of its own outputs, hasn't the gambit smuggled back in exactly the self-reference CDT was designed to avoid, so that what you're really defending is not CDT but CDT wearing a rule-sensitive costume? I want an honest answer, not a dodge. Finally, say something about scope: does the construction generalize to cases lumped together with this one, such as those where the payoffs make the recommendation cycle without settling, or where no stable mixed point exists at all — or does the argument only rescue CDT for a narrow family of predictor cases? Please give me the reasoning only; I'm not looking for citations or a bibliography, and the paper should stand on the arguments.
