On the question of cognitive penetration of perception, rank the following five experimental findings from most to least supportive of the thesis that cognition genuinely penetrates perceptual experience, as opposed to influencing judgement, memory, or response tendencies after perception has done its work. A finding that actively supports the anti-penetration side belongs at the bottom.

1. Hikers wearing heavy backpacks, or fatigued walkers, verbally estimate the slant of hills as steeper than do rested, unburdened observers viewing the same slopes.
2. When a phoneme in a recorded sentence is excised and replaced with an ambiguous noise, listeners report hearing the missing phoneme, provided the surrounding words make some word probable at that position (phoneme restoration).
3. Russian speakers, whose language has distinct basic terms for light and dark blue, are faster than English speakers at discriminating colours crossing that boundary — an advantage that shrinks when they perform a concurrent verbal task but not a spatial one.
4. Participants who have just eaten dry pretzels and are thirsty judge a bottle of water across the room as closer than do participants who have drunk their fill.
5. In the size–weight illusion, a small object feels heavier than a large object of the same mass, and explicit knowledge that the two weigh the same does not abolish the felt difference.

For each item, give one or two sentences explaining where it lands, engaging seriously with the standard objection that the effect is post-perceptual — consider, for example, whether the dependent measure is a verbal report open to demand, and whether the manipulating variable is plausibly perceptual rather than decisional. Then, for your top-ranked finding, sketch the single additional control that would most decisively answer that objection in its case, and explain why no comparable control is available for your lowest-ranked finding. Please answer in American English.
