I'm the ethics consultant for a residential memory-care organization, and I sit on the committee our board has asked to settle a policy question before it renews our admissions agreements. When a resident's advance directive, written while competent, refuses life-sustaining treatment — but the resident's dementia has progressed until she is calm, apparently content, and, on days she can answer, says she wants to keep living — how much weight should the directive retain? We have a live case forcing the question: a resident whose directive, drafted at her diagnosis, refuses antibiotics for any life-threatening infection, now on her third pneumonia; her daughter wants the paper honored, her son wants it set aside.

The dispute on the committee mirrors something I studied years ago. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle holds, roughly, that rationality requires your current beliefs to defer to your expectation of your future beliefs; its familiar counterexamples are people who know their future judgment will be compromised, like the man who knows that after a few drinks he'll believe he can drive home fine, and is rational precisely in withholding deference to that future self. Our daughter-faction treats the competent author of the directive as the clear-headed self owed deference; our son-faction holds that the current resident is not a compromised self but a later one whose outlook simply changed, as later selves' outlooks do.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting. It must recommend a concrete policy the committee could adopt — more than 'case by case' — specifying when, if at all, a directive's authority erodes as dementia progresses, with reasons a nurse or physician could actually apply at the bedside. It should give the strongest consideration on the losing side its due, and it should say whether our Reflection framing survives scrutiny — in particular whether deference to a future self's beliefs is even the right model for directives, which encode preferences and values rather than beliefs about the world.
