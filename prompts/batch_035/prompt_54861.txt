I am a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar in decision theory, and I want a one-page diagnostic handout that sorts students by how they actually react to the absent-minded driver problem, rather than by which solution they have read about. The problem, for the handout's preamble: a driver sets out on a motorway at night and knows he will pass two identical exits. He cannot tell the exits apart, and at each one he must simultaneously decide whether to leave the motorway. Exiting at the first exit has payoff 0, exiting at the second has payoff 4, and continuing past both leaves him stranded with payoff 1. He can only follow a single, mixed strategy — continue with some probability p at whatever intersection he finds himself at. The ex ante optimal plan is to continue with probability 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3; the trouble is that a driver sitting at the second intersection seems to have a clear reason to deviate and exit, since 4 beats 1. Please write the handout as a decision tree leading from a starting commitment (accepting the payoff structure as described) to a position on what the driver should do and what, if anything, the case shows about decision theory.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of famous names. The branching questions should be ones a student can answer without knowing where they lead, and they should include at least these distinctions, since they are what the seminar cares about: whether the driver, finding himself at some intersection, has a well-defined and non-trivial probability of being at the first rather than the second one (and if so, what fixes it); whether the driver's own current inclination to continue or exit is evidence about what he does or did at the other intersection, or merely a cause; whether the plan chosen before setting out has any rational authority over the driver at an intersection, or whether each temporal stage must justify its own action; and whether any advice we give the driver is required to be the same at both intersections by the very description of the problem, or could in principle differ.

Each leaf should carry a short label (feel free to coin one, or use an established label such as the planning or ex ante optimisation solution, the sophisticated or multi-selves approach, an evidentialist treatment, an updateless or policy-first treatment, or a sceptical line on which the problem is ill-posed or shows only that the notion of an optimal strategy needs reformulating), together with one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment — the bullet it must bite. Where a combination of answers is occupied by nobody, note it and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, rather than papering over the gap. Assume no access to books or the internet: everything needed to follow the tree should be in the handout itself. Please write the handout in British English.
