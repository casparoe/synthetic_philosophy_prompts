I teach a 200-level philosophy of mind course, ~20 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity where they have to draw a sharp line across a set of animals deciding which get pain relief for procedures and which don't — I want them to feel in their own hands that the distribution of consciousness is doing all the moral work, and that the evidence they have won't settle where it falls. Here's the twist: no real animals. They work from dossiers of invented creatures you make up, where the behavioral cues (guarding wounds, trading off injury against feeding), physiological cues (nociceptors, centralized vs. scattered nervous systems), and evolutionary closeness cues deliberately point in different directions, so that no single criterion classifies every creature the way their gut wants to classify it. Small groups each have to write down an actual policy line plus the criterion behind it, because the vet staff needs a rule they can apply, not case-by-case judgment calls. Then in the second half, build in something that makes the line hurt: their own criterion should misfire on at least one dossier they liked, and they should have to sit with the creatures below their line being treated without relief. The debrief should map what happened onto the real issue — that moral status tracks consciousness, that consciousness in others is always an inference, and that the inference is underdetermined by exactly the kind of evidence that was in the dossiers. Also: should the facilitator keep a hidden answer key about which creatures are "really" conscious, or run it with no fact of the matter available at all? Pick one and defend it, since I genuinely can't decide. And please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, plus be honest about which part of the actual problem the game can't reproduce. Students shouldn't hear any of the standard names or positions until the debrief at the earliest — I want the experience first, the vocabulary after. Printed dossiers are fine, no real money involved, and one more thing: don't let the activity quietly lean on how charasmatic an animal seems, that's exactly the prior I'm trying to expose.
