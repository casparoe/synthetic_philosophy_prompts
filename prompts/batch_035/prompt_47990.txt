So, I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist — 38 years measuring what electrons do in dirty crystals — and since retiring I've gone down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly consciousness stuff. I've written up where I've landed, and my little reading group (two retired engineers and one very patient ex-HoD) is going to poke at it on Tuesday. Before they do, I want to know if I've holed my own boat already. Here's the package:

(1) I'm a physicalist, full stop. Physics is causally closed, no spooky additions allowed. Four decades in a lab will do that to you.

(2) But physics only ever tells you what stuff DOES — mass, charge, spin are just dispositions to push other stuff around. It never tells you what anything IS in itself. So the intrinsic nature of matter is up for grabs, and saying that intrinsic nature is experiential — proto-experience at the bottom — adds nothing causally spooky. Russell and Eddington were musing along these lines a century ago and I don't think either was a fool.

(3) Substrate independence: organization is what counts. If you scanned my brain and ran a functionally perfect silicon copy, that thing is conscious, and I'd consider the upload me. Personal identity is pattern, not stuff.

(4) Valence is more primitive than cognition. A full-blown thought needs lots of machinery, but pleasant/unpleasant strikes me as something even a bacterium, maybe even a single neuron, could have a smear of. If proto-experience sits at the bottom, proto-valence does too.

(5) Ethically I've become a total-view guy about suffering: the total amount of suffering in the world is what matters, wherever it sits. I was honestly shaken by the wild-animal numbers when I first ran into them, and I've accepted the implications.

(6) That said, I think moral weight scales with complexity or intensity of experience — I am not about to loose sleep over the electrons in my old magnets, and frankly anyone who does has lost the plot.

(7) And I hold that suffering requires a subject — a there-it-is for whom things are bad — which needs at least some persistence and integration, not just a passing flash of proto-unpleasantness.

Does this hang together? I have vague suspicions about (3) and (4) but honestly can't tell which pair is the actual problem, and one of the engineers keeps insisting (1) and (2) are flatly contradictory, which I think is wrong but can't quite articulate why.

Format for your answer, please: (1) Quick verdict — 2-3 sentences on whether the package survives; (2) Apparent tensions — one short paragraph each on the pairs that dissolve on inspection, with the dissolution; (3) Real tensions, worst first — roughly 150 words per genuine tension, saying exactly which commitment pulls against which and why; (4) Cheapest fix — a short paragraph on the single revision that would clean up the most, and what it costs me. Skip the intro-to-philosophy-of-mind background, I've read the pop books — this is an audit of my views, not a syllabus.
