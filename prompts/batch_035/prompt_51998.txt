A friend who works on RLHF sent me a podcast episode that explained the "intent alignment vs value alignment" thing, and something about the summary felt off but I can't put my finger on it. Here's roughly what the episode said: the alignment problem, as the field actually uses the term, is getting AI systems to adopt human values. Within that, intent alignment means the system does whatever its operator literally tells it to do, while value alignment means it does what's genuinely good, which requires a complete specification of human values up front. The podcaster put it that intent alignment is really just value alignment with a sample size of one -- one person's values instead of everyone's -- so it's a weaker, not a different, target. His argument was that intent alignment is a non-starter anyway because it amounts to building an obedient sociopath that happily carries out a careless or malicious owner's commands, and that value alignment is equally hopeless since humans can't articulate their values, so the whole distinction collapses and the real goal should just be making systems corrigible and interpretable rather than "aligned" at all. How much of this would a safety researcher accept, where does it misrepresent the positions (especially the "literal commands" part and the "complete spec up front" part), and does his conclusion that the distinction collapses actually follow from the accurate parts, or does it only work because of the parts he got wrong? Also, is he even right that the field's default usage of "alignment" is the broad one?
