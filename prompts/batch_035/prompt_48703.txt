I am drafting an essay on sphexishness and free will, and I would appreciate critical comments on the argument before I develop the notes below into a full draft.

For background: the digger wasp Sphex, when provisioning its burrow, will drag a paralyzed cricket to the entrance, leave it on the threshold, enter to inspect the burrow, and only then pull the cricket inside. If an experimenter moves the cricket a short distance while the wasp is inside, the wasp emerges, retrieves the cricket, and — before pulling it in — re-enters to inspect the burrow again. The loop can be repeated indefinitely. Douglas Hofstadter coined the term "sphexishness" for this kind of rigid, seemingly intelligent behavior that is in fact the execution of a fixed routine insensitive to whether its goal has already been achieved. The contrast is with flexible, context-sensitive behavior that genuinely tracks what the situation demands.

My planned essay argues that this contrast is illusory, or at least badly located, because sphexishness is a property not of organisms but of the grain of description at which an observer chooses to describe them. My notes for the essay are as follows:

1. At a fine grain, everything is sphexish. Whatever flexibility a creature displays must be produced by some stable underlying mechanism — a fixed architecture of learning, planning, and response. The wasp's rigidity and a chess engine's brilliance are both rigid at the level of their implementation. If rigidity at some level of description sufficed for sphexishness, sphexishness would be universal and the concept would do no work.

2. At a coarse grain, even the wasp looks flexible. It does respond to its circumstances: it retrieves the moved cricket, it re-inspects, it copes with disturbances in a way that is locally adaptive. Its failure only becomes visible against a very specific and contrived manipulation. An observer with a coarse enough view — or an observer who never moves the cricket — would credit the wasp with sensible, goal-directed, and therefore non-sphexish behavior.

3. Conclusion of the first half: calling a creature sphexish is really a report about which grain of description the observer finds natural or informative, and about which interventions the observer has happened to try. Sphexishness is observer-relative.

4. Second half, where the essay turns constructive. If sphexishness is grain-relative, then the familiar thought that free will consists in the absence of sphexishness — in flexibility, in responsiveness to reasons, in not being "stuck in a loop" — inherits that relativity. Rather than asking whether an agent is genuinely flexible, we should ask at which grain of description it is appropriate to hold the agent responsible. My tentative proposal: responsibility attaches at the grain at which the agent's behavior is most sensitive to the considerations we cite in holding them responsible — the grain at which their actions would have differed had those considerations differed.

Please comment on the following:

(a) Does the argument for grain-relativity actually hold together? In particular, is the move in (1) legitimate, or does it equivocate between two senses of "fixed" (fixed as in unchanging architecture versus fixed as in insensitive to goals already achieved)? Note that the sphex wasp's defining failure is arguably the latter, and a chess engine's fixed architecture does not make it insensitive in that way — if so, perhaps rigidity is not grain-relative after all.

(b) Is the claim in (2) fair to the intuition behind the wasp example, or does the wasp's "local adaptivity" merely re-describe the same rigid routine at a different grain, so that the coarse-grained observer is simply wrong rather than the description legitimately coarse?

(c) Independently of whether the premises hold, does the conclusion about responsibility in (4) follow from anything in the first half? It seems to me it might be a completely separate proposal that I am dressing up as a consequence, and I would like to know whether that impression is right.

(d) Finally, is the essay worth developing? Is the grain-of-description move a genuinely illuminating contribution to debates about free will and mechanism, or is it a deflationary sidestep that would leave readers with nothing new to say about, say, habit-driven human behavior or the rigidities of automated systems?
