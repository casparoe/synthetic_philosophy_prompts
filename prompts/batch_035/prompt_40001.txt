I am a novelist, three hundred pages into a book whose turning point is a betrayal that the protagonist commits not under coercion but out of settled character, and I have reached the point where I must decide what I am licensed to say, in my own mind, about her freedom. Rather than apprentice myself to either camp in the compatibilism dispute, I would like the specification first. Please write out the desiderata a theory of free will must meet in order to be adequate for a novelist's use — and note that I am fixing the purpose here, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory need not tell me whether determinism is true; it must tell me what I may honestly narrate.

Among the requirements I would expect, and whose motivating cases you should spell out: it must adjudicate whether the past-tense sentence "she could have turned back at the gate" can ever be true when I, her author, have already fixed everything she does — and if it can be true, in what sense; it must preserve the distinctions my scenes depend on, between the coerced act and the weak one, the brainwashed character and the principled one, the addict's relapse and the coward's retreat, since a theory that flattens these into "all alike determined" leaves me nothing to dramatize; it must say whether the reader's retrospective sense that the ending was inevitable can be honest alongside the reader's page-by-page hope that she would do otherwise; and it must not contradict the plain fact that people change, slowly and through events, even though character is largely settled by midlife. There is also, I suspect, a requirement about levels: the theory must not let the fact that I have determined the plot render empty the deliberation that goes on inside a scene, and I want to know whether the author's relation to a character is a clean analogue for the determinist's relation to an agent or a trap that has misled the debate.

For each requirement, give the case behind it and mark which you take to be non-negotiable for my purpose and which are the prejudices of one camp dressed up as demands. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a feeling that the demand that the ending flow from who she is and the demand that the reader be able to want it otherwise are in genuine tension, and I want to know whether that tension is the same one the philosophers fight over or a specifically narrative one. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to decide between the camps, and close with a few sentences on how compatibilism, hard incompatibilism, and libertarianism fare against the list — briefly, as the requirements, not the scorecard, are what I need.
