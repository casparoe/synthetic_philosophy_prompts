My friend Devi and I spent most of last night arguing about what people deserve — the proximate cause was a podcast about a fraudster, but the real subject was whether karma, stripped of its metaphysics, is even a coherent theory of moral desert: whether the claim that welfare should track desert across persons could ever be true of a world run by biology and weather, or whether desert is something only human practices of punishing and rewarding can deliver. Her track record from the argument, as best I can reconstruct it: she insists the financier who defrauded hundreds of elderly clients and then died peacefully at ninety deserved punishment whether or not he ever received it — desert doesn't wait for a court. She says lottery winners don't deserve their winnings even though they're fully entitled to them. She flatly denies that a child born with leukemia deserves it, and when I pressed the fantasy of a genuinely vicious ten-year-old, she said even that child wouldn't deserve disease, only correction. On the two drunk drivers — equally reckless, one kills a pedestrian, one gets home untouched — she says the killer deserves more punishment than the lucky one, so actual outcomes, not just choices, feed into what's deserved. She says a reformed embezzler who confessed, repaid everyone, and rebuilt his life should not be made to suffer further: the account is settled. And on a tornado tearing through a town, she said the victims plainly didn't deserve it, but also that calling the tornado unjust would be a category mistake — desert talk just doesn't apply to weather.

The new case we never got to: a man who spent his forties defrauding elderly clients, was never caught, then fifteen years ago genuinely reformed — he quietly gave most of the money away, though he never confessed and never faced his victims — and has now been diagnosed with a painful degenerative disease. Did he deserve it? I want your prediction of her verdict, but even more I want the reasoning, because at least three rules seem to fit everything she already said. One reads her as grounding desert in current character: the reform wiped the slate, and the disease is undeserved, full stop. A second reads her as running a ledger with delayed settlement — past wrongs create an outstanding liability that can ripen long afterward, so the disease is at least partial payment — and her refusal to let the dead financier off the hook fits this; on this rule the embezzler's case came out as it did only because confession and repayment settle a debt, while private reform does not. A third takes her tornado remark literally: nature is never a vehicle of desert, only human institutions are — so the man may well deserve suffering, but this disease, arriving through biology rather than a judge, is not it. Her verdict about the child with leukemia is the reason my three rules survived the night: it's consistent with both the second and the third, since the child had done nothing, which is exactly why a case with a real culpable past is where they finally come apart. And there is a further wrinkle worth naming: Devi grew up in a household that took rebirth and karmic ripening seriously, and she now says she rejects all of that — but I never managed to establish whether her intuitions had gotten the memo.

So: predict her verdict, with your best guess and the reasons for preferring it; lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record, including any I've missed; say what each predicts for the new case; and tell me which further case would most cleanly separate the rules — I've been toying with the never-reformed fraudster catching the same disease, or the reformed one being struck by lightning rather than illness, but you tell me which contrast actually does the work. And if you think her verdicts as given are jointly inconsistent, or that two of my rules collapse into one, I'd genuinely rather hear that than a forced prediction.
