Something I've never managed to square: states claim they can turn foreigners away at the border for any reason or no reason -- a visa application gets refused without explanation, and nobody thinks a moral argument has been owed. But the very same legal systems accept non-refoulement, the idea that you cannot push someone back into persecution, even though on paper the refugee still has no right to be admitted in the first place. So the practice seems to be: your claim to enter counts for nothing, but your claim not to be returned to danger overrides the state's right to exclude. What view of the right to exclude does that pattern actually presuppose? Is it basically a doing/allowing distinction -- returning someone makes the state complicit in a harm, while refusing entry merely fails to confer a benefit -- and does that even hold up, given that border enforcement physically blocks people who are already fleeing? Or is the implicit thought something else: that exclusion is legitimate only as a means of protecting some collective project, and the project loses its point when it costs someone their life? And where does that leave the economic migrant, the person escaping starvation rather than a firing squad -- is the practice only coherent if you deny that severe poverty is the kind of harm that generates claims against other states, and is that denial actually defensible? Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could several rival theories of the right to exclude all license this exact arrangement, and what would have to change if a state took one of the other theories seriously?
