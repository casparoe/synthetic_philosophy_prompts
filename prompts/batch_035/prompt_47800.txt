At a workshop on insect welfare, a speaker argued roughly as follows: sentience is a dimmer switch, not an on-off toggle. It must have come on gradually through evolutionary history, by increments of neural machinery, so there is no principled place to draw a line; moral status therefore also comes in continuously graded degrees, and cost-benefit analyses of, say, insect farming or pest control should assign insects small but nonzero weights, with sliders such as neuron count or nociceptor density fixing the proportions — and, at the far end, even a thermostat's claim dwindles smoothly to a vanishing point rather than dropping to zero. Please audit the analogy. Set out what corresponds to what — the evolutionary ramp, the neural machinery, the intensity of experience, and the moral or legal categories the switch is supposedly dimming — and then say where the mapping breaks. In particular: does the analogy quietly import a feature of its source, a physical quantity that varies continuously, that moral status may not share, since a capacity could be graded while the claims it grounds are threshold-like (a twinge matters little, but perhaps nothing below a certain intensity of valenced experience matters at all)? And is epistemic uncertainty about which animals are sentient being conflated with metaphysical gradation in sentience itself — two very different things a dimmer can blur together? Finally, tell me which conclusions survive the disanalogies: the modest one, that some animals deserve intermediate moral weights, and the ambitious one, that the weights lie on a continuous scale running all the way down to thermostats. If the dimmer fails as an argument, is there a better analogy for whatever the underlying phenomenon turns out to be?
