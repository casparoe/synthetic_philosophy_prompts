Quick background: I'm a second-year philosophy grad student, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a prospectus (about 1500 words) that my commitee will actually sign off on by the end of term. The idea came to me while grading intro logic, of all things.

You know the discursive dilemma: three judges vote on premises P (the defendant did the act) and Q (the act was illegal) and conclusion R (so, liable). Majorities on each proposition, plus the obvious entailment from P and Q to R, give the panel an inconsistent collective position — the group is somehow committed to R both holding and not holding. The standard responses all pick a side: aggregate on the premises and let the conclusion follow, aggregate on the conclusion and let the premises go hang, or restrict the agenda. But every one of them accepts the same framing: collective inconsistency is the disease, and the response is the cure.

Here's my half-formed thought: why is inconsistency a disease in the first place? Compare the author in the preface paradox. They beleive each claim in their book, they also believe that any book of that length contains at least one error, and so they fail to believe the conjunction of their own book — and nearly everyone thinks that's fine, not a breakdown. The agent just isn't closed under conjunction, at least for beliefs held on that kind of ground. So maybe the "inconsistent" group in the discursive dilemma is in exactly the position of the prefaced author: each member's set of votes is a chapter, and the majority constraint plays the role of the preface. On this reading, the case only looks like a dilemma because everyone silently assumes the group's attitudes must be closed under logical consequence — and the preface paradox is precisely what makes that assumption shaky even for individuals.

What I want from you: write the prospectus. It needs to (a) state the question precisely — something like: if we drop the requirement that collective attitudes be closed under logical consequence, does the discursive dilemma dissolve, and where exactly does the strategy break down if it doesn't? (b) explain why the existing responses leave this open — they all cure the inconsistency rather than asking whether it needed curing; (c) lay out the actual method, roughly term by term: I'd go through the main kinds of bodies the literature worries about (courts, expert panels, committees that issue findings) and ask, for each, whether a non-closed group still counts as an agent in any serious sense — Pettit's rough thought that a collection of people is only a group agent if its attitudes are rational looks like the crux, and my hunch is that dropping closure works fine for advisory panels but not for bodies that have to decide and act; (d) say what the deliverable is and what would count as the project failing — my own guess at the failure mode is that dropping closure merely relabels the dilemma instead of removing it, and I want the prospectus to take a definite stance on whether that risk is real; and (e) be honest about scope for two terms of work.

One more thing: commit. No "some hold X while others hold Y" hedging — the prospectus should take a definite position (mine, if you can make it work, or a refined version of it) and give its reasons, flagging only where you're genuinely unsure. Don't include a bibliography or citations, I'll fill those in myself. And keep the language plain, my committee hates jargon.
