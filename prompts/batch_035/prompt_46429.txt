I'm taking a seminar on rational choice theory, and we've just finished a unit on backward induction in extensive-form games. Please write an essay on the backward induction paradox in the centipede game.

The essay should address whether common knowledge of rationality genuinely entails taking the small payoff at the very first node, given that a player who reaches a later node has typically observed a deviation that the theory itself labels irrational, and so has evidence about her opponent that the original common-knowledge assumption no longer captures. My professor claimed in lecture that the paradox is structurally parallel to the unexpected hanging paradox, because both involve a conclusion derived from a prediction whose own derivation undermines the prediction's basis; please assess whether that parallel is genuine or merely suggestive. My own tentative view is that the paradox dissolves if we distinguish, at each node, between what a player may assume about her opponent's future play and what she may infer from his past play — but you need not agree with me. Weigh the main considerations on both sides and reach a definite verdict rather than ending on a non-committal note.

Please also distinguish clearly between what is settled — the formal backward induction result itself, for instance — and what remains genuinely contested, such as which epistemic assumptions actually drive the result, and avoid presenting any particular resolution as the received view.
