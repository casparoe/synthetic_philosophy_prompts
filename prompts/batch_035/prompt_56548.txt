Background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent thirty-something years as an experimental physicist, mostly measuring things nobody outside my subfield cared about, and retired two years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy like it's my job, and my nephew (a philosophy grad student, thinks he's very clever, mostly is) walked me into an argument I can't get out of. He knows exactly which buttons to push with me, too.

Here's the argument as he gave it, numbered so we can fight about premises:

1. At any given moment, the total evidence the human race has is finite.
2. For any finite body of evidence, there are infinitely many incompatible theories consistent with it. The cheap way to build them: take our best theory T, keep all its predictions, and bolt on some extra baggage -- an invisible ether that produces the same readings, or a fifth force that interacts with nothing detectable, or the universe got created last Thursday with all the records in place. He calls these "gerrymandered rivals," which I gather is a real term of art.
3. If two theories agree on all the evidence there is, evidence can't decide between them.
4. So whenever physicists pick the theory without the invisible ether, the tiebreaker isn't evidence. It's parsimony, or elegance, or just what the community is used to.
5. But parsimony and elegance are exactly the kind of thing astrologers and phrenologists also cite in defense of their systems. So the difference between physics and astrology isn't that one tracks truth and the other doesn't -- it's that physics happened to win the social contest.
6. And if theory choice bottoms out in convention, then the fact that our theories make stunningly accurate predictions doesn't tell us they're true. The muon's magnetic moment coming out right to ten decimal places is just the convention working out.
7. Therefore nothing I did for 35 years taught anyone anything about how the world actually is. I was measuring a world we'd made up.

And here's the thing: I absolutely refuse (7). I watched a whole experimental community, people who barely agreed on where to have lunch, converge on the same picture of nature for reasons that didn't feel like taste. If you tell me that was all convention I think you owe me an explanation of why the conventions keep making the detectors click exactly where they were supposed to.

But when I push back on individual steps they all sound fine to me. (1) is trivially true. (2) seems right -- I can construct those gerrymandered rivals myself all day. (3) sounds like a tautology. (4) and (5) have this seductive "science studies" flavor that my nephew deploys like a trump card. My suspicion is that something slippery happens between "consistent with the evidence we happen to have" and something stronger, like "equally supported" or "guaranteed to survive the next round of data" -- the Lorentz ether and special relativity looked neck and neck in 1905, but nobody told the ether theorists that the next fifty years of experiments were coming. Also not sure "parsimony" should be lumped in with elegance as if counting entities is the same kind of aesthetic call as admiring an equation. But I can't quite pin down which step is the load-bearing mistake, or what I'd have to give up to reject it.

So: walk me through it like I'm a colleague who's read some Quine and Duhem secondhand but never had a philosophy teacher. Which premise do you reject, why did it look fine to me (and to a lot of real philosophers, from what I can tell), and what does rejecting it cost -- because I get the sense there's a version of this argument that's basically just Hume's induction problem wearing a lab coat, and if the fix for underdetermination doesn't also fix induction, I want to know whether I'm buying two solutions or one. For what it's worth, if it comes down to it, I'd sooner bite the bullet on some version of (2) than give up anything close to (7). My nephew is coming to visit in a month and I intend to be ready.
