so for my bioethics seminar we just did the Thomson unit (violinist, people-seeds, the good samaritan stuff) and I have to write something creative instead of an essay, and I want to try satire. The idea: write a patient information booklet, FAQ style, for a hospital that has opened a new ward where members of the public are surgically attached to ailing violinists who will die without the connection. The hospital is fictional (call it something bland like St. Cerebrum Regional), the ward is presented totally matter-of-factly, and the FAQ answers questions a confused "donor" might ask — how long they'll be plugged in, whether they can unplug, what happens if they try, whether the hospital got their consent in some fine print they never read, what the policy is if they got attached not by the society of music lovers but by some random person (or by a burglar), and so on.

The jokes have to come from the actual argument structure, not from slapstick. So the answers should quietly track the real positions: one clause should endorse the idea that being unplugged is different from being killed and have billing consequences accordingly; another should distinguish letting-die from active harm; there should be a tiered "samaritan policy" — like "Level 1: Minimally Decent Samaritan (mandatory), Level 2: Good Samaritan (voluntary, ask about our rewards program)" — and a separate clinic or annex for the people-seed situation, with a disclaimer about pollen filters that shifts blame onto the patient for having them installed and knowing they sometimes fail. A quote from an invented administrator is fine (something deadpan like a hospital spokesperson explaining that no one is *required* to be a good samaritan, only a minimally decent one), but don't quote any real text verbatim — paraphrase the thought experiments in your own words instead of quoting the paper.

Two constraints I care about: first, the tone stays deadpan the whole way — no winking at the reader, the booklet reads like it was written by a hospital that genuinely believes in its mission, and the reader figures out what's being ironized on their own. Second, and this is the hard part: I don't want a one-sided hit piece on either side of the actual abortion debate. The tacit-consent clause should be given its best real-world analog (consent via fine print, knowing the risks, etc.), and the absurdity should land on enforcing supererogation — the idea that anyone can be *made* a good samaritan — rather than on the pro-life position itself. Someone who knows the Thomson literature should read it and think the satire is fair to the argument, and someone who's never heard of it should still find it funny as a dystopian hospital brochure. Around 700 words, formatted as numbered questions with answers, maybe a small "ward policies" box at the end.

(oh and if you could avoid graphic medical description that would be good, my prof said keep it conceptual — the humor should be in the logic, not the gore.)
