Take the whole Lucas–Penrose business — Lucas's 1961 paper arguing from Gödel's incompleteness theorems that the human mind can't be a machine, and Penrose reviving the argument decades later in his books — and analyze it as a case study in what happens when Gödel gets taken outside mathematics. Not the standard "here are famous misuses of Gödel" rundown, which I've read a hundred times, but an actual case analysis, run in both directions.

For the theory side, lay out what the theorems actually say before anything gets exported: the conditions on the system (recursively axiomatizable, enough arithmetic, consistent — or sound, depending which version), what "unprovable" means inside an axiomatic system, and the gap between "true but unprovable in S" and the loose vernacular "there are truths reason cannot reach" that people slide to. Then map Lucas's argument onto that: for any machine M offered as a model of the mathematician, the mathematician can construct M's Gödel sentence and see that it's true, while M can't prove it, so M isn't an adequate model. And then map the mechanist replies that actually came back at him over the following decades — that the human has no way of knowing which machine is on the table, so can't produce its Gödel sentence; that the machine can just add the Gödel sentence as a new axiom and the argument restarts; that by the second theorem the human can't establish their own consistency either, so the "and I see it's true" step was never licensed by anything Gödel proved. Be disciplined about which of these replies is a real counter to the argument and which is a dodge.
