A coworker and I keep arguing about when someone deserves to be credited with knowing how to do something, and the argument has now moved to our code-assistant tooling. She has a strikingly consistent track record: she says a colleague who has read every book on bicycle repair and can answer any question about it, but has never fixed a bike, does not know how to fix bikes; a pianist paralyzed by ALS who can still teach students and mentally rehearse flawlessly still knows how to play; a man who has lost all episodic memory but still rides flawlessly knows how to ride a bike; a brute-force chess engine that was never given any human-legible heuristics knows how to play chess; and someone who has memorized the complete theory of swimming, has never swum, and would drown does not know how to swim.

Next sprint we have three cases to call: (1) an LLM pair-programmer that writes correct, novel solutions to problems it has never seen, but can neither run its code, observe results, nor act on anything; (2) a passenger with zero flight experience who lands a small plane by following step-by-step instructions radioed from the ground; and (3) a master silversmith who, after a stroke, still produces perfect work with her hands but can no longer say a single true thing about what she is doing, or recognize her own pieces afterward.

I am less interested in the yes/no calls than in the rule she seems to be running. Please work out the candidate rules that fit her track record equally well — I suspect more than one does — and say which way each rule calls each of the three new cases. Then give your best guess for each case, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her, and identify the further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart. I would also like to know whether any of her earlier verdicts already put pressure on the rules that fit them — the pianist and the chess engine seem to pull in opposite directions to me, but I may be missing something. Please write your answer in American English.
