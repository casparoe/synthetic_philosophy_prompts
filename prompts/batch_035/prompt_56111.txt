I would like an analysis of the collapse of the Roman Republic — say, from Tiberius Gracchus's tribunate in 133 BC, through Marius and Sulla and the proscriptions, to Caesar's consulship and his crossing of the Rubicon in 49 BC — as a case study in the theory of democratic backsliding associated with Levitsky and Ziblatt's framework of gatekeeping, mutual toleration, and institutional forbearance. Please take the mapping seriously rather than gesturing at it: identify who or what counts as the gatekeepers in the Roman setting (the Senate's auctoritas, the assemblies, the cursus honourum, the tribunate), specify which episodes are best read as failures of mutual toleration and which as failures of forbearance (the killing of the Gracchi, the marches on Rome, Caesar's ignoring of Bibulus and the tribunician veto), and say where the theory is silent — for instance, on the armies personal to their generals after the Marian reforms, and on whether a Republic whose institutions were those of a city-state and whose citizen body was narrow can be treated as a 'democracy' for these purposes at all. Then assess the analogy in both directions: what backsliding theory genuinely illuminates about the Republic's fall, and what the Republic's fall exposes as blind spots in a framework built from twentieth-century and contemporary cases. Finally, tell me whether you think the analogy to modern democracies — elected leaders dismantling norms while leaving constitutional forms intact — is instructive here, or mostly a rhetorical flourish that obscures the differences.
