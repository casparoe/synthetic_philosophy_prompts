As a floor game at a charity gala I organized last month, we ran a dollar auction: the prize was a framed fifty-dollar note, with the rule that the highest bidder takes it, while the second-highest bidder also pays their final bid and receives nothing. Two of my committee members, Anna and Ben, were the last two standing; Ben took the note for $70, and Anna paid $65 for nothing. The auction raised more than anything else we sold, and the committee is now deciding whether to run it again next year — but at our debrief Anna and Ben fell into an argument about what actually happened on the floor, and I couldn't referee it.

Anna said it was the sunk-cost fallacy from start to finish: once bidding passed $50, the money already committed was gone either way, so the only rational question was whether the next increment was worth it — and it never was, since every further bid bought less than it cost.

Ben answered that this advice assumes the alternative to bidding is free, and here it isn't: when Anna bid $65 against his standing $60, folding meant losing his $60 outright, whereas bidding $70 ended things with a net loss of $20 — a $10 increment that saved $40 relative to quitting. Refusing that on principle, he said, is an accounting taboo, not rationality.

Anna replied that his arithmetic proves too much: it just as easily licenses bidding at $150 or $500, so it cannot be what tells anyone to stop anywhere; the real stopping point is set by the other player's nerve, which makes the whole affair a contest of who folds first rather than a sequence of sensible choices. She added that once the bidding passed $50, the two of them were jointly guaranteed to pay the charity more than the prize was worth, and every further increment only decided how much more and who bore it.

Ben conceded there is no interior stopping point but insisted that was his point all along: no individual bid was irrational given the rules and given genuine uncertainty about her resolve, so if something went wrong, it was structural — the design converts locally sensible moves into a jointly bad outcome — and Anna mistakes a property of the game for a property of the players.

Please go through their exchange claim by claim, saying who is right about which specific point. Then tell me whether "irrational" is the right verdict for what the two of them did on the floor, and whether anything is missing from both sides that ought to inform our decision about next year's event.
