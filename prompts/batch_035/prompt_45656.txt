I'm a senior software engineer at a mid-sized company, and I've somehow ended up owning the go/no-go call for risky infrastructure changes — database migrations, dependency upgrades, failover drills, that sort of thing. Our current process is a lightweight pre-mortem: each engineer on the call gives a probability that the change will cause a user-visible incident and a probability that the rollback will fail if we need one. The trouble is that the numbers are all over the place and nobody trusts them; on a recent migration the incident estimates ranged from 1% to 25%, and for rollback failure from 2% to 40%. We currently average them, which I've come to suspect is wrong in a systematic way — averaging feels like it launders genuine disagreement and partial ignorance into a confident-looking point estimate that then gets treated as knowledge.

A friend who did a philosophy degree pointed me at the literature on imprecise probabilities, and the core idea — that when your evidence doesn't pin down a probability you should carry an interval or a set of probability assignments through your reasoning rather than collapsing it to a single number — matches my instinct, as does the Ellsberg-style observation that people sensibly treat a known 10% risk differently from a risk that could be anywhere between 1% and 25%. But I can't turn the theory into something my team can actually run at a weekly ops meeting. So I'd like you to act as a formal epistemologist who is used to translating theory into practice for people with no philosophy background. Please think carefully before answering — perhaps start by brainstorming which decision rules from that literature (I've seen the phrases "maximin expected utility", "E-admissibility", and "minimax regret" but don't really understand the trade-offs between them) could survive contact with a whiteboard and a deadline, and then design around the survivor.

The deliverable I want is a decision procedure we can run in about fifteen minutes per change: what we elicit and how, how we aggregate — or deliberately refuse to aggregate — individual estimates into an interval, which decision rule we apply to the interval for a go/no-go call with a clear stopping rule for "no", and what we do when the rule abstains but the business forces a binary choice anyway. It has to handle three cases: the migration above, with its embarrassingly wide intervals; a routine dependency patch where the interval is narrow but sits right on our risk threshold; and a failover drill that carries a small risk of its own but whose real value is information for a future emergency — and where I worry that a naive worst-case rule would talk us out of ever running it. Please be explicit about which steps are genuine theory and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, where the procedure will predictably misfire, and how to stop engineers from gaming the elicitation by quoting extreme numbers to veto changes they dislike for unrelated reasons. No reading lists or citations, please — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography, since I'll be working from this document alone.
