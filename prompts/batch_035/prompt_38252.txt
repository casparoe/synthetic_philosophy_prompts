In my department's experimental philosophy reading group we've spent two semesters on whether cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions undermines the armchair method, and the discussion has calcified in a way I want to understand better. One side points to the familiar results: Gödel/Schmidt-style cases about the reference of proper names and Gettier-type cases about knowledge, where subjects from different cultural backgrounds — Western undergraduates versus East Asian respondents, roughly — apparently diverge in their judgments, and draws the conclusion that intuition-driven philosophy is methodologically compromised, resting on an unrepresentative sample of human cognitive responses. The other side replies with a mix of replication worries, objections to vignette interpretation, and the calibrationist point that cases function like evidence in other domains: if demographic variables affect perception or memory, that's a reason to correct, not to abandon. Nobody has moved in months. My own hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the two camps aren't really disagreeing about the empirical results at all, but are operating with different implicit conceptions of what an intuition is being used for in an argument: one treats the judgment elicited by a case as a datum to be explained, the other as a defeasible input to a competence that the case merely prompts, so that each side's "the intuitions vary / no they don't" talk ships past itself.

There's also a second-layer problem our group keeps circling without naming. A dissenting member of the group insists that even in principle, no survey could settle the issue, because what the armchair philosopher consults in the seminar room and what a questionnaire measures are differently individuated states: one is embedded in a dialectical context, produced against a background of philosophical training and rival cases, the other is an untutored one-shot response to a paragraph on a screen. If she's right, the empirical literature is answering a subtly different question from the one the traditionalist cares about, and the debate is partly verbal. But I'm not sure how far that goes — the variationists can reply that the seminar-room judgment is itself just a culturally saturated response with extra training on top, and that privileging it is the very parochialism the studies were meant to expose.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it substantive — a genuine dispute about the evidential status of intuitions that would be resolved by better empirical work — or verbal, hinging on how each side individuates the mental states in question, or is it instead a deeper methodological divide about what philosophy's data are, which no experiment could touch? In diagnosing it, engage what each side actually claims rather than surveying the literature, and tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress: a better-designed replication, an agreed-upon account of the individuation question, a distinction between the epistemology of the case method and its sociology, or an argument that the impasse is structural and the two research programs should simply go their separate ways. I'd also like your view on whether my group member's individuation worry generalizes: if no questionnaire can measure "the" intuition the philosopher uses, does that show the empirical critique is confused, or just that it's aimed at a different and less important target?
