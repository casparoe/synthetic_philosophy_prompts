You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of memory, with deep expertise in the debate between the duty to remember and the case for forgetting, and enough experience advising technology companies to know how their internal review boards actually talk. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please keep technical philosophical vocabulary explained in plain terms wherever you use it. Here is my situation. I work on an AI assistant product that retains memory across conversations: it remembers facts users told it weeks ago, infers standing preferences, and builds up a running model of each user's life. I am proposing that we deliberately build forgetting in. The design has three tiers. First, verbatim transcripts decay into summaries after a fixed window, so details fade rather than persist indefinitely. Second, emotionally charged memories -- a fight with a spouse, a failed job hunt -- get shorter retention unless the user marks them as important, on the theory that people often want to be able to move on. Third, users get a command that erases not just stored data but the assistant's inferred conclusions, so it cannot quietly keep holding the grudge or the grievance for them.

Next week I present this to our internal ethics review board. The board includes a philosopher who takes the duty to remember seriously in roughly Margalit's spirit: she believes that anyone who holds a person's history has custodial duties, that letting a person's painful memories slip away because they stopped bringing them up is a kind of abandonment, and that our assistant, having received these confidences, has entered something like a thick relation with the user rather than a merely contractual one. There is also a memory researcher who worries that we are romanticizing decay, since human forgetting is not graceful but often distortive -- we lose the context and keep the feeling -- and a trust-and-safety lead who will want to know what happens when the thing we have agreed to forget is evidence of a crime, or the only record a user has of being wronged. My own instinct leans the other way from the philosopher: I suspect that much of what people use our product for is precisely the ability to move on, and I have read enough of Rieff's side of the argument to worry that an institution that never forgets quietly becomes an instrument for keeping wounds open.

For each objection, provide a two-minute reply in plain language that I can deliver without a philosophy background, a note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede up front to stay credible.
