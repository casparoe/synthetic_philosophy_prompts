One of my PhD students, a Thomist with a missionary streak, thinks he's finally got a knockdown version of the grounding objection to Molinism, and I keep telling him it proves too much, but I'll be damned if I can say exactly where it slips. Here's his argument as he ran it past me:

(1) Molinism says that logically prior to any decree to create, God knows all the true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom -- if Curley, everyone's favorite bribable mayor from the literature, were offered the bribe in circumstances C, he would freely take it.
(2) These counterfactuals are, if true at all, true independently of God's will. That's non-negotiable: the whole point of middle knowledge is to let God survey how creatures would freely act and then choose a world, without God being the author of what Curley does in it.
(3) Every contingent truth is grounded -- true in virtue of how things are, in facts about actual substances, their natures, and their powers.
(4) No creature grounds the Curley conditional, since in the world where it matters Curley is never even created, and an uncreated creature has no goings-on to ground anything.
(5) God doesn't ground it either: if God's will made it true that Curley would freely take the bribe, the truth would be a decree, and the taking wouldn't be free in any sense worth the name.
(6) So if the conditional is true, it's a contingent truth with no ground at all -- just floating there, true in virtue of nothing whatsoever.
(7) There are no ungrounded contingent truths. A proposition that nothing makes true isn't true, and so isn't knowable, not even by an omniscient being.
(8) So no counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are true, middle knoweldge has no objects, and Molinism turns out to be knowledge of nothing.

Now here's what keeps me up. Substitute "Peter will deny Christ tomorrow" for the Curley conditional and run the whole thing again. (2) still holds -- keep the freedom, no divine willing of the denial. (3) is the same grounding principle. (4) nothing actual yet grounds a claim about tomorrow. (5) God doesn't will the denial. (6) floating truth. (7) no floating truths. (8) no foreknowledge of free actions. My student just shrugs -- he's happy to take a bullet, says he'll appeal to God's timeless eternity and claim the future event itself is the ground, with all the tense talk transposed into the key of eternity. But when I ask whether whatever that move is doing for him is available to the Molinist too -- why can't Curley's counterfactual get grounded in the same eternity, somehow? -- he mutters about unactualized possibles and changes the subject, which is how I know he doesn't have an answer either. And to make things worse, he adds for good measure that even if (7) were false, you'd have brute contingent truths that God has to take as given -- truths he didn't make and couldn't alter -- which he says wrecks divine aseity on its own, so Molinism loses either way.

So: which premise or step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'll admit upfront I'd rather keep (7) than keep Molinism -- I'm not sentimental about middle knowledge -- but I've got another student writing her dissertation on it, and I'd like to hand her something more precise than "the grounding objection feels bad."
