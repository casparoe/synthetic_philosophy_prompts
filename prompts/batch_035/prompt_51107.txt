My oldest friend is an uncompromising Scanlonian contractualist — battered copy of What We Owe to Each Other, arguments framed as what principle no one could reasonably reject, answers owed to each person rather than to a sum — and over the years she has accumulated a settled track record on cases where one group's interests can be protected only at another group's expense. When two crews of trapped miners face identical harms and there is time to dig out only one, she digs for the larger crew. When a clinic can spend its last dose of a strong analgesic either to end one patient's severe pain or to relieve twenty patients' mild pain, she spends it on the one. When a broadcaster would have to interrupt a championship game watched by ten million to warn five line workers off a live cable, she holds the station not merely permitted but required to interrupt. And when a rescue team can save either one person from death or five from permanent paralysis, she sends it after the one.

Our reading group takes up the aggregation problem next month, and I want to arrive with a prediction. Three new cases are on the agenda: (a) a surgical team can use a single operating slot either for five patients who will each lose a hand or for one patient who will lose an entire arm at the shoulder; (b) a safety budget can be spent either on preventing one worker's certain death or on precautions that would spare two thousand workers each a one-in-a-thousand risk of death; (c) a public health agency can fund either treatment for one person's lifelong severe chronic pain or prevention of a week of moderate pain for a hundred thousand people.

Please predict her verdict on each case. Lay out at least two principles that fit her entire track record — for instance, a rule on which numbers only ever break ties between complaints of exactly equal strength, versus a rule on which numbers count whenever the competing harms fall within a common band of severity, versus any weighted-aggregation rule steep enough to reproduce her past refusals — and say what each principle predicts for the new cases. Explain which principle sits best with the contractualist machinery she actually invokes, and give your best guess for each verdict with your reasons. Finally, tell me which further case would most cleanly separate the candidate principles, and whether adopting any of them would force her to repudiate a verdict she has already given.
