I've been a practicing Stoic for several years, and I've finally tried to write down what I actually believe — partly for a discussion group I'm in, partly because I've started to suspect the pieces don't fit together as neatly as I assume. Here is my working set of commitments:

1. The strict dichotomy of control. Things divide cleanly into what is up to us — our judgments, our assent to impressions, our impulses — and what is not: health, reputation, wealth, other people, outcomes. I reject the popular modern trichotomy reformulation, the one that inserts a middle category of things only partially up to us, like winning a tennis match. That looks like a fudge to me, and a fudge that destroys the doctrine's therapeutic point.

2. Virtue is sufficient for happiness, and I mean this in the strong classical sense: the virtuous person on the rack, mid-torture, is doing as well as a human being can do. Externals are, at most, rationally preferable or dispreferable, and they make no difference to how well one's life is going.

3. I am a thoroughgoing naturalist about everything else: no providence, no cosmic reason, no design in nature, no divine order for the wise person to harmonize with. I regard the ancient Stoics' theological physics as a historical curiosity we are well rid of.

4. I take the connection between Stoicism and cognitive behavioral therapy very seriously. I believe the core Stoic psychological claim — that our emotions are constituted by our evaluative judgments — is basically right, and I take CBT's clinical success as evidence of that. At the same time, I accept what the psychological literature says about the rest of the mind: that our judgments are largely automatic, habituated, biased, and shaped by trauma and development, and that changing them takes months of structured practice, homework, and relapse.

5. I treat my health as a preferred indifferent, but I pursue it seriously: I exercise, I sleep carefully, I stay in therapy, and I take an antidepressant.

6. I spend a good deal of time on climate activism, I consider it one of the best uses of my life, and I am quite sure it matters whether we win.

When I try to audit this myself, I either find no tensions at all or so many that I suspect I'm reasoning badly, so I want a second opinion. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what each tension actually turns on. Rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each genuine one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I should add that I don't have any sense of where the problem is, if there is one — so don't let my ordering of the list mislead you.
