I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience, and I've realised that my considered reactions to questions about brain size and moral status don't obviously fit together, so I'd like your help extracting the principle, if there is one, behind them. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases, followed by a tentative principle of my own that I suspect doesn't quite work. Please test it against the cases, repair or replace it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts the repaired principle cannot save.

My verdicts:

1. An adult human with a congenitally very small brain (severe microcephaly) who, against the odds, lives a recognisably human life — relationships, language, humour, plans for the future — has exactly the same moral status as anyone else. Brain size is irrelevant here.
2. An elephant, whose brain is physically larger than mine, does not thereby have a higher moral status than I do. I'd even say its status is lower, though I feel uneasy writing that.
3. That said, I'm fairly convinced that elephants and corvids matter morally in a way that similarly sized-brained animals with far less cognitive sophistication do not — a raven's moral claim on us seems to me greater than that of an animal with ten times its brain mass but a much simpler behavioural repertoire.
4. A patient who has had an entire cerebral hemisphere removed in childhood and grown up cognitively typical retains full moral status, even though half the brain is gone.
5. A patient in a permanent vegetative state, with a fully intact, full-sized brain, has lost much of the moral status they had whilst conscious. Whatever grounds their status, it doesn't seem to be the brain sitting there in the skull.
6. An infant born with anencephaly, I have to admit, seems to me to have a profoundly diminished moral status — which is uncomfortable given verdict 1, since both cases involve drastic underdevelopment of the cortex.
7. If a Neanderthal walked into the lab tomorrow, their moral status would be exactly ours, and I don't think I'd be moved by the fact that their brain was somewhat larger than mine. More status for more brain seems obviously wrong when it runs in that direction.
8. Here's where I wobble: a hypothetical future person whose brain was engineered to be smaller but far more efficient — say, human-equivalent cognition from half the cortical tissue — would obviously keep full moral status. But I'm genuinely unsure whether a person engineered in the opposite direction, with human-equivalent cognition from twice the cortical tissue, would thereby have *greater* status. Part of me wants to say yes; part of me thinks that's monstrous.

My tentative principle, half-remembered from a seminar: moral status tracks the absolute number of cortical neurons, or something in that vicinity — not brain mass, not encephalisation quotient, not species membership. I like it because it explains why the elephant (whose cortical neuron count is, if my recollection of the comparative literature is right, a fair fraction of ours but well below it) matters less than a human but far more than I'd have guessed from the old brain-mass figures, and why small-brained but densely packed cortices might punch above their weight. But I can already see it struggling with several of my verdicts, and I can't tell whether the failures are fatal or fixable with amendments that don't collapse into something vacuous.

So, three requests. First, state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that it delivers a determinate verdict on each of my eight cases, and show me where it succeeds and where it forces me to give something up. Second, tell me what the best candidate says about cases I haven't listed — a person with normal brain size but profound congenital intellectual disability; a bottlenose dolphin; a hypothetical human-level artificial system with no neurons at all; and a person whose cognition is enhanced, not just preserved, by added cortical tissue. Third, be honest about which of my verdicts is least defensible, and about whether my verdicts 1 and 6, taken together, can be reconciled by any principle that keeps brain properties doing genuine work rather than smuggling in a personhood criterion through the back door.

Please write in British English, and don't pull your punches about the tensions — I'd rather have a principle that costs me a verdict than one that's been padded to fit them all.
