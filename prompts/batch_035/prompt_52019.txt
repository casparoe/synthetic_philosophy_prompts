I'd like a reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through Grice's distinction between sentence meaning and speaker meaning and his account of conversational implicature. Stevens is a narrator who speaks almost entirely in understatement: he flouts the maxims of quantity and manner as a matter of professional policy, and other characters — Miss Kenton above all — are left to compute the implicatures he refuses to make explicit. Take the scene in which Miss Kenton pries the romance novel from his hands: what does each of them say, what do they mean, what do they imply, and on Grice's account who bears responsibility for the conversation's failure? Consider whether Stevens's ideal of dignity can be understood as a standing refusal to have speaker meanings at all — a rule of saying things while meaning nothing beyond them — and whether that makes him, in Grice's terms, a cooperative or uncooperative conversationalist. Finally, address the narration itself: since the entire novel is Stevens's retrospective report, we only ever see implicatures as he presents them, which raises the possibility that he computes Miss Kenton's meanings perfectly well and reports them selectively. Where does the Gricean apparatus genuinely illuminate the novel, where does it strain, and does the novel show something about implicature that the theory itself does not — say, the cost of a life conducted entirely through what is meant but never said?
