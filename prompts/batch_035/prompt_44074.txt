My brother-in-law, who reads a lot of physics popularizations, recently explained a view called structural realism to me, and I've been chewing on it for a week without being able to decide how much of it to trust. Here is roughly what he said:

"Structural realism is the idea that physics gives us the structure of the world but not the stuff that has the structure. We know the equations and the relations between things, but the intrinsic nature of an electron is forever hidden from us. The view was invented by a philosopher named John Worrall in a 1989 paper, as a reply to the no-miracles argument. His favorite example is light: Fresnel's wave theory turned out to be wrong about what light is, but his equations carried over into Maxwell's theory almost untouched. So science gets the structure right even when it gets the objects wrong, which is why the pessimistic induction refutes scientific realism but leaves structural realism standing. In the end it amounts to the same thing as saying we can never know anything about the unobservable world: only the equations are real, and electrons and everything else are just bookkeeping devices."

Parts of this sounded genuinely convincing to me, especially the Fresnel example. But the last step bothers me: saying electrons are "just bookkeeping" sounds like an anti-realist position, the kind of view that says science tells us nothing about a hidden world — and I thought the whole point of structural realism was to resist that. I also can't work out how a view can be a reply to the no-miracles argument and at the same time be vindicated by the pessimistic induction; those sound like opposite arguments, so one of those claims is probably misstated, but I don't know which.

Please go through his summary piece by piece and tell me what a philosopher working on scientific realism would accept, what a structural realist would object to, and what is simply confused.
