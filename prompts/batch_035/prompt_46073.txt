I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on population ethics, and we've just finished the unit on the procreation asymmetry — the claim that there's a moral asymmetry between creating people whose lives will be predictably miserable and failing to create people whose lives would have been happy, such that the first is a wrongdoing while the second is at most a missed opportunity rather than an injustice to anyone. For the final week, I want to pair the theory with Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, and I'd like your help working out how to teach it as a philosophical text.

Here is my tentative framing, which I'd like you to test and push back on. The clones in the novel are created for their organs, under conditions that many readers intuit as deeply wrong. Yet their lives seem, by Kathy's account, to be worth living — full of friendship, art, small hopes, and even a kind of contentment — and, crucially, the non-identity problem appears to apply: the donors could not have existed at all except as donors, so no alternative life was available to them, and it's unclear who is wronged or what the wronging consists in. In one way this seems to sharpen the case for something like the asymmetry: the wrong of creating the donors doesn't look like a wrong done to them in virtue of their lives going badly, so population ethicists who insist that wrongs to future people must show up as complaints those people can make seem to be missing something the novel makes vivid. In another way the novel seems to complicate the asymmetry, because the felt wrongness has less to do with the donors' lifetime welfare than with their status — being created as means, denied knowledge, kept for later harvesting — which suggests the morally relevant asymmetry may not run between happy and miserable lives but between creating people for acceptable and unacceptable reasons.

Please develop a reading along the following lines. First, assess whether the novel's setup really is a case for the procreation asymmetry specifically, or whether it instead supports a different view in the neighborhood — a person-affecting view that bites the bullet on non-identity, a sufficientarian or threshold view about lives worth living, or a view on which creating people can wrong them by their own lights even when their lives go well. Second, attend to features of the novel that resist a tidy philosophical use: the donors' own near-total absence of protest, their attachment to Hailsham and to each other, the rumor of deferrals for couples truly in love, and the revelation that Hailsham existed to demonstrate to the outside world that the clones had inner lives — the world, that is, granted them souls only as an argument for better treatment, never as a reason not to create them at all. Does Kathy's serene narration support the claim that her life was worth living, or should we distrust her as narrator precisely because she was raised never to protest, and does that epistemic problem have an analogue in the philosophy, where we ask whether a created person's own endorsement of their life settles the question? Third, tell me which scenes or plot elements you would actually put in front of students to generate discussion, and what each one is meant to show — and be honest about where the novel cuts against the asymmetry rather than for it, including whether it leaves us unable to say why, exactly, a world with the donors in it is worse than the actual world without them.

I'd like the reading to take both the novel and the population ethics seriously — faithful to the text's tone and to the actual structure of the debate, not reducing the one to an illustration of the other — and to end by saying what the novel gives the argument that the argument alone doesn't give us.
