I write backend systems for a living and have no background in biology or philosophy beyond a couple of popular science books and some late-night Wikipedia spiral, so please bear with what may be a badly-posed question. I've become obsessed with trying to figure out, when a trait is an adaptation, what the trait is an adaptation *of* — which entity is the one whose benefit explains why the trait exists. At work we constantly ask which layer of a stack a behavior belongs to, and it's usually decidable because a person designed the layers. Evolution has what look like layers — genes, organisms, colonies, populations — but nobody assigned functionality to them, and I can't find the rule. So I did what I'd do with a flaky distributed system: I collected the cases where my gut is confident and I'm asking you to reverse-engineer the rule from them, because I've tried and failed.

My verdicts, as concretely as I can state them. When a wolf pack chases a deer herd, each deer running faster than its herdmates is obviously an adaptation of the individual deer, not of the herd. The honeybee colony's foraging system — sterile workers, the waggle dance — I want to say is an adaptation of the *colony*, not of any bee, since no worker can reproduce and everything is organized around the queen's output. There's a gene in mice that cheats during meiosis so that it ends up in far more than half the sperm, at the cost of the male's fertility; that one feels clearly like an adaptation of the gene itself, at the mouse's expense. Sickle-cell: heterozygotes get malaria resistance, homozygotes get a terrible disease; my gut says this is an adaptation of the *organism* (malaria resistance), even though you could just as well tell the story as the allele looking out for itself. Older female killer whales stop reproducing and instead boost their daughters' offspring — I'd call that an adaptation of the grandmother, not of any group, since it's her fitness being maximized through relatives. When slime mold cells starve they aggregate into a slug and roughly a fifth of them become a dead stalk so the rest can be carried up and sporulate; I'm committed to saying that's an adaptation of the slug, treated as an individual, though I admit I waver because the slug dissolves afterward. Clonal bacteria in a biofilm secrete a shared protective matrix — by my ant-colony logic (individually useless, benefits only neighbors, and the neighbors are genetically identical) this should be an even stronger case of a group adaptation, but my gut refuses, and says it's just individual cells whose secretion happens to help identical copies of themselves. And finally, a cancer cell line proliferating through a body: that's selection happening, sure, but I want to say it's not an *adaptation* at all, it's malfunction — even though structurally it looks just like the meiotic-driving mouse gene, which I did grant was an adaptation. I already suspect my biofilm verdict and my ant-colony verdict can't both survive, and I can't tell whether the cancer one is consistent with anything, but I genuinely don't know which of my calls is the bad one.

So: lead with your candidate principle — or your verdict that no single principle captures all of these — in your first sentence, and then walk me through how it does on each case above, including exactly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up if any. Then tell me what it predicts for three cases I haven't listed: a siphonophore like the Portuguese man o' war, a colony of specialized, genetically near-identical zooids that functions as one animal; a mitochondrial mutation that skews its host's offspring toward daughters because mitochondria pass only through eggs; and a wolf pack's coordinated cooperative hunt, which sits somewhere between the deer herd and the beehive in my head and I can't place it. If your principle needs a secondary rule to handle the stragglers, say so explicitly rather than pretending it's clean — I'd rather have an honest two-part rule with the motivating case for each part than a single elegant rule that quietly misses my slime-mold call.
