For some years now I have been thinking, entirely on my own, about the difference between guilt and shame, and about whether a culture can actually choose which of the two to lean on when it wants people to behave. My starting hunch is that it can: a village where everyone knows everyone seems to run naturally on shame, while a big anonymous city seems forced to fall back on guilt, since strangers can't give you the look your neighbors can. When I test this hunch against concrete cases, though, my reactions don't line up, and I would like to know whether some principle captures them or whether I am simply inconsistent. Let me lay out the cases with my honest verdicts, then the tentative principle I have half-formed, and you can tell me whether it survives.

The cases. A neighbor realizes that everyone on his street knows he cheats on his taxes, and he starts keeping his accounts straight because of that: shame doing its job, and I am fine with it. A man who wronged someone privately, with no one the wiser, lies awake sick about it and quietly makes amends: guilt doing its job, also fine. A woman who has done nothing wrong carries a constant low hum of shame about her accent and her family's poverty, things she cannot control: I think that shame is a wound doing no work at all, not a tool. A man who fully repaired a betrayal fifteen years ago and still feels the guilt every day: I think the guilt has gone bad, become useless. An online mob of strangers shames someone over a stupid joke he made a decade ago: not fine, and yet when his own small community gives him the cold shoulder over a fresh, deliberate act of cruelty, that seems legitimate to me, which is awkward because both are audiences. A parent shames a child for bedwetting: clearly not fine; a parent shames a child for lying: somehow fine, though I cannot say what changed. And finally, a society that uses shame to keep women deferential: I condemn it without hesitation, even though it is a community enforcing a norm it genuinely holds, which seems to wreck the comfortable thought that communities are entitled to their own shame culture.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is half right and half confused, goes something like this: shame is the emotion of being seen failing, and guilt is the emotion of having failed. Shame therefore needs an audience, guilt does not, and a culture should lean on shame when it can supply a stable audience that the person actually cares about, and on guilt when it cannot. Please test this principle against my cases and tell me where it breaks. It seems to explain the village and the private guilt, and maybe the online case if strangers somehow don't count as a real audience, but it has nothing to say about the bedwetting child or the deference norm, and I cannot tell whether that is a flaw in the principle or in my verdicts.

So my question, stated plainly: is there a principle about which emotion a culture should cultivate, aimed at which targets, imposed by which audiences, that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts? If several principles compete, say which verdicts each one must throw overboard, and tell me honestly which of my reactions you think is simply wrong and should be surrendered. I would also like you to use the principle you settle on, or the best candidate, to predict a case I have not mentioned: a state government considering billboards that publicly name and shame men who are delinquent on child support. Say what the principle implies about the billboards and why, and, if you can, where the principle would draw the outer boundary beyond which any shame, however useful, stops being something a decent society would deliberately deploy.

A few requests about the form of your reply. Please write it as continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter or email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs. And please use American English throughout.
