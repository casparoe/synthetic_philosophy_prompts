Two of my friends have been arguing about effective altruism for like three years now and it's gotten to the point where neither of them actually hears the other one anymore, so I want to commission the document they should have written instead. Friend A is pretty deep in EA — works at a meta-charity-ish org, used to be really into earning to give, defends the whole cost-effectiveness framework on basically every point. Friend B was into it in college, then drifted out and now does labor organizing, and her whole critique is institutional: that EA's obsession with measurability structurally steers money and people toward whatever can be randomized-controlled-trialed (deworming, bednets) and away from anything that works through institutions or politics, that earning to give mostly launders ambitious career choices people already wanted to make anyway, and that a movement funded by tech fortunes is never going to ask the questions that threaten tech fortunes. Friend A says all of this is either wrong or actually a feature: he claims legibility isn't a bias, it's just epistemic hygiene — you do what you can actually measure, and vague appeals to "systemic change" have a bad track record compared to things you can audit.

They finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and somehow I got voluntold to draft the joint statement. Here's what I need: a numbered section of claims they can both actually sign their names to (I suspect there's more than they think — e.g. about replacement effects in labor markets, or that some EA causes like biosecurity and policy work already don't fit the "measurable only" stereotype). Then a disagreements table where each disagreement is phrased so the person who holds that view would accept the wording — Friend A keeps saying Friend B thinks "EA is bad" and she keeps saying he thinks "nothing EA does could be improved," and I'm pretty sure neither of those is right. Then the cruxes. I have a suspicion about what at least one of them is: whether the measurability constraint is a *selection filter* (it changes which problems get worked on at all, so it compounds over decades) or just a *prioritization tiebreaker* among equally worthy options. But there's also the talent-drift question — whether the sharp kids who go into quant-adjacent EA careers would otherwise have gone into congress or journalism or union stuff or just more quant finance — and the funder-dependence question, which Friend A thinks is just an ad hominem and Friend B thinks is about incentive gradients over time.

For each crux, if you can, give the concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so they can't weasel out later — e.g., what each would expect to see if measurability were really a selection filter, or what the talent question would even look like answered empirically. And if any chunk of their fight turns out to be verbal — I have a feeling "systemic change" means different things to each of them, like policy reform vs. structural change to who holds power — please call that out explicitly and say which part, because at this point I'd happily settle for them discovering they agree more than they admit. Also feel free to note if one side's disagreement is doing less work than its owner thinks, but the goal is a document both could sign, not a verdict on who won.
