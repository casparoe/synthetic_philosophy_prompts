I teach a graduate seminar on intergenerational ethics, and I am preparing a session on the social discount rate. My co-teacher, an economist, holds that a positive rate of pure time preference is rationally permissible: on his view, preferring welfare now to qualitatively identical welfare later is no more irrational than preferring one's own welfare to a stranger's, and moral theory has no business legislating individual patience. I hold roughly the opposite view, on broadly Parfitian grounds: if personal identity over time consists in nothing over and above psychological continuity and connectedness, then privileging one's near future over one's far future looks structurally like privileging oneself over others — something that might be tolerated as a prerogative but can hardly be insisted upon, and certainly cannot be built into public policy, where something like Sidgwickian temporal impartiality should govern.

Rather than simply rehash our seminar argument, I want a candid credence from you: what is your probability that a positive rate of pure time preference — discounting welfare merely because it arrives later, not because of risk, uncertainty, or diminishing marginal returns — is rationally permissible for an individual agent? Please decompose the number. Suggested terms, though you may restructure if you think the question carves differently: the probability that reductionism about personal identity is roughly correct; the probability that, conditional on reductionism, one's near-future selves are genuinely disanalogous to near strangers — for instance because a single planning agency must be allowed to weight the stages of its own projects, or because near selves stand to benefit from present sacrifice in ways that distant selves and foreigners do not; the probability that permissibility at the individual level transfers to the aggregate or policy level, which is where my colleague's conclusion does its real work; and any residual discount for the possibility that rational permissibility is not one notion here, since prudential, moral, and policy senses of the term may quietly come apart, and the classical literature is notorious for sliding among them. Assign a rough probability and a one-line justification to each term, then show me how they combine. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in and what argument or evidence would move it most, and whether you think my credence of about 0.3 or his of about 0.7 is closer to the truth — but more importantly, where our disagreement most likely lives: in the metaphysics of identity over time, or in the step from what individuals may do to what institutions may assume.
