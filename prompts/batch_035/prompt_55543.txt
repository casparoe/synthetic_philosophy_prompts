Moore's famous proof in passing, so I asked my roommate — a philosophy major who took a whole seminar on skepticism last year — to fill me in over coffee. Afterward I wrote down her summary as best I could remember it: Moore, in his 1939 lecture, held up his hands and said "here is one hand" and "and here is another," and since hands are external things, he concluded that an external world exists. As a proof, she said, it's a joke: the premise just assumes the conclusion, so it's circular; Moore himself admitted he had no way of knowing the premise was true, saying the most he could do was prove that he had hands, not that he knew he had hands; the proof was supposed to answer Descartes, but it plainly begs the question against the dream argument, since Moore just asserts the very thing the skeptic doubts, and question-begging is a logical fallacy, not an answer. She added that Wittgenstein understood all this and demolished the proof in On Certainty, and that no serious philosopher has defended it since.

I'm inclined to agree with her — the whole thing does sound like Moore was just being stubborn — but something nags at me: our professor said Moore's paper is still the standard starting point for the contemporary debate about skepticism, which would be a strange fate for a famous joke. So I'd like a careful audit. Please go through my roommate's summary point by point and tell me what a specialist on Moore would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong; and then tell me whether her overall verdict — that the proof has no serious philosophical standing — follows from whatever parts of her summary survive the corrections. I'd especially like to know whether "circular" and "begging the question" are being used in a technical sense or an everyday one here, what Moore actually said about his own inability to prove the premise, and whether Wittgenstein's late remarks on Moore's argument really amount to a demolition.
