I am a novelist some years into a book in which Johannes Kepler appears as a character, and I have come to distrust my own instincts about him. The ready-made picture — the man who found that the planets move on ellipses and, apparently in the same breath, read nativities for money — makes him either a modern rationalist with an embarrassing habit or a credulous crank with a genius bolted on. Either version ruins the book. What I find in the sources is someone far more tangled: in his 1610 pamphlet Tertius Interveniens he positions himself as a third party between the fanatics and the scoffers, conceding that "this Astrology is a foolish daughter" while asking what would become of "her mother, the highly reasonable Astronomy" if the daughter did not earn the bread; he keeps the geometrical aspects between planets while discarding much of the inherited apparatus of signs and houses; he holds that configurations physically affect the sublunary world and human temperament, yet incline rather than necessitate; and in the fourth book of the Harmonices Mundi he folds the whole business into a theology of divine harmony. Sixteen years on, in the preface to the Rudolphine Tables, he is still working the same vein, insisting the foolish daughter must support her wise but needy mother. I want questions that will let me pin the character down before I write another scene in which he is wrong in a way I can respect.

To that end: draft for me a sequence of nine or ten interview questions, to be put to Kepler as though he had agreed to sit for a structured interrogation — questions that press on the actual tensions rather than inviting him to restate his piety or confess his embarrassment. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give.

At minimum I want coverage of the following, in whatever order serves the arc of the interrogation:

The mechanism. If the heavens act upon the earth, what carries the action — light, some species of geometric ray, something about the angles as such — and why should the aspect between planets matter while the zodiacal sign a planet occupies matters hardly at all? What is it about geometry that he thinks makes influence intelligible rather than occult?

Inclination without necessity. He maintains that the stars incline but do not compel. I want a question that asks whether a merely inclining influence is distinguishable, in any observation he could actually cite, from no influence at all — and a follow-up for when he reaches, as I suspect he will, for the analogy of weather, or of humors predisposing a body to illness.

The rational soul. Temperament and rain are one thing; judgment and the will another. Where exactly does he draw the boundary of celestial influence, and what does he take the soul to contribute that no configuration can?

The bread. He fed his household in part by casting horoscopes and issuing prognostications, and by his own account despised the fortune-tellers who pander to common taste. I do not want a gotcha about selling prophecy; I want the question that makes him explain, in his own terms, how taking the general's silver for a nativity is compatible with that contempt — and a follow-up for when he answers that the profession subsidizes the true philosophy.

Harmony. Whether his astrology is a corollary of the rest of his cosmology — the nested regular solids, the harmonic ratios, the God who geometrizes — such that the same man could not have held the one without the other. If it is a corollary, the character changes: the error, if it is an error, is architectonic rather than venal, and I need to write him that way.

The scoffer. One question posed as the hardest of his contemporaries might pose it — what he would concede to a man who grants him his orbits and denies him everything else — and a follow-up for when he distinguishes his genuine astrology from the vulgar kind.

Two further constraints. First, the questions must be phrased so that a man of 1610 could understand them — no statistics, no psychology of the modern sort — though the follow-ups may sharpen the edges as far as the language allows. Second, at the end, flag any question in your sequence where the temptation is strongest to smuggle in a modern sensibility, since that is where the character is likeliest to come out false; I would rather know now than find out in proofs.
