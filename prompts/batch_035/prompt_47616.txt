I'd like a parody of a startup's help-center FAQ page. The fake company (call it something like SplitIt, or Coinflip Legal, your choice) sells "safe Pareto improvements" as a service for disputes. Quick explainer of the idea so you don't need to search anything: when two parties are heading into something mutually costly -- litigation, a price war, an actual fistfight over a parking spot -- there's often a third option both of them would rather have than the conflict: flip a fair coin over who gets the disputed thing, or let a mutually trusted third party pick one of the two claims, and both agree in advance to abide by it. Each side, in expectation, does better than paying the costs of the fight, so agreeing to the gamble is a strict improvement for both, and it's "safe" in a specific sense: nobody can get exploited through the agreement, because the deal is structured so each party's expected outcome beats the conflict no matter how good the other side is at bargaining, threatening, or bluffing. No splitting the difference, no compromise -- the winner takes everything, it's just decided by something cheaper than the fight.

The jokes have to come from getting the mechanics right, not from sneering at startups generically. The FAQ should quietly handle at least these, in some form:

1. the enforcement problem -- a liability/refunds section that deadpans that they cannot actually make the loser comply, compliance is the parties' own commitment device, see terms;
2. the information problem -- a question like "should I accept the flip only when I'm pretty sure I'd lose the fight anyway?" and an answer that never quite admits that your willingness to accept leaks information about how tough you are, so universal adoption has to be common knowledge or the whole thing wobbles;
3. the exclusion of disputes where the conflict itself is the point -- a list of ineligible dispute types ("principle-based disputes," precedent-setting cases, anything where the fight is doing signaling work) presented as boring policy, never as a concession that the product doesn't do what it says.

Format: a short "How it works" blurb at top (two or three sentences of pure startup-speak), then 6-8 question/answer pairs, then a one-paragraph closing "Our mission" bit that should be the deadest-pan part of the whole thing. Around 600 words total. Voice: polished help-center cheerful, the kind that signs off hoping this "resolved your issue." The reader who knows the actual literature on this (there is some -- bargaining, precommitment, arbitration) should feel the author understands the idea well enough to bend it, and the reader who doesn't should still find it funny as a FAQ for a company that sells coin flips as a legal service. Don't wink, don't have the narrator break character, and don't let the satire turn into "startups dumb" -- the target is the tempting idea that every conflict is just a wasteful negotiation error waiting to be engineered away.

One more thing: work in one eligible-dispute example that's small and domestic (security deposit, food-truck territory, whatever) and one that's absurdly large (nations, or an inheritance) -- the comedy of the product is that it treats them as the same product.
