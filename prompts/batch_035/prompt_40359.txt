The Buddhist pramāṇavādin raises a regress-style objection: if a cognition counts as knowledge only once something confirms it, that confirming cognition needs its own confirmation, and so on forever. The Nyāya answer, at least on the standard reading, is roughly that truth is established by the cognition itself (svataḥ prāmāṇya) while falsity is established by something else — you only need a second look when something goes wrong, and you find out it went wrong from later experience, like taking the rope for a snake and then seeing it's a rope. So the default is trust, and doubt has to earn its keep: Nyāya insists doubt is never free-floating, it always needs a ground, like seeing the rope in bad light, and the very fact that you can cite the ground means you already know something. My question is whether that whole package actually dissolves the regress or just hides it. Because a skeptic can ask: when you later "see" the rope, what certifies that second cognition? If it's again self-certifying, fine, but then it looks like truth just defaults to whatever shows up until disconfirmed, which feels less like an answer to the regress and more like a decision to stop asking.
