Write a satirical white paper, roughly 750–900 words, in the voice of a management consultancy pitching "sunk cost optimization" to a corporate board as a core leadership discipline. Invent the firm; give it a name that would fit unremarkably on a real strategy boutique's masthead. Structure it like the genuine article: an executive summary, three or four numbered recommendations, and a client case study.

The target of the satire is not the sunk cost fallacy itself but the revisionist case for honoring sunk costs — the argument that treating each decision as separable from its history is a false idealization, and that past investment can be a genuine reason to continue. So the humor must come from valid extrapolation rather than caricature: every recommendation should follow honestly from the premise that money already spent is an asset to be defended, so that a decision theorist reads it with uneasy recognition rather than contempt. Equip the consultant with the actual machinery, delivered straight-faced: past spending entered as an asset on a "commitment balance sheet"; a "continuation audit" that any project past a certain threshold of cumulative investment must pass before cancellation is even tabled; the deterrence argument stated frankly — an organization known to abandon projects will find itself holding only projects worth abandoning; the talent argument — employees who trust the firm to finish what it starts will select into longer-horizon work; and a quiet warning that "marginal analysis" is a slippery tool in the hands of people who would prefer not to finish things. The case study should concern a client who, on the firm's advice, declined to cancel a long-over-budget program with no plausible remaining payoff, and the piece should present this as an unambiguous triumph; somewhere in its ancestry, without ever being named as a cautionary tale, there should be a supersonic airliner.

Keep the register deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader, and the most alarming lines should be the ones phrased most reasonably. At least one recommendation should contain, without comment, the strongest genuine objection to the whole program, dressed up as a benefit: for instance, that the discipline's chief value is making the firm's future self predictable to its present self, and its present self to its counterparties.

Append an author's note of 150–200 words in which you take definite positions rather than surveying options. Identify precisely which of the consultant's arguments have real analogues in the behavioral-economics and decision-theory literature on escalation of commitment and on commitment devices, which are extrapolations no serious defender of sunk-cost honoring would endorse, and exactly where you would locate the line between the two. Then commit to a verdict: does the ordinary cultural norm of "finishing what you start" fall on the defensible or the satirized side of that line, and why — not an outline of what could be said on both sides, but your answer.
