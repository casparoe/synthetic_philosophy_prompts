A gloss on the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem that gets repeated among the traders I know runs like this: an offer to trade is like an argument. Someone who offers to buy from you is, in effect, asserting that the asset is worth more than you think; you should update on the offer just as you update on an argument before accepting its conclusion; and since rational arguers with common priors can never knowingly disagree, rational traders can never knowingly trade. The gloss is then used to conclude that real trading volume must be driven by something other than rational disagreement — noise trading, overconfidence, or asymmetric information that never becomes common knowledge — so markets are less like epistemic conversations and more like rooms full of people talking past each other. Audit the analogy. Lay out the correspondence: what plays the role of the assertion, of the premises, of common knowledge, of accepting versus rejecting an argument? Then say where the mapping breaks — risk aversion and differing endowments, liquidity needs, hedging, the zero-sum character of trade versus the non-rivalrous character of argument — and distinguish the disanalogies that threaten the intended conclusion from the ones that don't. Does the inference from the theorem to "volume is noise" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the conversation framing doing the real work? And if the argument is the wrong source domain, what would be a better one?
