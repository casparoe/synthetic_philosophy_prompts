I am a sixth-form student preparing a project on organ transplantation for my Extended Project Qualification, and I have become stuck on a puzzle about the dead donor rule. In the UK, surgeons may only remove organs from someone who has already died, but the transplant system seems to use two different tests for dying. For donors on ventilators, death is declared by neurological criteria — the permanent absence of all brain function — and the ventilator is stopped only after that declaration. For other donors, called DCD donors, death is instead declared a few minutes after the heart stops beating on its own; the doctors wait a short interval to be confident the heart will not restart, and only then proceed. But a patient whose heart has just stopped and will not restart on its own could often be resuscitated by doctors if they chose to, which suggests the two tests do not track the same event. What definition of death does this practice presuppose? Is it that death is the loss of integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, that it is the death of the brain, or something else? Can one institution consistently use both criteria, or does it secretly need to pick one and merely tolerate the other? I would also like to know whether the practice strictly requires any particular definition at all, or whether several accounts of death would equally license it, and what would have to change in transplant procedure if we settled on one definition rather than the other — for instance, if death were defined purely neurologically, or purely as the irreversible rather than merely permanent stopping of the heart.
