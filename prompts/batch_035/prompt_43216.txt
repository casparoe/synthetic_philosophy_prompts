I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and over the past two decades I have interviewed philosophers of science on both sides of the structural realism debate often enough to notice that I could file the same story every few years with only the dates changed. I would like a diagnosis of why the debate has stalled, and I want to offer, as a hypothesis only, my outsider's suspicion about what kind of disagreement it really is.

For readers who need the shape of it: John Worrall's 1989 paper proposed structural realism as a way of having the best of both worlds in the scientific realism dispute — preserving the force of the no-miracles argument, which says the empirical success of mature theories would be inexplicable unless they were at least approximately true, while conceding the pessimistic meta-induction, which notes that history is a graveyard of once-successful theories whose unobservable furniture (phlogiston, caloric, the elastic-solid ether) we no longer believe in. Worrall's emblematic case is the shift from Fresnel's ether theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory: the ether went, but the mathematical equations carried over. His proposal was to be a realist about that surviving structure while withholding belief about the underlying nature of things. Since then the position has bifurcated. Epistemic structural realists, Worrall's own camp, hold that structure is all we can know. Ontic structural realists, associated with James Ladyman and Steven French, go further: structure is all there is, and the traditional picture of objects bearing properties and standing in relations should give way to a picture in which relations are prior and objects are, at best, nodes — a view they motivate principally from quantum mechanics, where the statistics of identical particles seem to defy the assignment of individuality to the particles at all.

What I have watched stall, specifically, is this. On the epistemic side, everyone concedes the force of what Stathis Psillos revived as the Newman problem, after Frank Ramsey's old objection to Bertrand Russell: if all you know is structure, and structure is understood as what the formalism says about relations without saying what relates or how, then you know, roughly, only how many things there are of each kind — which is trivial, and certainly too thin to explain the success of science. The epistemic camp's response has been to fatten up the notion of structure, but no one seems satisfied that the fattened notion doesn't quietly reintroduce the knowledge of natures that structural realism was supposed to forswear. On the ontic side, the standard charge is that the position reads metaphysics off current physics — that quantum theory tells us particles lack individuality, so individuality is not part of the world's furniture — and the standard reply is that this is no different in kind from the scientific realist's own inference, which also reads ontology off our best theories. That exchange has now been repeated, in nearly identical form, for a very long time. Nobody moves.

Here is my hypothesis, and I want you to treat it exactly as that. It strikes me that the word "structure" means quite different things across this literature, and that the two camps may in part be answering different questions under one heading. When the epistemic camp says structure, it seems to mean the mathematical form of our theories — the thing that survives theory change, which is a claim about scientific knowledge across history. When the ontic camp says structure, it seems to mean a web of physical relations in which the relata dissolve — which is a claim about what the world is like, and is motivated almost entirely by present physics rather than by historical continuity. If that is right, then part of the dispute is verbal or frame-shifting rather than substantive: a theory about the epistemology of science and a theory about the metaphysics of objects are colliding at a single word. But I am a journalist, not a philosopher, and I may be flattening genuine disagreements — for example, about whether any notion of structure can be both thin enough to survive theory change and thick enough to answer the Newman problem, which would be a real tension and not a pun.

So: diagnose the impasse. Is it a verbal disagreement about "structure," a substantive disagreement about whether the no-miracles inference or the pessimistic meta-induction deserves priority, two research programs answering different questions (epistemology of science versus metaphysics of what there is) under a single name, or something else — perhaps a genuine but ill-posed question that takes the continuity of equations to settle questions equations cannot settle? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, not with a survey of the field. And then tell me, honestly, what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a precise formal account of structure that evades the Newman problem, a case study of theory change that goes the wrong way for one side, a development in physics itself (a theory of quantum gravity in which even spatiotemporal structure becomes dynamical, say), or an argument that no progress should be expected and the debate is destined to be re-litigated with every major shift in physics. If the answer is the last one, I would at least like to know it before I accept the next interview.
