Me and two people I play pickup basketball with have been having the same argument for about two years now and it never moves, and I want to figure out what kind of argument it actually is. It's the Hume thing about miracles. One of them, Dev, is an actuary, and he says Hume basically discovered Bayes' rule before Bayes: no testimony is enough to establish a miracle because the prior improbability of a law of nature being violated is so enormous that no realistic amount of witness testimony can overcome it, the same way you wouldn't believe someone who said they won the lottery twice. My cousin Marcus, who's in seminary, says that's circular: Hume says experience is uniformly against miracles, but whether experience is uniform against them depends entirely on whether any miracle reports are true, which is the very question at issue — so the argument assumes what it's proving. Dev's answer is always "that's just what a prior IS, complaining about it is like complaining the house edge exists," and then they go around again. I read the actual Hume essay (well, most of it) and what struck me is that the famous line, "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence," gets cashed out in a way that treats our trust in witnesses as itself a frequency — like we've kept a giant ledger of every report anyone ever made and checked it against the facts. And it hit me that nobody, including Hume, has that ledger, and the entries we'd need to add to it are precisely the disputed ones. So is this debate empirical (a missing-data problem), definitional (Marcus thinks "violation of a law of nature" is confused, since laws are just descriptions of what happens, and nothing can violate a description), about standards of proof (Dev says Hume only needs balance-of-probabilities, Marcus says extraordinary claims need an impossible standard by construction), or just two prior worldviews wearing philosophy costumes?

My working theory is that they're answering different questions under one word: Dev is answering "what should a betting agent assign as odds" and Marcus is answering "what is it legitimate to stake your life on," and those come apart for any low-probability event, not just miracles. But I'm honestly not sure, and I might just be trying to make peace instead of figuring out who's right. What I want from you is a diagnosis with reasons, based on what each side is actually claiming, and not a history lecture about who responded to Hume in what decade. And then tell me what would even count as progress here — some distinction, or a reframed question, or a hypothetical case they'd be forced to answer differently — or whether I should accept that this one is stuck at bedrock and the two of them will be replaying it forever. The lottery thing bugs me too, if it matters: Dev concedes you'd rationally believe a friend who said they won the lottery, even though the prior is astronomically low, which looks like a hole in his side of the argument. Does that actually break the Humean position, or is there some difference between "improbable" and "law-violating" that saves it?
