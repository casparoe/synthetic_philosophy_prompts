I'm part of a graduate reading group on predictive processing that has spent the better part of a semester on the free energy principle, and we've hit a wall I'd like help diagnosing rather than resolving. On one side are people who take Friston's presentation at face value: the principle is supposed to follow from the mere fact that some systems persist in a fluctuating environment — any such system, they argue, must in the long run behave as if it minimizes variational free energy, whether it's a bacterium, a brain, or an economy, and so the principle can't be an empirical hypothesis that might have come out false. On this view, its unfalsifiability is a feature, not a bug: it's meant to function like a conservation law or a selection principle, a framework within which testable models of particular systems get built, not a competitor with those models. On the other side are people who press what our instructor called the triviality dilemma: either the principle is read substantively, as saying something about how brains specifically do things — hierarchical message passing, prediction-error minimization, active inference steering perception and action — in which case it's an ordinary empirical claim that stands or falls on neuroscientific evidence, or it's read abstractly enough to cover thermostats and hurricanes, in which case it's compatible with every possible observation and does no explanatory work that couldn't be done by saying "systems that persist persist."

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of cognitive science and think carefully before answering, perhaps starting with a short brainstorm of the possible diagnoses before committing to one. My working hypothesis — offered only as a hypothesis — is that this is not really a disagreement about the mathematics, since both sides happily grant the formal machinery and the Markov blanket framing, but rather a hidden disagreement about what an explanation at this level of abstraction is supposed to do: one side demands that a theory partition the possible worlds it excludes, while the other prizes unification across domains and treats non-empirical presuppositions of empirical modeling as themselves intellectually respectable. But I can't tell whether that's right, or whether the sides are instead answering two different questions under one name — a formal claim about self-organizing systems versus a mechanistic claim about neural implementation — and merely talking past each other when they seem to clash. It also matters to me whether the debate over prediction error minimization as a theory of cortical function, which is empirically live, is being illegitimately propped up by the grander principle, or shielded by it from refutation.

So what I'd like from you is a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the literature: classify the disagreement (verbal, substantive-empirical, substantive-but-methodological, or a sign that the question of the principle's "status" is ill-posed), say what each side is actually committed to given how they respond to the dilemma, and be explicit about which disanalogies in the conservation-law comparison matter for the debate and which don't. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a discriminating novel prediction, a conceptual distinction separating the framework from its instantiations, or a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and why. Feel free to conclude that my hypothesis is wrong if it is; the group has been going in circles for weeks, and what we need is a map of the terrain, not another lap.
