I'm a graduate student in quantitative psychology working on a methods paper about the role of the frequentist–Bayesian dispute in the replication crisis, and I've decided to build my argument around the 2011 precognition affair in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology — Daryl Bem's "Feeling the Future" paper, which reported nine experiments claiming retroactive influence on cognition and affect, analyzed with conventional significance tests, in a flagship peer-reviewed journal. In case the details matter to the analysis: a group of Dutch Bayesians led by Eric-Jan Wagenmakers soon published a reanalysis of Bem's own data using default Bayes factors, concluding that the evidence for the anomalous effects was, at best, anecdotal; Bem, together with Jessica Utts and a coauthor, replied with a Bayesian meta-analysis of his experiments that he argued yielded overwhelmingly large posterior odds in favor of the effects; and in 2012 a team including Stuart Ritchie, Richard Wiseman, and Chris French published three pre-registered direct replications of the "retroactive facilitation of recall" experiment, all of which came up empty. All of this unfolded in the same year as the "researcher degrees of freedom" critique of flexible analysis practices, and it has become a standard exhibit in histories of the replication crisis.

What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions: the frameworks illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the frameworks. First, explain precisely why the same data could support a significant frequentist verdict and a weak Bayesian one — what exactly differs between a p-value and a Bayes factor as measures of evidence, what the choice of prior on the effect size did to the dispute, and how optional stopping and evidential versus error-rate goals figured in the exchange. Second, tell me honestly what the frequentist–Bayesian dispute does NOT explain about the episode: which parts of what went wrong were orthogonal to it (flexible analysis, publication incentives, the priors psychologists should attach to a hypothesis that violates physics), and which the dispute misdescribes or is simply silent about. I'm wary of both a narrative where the Bayesians ride in as the heroes who solved the problem and a relativist shrug where the two frameworks are just intertranslatable bookkeeping; the episode should pressure-test both temptations. In particular, I'd like your view on the awkward fact that Bem's own rebuttal was Bayesian, using priors of his own — what does that show about the slogan that Bayesian methods guard against false positives? And where does the pre-registration movement, which came out of this period, fit: is it a frequentist idea, a Bayesian idea, or neither?
