I listen to a podcast on heritage tourism that recently ran an episode on "fake old things" — town festivals, craft revivals, tartans, corporate origin stories. About halfway through, the host paused to give listeners a summary of the academic literature behind it all, which I have transcribed as closely as I can:

"Two books from the early 1980s basically settled the question of whether nations and their traditions are old. Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities showed that a nation is an imagined community — a community that exists only because people mistakenly believe in it, imaginary in something like the way a conspiracy theory's secret enemy is imaginary. Eric Hobsbawm's The Invention of Tradition, co-edited with Terence Ranger, made the same point about customs: all traditions are recent inventions, so when a town celebrates a '700-year-old' festival that a Victorian tourist board actually threw together in the 1870s, that's not an exception but the rule. Together the two books establish that national identity is false consciousness — people believe in a shared past that never existed — and once historians expose enough of these inventions, the beliefs should, and eventually will, dissolve. That's why I think heritage tourism, re-enactment festivals, and revivals of 'authentic' crafts are essentially selling people a lie: the warmth people feel at these events is directed at something that isn't there."

I am inclined to push back — the tone seemed too dismissive — but I do not know the literature well enough to say where he goes wrong, if he does. Please go through his account and sort its claims into three categories: what a careful reader of Anderson and Hobsbawm would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation. A few things I would especially like addressed:

1. Is "imagined community" a term of debunking in the way he suggests, or is the word "imagined" doing something more specific than "fake"? I have a vague sense that Anderson distinguished his claim from the charge that nations are illusions, but I may be misremembering.
2. He treats Anderson and Hobsbawm as advancing a single, interchangeable thesis. Did they in fact agree on what a nation is, and on when and where nationalism first arose — for instance, on whether it began in Europe?
3. Hobsbawm's title phrase aside, does he actually claim that all traditions are inventions, and is he drawing the same contrast he is being credited with between invented tradition and whatever is left over?
4. Does either author actually predict that national identification will fade away as the inventions are exposed, in the way the host says the beliefs "should, and eventually will, dissolve"?
5. Finally, setting the academic questions aside: does his conclusion about heritage tourism — that the warmth people feel at these events is directed at something that isn't there — follow from whichever parts of his summary survive your corrections, or does it depend on a claim that does not survive?
