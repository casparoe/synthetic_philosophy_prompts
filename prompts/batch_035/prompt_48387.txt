I am a PhD student in metaethics preparing a paper draft on the wrong kind of reasons problem for fitting-attitude analyses of value, and I would be grateful for detailed comments on the argument below — in particular on whether it works, whether it is genuinely interesting, and where a referee would be most likely to push back. Please write your comments in British English.

The paper's thesis is that the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction tracks what I call proxy-satisfiability. On the familiar fitting-attitude analysis, something is valuable just in case it is a fitting (appropriate) object of pro-attitudes such as admiration or desire. The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is that there appear to be decisive reasons for attitudes toward worthless objects — the cruel tyrant will torture you unless you admire him; a demon will reward you for desiring the empty glass — yet admiring the repugnant or desiring the worthless remains unfitting, so not every reason for an attitude is the sort of reason that analyses value. My proposed criterion: a reason for an attitude toward an object is right-kind if and only if it can be responded to in the very taking of the attitude — if it can figure in the attitude's presentation of its object, so that the agent admires the tyrant, say, on the strength of that very feature. Wrong-kind reasons, by contrast, are indifferent to how the attitude is realised: they can be satisfied by proxy, so that hypnosis, medication, a bribe to a neurosurgeon, or outright pretence discharge them just as well as genuine admiration would. This yields a diagnostic test: a reason is wrong-kind exactly when an agent could fully comply with it while remaining blind to the content of the attitude it favours.

I have three specific worries I would like you to address. (1) Extensional adequacy: does the criterion classify the hard cases correctly? Consider the gossip case, where your friends will shun you unless you share their contempt for a colleague who is in fact admirable; the pragmatic-encroachment cases, where pragmatic reasons for belief seem similarly proxy-satisfiable (is that a bug or a feature?); and the evidential cases, where the mere fact that evidence supports the fittingness of an attitude is treated by some as generating right-kind reasons, even though the agent still need not register the attitude's content — my criterion appears to condemn these as wrong-kind, and I am unsure whether that is a fatal misclassification or a defensible revision of the dialectic. (2) Derivativeness: does the view collapse into the nearby proposal, associated in the literature with Mark Schroeder, on which wrong-kind reasons for an attitude are reanalysed as ordinary reasons against the state of affairs of that attitude's obtaining — and if it does not collapse, what exactly is the difference? (3) Circularity: does characterising right-kind reasons via what an attitude's "presentation of its object" can register simply smuggle in a prior grasp of fittingness, so that the criterion presupposes the very distinction it was meant to illuminate? Beyond these questions, please comment freely on anything in the notes that seems inaccurate, unclear, or philosophically weak, and tell me frankly whether the core idea is worth developing into a full paper or is a dead end.
