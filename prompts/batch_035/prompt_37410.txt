so, bit of a career pivot here: I recently left my philosophy PhD (mid-program, don't ask) to work at a small AI safety org, doing conceptual and strategy stuff rather than actual ML. I've got maybe a year and a half before people expect me to have picked a lane, and the thing I keep bumping into is what everyone calls the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, that for any strategy a misaligned AI could use to grab influence over the long-term future, an aligned AI has some roughly-as-good counterpart available, so the team that builds the first powerful AI doesn't lose by making it aligned — the aligned AI can "steal" whatever approach the misaligned one would have used. A lot of the field's default optimism quietly rides on this (e.g. the idea that alignment only needs to win once, for the first capable system, rather than prevail in some repeated contest), but as far as I can tell it's barely been examined as a question in its own right.

I want to figure out which of the open issues around strategy-stealing actually matter most, and where somone with my background (decent at decision theory and game theory, mediocre at ML) should be spending his effort. The candidates I've been chewing on, no idea if these are the right ones:

1. whether the assumption holds mainly by symmetry — i.e. it falls out almost analytically once the two systems are comparably capable — or whether it needs an actual edge, and what kind;
2. whether it survives multipolarity, where several teams get capable systems at nearly the same time and at comparable cost;
3. whether the stealing goes both ways in a bad sense — if alignment knowledge spreads easily, does it also hand powerful misuse capabilities to bad human actors, so the assumption quietly turns on how dual-use the relevant research turns out to be;
4. whether offense/defense asymmetries in the surrounding AI security landscape make strategy-stealing too slow in exactly the worlds where it matters most.

Please give me a defended ordering of these (or of others you'd argue matter more), not a survey — which ones are upstream of which, which are actually tractable at the conceptual level for somone like me rather than for ML people, and what developments over the next couple of years would move things up or down the list. also, and I'd honestly take a hot take here if it's where the analysis lands: is the whole thing kind of a pseudo-question that just rebrands "will the good guys win"? if so I'd rather know now than in eighteen months.
