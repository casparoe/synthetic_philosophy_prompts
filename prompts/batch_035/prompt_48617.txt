I am three-quarters of the way through my third novel, and I have written myself into a corner I do not want to write my way out of cheaply. The book is set in a river town in the weeks after a catastrophic flood. My central character, Ruth, piloted a rescue boat on the first night and made a choice: she passed her own street, where she could see her husband's flashlight on the roof, to take five strangers off a collapsing bridge first. Her husband survived — I want the confrontation to be about her reasoning, not her outcome, because that is where the actual philosophical wound is. Three weeks later, on her porch at night, her sister Corinne, who did a philosophy degree before dropping out to run the family hardware store, finally says what the whole town has not said to Ruth's face. The chapter is that single, unbroken conversation.

What I want from you is the dialogue itself, roughly 1300–1600 words, with only minimal stage direction. I have done my homework, and I need you to have done yours. Corinne's position is essentially Bernard Williams's: that impartialist moral theory, in treating Ruth's husband's life as worth no more than any stranger's, makes Ruth's integrity — her life structured around projects and attachments that are hers, the things that give her a reason to go on at all — into something the theory merely has to accommodate, and badly. At the crucial moment, she should make the point Williams made about the man who can save only one of two drowning people and happens to have a wife: that saving his wife is fine, but that there is something wrong with the man who needs the additional thought that in such situations it is permissible to save one's wife. I want that argument to arrive organically, out of Ruth's own account of what went through her head that night on the water — perhaps out of something Ruth says in her own defense.

But Ruth cannot be a strawman, because I refuse to write the scene everyone writes, where the utilitarian is an emotional defective who gets lectured. She works in public-health cost-effectiveness; she has thought about this for years; she chose with her eyes open. She should be given her real replies, in whatever register a person under this much pressure can manage them: that a theory of what makes acts right need not be the procedure she runs in her head mid-flood; that a consequentialist can rationally cultivate deep partial love, and that loving her husband wholeheartedly is exactly what her view predicts and permits; that the one-thought-too-many point, at its strongest, confuses what justifies a decision with what the agent must think while deciding; and that Corinne, pressed, should have to answer whether she really believes her brother-in-law's life counted for more than five strangers' — the negative-responsibility challenge turned around and put to the critic as a question she must squirm under, not dodge.

The two of them must actually engage. When Ruth invokes the distinction between criterion and decision procedure, Corinne should attack it rather than ignore it — her line of attack, if you want one, is that the distinction saves the theory only by conceding that the theory is not what Ruth lives by, and that what she lives by is doing all the work. When Corinne presses integrity, Ruth should demand to know what integrity is supposed to add to the argument beyond its own applause. Neither may win. The chapter ends unresolved, or resolved at most by some small physical gesture that shows what both of them are actually fighting over.

Craft constraints: they are sisters, not debaters; the philosophy has to surface under emotional pressure, in their own idiom, with interruptions, evasions, and at least one moment where one of them deliberately wounds the other about something adjacent. No seminar diction unless one of them is being mocked for it. My test for the draft is that a moral philosopher would recognize both positions as held by their best advocates, and that a reader who has never heard of Williams would still understand exactly what these two women cannot agree about.
