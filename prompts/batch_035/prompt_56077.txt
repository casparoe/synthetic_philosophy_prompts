My book club is doing All Quiet on the Western Front next month (the Remarque novel — a few of us will watch the 2022 film too, but work from the book), and I'd like to bring a philosophy angle to the discussion: the moral equality of combatants. As I understand it, the traditional just war view holds that soldiers on both sides are moral equals — each is liable to be killed by the other because each poses a threat, and the question of which war is just is one for the politicians and generals, not the poor sod in the trench. The revisionist line (the Jeff McMahan sort) says that can't be right: a soldier fighting for an unjust cause doesn't get the same permission to kill as a soldier defending against him, though duress and conscription may excuse him even if they don't justify him.

Now, the novel seems to cut both ways, which is why I want a proper reading. On one hand you've got the shell-hole scene: Paul stabs the French soldier Duval in hand-to-hand combat, then spends the day trapped with him as he slowly dies, spirals into guilt, promises to write to his wife, and ends up calling him his comrade. A traditionalist could say Paul's guilt is misplaced sentimentality — the intimacy of a knife kill has confused him about something that a bullet from 300 yards would have left him clear-headed about. A revisionist could say the guilt tracks the truth: something really was wrong there, and Paul's breakdown is the last honest reaction he has left. On the other hand you've got the earlier barracks conversation where Kropp and Tjaden ask what a war even is — why one lot of men shooting at another lot in a field counts — and half-joke that the leaders should slug it out with clubs in an arena. Is that the moral equality view (soldiers are just pawns, all equally innocent), or actually its opposite (the soldiers shouldn't be fighting at all, so the whole framing is rotten)? And I keep coming back to Kantorek, the schoolmaster who shamed the boys into enlisting — if responsibility sits with him rather than Paul, doesn't that just reassert the traditionalist picture at one remove?

So: please read the novel through this debate. Which scenes should I point the club to, and what does each one actually show about the moral equality thesis — where does the book dramatise it, where does it complicate it, and where does it resist the whole framing so hard that an honest reading has to admit the lens doesn't fit? My own suspicion is that Paul's guilt attaches to the intimacy of the killing rather than to the justice of the war, and that this matters for which side the book is really on, but I might be mangling that. Also, don't let the book just illustrate the philosophers — tell me if Remarque sees something the Walzer/McMahan exchange misses, maybe something about what happens to Duval's humanity in Paul's eyes once he's no longer a silhouette.

Two requests on how you answer: give me your bottom line in the very first sentence (I don't want a warm-up paragraph), and at the end tell me what would change your mind about your reading — which specific bit of the novel, or which move in the philosophical argument, would push you the other way, and where you think your interpretation is most likely leaning on a contested reading of either the book or the theory.
