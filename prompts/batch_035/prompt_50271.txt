I'm TA-ing a philosophy of mind course -- third-year undergrads, ~16 of them, one 90-minute seminar slot, next week's reading is Dennett on the three stances and real patterns. The lecture covers the theory, so I don't want my seminar to just be me re-explaining it. I want an activity where they *feel* why the intentional stance is something you'd take seriously rather than a polite fiction, ideally by getting beaten at a prediction task.

Here's the idea I had but can't flesh out: a prediction game with one target system and several teams, where each team gets a different kind of information about the same system, and the teams reasoning at the higher level reliably beat (or at least match) the team drowning in the lower-level stuff, even though the lower-level info is strictly more complete. Something like: one team gets a full dump of the system's internal state or code but has to actually compute from it, another team gets the rules and design, another team just gets logs of its past behavior and has to find the pattern themselves. I want the "more information is useless without compression" thing to show up in the scores, not in my slides.

Requirements:

- Runnable by one person with a laptop, a projector, index cards, and whatever free/browser stuff exists. No coding assignments for the students beforehand, no special software I have to install at 9am.
- The target system should be boring enough to be fully knowable but not so boring that the intentional-level predictions are trivially right. You pick it -- a simple game bot, a little maze agent, a group of students playing a fixed strategy, whatever actually works. Justify the choice briefly.
- There need to be real stakes of some kind (points, candy, bragging rights -- no real money), and ties need a story, because I suspect ties will happen.
- Somewhere in the debrief I want the "okay but is the pattern *real* or just a convenient compression trick" question to come out of *their* mouths, not mine. Build the activity so it has a natural pressure point where that question becomes urgent, and tell me where it is so I can shut up and wait.

Please structure your answer with these sections, in this order, roughly these lengths:

1. **The target system and why** -- one paragraph.
2. **How to run it** -- step by step with timing across the 90 minutes, including team assignments and what info each team recieves and when; about a page.
3. **Scoring and stakes** -- half a page, including tie-breaking and what to do if one level of description just doesn't win on the day.
4. **Debrief script** -- what to ask, in what order, and how to map specific moments in the game onto the physical/design/intentional stances and onto what makes a pattern "real" for Dennett; half a page to a page.
5. **What will go wrong the first time** -- a short list, honestly. I've run enough seminars to know something will.
6. **What the game can't reproduce** -- a short paragraph on which part of the position this activity genuinely cannot make them feel, and that I'll have to supply in conversation myself.

One more thing: I'd rather the game not stack the deck so hard that the intentional team always wins. If the design-level team can win sometimes, or the log-readers stumble onto the pattern first, that's fine -- arguably better. Tell me whether your design allows that and whether that helps or hurts the point I'm trying to make.
