I'm a PhD student in economic history, co-teaching a 90-minute intro seminar on the Annales school to a group of 18 undergraduates who have read a short excerpt of Braudel but keep treating history as a parade of kings and battles. Design an in-class simulation that makes them feel the three-tiered structure of historical time — événements, conjunctures, and the longue durée — rather than just nodding along to the terminology. Concretely: I want a multi-round game in which different groups manage a coastal trading city, receiving "news cards" every round (wars, harvests, treaties) while a second, slower layer of variables — silting of the harbor, demographic drift, shifts in trade routes, land tenure arrangements — is recorded on a separate board that players may consult only at designated "census" rounds every fourth turn or so, and which changes too gradually to make the news. The final scoring should be dominated by the slow variables even though most of the players' attention, and most of the arguing, will predictably be consumed by the news cards; ideally at least one role (a "geography" player?) should have information about the slow layer but be constrained in how often they may speak, mimicking how structures communicate themselves to contemporaries. Include the rules, materials I can print in advance, the payoff/scoring scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Braudel's temporal tiers and onto his claim that events are surface agitation, "foam" on the wave. Be honest about which feature of the longue durée no classroom-length simulation can reproduce (hint: I suspect it's the timescale itself, and the fact that structures feel like fate from inside), and tell me what will go wrong the first time I run it. Close your design with a single line of the form "Core mechanic:" followed by one sentence naming the central mechanism that does the pedagogical work.
