I keep going back and forth on gambles where expected value breaks down, and I've decided the way to settle it is to just write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see whether there's a principle underneath them. So here they are.

1. The classic St. Petersburg setup: fair coin, pot starts at $2 and doubles every time heads comes up, game ends on tails, I get the pot. My verdict: I wouldn't pay more than $20 to play, probably less, even though the "expected value" is infinite. Fine, I'm risk averse, whatever.

2. Here's the twist, the Pasadena-style version. Same coin, but now the payoff I recieve alternates sign depending on the parity of the flip count, with magnitudes that grow like 2^n over n, so the series of possible payoffs, summed in the natural order, is one of those series that only converges conditionally — rearrange the terms and you can make it "equal" basically anything you like. My verdict: I won't play this even for free. It feels like being asked to sign a contract with no price on it.

3. The Altadena version: exactly the same game as 2 but every payoff doubled. Every single outcome is at least as good as in game 2, most strictly better. My verdict: sure, Altadena is strictly better, I'd take it over Pasadena if forced. But I wouldn't pay more than a couple dollars extra for it, and honestly if both were offered free side by side I'd have to stand there and think, which bugs me, because dominance should make that a no-brainer.

4. A Pasadena-type game where the negative payoffs are capped at $5 but the positive ones still alternate and blow up. My verdict: play it for free gladly, and I'd even pay a small entry fee. So "undefined" is apparently fine with me as long as the downside is small.

5. A ticket costing $10 that pays $10^50 with probability 1 in 10^40. Expected value is astronomical and perfectly well defined. My verdict: hard no. And I genuinely can't tell whether this is the same "no" as in case 2 or a different one.

6. The one I keep going back and forth on: I already own a St. Petersburg ticket (game 1, to be played tonight), and someone offers to buy it off me for $30. Selling feels foolish, holding out for more feels like I'm being suckered by my own math — since I wouldn't pay $30 to play it, shouldn't I be delighted to sell it at $30? But I'm not delighted. I'm not even sure I'd sell.

Is there one principle that gets all six? If not, say which of my verdicts has to go, and whether the repaired principle gives an answer on a case I haven't listed — for instance, a casino that lets me choose, before the coin is flipped, the order in which the conditional series gets summed for a Pasadena game. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence (one principle, or no), then the reasoning, and no preamble or restating my question.
