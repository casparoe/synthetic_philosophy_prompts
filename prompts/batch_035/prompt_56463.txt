Two of my friends got into it at a party last weekend over whether dying is bad *for you* -- not for your family, just for you. It went on long enough that they finally asked me to settle it, and I couldn't, so I'm handing it off.

Maya's position was basically the Epicurean one. She said death can't be bad for the person who dies, because there's no subject left to suffer it: when you exist, death isn't present, and when death is present, you aren't. Bad things, she said, have to feel bad to someone, and a corpse isn't a someone. Her example was that nobody thinks the year 2300 is already full of people being harmed.

Dan said that confuses death with dying. Dying might be painful, but the badness of *being dead* isn't pain, it's deprivation: the goods you would have had if you'd kept living -- seeing your kid grow up, the books you'd have read. Losing something valuable is bad for you even if you never feel the loss, and he pointed out we say exactly that about other cases: someone who's brainwashed into bliss by people who robbed him seems harmed even though he feels fine.

Maya said deprivation still needs a subject, and the brainwashed man exists and is the one who was robbed. But once you're dead, there's no one to point to as the victim. You can't say the corpse is deprived, and you can't say the person is deprived, because there's no longer such a person.

Dan then brought up the example of a man betrayed by his friends while away on a long trip -- he's harmed by the betrayal during the whole time it's happening, before he ever learns of it, even though he never experiences it. So being harmed doesn't require experiencing the harm, and death could be like the betrayal interval.

Maya said the analogy fails at exactly the crucial spot: the betrayed man is alive throughout, so there's a standing person for the harm to attach to. Death has no such interval with a standing subject.

Dan's last move was the symmetry argument in reverse: he said Maya's own logic implies prenatal nonexistence should worry us just as much as posthumous nonexistence, since in both cases there's no subject, but nobody lies awake dreading the time before they were born. So, he said, her view either accepts that we're harmed by not having been born earlier, or it's inconsistent.

Maya replied that the asymmetry is easy to explain and totally legit: my future death cuts short a life I have and have investments in; the time before my birth couldn't have contained me without being a different person entirely, so there was never a *me* who missed anything. Dan said that just smuggles in what needs proving -- that the goods death takes are genuinely *mine* rather than merely goods that occur in the world.

They both looked at me expectantly and I had nothing.
