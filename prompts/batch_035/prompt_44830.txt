I'm a second-year graduate student in political theory, currently drafting a term paper that turns on Amartya Sen's impossibility result from the late 1960s, commonly called the Paretian liberal theorem. Before I build the argument on it, I want to check that I have the logical geography of the theorem right, because my reading strikes me as strong enough that it may be too strong.

Here is my reading. Sen proved that no collective decision procedure whatsoever can combine the Pareto condition with even a minimal concession to individual liberty — namely, that each of at least two persons should be decisive, on their own, over at least one pair of social states. My understanding is that this establishes the following: the very concept of a personal right is internally contradictory, in the way that the concept of a largest natural number is internally contradictory. That is, no possible assignment of rights, however modest, could be collectively rational; once a right is granted it holds unconditionally, so any social choice rule that respects rights will cycle whenever citizens hold preferences over one another's private business — the sort of case Sen constructs with his prude and his lascivious reader — and in the remaining cases the Pareto condition will clash with the rights outright. On this basis I want to defend two conclusions in the paper: first, that rights-talk in liberal political theory is not merely difficult to reconcile with collective rationality but flatly incoherent, so that the sensible response is to abandon rights-based constraint talk and let Pareto settle everything; and second, that John Stuart Mill's harm principle is thereby refuted as a decision rule, since the harm principle just is the minimal form of liberalism that Sen proved impossible.

Please evaluate this reading. If any part of it misfires, I want to know precisely where: which of my two conclusions misdescribes the theorem, and what the theorem actually licenses one to say. I am especially interested in whether the result is a fact about the concept of rights as such, or about a particular way of formalizing rights within social choice theory, and in whether anything at all follows for the harm principle once one attends to how the paradox is generated. Please confine yourself to the conceptual assessment; I do not need a bibliography or reading list.

Please write your response in American English. Close with a final line of the form "Verdict: <short phrase>" naming, in a few words, the single most fundamental error in my framing.
