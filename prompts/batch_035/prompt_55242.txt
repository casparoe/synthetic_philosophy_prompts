I am preparing a workshop on the philosophy of biology built around the question of what, if anything, "life" is. My working hypothesis is that people's stated definitions and their case judgments come apart, and that the case judgments are what really do the work when the cases get strange. To test this on the participants, I would like a "which definition of life do you actually hold?" quiz — and I want it to sort people by verdicts, not by self-identification.

Please write ten questions. Each question should present one concrete case, and the respondent should give a verdict — counts as alive, does not count as alive, or borderline in a way I will ask you to specify — without ever seeing the name of any definition. At least the following cases must appear: a virus; a prion; a sterile hybrid animal such as a mule; a bacterial spore that has been dormant for several thousand years and is revived; a growing crystal or a flame, which superficially satisfy metabolism-style criteria; a self-replicating pattern inside a cellular automaton such as the Game of Life; a computer virus; an autocatalytic reaction network sustained in a laboratory reactor with no genetic material; and a hypothetical extraterrestrial microorganism that reproduces and evolves but shares no chemistry with terrestrial life. The remaining slot is yours: pick a case that does work none of the others can do, and say in a parenthetical note what that work is.

Then provide a scoring key mapping patterns of verdicts onto named positions in the debate: the metabolic/autopoietic view, the evolutionary view (on the order of the NASA working definition, i.e., a chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution), the thermodynamic or dissipative-structures view, an information-theoretic view, a family-resemblance or cluster-concept deflationism, and an eliminativist view on which "life" is not a natural kind at all. If two positions in your key cannot be separated by any of the ten cases — I suspect the autopoietic and metabolic views will be hard to pry apart, and that the evolutionary and autopoietic views may agree on everything except, perhaps, the reactor network and the cellular-automaton pattern — flag this explicitly rather than inventing a fake discriminator. Finally, note which combinations of verdicts correspond to no defended position, and for each empty cell say whether you think it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Keep the questions answerable by a non-biologist; no question should require specialized knowledge beyond the description given in the question itself.
