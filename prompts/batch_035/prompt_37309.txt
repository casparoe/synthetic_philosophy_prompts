I'm a third-year undergrad taking a philosophy-of-decision-theory course that's cross-listed with econ, and this week's section was on bargaining, commitment, and threats. At the end the TA mentioned an idea people call a "safe Pareto improvement": you take a game that has some catastrophic outcome both players hate — a war that destroys the prize, litigation that eats an inheritance — and you install, in advance, a rule that if play ever reaches that outcome you replace it with something both players prefer, like flipping a fair coin over who gets the disputed thing. The "safe" part is supposed to mean the modification leaves everyone weakly better off no matter what would have happened, even if the other player turns out to be irrational or has totally wrong beliefs about you, because you've only touched the branch that was disastrous for both sides anyway. Afterwards my friends Marcus (an econ grad student who audits the course) and Dana (philosophy) argued about it for like an hour and then asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't, so I'm asking you. Please act as a professional game theorist who also knows the philosophy of decision theory literature, do some brainstorming about what each side is implicitly assuming before you commit to a verdict, and then go through their exchange move by move.

Here's the argument, as close to verbatim as I can remember:

1. Marcus said the whole thing is just a Pareto improvement with a scary name — moving from an outcome both hate to a lottery both prefer is bog-standard mutually beneficial trade, insurers and mediators have been doing it forever, and there's no new idea here.
2. Dana said no, the safety clause is the entire point: an ordinary Pareto improvement has to be negotiated at the moment of crisis, exactly when you can't trust the other side and your threats only work if control is somehow out of your hands (this is basically Schelling on brinkmanship); a safe one gets installed in advance and has to leave every other part of the game untouched, so neither side can exploit the change, and that's why both sides can actually sign it beforehand.
3. Marcus answered that if the modification can't touch anything except the disaster outcome, then it's insurance against the other player being irrational, and only that — rational players avoid the disaster anyway, so it can't be about improving the actual crisis. And worse, if the war outcome degrades into a coin flip, war gets cheap, so people get careless, and the pointwise safety was computed on the old game — the changed incentives can make everyone worse off. Plus, who flips the coin while the tanks are rolling? The guarantee is only as good as the enforcement machinery, and that machinery is exactly whats missing in the situations these things are supposed to be for.
4. Dana replied that the carelessness point would count equally against every dispute-resolution mechanism we actually use — heirs agreeing in advance that any fight over the estate goes to arbitration instead of lawyers, countries pre-committing to arbitration over borders — and those haven't collapsed into aggression; and both parties want the enforcement device to work, so building it is itself mutually beneficial.
5. Marcus said those real cases are just ordinary bargaining, so by Dana's own examples the concept adds nothing — she's rebranding arbitration.
6. Dana said no, the distinctive thing is that signing doesn't require trust in the other side's rationality at the moment of play, which is why it can be agreed to before anyone even knows who'll be in power or how desperate they'll get.
