Some background on why I'm asking. I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years — the whole lineage of outside-view arguments, base-rate neglect, planning fallacy pieces, reference-class forecasting in infrastructure megaprojects, and the recurring fights about anthropic reasoning. A few years ago I started doing personal forecasting seriously, keeping scored predictions, and I've become uncomfortably aware of a gap between the practice and the theory. When I forecast how long my own software project will take, I know I should use an outside view: find a base rate in some class of similar projects and defer to it. But which class? Projects by people with my experience? Projects of this kind of codebase? Projects with this team size? These give me visibly different frequencies, and nothing in my training as a forecaster tells me which one to use. Calibration scoring can punish my overall record, but it can't reach inside a single question and tell me I picked the wrong class — and I can often reverse-engineer whatever final number I want by shifting the class and adjusting how I weight my inside view against each base rate. That makes me suspect the outside view is less of a discipline than it looks, at least at the level of the individual forecast.

So here is the proposition I want your credence on: for a given prediction question, there exists a fact of the matter about which reference class is the correct or best one to draw the base rate from — something discoverable in principle, not merely stipulated — such that two forecasters with the same evidence who choose differently are making at least one genuine mistake, rather than both being within their epistemic rights. If that proposition is false, then reference-class selection is in some deep sense permissive: the class is chosen, not found, and the resulting credence inherits an arbitrariness that no amount of calibration training will wash out.
