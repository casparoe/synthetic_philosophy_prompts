After a career of some forty years in geophysics, I have devoted my retirement to reading widely in philosophy and, more recently, in the decision-theoretic literature produced by the AI safety community. There I encountered a family of arguments about what its authors call acausal threats, and I have gradually come to find its central prescription persuasive. As I understand the idea, an agent that can predict your behavior with reasonable accuracy can threaten you without any causal interaction ever taking place: it never needs to contact you, but merely computes in advance whether you would capitulate if threatened, and issues a threat only if it predicts that you would pay. The proposed defense is a form of precommitment: adopt, here and now, an unbreakable policy of never yielding to threats, so that any sufficiently good predictor will forecast your refusal and, anticipating the cost of carrying out its threat, decline to issue one at all. The refined version, which goes by the name of surrogate goals, holds that one should deliberately invest some trivial, arbitrary matter with the same importance one attaches to one's genuine aims, so that even the pettiest threat aimed at the surrogate must be met with the same total resistance one would mount for the things one actually cares about. The claim is that this renders one an unattractive target across the board, and that the policy costs nothing in expectation, because a fully deterred threatener never calls the bluff.

I confess the logic appeals to me. The underlying point about commitment devices will be familiar to anyone who, like me, followed the strategic debates of the Cold War as a young scientist; and yet I also remember how often the literature of that period mistook formal elegance for soundness, with sobering consequences for how deterrence was actually practiced. I would therefore like to treat my new conviction as a hypothesis rather than a settled matter. What would count against it? I would like the candidate defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted by their decisiveness. Some possibilities that occur to me: cases where the predictor is good but imperfect, so that it issues the occasional threat it would have been better to absorb; cases where the threatener can distinguish my genuine goals from my surrogate ones and simply aims at the former; cases where carrying out the threatened harm is cheap for the threatener and ruinous for me, so that my all-out resistance is a losing bet whenever deterrence fails even once; and, more fundamentally, cases where the entire scheme presupposes that the counterparty reasons as the theory says it does, when real agents may not. I would also like to know which of these a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine exposure and which would be explained away — and whether the concession and the explanation can be told apart in advance, or only after the fact.

Finally, I want a candid assessment of whether a view of this kind is exposed to evidence at all. It concerns hypothetical agents in hypothetical encounters, most of which will presumably never occur; and I sometimes suspect that every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by adjusting the assumptions about the threatener's predictive power or rationality, until nothing could ever count against the theory. If it has indeed been qualified into unfalsifiability, please say so plainly, and then tell me whether that is a defect in this particular domain or merely what one should expect of a branch of a priori decision theory — where, perhaps, the analogue of an experiment is a worked-out case, and the analogue of a failed prediction is a case the theory cannot describe without embarrassment. I would rather be corrected than reassured, and I am too old to be protective of a position I have held for only two years.
