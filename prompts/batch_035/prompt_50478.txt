I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past two years much of my reporting has been on debates within the AI safety community about what it would actually look like for things to go wrong. What strikes me most is that these researchers seem to hold two quite different pictures of failure. In one picture, the failure is an event with an agent behind it: a sufficiently capable system develops something like goals of its own, recognizes that human oversight is an obstacle, and deliberately accumulates influence while appearing compliant until it no longer needs to appear so. In the other picture, there is no event and no agent scheming at the center: every individual deployment of AI is sensible, reviewed, and reversible on its own terms, and yet the cumulative effect of millions of such deployments is that human judgment, human agency, and eventually human control over what we care about quietly erode. The first picture gets the dramatic features; the second, as far as I can tell, is what several of the most careful people I interview actually expect.

Here is the pattern I keep running into when I press people on the second picture, and it's what I'm writing to ask about. In the drift story, no step is a mistake. Suppose I ask an interviewee which particular adoption of an AI system we should have declined — which hiring screen, which military targeting aid, which content-ranking model, which parliamentary drafting assistant. The answer is always that the question is malformed: each step was defensible, locally beneficial, and reversible, and the harm exists only in the trajectory, not in any step of it. Yet the same people will insist that the end state is catastrophic and even foreseeable in outline. So I am describing a configuration in which a bad outcome is produced by a long chain of individually justified, individually reversible decisions, such that no single decision was wrong, no single reversal would have averted the outcome, and accountability seems to have no place to attach. It resembles a slippery slope, but the people making the drift argument typically deny that there is any mechanism by which each step makes the next more likely, which I had thought was the distinctive content of slippery-slope reasoning. It resembles the boiling-frog metaphor, but that seems to be a claim about the insensitivity of the observer, not a structural claim about how the harm is distributed across the decisions themselves.

Is there an established concept for exactly this — aggregate, decision-distributed harm with no wrongful step — and if so, what is it and where do its edges lie? I would want to know how it differs not only from slippery-slope arguments but from the economics and public-policy treatments of individually rational choices that aggregate badly, and from the systems-engineering idea that catastrophes can arise from the interaction of individually unremarkable components. If nothing in the literature carves this exact joint, I would like you to coin a term and define it precisely enough that I can use it in the piece: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where the boundary cases fall. I am less interested in the label than in getting the carving right, because my feature's thesis is that which failure picture you adopt determines what you think monitoring, regulation, and coordination can even do — and I want to know whether the drift picture is intelligible as a claim about a distinctive kind of harm, or whether it dissolves into these better-studied shapes when pressed.
