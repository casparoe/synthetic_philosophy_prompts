This month the question we set was "Does Nozick's experience machine show that hedonism about well-being is false?" and unfortunatly I've drawn the short straw for giving feedback on one member's answer. I can follow most of it but I honestly can't tell how much is solid and how much sounds solid because it's well written, so I want a proper grader's eye on it.

Here's what he wrote (his name's Rob):

"Nozick asks us to imagine a machine that psychologists have built which can give you any experience you want. While you're floating in the tank your brain is being stimulated, so you could be writing a great novel, or making a friend, or anything else, and you'd never know it wasn't real. Hedonism says pleasure, or pleasant experience, is the only thing that's good for you. So if hedonism is true we'd have every reason to plug in for life, and since almost nobody would, hedonism is false. Nozick gives three reasons we'd refuse: we want to actually do certain things and not merely have the experience of doing them, we want to be a certain sort of person (someone floating in a tank isn't courageous or kind or witty in any real sense), and we want contact with reality itself, not a man-made simulation of it.

A better view is the desire satisfaction theory, which says what's good for you is getting what you actually want. Writing the novel in real life is good for you because it really satisfies your desire, whereas in the machine the desire only seems to be satisfied, so this theory explains our refusal where hedonism can't.

Could the hedonist reply? One option is Mill, who distinguished higher pleasures (poetry, philosophy) from lower ones (bodily enjoyment) and said it's better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. The machine can only offer the lower, bodily kind, so Mill's person of quality would refuse to plug in on purely hedonist grounds. On this reading the objection only works against Bentham's crude version.

There's also an empirical objection. Apparently some philosophers ran experiments where they told people they were already inside the machine and asked whether they'd disconnect, and lots of people said they'd stay plugged in — though far fewer would stay if they were told their real life was as a prisoner, and about half would leave even if the real life was being a rich artist. That's just status quo bias, people preferring whatever they take their situation to be. So the experiments actually backfire against the hedonist: when people genuinely imagine starting from reality, they refuse the machine without hesitation. That's the intuition doing real work, and I think it sinks hedonism for good."
