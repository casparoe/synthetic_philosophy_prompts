Here's the opening half of a philosophy essay I'm drafting, tentatively titled "Molyneux's Patients Keep Cheating." I'd love feedback, especially on accuracy and on whether my planned second half actually follows from the first.

The draft so far: Molyneux's question, posed to Locke in the late 1600s, asks whether a person born blind, now newly sighted and familiar with a cube and a sphere by touch, could distinguish them by sight alone before touching them. The received history is that Locke's empiricism predicted no, and that the eventual experiments vindicated him. I argue the experiments never actually tested Molyneux's question, because Molyneux specified a sober adult with normal touch experience, while the famous cases keep being children, motivated subjects, or patients with visually abnormal histories. Cheselden's celebrated 1728 cataract patient was a young boy whose reports were filtered through expectations about what he was supposed to be impressed by. More recent studies of patients whose congenital cataracts were removed later in life find chance performance on touch-sight shape matching at first, with rapid improvement — but these are exactly the people whose visual cortex developed under deprivation, so whatever we're measuring, it isn't the untouched-by-experience faculty Molyneux was asking about. My move in the second half: Molyneux's question silently assumes that "seeing a shape" and "reporting a crossmodal match" are the same capacity. I want to argue they come apart. A patient could have rich visual shape experience and still fail the matching task for reasons of task interpretation, attention, or lack of any practice translating between sensory vocabularies — a bit like how someone might recognise a face perfectly yet be terrible at drawing it. On this reading, all the empirical "no" answers show only that crossmodal identification needs learning, not that visual shape perception itself does. If that's right, the interesting version of the question isn't empirical at all but about whether shape is computed in a shared, modality-neutral spatial format or separately per sense, and the answer to that might be a boring "both, at different stages of processing." My worry is that this deflates the question rather than rescuing it.
