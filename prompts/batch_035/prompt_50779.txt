I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a university medical center, and we're reviewing a research protocol attached to an expanded-access program for a gene therapy that restores partial vision in adults with a rare inherited retinal dystrophy. The first candidate is a 34-year-old woman blind from early infancy. The clinical intervention itself is not controversial; what's splitting the research team is the observational component. The majority of the co-investigators want to run a year-long structured interview study, asking the patient to describe her visual experiences as they emerge — how things look, what surprises her, whether familiar objects she knows by touch are recognizable by sight alone — on the theory that such first-person transcripts are a genuinely rare scientific resource. A minority objects that the whole exercise is confused in principle: the patient will be trying to describe experiences she has no concepts for, using a vocabulary she built while blind, so the transcripts cannot in principle capture whatever qualitative novelty is at issue. They explicitly invoked the philosophers' thought experiment about the color scientist Mary, who knows every physical fact about color vision but has never seen color, and the question of what, if anything, she learns the first time she sees red. The minority proposes scrapping the interviews in favor of behavioral discrimination tasks — can she sort objects by shape or color, can she match seen objects to touched ones — on the grounds that these at least measure something. The majority replies that this doesn't escape the problem but relocates it: if the patient can sort by color, a philosopher could say she has merely acquired a new ability rather than new knowledge of any fact, and the protocol has no way of distinguishing those, whereas the interviews at least give the patient the chance to tell us in her own words. A few of them also cited the mid-century case reports of congenitally blind adults whose cataracts were removed late in life, some of whom reportedly could not visually recognize objects they knew perfectly well by touch.

The committee meets in three weeks and must approve, reject, or approve with modifications the interview component. Please draft the memo I will circulate beforehand. It should recommend one concrete course of action (a defensible modification is fine — e.g., conditions on how the interviews are conducted, framed, or analyzed, rather than a simple yes or no), with reasoning the committee could actually act on, and it should give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the side it rejects rather than dismissing it. In particular, work out what the committee should take the interview study to be evidence of: if the minority is right that language learned without vision cannot carry visual novelty, is the study worthless, or could it still be informative in some more modest way; and if the majority is right, what exactly would the transcripts add over the discrimination data.

Two constraints on the epistemics of the memo. First, wherever it touches the philosophy — what the Mary case is supposed to show, whether what's gained on first seeing is best analyzed as a new ability, a new mode of acquaintance, or something the physicalist has other ways to accommodate — please keep clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and don't present any one resolution as the consensus when it isn't; the same care should apply to what is actually known empirically about congenitally blind adults gaining sight, as opposed to what is anecdote. Second, where the memo must take a stand on contested ground in order to reach a recommendation, it should say plainly that it is doing so and why. Please write it in American English.
