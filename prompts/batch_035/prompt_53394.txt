For my upper-level philosophy of mind course, I have to write a term essay of at most 900 words on whether global workspace theory is a theory of consciousness in the full sense or only a theory of what Ned Block calls access consciousness. The essay must reach and defend a definite verdict, not just survey the debate.

Please develop the essay around the following thought experiment, which I want to be the crux of the argument. Imagine a cognitive architecture like ours — specialized modules for vision, audition, memory, motor control, and so on — except that it contains, in addition to the usual global workspace, a dedicated private channel connecting exactly two modules. Through this narrowcast channel the two modules exchange very rich information, rich enough that each can use the other's output in reasoning, planning, and fine-grained discrimination, exactly as we use our own percepts. But nothing that travels on this channel is ever broadcast to the rest of the system: the workspace never receives it, no report mechanism can reach it, and it leaves no traces in memory that the rest of the architecture could consult. Call the information on this channel narrow-access information: it is available for a great deal of cognitive use, but it is not globally available.

The essay should weigh two readings of this case. On one reading, the global workspace theorist must say that narrow-access information is entirely unconscious — it never enters the workspace, so on a theory that identifies consciousness with global broadcast, there is nothing it is like to carry it. But then it looks like the theory has conflated consciousness with a specific, contingently global pattern of access, since the information seems fully accessible, just not widely so. On the other reading, the theorist can insist that globality is not an arbitrary add-on: perhaps the function consciousness evolved to perform is precisely to make information available to a consumer set that is open-ended, and information confined to a fixed pair of consumers is not access in the relevant sense. The essay should evaluate which reading is stronger, and say what follows about whether the access/phenomenal distinction itself survives the experiment: does the case show a gap between phenomenality and global availability, or does it merely show that availability comes in degrees and patterns?

Constraints: at most 900 words; a brief introduction stating the verdict up front, then the argument, then a short conclusion; no section headers; and no citations or bibliography, though you may refer in passing to Baars's theatre metaphor and to Dehaene's work on the neural correlates of reportable perception, since the assessment is closed-book and what is being graded is the reasoning, not recall of the literature. The essay will be marked down for hedging the verdict, for treating the thought experiment as merely an illustration of a position already argued on independent grounds, and for assuming rather than arguing that anything accessible but unreportable must be phenomenally conscious.
