I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about how we ought to reason about very unlikely events with very large stakes — asteroid impacts, engineered pandemics, hypothetical superintelligence. As background for the piece, I want to see how a smart lay audience actually handles the reasoning when it's live. I host a monthly salon at a bookstore: roughly twenty attendees, well-read general readers with no technical training, ninety minutes. Design an activity for that session that makes them feel the force of the problem usually called Pascal's mugging — an offer backed by an absurdly large claimed payoff attached to a vanishingly small claimed probability, such that expected-value reasoning seems to compel acceptance while common sense says walk away. The name and the standard philosophical responses must be withheld until the end: the rules, roles, and incentives should lead participants into the bind themselves. My initial idea is that some attendees play the mugger and invent their own offers, so the group gets to see what makes an offer compelling versus transparently empty — please build on that or improve it. Use token stakes rather than money, and keep the whole thing runnable by one facilitator. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the problem: why expected-value reasoning seems to force the payment, what it costs to adopt a rule that lets one refuse, and how that same rule threatens to make one refuse low-probability, high-stakes risk reduction — which is precisely the subject of my article. Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order: "Setup and materials" (about 150 words), "How it runs" (about 350 words), "The debrief" (about 300 words), "What will go wrong the first time" (about 150 words), and "What the activity cannot reproduce" (about 100 words).
