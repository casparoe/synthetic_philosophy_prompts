I'm a PhD student writing in philosophy of law, and I'm putting together a position piece on capital punishment for a workshop on punishment theory. Before I circulate a draft, I want an audit of whether my actual views form a stable package, because I have been living with them long enough that I can no longer tell the load-bearing tensions from the surface ones. The views, roughly as I would defend them at a talk:

(1) I am a retributivist: punishment is justified by desert, severity must be proportioned to desert, and deterrence and other forward-looking benefits form no part of the justification, even where they predictably accrue.

(2) I am a retentionist in principle: for a narrow class of crimes — premeditated mass murder, say — death is the proportionate penalty, and a state would act permissibly in imposing it on the deserving.

(3) I am an abolitionist in practice: since any real legal system will convict some innocent people, the death penalty as actually administered cannot be justified and ought to be abolished now, whatever one says in principle.

(4) I believe that life imprisonment without parole, especially as it is actually served in prolonged solitary or near-solitary conditions, is on the whole a worse fate for the person undergoing it than a comparatively quick execution.

(5) I believe that what is distinctively wrong about executing an innocent person is not the severity of death but its irrevocability: the wrong cannot be rectified even in part, whereas the wrongfully imprisoned can at least be released and, to some extent, compensated.

(6) I believe that proof beyond reasonable doubt, combined with appellate and post-conviction review, yields a residual rate of wrongful conviction that is tolerable for life imprisonment — I have no innocence-based objection to LWOP as such — but intolerable for death.

(7) I accept plea bargaining in capital cases, in which a defendant pleads guilty in exchange for LWOP and the state forgoes seeking death.

Please do not give me a survey of the literature or a lecture on retributivism versus consequentialism. What I want is a careful audit of this set specifically: which pairs or clusters are in genuine tension and how badly; which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve once stated precisely; and, for the genuine ones, what would have to give and what holding on to the rest would then cost. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and end by identifying the single revision that would clean up the most. I would also like you to be candid about the audit itself: tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument or case — including a hypothetical I have not listed — would most plausibly move you to a different verdict about which commitment has to go.
