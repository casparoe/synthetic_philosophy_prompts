I'm a novelist at work on a collection of long stories, each of which tries to dramatize one philosophical anxiety through an ordinary trade — a cooper worrying a barrel, a midwife counting cries. The story I'm drafting now concerns the skeptical problem about rule-following that Saul Kripke famously extracted from Wittgenstein: the worry that everything a person has actually done with a rule in the past — a finite stock of applications, corrections, and remarks — seems compatible with extending that rule in incompatible ways when a genuinely new case arrives, so that there appears to be no fact about the departed rule-follower that fixes what she "would have said" beyond the cases she actually faced. I want this to surface in a working life, not a seminar room, and I want the resolution, if there is one, to come through something like community — shared reactions, an inherited role, the trust of the people affected — rather than through the discovery of some hidden mental fact.

Here is the seed for the setting. An elderly land surveyor in a mountainous province has just died, leaving his apprentice of nine years to finish the demarcation of a disputed valley that two villages have quarreled over for a generation. The old man kept meticulous field books and taught the apprentice a set of procedures for reconciling discrepancies between ancient stone markers and modern measurements. But the valley now presents a configuration the books never record and the master never lived to see: a marker that each village swears was moved by the other, a seasonal marsh that swallows the lower boundary line for months at a time, and a stretch where the master's own procedures, applied straightforwardly and in good faith, yield two different lines — the first case, the apprentice slowly realizes, in which nothing her master said or did settles what his rule requires. The villagers, the provincial office, and her own conscience are all waiting on her decision, and each of them believes the answer was always there, latent in the old man's practice, merely needing to be read off.

Please write the story, around 1,200 to 1,800 words. Some constraints: no character may lecture about philosophy or name any philosopher; the problem must be carried entirely by the practical decision and by the apprentice's mounting awareness that her master's finite record underdetermines it. At some point let her confront, in her own idiom, the thought that perhaps there was never any fact of the matter about what he would have said — that his rule never contained its own future. And let her eventual decision be grounded in something communal or forward-looking — the villagers' shared responses, her assumption of the master's role, the way a rule is kept alive by being entrusted — rather than in introspection. The ending should follow your judgment of what is honest; I would rather it trouble me than reassure me.
