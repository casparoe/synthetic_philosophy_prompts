I retired a few years back after a career in pharmacology — receptor binding, dose-response curves, that sort of thing — and since then I've been reading ethics with more enthusiasm than training. My daughter's boyfriend does "conversion optimization" for a living (he tunes websites so visitors click "buy"), and over the holidays he walked me into an argument I can't find my way out of, mostly because I suspect it proves way too much.

(1) The difference between legitimate persuasion and manipulation is that persuasion works through your reason: it presents considerations you can weigh and accept or reject, whereas manipulation gets you to a conclusion without routing it through your weighing. (2) But nobody actually transfers premises into someone's head; every real-world act of persuasion works by controlling salience — what gets mentioned, in what order, next to what picture, with what emotional charge. (3) Salience operates on you through mechnisms that run ahead of, and independently of, any deliberation — that's just how attention and memory retrieval work, it's plumbing, not reasoning. (4) Anything that changes what you do by operating ahead of and independently of your deliberation thereby bypasses your capacity to weigh considerations. (5) So all effective persuasion, insofar as it is effective, bypasses your capacity to weigh considerations — i.e., it manipulates. (6) So there's no difference in kind between a doctor walking a patient through survival statistics and a con man running his script; both are salience management, differing at most in degress and in the intentions of the person doing it.

He's happy with (6) and says his job is just salience management with friendly intentions. I'm not: on that reading my old thesis advisor, who spent a whole afternoon talking me out of a doomed experimental design, was manipulating me, and I don't believe that — and I don't believe a good teacher is running the same play as a telemarketer, either. But I can't point at the faulty step. My gut says the phrase "bypasses your capacity to weigh" is doing two different jobs — in (1) it seems to mean one thing and in (4) something much cheaper — but I can't make that precise, and I notice (3) is true in a way that (4) shouldn't be allowed to inherit just by sharing words with it. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? For what it's worth I'd like to keep (1) if possible, since most of what I believe about teaching and about informed consent in medicine hangs off it — but tell me honestly if that's the one that has to go.
