My closest friend and I have just come off the worst argument we've had in years. Over about five hours, in increasingly formal tones, we fought about whether utilitarianism fails to respect what Rawls called the separateness of persons — the thought that because benefits and burdens fall on distinct individuals, each with a single life to live, it can't simply be permissible to impose losses on some whenever this maximizes the total. She defends utilitarianism in roughly Harsanyi's mold; I keep feeling the pull of the Rawlsian and contractualist line without being able to state it crisply. We finished exactly where we started, each accusing the other of changing the subject, and we have agreed to try a written exercise instead. Please draft the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two of us would produce.

Some background so the document can be substantive rather than gestural. She is fond of pointing out that Harsanyi gave two quite different arguments: an aggregation theorem showing that if each person's welfare is measured on a common scale and social choices must respect everyone's preferences, the social ranking must be the sum; and a separate impartial-observer argument, in which a rational chooser behind a veil of ignorance, unaware of which person she will be, would maximize expected welfare. I accept the mathematics of the first and dispute its relevance — it fixes how to rank outcomes given that we are aggregating, but the objection is precisely to the aggregating. On the second, I suspect the veil of ignorance doesn't show what she thinks: a chooser who might be anyone is still a single decision-maker trading off risks across what are, for her, possible fates, whereas the complaint is about trading off across actual persons. She replies that this "complaint" is never stated in a form that couldn't with equal right be run within a single life: we routinely sum a person's pleasures and pains across decades and discount one period's suffering for another's gain, so if temporal boundaries within a life don't block aggregation, why should boundaries between lives? I tried invoking Parfit's view that personal identity is not a deep further fact, which she, to my irritation, promptly adopted as further support for her side.

Two confusions from our argument that the document must clean up. First, we kept sliding between two quite different versions of the objection: Rawls's, in terms of the inviolability of each person and the strains of commitment — no one could accept principles under which they'd be sacrificed, knowing they might be the one sacrificed, and a just society must be stable in that light — and Scanlon's, in terms of justifiability to each individual, where an act is wrong if there is some personal complaint that no principle permitting it could reasonably rebut. The document should say which of these the utilitarian owes an answer to, or whether the answer differs. Second, we traded one-shot cases at each other without ever recording predictions. The one we got stuck on longest was a choice between, on the one hand, one identified person suffering a bad headache with certainty and, on the other, each of many people facing a small chance of the very same headache, arranged so the expected totals match. I claimed indifference between these erases the fact that in the second option no one suffers for the sake of others; she claimed my intuition there is a bias against compensating the many for the risk imposed on them, a bias people abandon once the same risks are framed as falling within each chooser's own future. We also never settled whether some of our dispute was merely verbal — for instance over whether "taking separateness seriously" is a constraint on aggregation or, as she would put it, just a refusal to say what it constrains, which is a way of conceding the point.

So write the joint statement as follows. Open with a numbered section of claims both of us could sign, stated without euphemism — including claims about what the separateness objection must establish to succeed, about the distinction between the two Harsanyi arguments and where exactly our dispute attaches, and about which formulations of the objection are too weak to be of interest (we agreed, near the end, that merely asserting that persons are numerically distinct could not be the whole of it, since the utilitarian accepts that and sums anyway). Follow with a table of our residual disagreements, each phrased so that the person holding the view would accept the wording as a fair statement of her own position, not a caricature. Then isolate the cruxes — I count at least two: whether there is a principled asymmetry between aggregation within a life and aggregation across lives, and whether the objection, once made precise, supports a maximin-style floor, a Scanlonian per-person justification requirement, or something weaker. For each crux, give the case or argument we both accept as bearing on it, with her prediction and mine written down in advance and separated from whatever the drafter takes the right answer to be. Close by addressing our two housekeeping questions: which parts of our argument were cross-purposes over words, and what, if anything, we both agree would genuinely move one of us. Keep the register measured — the document is meant to be signed by people who spent five hours failing to persuade each other and would like, this time, to fail more precisely.
