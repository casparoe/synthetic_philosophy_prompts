I'm in a small reading group on anthropic reasoning and the doomsday argument fight has been going on for decades and I genuinely can't tell anymore what the disagreement is *about*. Here's the setup so we're on the same page. One camp (the self-sampling people, following Carter and Leslie) says you should reason as if you're a random sample from the humans in your reference class, so your birth rank — roughly the hundred billionth human — is evidence that there won't be trillions more, since under that hypothesis your rank would be absurdly early. The other camp (the self-indication people) says you should weight hypotheses by how many observers they contain, because you were more likely to find yourself existing at all in worlds with more observers, and this exactly cancels the doomsday shift, at the cost of buying into something like the presumptuous philosopher problem: betting, before any data at all, on theories that posit more observers even when they're otherwise implausible. Both camps have known about the cancellation and the presumptuous philosopher for a long time, both agree on all the physics, both agree that I exist, and nobody has moved an inch. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what counts as a single hypothesis when two worlds differ only in how many observers are never asked about it, or two camps answering different questions — one about credences, one about what bets it's rational to place? My working theory is that the real fight is about what your own existence is allowed to be evidence *for*: whether "I exist" is a sample that constrains the world or whether it's no news at all, since some observer was guaranteed to be having this thought. But someone in my group insists it's even shallower than that: she claims the two camps agree on every conditional probability and just differ on which priors they write down before conditioning on "someone exists," which would make it pure bookkeeping. And someone else claims it only matters for bets, not beliefs, since an SSA-er and an SIA-er who bet on humanity's lifespan can't actually disagree once you fix the payouts — either you survive to collect or you don't, and conditioning on being around to collect quietly does something to both sides' odds. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is, using what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress: a thought experiment both sides would accept, a decision-theoretic reframing, a formal result about when the reference class can matter — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
