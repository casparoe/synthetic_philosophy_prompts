I'm putting together the reading list for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mathematics, and the unit on Zeno's paradoxes of motion has me stuck. The literature is full of people declaring victory — the mathematically inclined because a geometric series converges, the metaphysicians because supertasks like Thomson's lamp supposedly show a residual puzzle — but nobody states in advance what would actually count as winning. It strikes me that the dispute is partly a disguised dispute about the standard of success: whether "solving" Zeno means refuting the conclusion, exposing a false premise, dissolving the argument by showing no contradiction was ever there, or rendering the whole thing a non-issue from the standpoint of modern analysis. Before I assign another round of papers, I want the specification first, so that my students and I aren't just grading solutions against unstated and incompatible expectations.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate solution to the four paradoxes of motion — Achilles, the Dichotomy, the Arrow, and the Stadium — must deliver, with the case behind each requirement. To keep you from producing the generic list, here are the demands I'd want considered, and I'd like you to argue with them rather than merely endorse them: (i) a solution should not be satisfied with showing that the conclusion is acceptable (Achilles does pass the tortoise, motion occurs) but should locate where the argument goes wrong — and note the old complaint, associated with Benacerraf, that Zeno never actually states an explicit contradiction, so "locating the error" may be harder than it sounds, since there may be no error in any ordinary sense to locate; (ii) the solution must distinguish the finitude of the sum of an infinite series from the completability of an infinite series of tasks, and say whether "completing" a supertask is even well-defined when there is no last task — and whether any account of that can avoid leaning on the very limit concept that the Dichotomy is usually taken to challenge, or whether that reliance is a fatal circularity or merely a vindication; (iii) for the Arrow, it must either vindicate or reject the thought that a moving arrow and a stationary one are in exactly the same intrinsic state at any instant, and explain what, if anything, distinguishes resting on a derivative from changing the subject; (iv) for the Stadium, it must say whether anything more than a muddle about relative motion is going on, or whether the paradox gestures at something like the relativity of simultaneity or the structure of discrete spaces; (v) it must be consistent with standard mathematics without merely deferring to it — I have in mind the Grünbaum-style position that once the concepts are made precise, no contradiction survives anywhere, versus diagnoses that identify a substantive false premise about, say, tasks, instants, or the structure of space and time; and (vi) it should take a stand on whether the four paradoxes admit one unified diagnosis or several unrelated ones, and whether a solution confined to the motion paradoxes is stable if the plurality paradoxes are lurking in the background.

Please mark which of the requirements you end up with are non-negotiable and which are camp preferences masquerading as demands — I suspect the "must expose a false premise" requirement and the "mathematical dissolution suffices" requirement each have partisans who treat the other's favorite as optional. Then tell me which pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, if any, since that tension seems to be exactly what the two camps are fighting over without admitting it; and identify the single requirement that does the most work in knocking out the familiar solutions — the convergent-series answer, the at-at theory of motion, and the deflationary view that there simply are no tasks or instants for the paradoxes to be about. Skip a full scorecard against the contenders; the criteria and the tensions among them are what I want to bring into the seminar, and I'd rather the list be one a grumpy realist about infinity and a clean dissolutionist could both sign their names to, or could clearly see why they cannot.
