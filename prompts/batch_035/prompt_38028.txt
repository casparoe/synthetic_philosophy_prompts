The Frege–Geach problem has been with metaethics since the 1960s, and in that time expressivists have produced an extraordinary amount of apparatus in response — quasi-realism, norm-expressivism, hybrid theories, deflationary rescues — yet the problem is still routinely described as the standing objection to expressivism, and the literature reads as though no one has moved. My working hypothesis is that the two sides do not disagree about the cases: everyone grants that a conditional like 'if kicking the cat for fun is wrong, paying someone else to do it is wrong' is valid, and everyone agrees that a theory has to say something non-ad hoc about why, when the antecedent is asserted, denied, embedded, and used in modus ponens. The real disagreement, I suspect, is about what an explanation of logical validity must consist in: the critics tacitly require a truth-conditional story, which arguably begs the question against the expressivist, while the expressivists offer an account of how states of mind combine and are content to call that 'logic', which the critics hear as changing the subject. Is that diagnosis right, or does it flatter the expressivists? In particular, I cannot decide whether hybrid expressivism — moral predicates conventionally expressing both belief and attitude — is genuine progress, a smuggling of realism back in through the predicate, or merely a relocation of the difficulty into saying what the belief component is a belief about; and the standard objection that hybrids collapse suggests some critics think the answer is obvious in a way I don't find it. Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — substantive, verbal, or two questions under one heading — using what each side actually maintains, and tell me what, if anything, would count as settling it: a formal semantics, a distinction, or nothing short of abandoning one of the starting points.
