I'm a PhD student in intellectual history writing a chapter on how the Victorian realists handled religious loss, and I want to make Silas Marner my central text, but I'm honestly not sure how far the Feuerbachian reading can be pushed before it snaps. The background I'm working from: Eliot translated The Essence of Christianity back in the early 1850s, and Feuerbach's line is basically that theological predicates are human predicates misattributed to a divine subject — which is the religion of humanity stated in one sentence. So on that reading Silas Marner is almost a controlled experiment: strip a man of his religious community through a betrayal that the community itself blesses, give him money-worship as the pure distilation of projection onto the wrong object, then replace both with Eppie — the actual human referent that was hiding behind the symbol all along. The hearth, Eppie's golden hair echoing the stolen gold on the floor, the way Silas's cataleptic fits stop mattering to anyone once somebody loves him — all of that looks built to make the point.

But there are scenes that resist the reading, and I'd like one that takes them as seriously as the ones that fit. The drawing of lots at Lantern Yard isn't really a case of projection — it's an epistemic procedure that fails publicly and ruins him, and the novel seems to care about the procedural injustice as much as the metaphysics underneath. And near the end, when Silas and Eppie go back to Lantern Yard and find the old chapel swallowed up by a factory, with no records and nobody left who can answer for what was done to him, Silas doesn't draw the Feuerbachian conclusion at all — he lands on something closer to a modest theism, that there's a working of goodness and truth beyond what he can see the ends of (paraphrasing from memory here, my copy is at the office). So which is it: does the novel dramatize Feuerbach, complicate him, or quietly disagree with him? Does Eppie-as-sacrament ever work as more than a sentimental substitution — does the novel show why the misattribution of human qualities to a divine subject happened in the first place, or what it cost, or is that exactly the thing Feuerbach's argument and the novel both hand-wave? And is there something the novel catches about lived religion — the felt need for an external verdict, the way the chapel community needed Silas to be guilty — that the projection story just misses? I'd rather end the chapter saying what the novel adds to the debate than that it decorates it, so tell me honestly where my reading is forcing things.
