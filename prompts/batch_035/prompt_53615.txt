I am a sixth-form student working on an extended essay that touches on how social scientists explain things like unemployment and regional inequality, and I would be grateful for a careful, formal answer, written in British English please.

In a recent seminar, a classmate of mine offered an explanation of persistent unemployment in our region in structural terms: the closure of the dominant industry decades ago, the state of the housing market, school funding, and the way employers now recruit. Our teacher dismissed this with Margaret Thatcher's famous remark, from a magazine interview published in 1987, to the effect that "there is no such thing as society" — that there are only individual men and women, and families. He then used the remark to argue that any legitimate explanation of unemployment must ultimately be an explanation of individual decisions, because society, being nothing, can explain nothing. Something about this move feels unfair to me, but I cannot yet say precisely why, and I do not want to challenge it armed only with a feeling.

My request is this: treat the remark as a slogan that compresses several distinct theses, and lay out for me the precise claims it could be expressing. As far as I can tell from my own reading, the candidates include at least the following, and probably others besides: a trivially true point that society is not an additional object in the world over and above people standing in various relations; a methodological thesis about what legitimate explanations in the social sciences must ultimately bottom out in; a semantic claim about what words like unemployment and recession refer to; an ontological thesis about whether structures, institutions, class positions or norms exist in their own right or only as constructions out of individual actions; and a normative claim about who bears obligations and responsibilities. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or simply false, and — most importantly — whether that reading actually supports the move my teacher made, namely rejecting structural explanations of unemployment in favour of explanations in terms of individual choices. If some readings support that move and others do not, I need to see clearly which are which, since the plausibility of the slogan should not be allowed to leak from the harmless readings into the radical ones.

Two things puzzle me in particular, and I would like the answer to address them. First, my teacher appeared to move from society does not exist to only individuals act to only individual-level explanations are legitimate, as though these were one step. It seems to me that an explanation might mention entities that are in some sense built out of individual actions — a recession, a currency, a school system — while still being genuinely explanatory, and I would like to know whether the slogan, on any of its readings, gives a principled reason to forbid this. Second, I suspect the demand that explanations bottom out in individuals would also rule out explanations my teacher happily accepts, such as saying that the Bank of England raised interest rates and this caused a fall in hiring — the Bank being an institution rather than a person. If the slogan cuts against explanations my teacher likes as well as those he dislikes, that seems worth stating plainly.

Finally, if the published interview context (the remark was given to a journalist, and the published wording was edited from the transcript) bears on which reading the original speaker most plausibly intended, please include a brief note on that, distinguishing what the slogan probably meant originally from what people do with it now. Please do not recommend readings or sources; I have only my notes, and I want the reasoning itself. Kindly write the whole answer in British English.
