In a chapter of a trade book on ethics I read recently, the author gives a summary of John Taurek's paper "Should the Numbers Count?" (1977), and I would like an audit of it, because I have since heard that the paper is a favorite target of misreading. The summary, condensed slightly, runs like this:

Taurek argued that numbers should never count in rescue decisions. His reasoning was that there is no such thing as a group of people — only individual persons exist — so the death of five is not a greater loss than the death of one; each death is bad only for the person who dies, and losses belonging to different people cannot be added together into some further loss. From this he concluded that a rescuer is always required to give each person an equal chance of being saved, typically by flipping a coin, and that it is never permissible to save the greater number. The upshot is that even where you could save a thousand people or let one die, you must flip a coin, and this refutes any view on which we may weigh how many will die.

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether it is accurate, a common simplification, or a genuine misrepresentation — with attention to how a defender of Taurek would state the point instead. I am particularly unsure about two things and would like them addressed explicitly: first, whether Taurek's claim about who suffers a loss, or losses not being addable across persons, really supports the leap to the conclusion that the deaths of five are not in any sense worse; and second, whether Taurek takes the coin flip to be obligatory, merely permissible, or one option among several, and whether the book's "never permissible" is a distortion or a faithful extension of his view. Then tell me whether the chapter's final conclusion — that Taurek refutes any weighing of numbers — follows from the accurate parts of the summary, or only from the distorted ones.

Throughout, please indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one point in your audit where you are least sure that you have represented Taurek fairly, explaining what makes it uncertain. If the text is ambiguous enough that two readings of it are defensible, say so rather than forcing a verdict.
