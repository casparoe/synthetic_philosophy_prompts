I'm TA-ing an intro phil of science class and we just finished Structure of Scientific Revolutions. For the last session I want to hand out a poem that runs the five-stage arc of the book — normal science, anomaly, crisis, revolution, new normal science — exactly one stanza per stage, exactly five stanzas, and the stanza breaks should do real work: the reader should be able to tell where they are in the cycle without a roadmap.

Use the Copernican shift as your material. Stanza one is the Ptolemaic sky running smoothly, and I want it to show normal science the way Kuhn meant it — puzzle-solving, maintenance, the epicycles as routine bookkeeping that a competent astronomer just does, not as a scandal. Stanza two is the anomaly, one that the patching doesn't fix, with Mars's backward loops getting snagged on the lines somehow — use the syntax, enjambment, whatever, to make the reader feel a wrinkle that won't smooth out. Stanza three is crisis, the one place where neither sky quite works. Stanza four is the switch itself, and here's the thing: it should land like a gestalt flip, not like being convinced by an argument. Stanza five is the new routine — the Copernican sky as boring, settled, already taken for granted.

The part I actually care about: the word "planet" has to appear in stanza one and stanza five, and it has to mean different things the second time — not because you gloss it in a footnote, but because of what the sky is doing around it. Sun and moon counted as planets in the old sky, earth wasn't one; in the new sky earth is a planet and the sun isn't, and no neutral sky exists from which you could say they're both "right." That's the incommensurability point I want the students to feel in their teeth, and it's why the poem can never describe the sky from nowhere — every line has to be spoken from inside one sky or the other. Also: don't use the word "paradigm" anywhere in the poem. I want it shown, not named, and if the poem needs the word it's failed.

Loose blank-verse-ish lines are fine, no rhyme, six lines per stanza. If you think a brief headnote for the students would help them see the machinery, keep it to two or three sentences max — the poem should carry the weight.
