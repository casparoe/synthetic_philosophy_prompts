After eight years on the ward I now train incoming nurses at a chronic-pain clinic, and the hardest thing to teach is not any clinical skill but how much weight to give a patient's own account of her pain — especially when the workup keeps coming back normal, or the chart flags a history of lost prescriptions, or the patient describes her symptoms in words that match nothing in the textbook. I want to give them something better than an instruction to use their judgment, because judgment under pressure, with twenty intakes a day and two minutes each, is exactly where the discounting of self-reports does its work: the well-documented pattern in which some patients are believed and others are not for reasons about who they are rather than what they say. Please turn the relevant philosophy — the kind that distinguishes warranted skepticism from identity-based discounting of a speaker, and that recognizes cases where patient and clinician simply lack a shared vocabulary for the experience — into a procedure a nurse can actually run at intake: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a referral to the clinician, and where it forces a check on the nurse's own first reaction. Then run it on three cases: a middle-aged woman with three years of normal tests and incapacitating fatigue; a young man with two documented incidents of misleading staff, now reporting acute pain; and a patient whose symptoms are conveyed mostly through a relative who translates for her. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the clinic's opioid policy will not welcome, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Please open with the bottom line — one sentence stating the core rule the procedure rests on — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
