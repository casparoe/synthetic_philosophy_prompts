I do organizational consulting, and nearly every workshop on "managing across generations" I sit through runs on the same implicit picture, even though nobody ever states it: a generation is like an organism. The presenter will say that each cohort has a formative window, roughly ages ten to twenty-five, when the events of the day — a recession, a war, the arrival of smartphones — imprint enduring traits on everyone passing through that window, much the way ethologists describe critical periods in development: ducklings imprint on the first moving thing they see, children acquire native accents before a certain age, and Gen Z, having come of age with the internet in their pockets, now has communication norms that are fixed for life. From there the conclusions follow quickly: that generational traits are essentially stable post-imprint, so a Boomer manager can't really be trained into Gen Z's expectations, only accommodated; that each named cohort has a distinct personality that policies should be tailored to; and that generations succeed one another in a quasi-biological sequence, each defined against the last, the way one generation of an organism gives way to the next. My colleagues treat all this as obvious common sense; it bugs me that I can't say precisely where it goes wrong, only that it smells wrong.

Please audit the organism picture properly. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the organism, the genome, the environment during the critical period, imprinting itself, the lifecycle, and the boundary events that would make two adjacent cohorts distinct "species" rather than arbitrary slices of a continuum (demographers, after all, happily use tidy five-year birth cohorts that map onto nothing anyone calls a generation). Then the breakdowns, and here I'd especially like you to sort them into those that matter for the conclusions my colleagues draw and those that don't: it seems to me that the lack of any heredity mechanism is a disanalogy, but possibly an idle one, whereas the fact that cohort members age together — so that what looks like a generational trait may just be a life-cycle effect, or a period effect that everyone alive shared — is the kind of thing that should sink the whole exercise, since a manager who waits long enough will find Gen Z exhibiting the very traits they attributed to Boomers. And weigh what the analogy quietly imports: an essence, a fixity, a between-cohort homogeneity that the survey data rarely shows, given that variation within any generation seems to dwarf the difference between generational averages. Finally, tell me whether the practical conclusion — generationally tailored management — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was only ever being carried by the analogy; and if there's a better analogy for what a generation actually is (a cohort as a vintage, maybe — shaped by conditions at formation but confounded by when it's tasted and how old it is), sketch it. I'd rather bring a defensible picture to the next workshop than just an itch of skepticism.
