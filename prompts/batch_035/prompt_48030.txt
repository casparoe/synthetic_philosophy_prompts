I teach an intro epistemology course, 16 undergrads, one 75-minute session. They've already had the standard lecture on knowledge as justified true belief, and next session I want them to discover the Gettier problem themselves, before I ever say the name. Here's my sketch: some kind of game where teams act as a knowledge certification bureau. They get a batch of belief files — a detective's belief about a suspect, a farmer's belief that there's a sheep in his field, a student's belief about who will get the job — each file has the believer, the belief, and the evidence for it. The teams apply a JTB checklist I hand them, stamp the ones that pass as KNOWLEDGE, and then I reveal the hidden world-states, and some of the beliefs they certified turn out true only by pure dumb luck. The gut-punch has to happen in the reveal, not in anything I say beforehand.

Second half: a rule-repair tournament. Each team gets to amend the certification test — add clauses, redefine justification, whatever — and then swaps their amended test with another team whose whole job is to find a counterexample to it. I want them to rediscover for themselves why the obvious patches fail: the no-false-lemmas fix that dies to fake barn type scenarios, a defeasibility clause that dies to lottery-type cases, and eventually someone will just write epistemic luck out of the rulebook, and I want the class to see that that's circular, not a solution. Points for patches that survive a round of attacks, bonus points for an attack that kills a patch.

So what I need from you: the actual materials — a set of belief file cards with a public face and a hidden truth envelope, some of which are Gettiered and some of which are clean true knowledge plus maybe one plain false belief so they can't just pattern-match, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, and the JTB checklist handout. Don't just recycle the famous cases straight — hide one or two of them in disguise so it lands in the debrief, but most should be fresh. Also a timeline that fits 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the real literature: Gettier's original cases, the barn case, the lottery problem, and, if there's time, a taste of the safety vs. sensitivity response for the couple of stronger students. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce — nobody in the room actually holds these beliefs, the certifiers are third parties, the hidden truths are staged by me — and what I'll have to supply in the debrief.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guesses are that some teams will go ultra-conservative and certify nothing, others will just keep lawyering the rule until it says knowledge is whatever doesn't Gettier, and that the reveal will fall flat if there are too few Gettiered files per round — but tell me if you see other failure modes, and roughly how many files I need per round so the reveals actually sting.
