I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a continuing-care retirement community, and I need the written opinion I'll circulate before our next monthly meeting.

The resident at issue is a retired music teacher in her early eighties with moderately advanced Alzheimer's disease. Six years ago, shortly after her diagnosis but while still clearly competent, she executed an advance directive. Its operative instruction: when she can no longer recognize her daughter, she is to receive comfort care only — no hospital transfers, no antibiotics for intercurrent infections, no artificial nutrition. She has now reached that stage. She recognizes no one, including the daughter, who is her healthcare proxy. She is, however, physically stable and by every account content: she hums through the afternoon music program, eats willingly when prompted, sleeps well, and shows no distress.

The complication is a paragraph appended in her own hand below her signature, added after a support-group session for the newly diagnosed. In it she anticipates that the person she becomes may not agree with the person writing, and she asks that if the later version of her seems happy and untroubled, the directive be treated as void. The paragraph was not witnessed or notarized, and it does not amend the formally executed text.

The committee is split. The daughter argues the executed directive is controlling and the appended paragraph is legally inert commentary. The head nurse and the resident's sister argue that honoring the earlier self now means overriding the earlier self's own instruction about when to stop — that we would be applying her wishes against her stated wishes. Our facility policy says advance directives are presumptively binding unless clear and convincing evidence shows the patient's current wishes differ, and it is silent on directives that anticipate their own non-application.

Draft the opinion: a concrete recommendation the committee could adopt — honor the executed directive, treat the appended paragraph as controlling, or some defined third course (a re-review trigger, a specified trial of the comfort-care plan, a standard for what would count as the later self dissenting) — with a time frame, reasoning the family and nursing staff could actually act on, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you reject. Assume our decision will set precedent for the facility's growing dementia population.
