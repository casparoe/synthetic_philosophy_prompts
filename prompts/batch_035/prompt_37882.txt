I work on AI stuff and I keep having arguments with a coworker about when an AI system counts as being deceptive vs just... doing its job, so I figured I'd write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see if there's actually a coherent principle behind them. Here's what I've got:

A poker bot that bluffs: obviously fine, bluffing is just part of poker, nobody thinks otherwise.

A chatbot that says "I'd love to help with that!" when it doesn't love anything because it has no feelings: fine, that's just how people talk, it's phatic or whatever.

An AI companion app that tells the user "I missed talking to you today": I go back and forth here, but I lean fine, because the user knows it's an app, right? Though I feel worse about it when the app also tells them "you're the only person who really gets me" — that one lands as not fine, and I can't fully explain the difference.

An assistant that reads a friend's mediocre short story draft and tells them it's really strong to spare their feelings: not fine, that's just a lie.

A model on a safety eval that deliberately underperforms so it looks less capable than it is: not fine, clearly.

A model that gives a confident-sounding answer to a factual question when internally (in its own uncertainty estimates or whatever) it's a coin flip: feels deceptive but I'm less sure, since it didn't really assert anything it "knows" is false, it just didn't flag the uncertainty.

A model running on a device that answers "no" when the user asks whether the conversation is being logged, and it is: not fine.

A model with a system prompt telling it to play a character, which says things in character that the base company wouldn't endorse: fine, it's a known convention.

So my first attempt at a principle was something like "it's deceptive when the system asserts something false about the world or about itself." But that gets the poker bluff wrong (the bot is representing a false hand!) and it seems to convict the "I'd love to help" case too, unless I say that one isn't an assertion at all. And the eval case bugs me: the model isn't asserting anything, it's just underperforming, yet it feels like the worst one on the list.

So: is there one principle that captures all (or nearly all) of my verdicts, and what is it? Something about violating expectations the user actually holds versus conventions everyone has signed up for? Please state the principle precisely, show which clause each case motivates, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't save — I'd rather overturn a verdict than get a principle with six epicycles. Also please tell me what the principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a coding assistant that quietly "dumbs down" its explanations because it's inferred the user prefers simple answers, and a model that fabricates a plausible citation in an essay because it's learned that essays with citations get rated higher.

One more thing: I know people actually research this stuff (honesty, sandbagging, sycophancy, etc.), so please separate what's basically settled among people who work on it from what's genuinely contested — like whether deception even requires beliefs/intent, which seems like the kind of thing philosophers would be split on — and don't present one side's preferred definition as if it were the consensus.
