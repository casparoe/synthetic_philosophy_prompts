I write about science and ideas for a mid-sized outlet, and a quant friend of mine has ruined my week with a probability puzzle I can't leave alone. It's the old two-envelope thing — one envelope holds twice what the other holds, you pick one, you're offered a switch — but he insists the famous version is a red herring, and his version, which he worked out with actual numbers, seems to show that expected value reasoning eats itself. The classic arguement goes: call your amount X, the other envelope has 2X or X/2 with equal chances, so switching is worth 1.25X and you should switch — and everyone knows that fails, because X is different amounts in the two cases and you can't just stipulate 50/50. My friend says fine, let's do it properly, with an explicit rule for how the money got there. His setup: the envelopes hold the pair (2^n, 2^{n+1}) dollars with probability proportional to (3/4)^n, for n = 0, 1, 2, and so on, and you're handed a random envelope from the pair. His steps: (1) you open your envelope and see some amount x; if x = 2^n with n at least 1, then either you're holding the smaller envelope of pair n or the larger envelope of pair n−1, and those scenarios have probabilities proportional to (3/4)^n and (3/4)^(n−1) — a ratio of 3 to 4, so probability 3/7 you hold the smaller one. (2) Given that, switching is worth (3/7)(2x) + (4/7)(x/2) = 8x/7, an expected gain of x/7 for every single x you could possibly see — no vague priors, no stipulated 50/50, honest conditioning all the way down. (3) So whatever you see, you should switch; and if you should switch whatever you see, you should switch before opening too. (4) But the setup is completely symmetric between the two envelopes — a player who always switches and a player who never switch end up with literally identical distributions of final amounts — so switching can't do anything. (5) So either expected value is incoherent or the symmetry reasoning is, and my friend says this is exactly why we should distrust expected value arguments generally, a claim he would very much like me to put in print.

I've checked his arithmetic and it's right; 8x/7 really does come out, for every x, and it even works at x = 1 since there you must hold the smaller envelope. Something has to give between (3) and (4) but I can't name it, and the explainers I remeber all stop at debunking the sloppy version and never reach his. My hunch is the answer lives in what happens when you add up the (3/4)^n's against the 2^n's out at the tail, but every time I try to finish that thought I lose the thread. So tell me: which step fails, what did that step look entitled to that it wasn't actually entitled to, and what does rejecting it cost — is there some other situation where the same move is perfectly fine? If the tail really is the culprit, please spell out how a positive expected gain conditional on every possible observation can somehow add up to nothing, because that's the part I need to explain to readers without hand-waving.
