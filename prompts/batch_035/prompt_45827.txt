A friend and I got into a huge argument last night (three hours, at a bar, we got shushed) about whether "culture" can actually be a CAUSE of things, and I've been sitting with my gut reactions to a bunch of cases trying to figure out if there's any pattern to them. I mostly went in via Weber -- the Protestant ethic thing, where Calvinist anxiety about salvation supposedly pushed people into disciplined, savings-oriented behavior that ended up feeding capitalism. My friend thinks that's not a causal explanation at all, it's just a story we tell after the fact. Anyway, here are my verdicts on various culture-as-explanation cases, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures them:

- Weber's actual thesis (Ascetic Protestantism -> spirit of capitalism): feels like a real explanation to me. Maybe not proven, but legitimate.
- Putnam's thing about northern Italian towns having dense civic traditions going back to the medieval communes, which supposedly explains why their regional governments work better today: also feels legit to me.
- The "culture of poverty" explaining why poor families stay poor across generations: feels bad, like it's victim-blaming dressed up as explanation. Though I can't articulate why it's worse than the Weber one.
- A "Confucian work ethic" explaining East Asian growth in the late 20th century: feels fine to me. BUT my friend pointed out that a hundred years earlier people used Confucianism to explain why East Asia would NEVER modernize (too hierarchical, too deferential to tradition). Same culture, opposite predictions, both sounded plausible in their moment. I had no answer for that and it bugs me, but I notice I still don't want to give the growth explanation up.
- Explaining why Americans tip so much (and why service workers depend on tips) by "tipping culture": feels fine, almost obviously correct, culture causing stuff.
- Explaining the 2008 financial crisis by "greed on Wall Street" or "a culture of risk": feels like it's not explaining anything, just redescribing the outcome in evaluative language.
- Explaining why my grandparents' town in Quebec has different business practices than neighboring English-speaking towns by "distinct Quebecois Catholic culture": feels okay-ish but shakier than the others, and I can't say why.
- Explaining persistent high emigration from a region by saying the region has "an emigration culture": feels circular, like the culture just IS the pattern you're trying to explain.

So: is there one principle that sorts these into the piles I put them in? My friend's tentative view was that culture-as-cause is legit only when you can name the mechanism through individual people -- specific beliefs and anxieties (like the Calvinist ones) getting internalized and changing behavior -- and illegitimate when it's just a label for an aggregate outcome. But that seems to let the "culture of poverty" one back in (you can tell a mechanism story there too: internalized fatalism, etc.), and I don't want that. Or maybe I should want it?

Also please tell me what your principle says about two cases I didn't list: (1) my friend's company says a merger failed because of "corporate culture clash" -- can the culture of one 200-person firm be a cause in the same sense as the culture of a religious movement or a region, or is something different going on there? and (2) whether "honor culture" explanations for higher violence in the US South are more like the Weber case or the circular emigration case.

I want you to actually commit. Don't give me a survey of possible principles and leave the picking to me -- pick one, state it as precisely as you can, say which of my verdicts it saves and which it can't, and be upfront about which of my gut reactions you think I should just drop. If some of my verdicts are genuinely irreconcilable, tell me which ones, don't split the difference. I'd rather find out I'm wrong about the Confucianism thing than keep holding positions that can't all be true.
