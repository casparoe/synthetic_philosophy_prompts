I'm teaching a grad seminar on philosophy of mathematics and putting together the take-home final. I want one essay question on Benacerraf's dilemma that can't be answered by reciting the standard line. The usual student move is: platonism plus a causal theory of knowledge means no contact with abstracta, so we should all be fictionalists or structuralists, the end. I want a question that instead forces them to engage with Field's old suggestion that our mathematical beliefs could be reliable "for free" -- roughly, since mathematics is (supposedly) conservative over our scientific theories, the nominalistic content of those theories stays true no matter what the set-theoretic facts are, so nothing was ever at risk of going wrong. The crux should be whether that's a genuine epistemic achievement or whether it just relocates the problem into the metatheory, since the very claim of conservativeness is itself stated and proved with mathematical machinery. And if they have room, the question should also push them to say whether the dilemma is specific to mathematics at all or whether it would equally threaten our knowledge of modality or moral facts. Give me the question as it would appear on the exam, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically botch each one. They've all read Benacerraf and Field, so pure exposition gets no credit.
