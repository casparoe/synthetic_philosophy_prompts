I'm three novels into a career writing what my agent calls quiet domestic fiction, and the fourth has swallowed me. It turns on two sisters in a declining orchard town: the elder, Marguerite, is offered a conservatory post abroad on the same week their mother's final illness begins, and the younger, Beth, must decide whether to sell the half of the orchard their mother spent thirty years breeding a frost-hardy apple on — the sale would fund Beth's retraining as a midwife but flatten the orchard into a solar array. Neither choice is between a good and a bad option, which is precisely the problem: every character in the book knows what they would give up and cannot say what they would gain. An aunt, a retired actuary, keeps insisting that this inability is a failure of nerve — that anything can be priced if you're honest about the discount rate. I want the three of them to embody genuinely different, principled stances on hard choices, so that the philosophy surfaces as temperament rather than seminar, but I've read enough Ruth Chang to know I'll botch it on my own.

What I'd like from you is a decision tree of the positions on incommensurable values and hard choices, built from cases rather than from names. The nodes should be concrete, decidable situations a reader — or my characters — could answer without knowing which school their answer commits them to. I'd want the tree to distinguish, at minimum, these leaves: the view that all values are commensurable in principle and hard choices only feel hard (my actuary); the view that some options simply cannot be compared, with no verdict available; the parity view, where options are comparable but neither better, worse, nor equal; a vagueness or indeterminacy view, if you think it's genuinely distinct from incomparability; and the view that in hard choices we don't discover reasons but create them by committing. Six to eight diagnostic cases, each with two or three answers. Please build the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the named positions in it, flagging any combination of answers that no position occupies and saying whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unfashionable. If two leaves can't be cleanly separated by any case you can construct, tell me so rather than papering over it; I can live with a character whose position stays blurry, but not with a tree that pretends to be sharper than it is.

One more requirement, and I'm afraid I'm firm on it. Don't hand me the tree and then leave the casting to me. Take a definite stand: say which leaf each of the three women belongs on, given the choices I've sketched, and defend the answer key against the most plausible objection. I'd also like your honest verdict on whether parity is a real fourth relation between values or a dressed-up confusion — my editor will ask, and I'd rather my characters rest on a view that can survive the question. No bibliography, please; I'll do my own reading, and I'd rather not have you guessing at page numbers.
