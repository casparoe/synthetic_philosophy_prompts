I teach an upper-level bioethics course, and every year the unit on killing vs. letting die runs into a wall that comes from the law rather than the seminar room. Here's the pattern. In most jurisdictions a doctor may lawfully withdraw a ventilator from a patient who has made her wishes clear, knowing death will follow within hours; the same doctor commits homicide if she administers a painless lethal injection at the same patient's request. Courts explain this by saying the withdrawal merely lets the underlying disease do its work while the injection kills — even though the doctor in both cases acts deliberately, predicts the outcome with the same confidence, and typically has the same end in view. And the practice gets stranger at the edges. A patient who arrives needing a ventilator can simply not be given one (the machine is better used elsewhere, or the case is judged futile), but once she's on it, hospitals get very nervous about reassigning the machine to a patient with a better prognosis — even in a genuine crunch. Meanwhile the talk of "letting the disease do its work" sits oddly with the fact that the patient dies only because the doctor unplugged something.

So my question: what view of doing vs. allowing does this legal and medical practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory — is it really about the doing/allowing distinction at all, or is it secretly a theory about bodily invasion, or about consent to ongoing treatment? Is that theory coherent, given how similar the doctor's intention looks across cases? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me what would have to change in the law and in bedside practice if we swapped in a straight utilitarian view, or Rachels's line that the bare difference between killing and letting die is morally irrelevant.
