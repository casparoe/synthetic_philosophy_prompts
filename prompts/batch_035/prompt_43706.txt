I have recently moved back to the small town I grew up in, and almost immediately found myself volunteered onto its heritage committee, which has to decide what to do about the Coyle memorial — the plaque and the annual wreath-laying in the square that honours Nathaniel Coyle, a nineteenth-century magistrate whom local tradition credits with refusing to enforce the famine-era evictions and, according to the story every child here learns, hiding several families in his mill until the worst had passed. An amateur historian on the committee has spent a year in the estate papers and county archives, and her findings are awkward: Coyle appears repeatedly as a signatory on eviction notices, no contemporaneous source so much as mentions anyone being sheltered, and the sheltering story enters the written record only with a centennial pageant staged in the late 1930s. At our last meeting two fellow members, Ruth and Denis, argued about what follows, and I was asked to summarise the dispute fairly before we vote. I would rather not vote until I understand it.

Ruth's position: the record should simply win. When documents and tradition conflict, and the documents are contemporaneous, independent of one another, and convergent, while the tradition is late, single-stranded, and traceable to a pageant script, then the tradition is not community memory at all — it is invented tradition, a fabricated record — and treating it as a rival to the archive dignifies a fiction. Keeping the plaque as it stands would be knowingly teaching children a falsehood, which is propaganda with good manners.

Denis's reply: archives are not the clean tribunal Ruth pretends. They are what whoever could write, and whoever kept papers, chose to record; a magistrate who quietly defied orders is exactly the sort of person who leaves no paper trail of defiance while the paperwork of compliance gets filed away. Oral tradition can preserve precisely what the record suppressed, so an empty archive is not evidence of an empty past.

Ruth rejoins that this would be fair if we had an oral tradition, but we do not — we have a story that demonstrably post-dates the people it describes, so Denis is defending a "memory" that never touched the events at all.

Denis concedes the origin but not the conclusion: even if the story entered through a pageant, it has since been lived in for nearly a century — grandparents told it, the town organised itself partly around it — so it is now genuinely the community's memory regardless of where it came from. Commemoration, he insists, is not testimony; its job is not to assert what happened but to hold up what the community aspires to, and Coyle functions as a symbol of the sheltering this town did in fact practise in other ways during other crises. Correcting the plaque will not undo the identity built on the story; it will just cut away its anchor and leave people with a hollowed-out past, and every community identity is selective anyway, Ruth's beloved archive included.

Ruth's final word is that Denis has slid from "history is fallible" to "memory is no worse than it", which does not follow: convergent independent contemporaneous sources are not on a par with one late, motivated story, and an identity that survives only by refusing to know its own past is already discredited — the only question is whether the town becomes complicit in its own miseducation.

They have now stopped speaking to each other and are both waiting for my summary. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who is right about what at each step, not merely who prevails overall — and, since I suspect the pair of them share some blind spot, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. I would be grateful if you could write your answer in British English.
