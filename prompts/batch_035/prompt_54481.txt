I'm the patient representative on the ethics committee at our county behavioral health center, and we have a case next week that I suspect is going to split us down the middle. A woman in her late 30s with schizoaffective disorder — she's had maybe five involuntary holds over the past decade, each one following the same script where she stops her meds, stops sleeping, and by the time anyone intervenes she's lost her apartment or her job and is pretty angry at everyone involved. Last year, while stable and with a documented capacity assessment, she worked out a self-binding agreement with her outpatient psychiatrist: she named a proxy (her sister), pre-consented to a two-week involuntary stay and a specific antipsychotic if she relapsed and refused treatment, and even wrote a letter to her future self acknowledging she'd probably be furious and asking everyone to go ahead anyway. Classic Ulysses contract stuff, she actually used the phrase about tying herself to the mast.

So now she's relapsed, brought in by her sister, and objecting — except the wrinkle is that her objection is actually pretty articulate, not floridly psychotic. And there's a second wrinkle: the drug she pre-consented to caused her serious akathisia in the months after she signed (a side effect nobody anticipated when the contract was drafted), she's asking for a different medication, and the treating team thinks the alternative is clinically reasonable. Half the committee says we should honor the agreement exactly as written because that's the whole point of it, and half says forcing her to take a drug that's now known to torture her when she's asking for a reasonable substitute turns the contract into a suicide pact rather than a safety net. One of the psychiatrists on the committee keeps insisting this is all just constitutional precommitment at small scale — that honoring the directive is like enforcing an old constitution against a living majority, which we do all the time without blinking — while our lawyer counters that we at least amend constitutions, and she's arguably trying to amend hers right now from the floor.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It should give a clear recommendation (honor it as written, honor it with a narrow exception for the medication substitution, or treat it as nonbinding given her current objection), reasoning that the clinicians and the patient advocate could actually engage with rather than just hand-waving about autonomy, and it should take the strongest version of the losing side seriously. We also want a paragraph at the end recommending what our standing policy should be for these agreements going forward, since her case has prompted two other patients to ask about writing their own. Keep in mind our committee has to live with each other after this, so if you're going to say her past self gets to overrule her present self, I'd like a real answer to why that past self is the *real* one rather than just the more convenient one.
