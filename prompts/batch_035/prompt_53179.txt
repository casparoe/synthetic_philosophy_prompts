My friend and I spent most of last Friday evening arguing about the Turing test, and we ended exactly where we started, which has made me curious about what kind of disagreement this actually is. My position was roughly the operationalist one: if a machine could sustain an arbitrary, unscripted conversation with a competent interrogator indefinitely, that would be intelligence, full stop, because sustained linguistic behavior of that richness simply is what we have to go on with other people, and we extend the attribution to humans on no better basis. His position leaned on the Chinese Room style of objection: the test only establishes conversational competence, and for all the test shows there could be the right inputs and outputs with nothing resembling understanding, intentionality, or experience behind them — the interrogator's judgment tracks a performance, not a fact about the machine's interior. We are both familiar with the standard moves and countermoves (other minds, the systems reply, silicon substitution arguments, whether the test's criterion is induction or stipulation), and that's exactly what worries me: the debate has been running in essentially this shape since the 1950s, extremely smart people keep having it, and nobody seems to move.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to evaluate rather than assume, is that the stalled disagreement is not really about machines at all — it's a disguised disagreement about the epistemology of other minds, one that was latent in our attribution of mentality to humans and only becomes visible when a machine forces the question of what our ordinary behavioral evidence is evidence of. On that diagnosis, the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side treating the test as a criterion (a rule fixing what we mean), the other as an evidential proxy (a fallible symptom of something independently specified), so that they can both accept every behavioral fact and still not disagree about anything flat-out factual. But I can see problems with this: the Chinese Room enthusiast seems to be making a substantive metaphysical claim about what syntax can and cannot generate, not merely a point about the word "understanding," and if so the dispute is not verbal but a real, if stubborn, question about whether the right functional organization is sufficient for understanding.

Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, substantive-metaphysical, a difference in epistemic standards, or two questions run under one name? Support your diagnosis with what the sides actually claim rather than a survey of the literature, and be honest about whether anything could count as progress here — a conceptual distinction, a new empirical capacity, a revision of the question — or whether this is one of those impasses that signals the question is badly posed, and why.
