Got into a big argument with my brother-in-law over the holidays about the Euthyphro dilemma, of all things. He's a theist who takes a pretty straightforward divine command line — whatever God commands is thereby good. I hit him with the classic fork: is the good good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good? And he just shrugged and said the second one, obviously — God commands what's already good, no problem. That kind of bugs me, because I thought the whole point is that the second horn makes morality independent of God, which is supposedly what a divine command theorist can't accept. But he seemed totally chill about it, and honestly I couldn't get him to see why he should care.

Here's what I want from you, since I keep going back and forth on it. Give me your credence, as an actual number, that grabbing the first horn — good *because* commanded — is the right move for a divine command theorist. But decompose it, because I think my own uncertainty here isn't really one question, it's several tangled together. At minimum it seems like: (a) whether the dilemma is actually exhaustive, i.e. whether all the famous escapes (God's nature being identical to the good, commands somehow constituting the good in a non-arbitrary way, etc.) just restate one horn or the other, or smuggle in a third thing that collapses back into a horn when you push on it; (b) whether the first horn is even coherent once you hold it, or whether it just makes goodness arbitrary in a way that destroys the normativity the theist wanted God to secure in the first place — the torture-for-no-reason problem; and (c) some prior about what divine command theorists are even trying to do — whether 'good' is supposed to be a property that needs grounding at all, or more like a title that only makes sense relative to an authority.

But here's the part that actually keeps me up at night, and I want you to weigh in on it too, with its own number: I've started to suspect the same fork shows up in places with no God anywhere in sight. Constructivism in ethics, for instance — the view that principles are right because (roughly) we'd endorse them under ideal conditions. Fine: are those principles right independently of our endorsing them — in which case what work is the endorsing doing? Or does the endorsing make them right — in which case why is *that* the right thing to do the making? Who commanded the endorsing? Same shape with 'truth is what ideal inquiry converges on': does inquiry converge because it's true, or is it true because ideal inquiry converges? So second question: your credence that the Euthyphro is a genuinely general structure — that it's really about any proposed source of normativity whatever, not just a clever gotcha aimed at one theological doctrine — and tell me whether that number should move your first one or whether they're independent.

For each component give me a probability and a line of justification, then however you combine them into the final numbers, and then tell me which single component you'd most want resolved before you'd trust your own answer. And if you think one of my decompositions is off — like if (b) isn't really seperable from (a), or the constructivist case is importantly disanalogous to the theological one in a way I'm missing — push back on that instead of just running with it, but still give me numbers. I like having something concrete to argue against when he brings it up again at thanksgiving.
