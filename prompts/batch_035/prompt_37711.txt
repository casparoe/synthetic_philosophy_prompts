I'm a retired neuroscientist — thirty years recording from rat hippocampus, mostly spatial coding stuff — and since retiring I've fallen hard for philosophy of mind. Right now I'm working through the higher-order theories of consciousness, the idea that a mental state only becomes conscious when there's some further state representing that first state. And I've noticed something uncomfortable: taken seriously, this seems to imply that a lot of animals — maybe my own rats — don't feel anything at all, because there's no reason to think they meta-represent their own states. The first-order crowd thinks that's a reductio of the higher-order view; some of the higher-order people just bite the bullet.

Here's my actual situation: I volunteer on the animal care committee at the university where I used to work. We review protocols monthly and decide on analgesia, enrichment, humane endpoints, that sort of thing, and honestly those decisions currently get made on vibes and whatever the attending vet feels like that week. I'd like a procedure the committee can actually run: for a given species and protocol, an ordered list of questions about the evidence — nociception vs pain trade-offs, uncertainty-monitoring tasks, trace conditioning, whatever genuinely bears on the first-order vs higher-order distinction — with a criterion for what counts as a good-enough answer to each, a default to protection when the evidence is thin (I'm not letting philosophy take away pain relief), and clear points where the procedure hands off to the committee's values instead of pretending the theory settles it. Be blunt about which steps are really just judgment dressed up as science.

Then run it on three protocols: mice in a chronic pain model, cuttlefish in a learning study where the students swear they show something like uncertainty monitoring, and crabs in a water-quality assay. And one more thing I want honesty about: if the procedure, run straight, gives mouse pain a low standing because the higher-order reading says it isn't felt, tell me that — don't sand it down for my benefit. Likewise, if you think the whole project is confused — that an ethics committee has no business adjudicating between theories of consciousness the philosophers haven't settled themselves, or that the theories are too vague to operationalize at all — say so plainly instead of politely building me a checklist anyway. I'd rather hear the objection up front then waste the committee's time. Keep the deliverable short enough to print on one page; we get maybe ten minutes per protocol and the vet has final sign-off.
