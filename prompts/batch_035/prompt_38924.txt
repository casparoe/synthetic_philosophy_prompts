I have just come out of an argument that lasted most of the evening with a close friend of mine — she works in quantitative risk management, which made the whole thing more frustrating, not less — and I would like your help building a rigorous case for the position I ended up defending by the end of it. Since this is a technical matter in decision theory, I'd ask you to take on the role of a professional philosopher and decision theorist: someone equally at home with the formal apparatus of expected utility and with the debates about its normative status.

Let me lay out the disagreement as neutrously as I can. My friend holds what I take to be the orthodox view: if you prefer more money to less, then the only rational way to be wary of a gamble is through the shape of your utility function over outcomes — that is, through diminishing marginal utility. Once your utilities over final states are fixed, you must evaluate every option by the probability-weighted average of those utilities, and rank options accordingly. She is happy to concede that a poor person should refuse a coin flip between doubling and losing a month's rent; the loss is catastrophic and the utility curve is steep there. But she insists that a wealthy person, for whom either outcome would be a rounding error, must accept any gamble with positive expected monetary value, and that refusing one is a cognitive failing — "risk aversion," she says, "is just utility you forgot to measure."

By the end I was defending something like the following picture, though I'm not sure I can articulate it well. Perhaps rationality permits an agent to care not only about how good each outcome is, but also about where in the distribution of her possible lives each outcome sits — so that a gamble can be evaluated by something other than the plain probability-weighted average of utilities, even holding fixed both her probabilities and her utilities over final outcomes. Concretely, imagine the agent ranks the possible outcomes of a gamble from worst to best and assigns the utility increments extra weight when they sit near the bottom of that ranking, and reduced weight when they sit near the top; two options could then be rationally ranked apart despite identical expected utility, even for an agent whose utility is linear in money. On this view, the wealthy person can rationally decline a favorable gamble — not because losing hurts more than winning helps (it doesn't, for her), and not because she has miscalculated, but because she legitimately cares about the structure of risk itself. I gather the best-known development of this idea is something called risk-weighted expected utility theory, though I only know of it secondhand and don't need you to focus on the literature; I want the philosophical case.

So, please make the strongest argument you can for my side of the disagreement. I'd like you to proceed carefully:

1. Begin with some brainstorming: before committing to any line of argument, sketch the candidate routes you see — for example, appeals to widely shared patterns of choice that the orthodox theory has to classify as irrational, appeals to what an agent could rationally care about over and above final outcomes, and appeals to the track record of the orthodox theory's own idealizations. Say which strike you as most promising and why, and then develop those.

2. Address head-on the objection that I could see coming even as I was arguing: that this view licenses rational timidity — that on my picture someone could apparently rationally refuse every favorable gamble, including ones that are practically riskless, and that something has gone badly wrong if rationality permits a person to decline free money. Where exactly is the line, on my view, between legitimate concern with the distribution of risk and mere loss-aversion bias?

3. Address the structural objection my friend pressed hardest: that evaluating a gamble by where outcomes sit in a ranking breaks the separability of outcomes — the idea that what happens in one state should not affect how you value what happens in another state — and that separability is precisely what makes decision theory a theory of coherent preference under uncertainty rather than a heap of intuitions.

4. Close by telling me honestly where my position is most vulnerable, and what concession I should be prepared to make to my friend when we inevitably have this argument again.

Please think through this step by step and be willing to revise your initial framing if you notice a better one partway through. Precision matters more to me here than rhetorical force — my friend will smell any hand-waving immediately.
