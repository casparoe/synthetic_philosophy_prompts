My most analytically relentless friend has produced an argument that moderate deontology — the view that there are agent-relative moral constraints, but that they give way when the stakes are severe enough — is not a stable position. Either it collapses into consequentialism or it floats free of any principle. I can't accept that conclusion, and I can't locate the fault. Here is the argument, as she gives it.

Some common ground first. We both accept that if deontology is true, its constraints are agent-relative: the constraint against killing binds me against killing, not against letting others kill. That is how the familiar self-defeat charge gets answered — if I refuse to kill one person to stop five killers, five killings occur, but none of them are mine, so I have violated nothing. She is not contesting that reply. Her target is the threshold.

(1) Absolute constraints are indefensible. A constraint that holds even when honoring it permits a million killings rather than my committing one is not a principle anyone can accept. So every constraint yields at some point — call that point its threshold.
(2) A threshold cannot be an arbitrary number. To be principled, the threshold must track the point of the constraint it belongs to.
(3) The point of a constraint against killing the innocent is protecting innocents from being killed. The victim is equally dead whether I kill or merely allow, so the constraint's point is indifferent to whose hand is on the trigger.
(4) It follows that the principled threshold is reached exactly when the killings my compliance would permit exceed the killing my violation would commit. Any lower threshold protects fewer innocents than it costs; any higher one sacrifices innocents for nothing.
(5) She then runs a ladder: a case where I must kill one to prevent two killings; a case where I must kill one to prevent three; and so on up to a million. Either the constraint yields at every rung — in which case, given (4), it yields as soon as two killings are at stake, and the constraint is cost-benefit reasoning in moral dress — or it yields at some rung but not at the rung immediately below, and that single-death difference does all the normative work, which (2) condemns as arbitrary.
(6) Therefore: either absolutism, which (1) refutes, or consequentialism. Threshold deontology is not a coherent middle position; its threshold either collapses into a cost-benefit line or hovers unprincipledly above one.

She concludes that philosophers who describe themselves as threshold deontologists are consequentialists with squeamishness in the middle of the distribution.

I want to reject her conclusion, and I suspect that (1) and (6) misdescribe the middle position, but I cannot say exactly how. Is the slide from (3) to (4) — from "protecting people from being killed" to "minimizing killings" — a genuine equivocation, or does the agent-relativity I already conceded at the outset commit me to (3) as she reads it? Does the ladder in (5) really force a choice between yielding at every rung and yielding at none, or is there a principled account of thresholds — vague ones, catastrophe-indexed ones, ones that track what I do rather than what happens — that escapes the dilemma? Walk me through the argument step by step, tell me which premise to give up or which inference to block, and be explicit about what each option costs: not only what the denial does to my deontology, but whether the same ratchet, if it works, also takes down the thresholds we accept elsewhere in moral and legal thought — proportionality in self-defense, the necessity defense, the proportionality condition in double effect — or whether those survive for reasons her argument never touches.
