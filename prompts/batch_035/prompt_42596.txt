"Explain the asymmetry between pain and pleasure that David Benatar uses to argue that coming into existence is a harm. Set out the relevant claims about the presence and absence of pain and pleasure, show how the asymmetry is supposed to yield the conclusion that procreation is wrong, and discuss one objection to the argument."

Here's the student's answer, lightly reformatted from what they submitted:

"Benatar's asymmetry says that (1) the presence of pain is bad, (2) the presence of pleasure is good, (3) the absence of pain is good, even if there is nobody around to enjoy that absence, and (4) the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody who is deprived of it. So if a person is never born, all the badness of the pains they would have had is avoided, while the non-occurrence of their pleasures is not a bad thing, since there's no one who misses out. Given that hedonism is true and all value ultimately reduces to pleasure and the absence of pain, we can just run the utilitarian calculus: non-existence comes out with net positive value, and any life that contains any pain at all comes out with net negative value. So non-existence is better for the person who is never born. And once you accept that non-existence is better than existence, the logic pushes one step further: dying is the move from existence to non-existence, so Benatar's position also implies that once you do exist, dying sooner is better than continuing to live — his antinatalism is basically a defense of suicide. One objection is that most people say they're glad to have been born, but Benatar can reply that people are systematically optimistic about their own lives and adapt to their circumstances, so self-reports of life satisfaction aren't trustworthy evidence."
