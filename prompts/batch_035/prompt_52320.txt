In a graduate seminar on constitutional precommitment, a classmate argued that emergency powers should be understood on the model of the Ulysses contract: the framers bound the government in advance precisely so that panic in the moment could not loosen the constraints, just as we honor an advance directive even after the patient's current wishes change. Our professor replied, to general nodding, that the Constitution is not a suicide pact, and the discussion moved on. Afterwards I traced the line to Justice Robert Jackson's 1949 dissent in Terminiello v. City of Chicago, which warned that the Court would "convert the constitutional Bill of Rights into a suicide pact" if it did not temper doctrinaire logic with practical wisdom, and I found that versions of the sentiment have been invoked to defend everything from Jefferson's Louisiana Purchase to Lincoln's suspension of habeas corpus. My worry is that the retort, as used in seminar, trades on an ambiguity. A precommitment device is built to bind at exactly the moment it becomes costly to honor, so any reading of the slogan strong enough to release us from constitutional constraints in an emergency seems to release us from advance directives, wills, and Ulysses contracts too, while any reading weak enough to preserve those devices seems to leave the Constitution doing its ordinary work of constraining the present. Please give me exactly five distinct and precise claims the slogan could be expressing, ordered from the least to the most revisionary — from readings on which it is a near-tautology to readings on which it licenses unilateral release from the precommitment itself — and in each item state whether that reading is defensible and whether it would actually entitle my professor to wave off the Ulysses analogy. Present the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
