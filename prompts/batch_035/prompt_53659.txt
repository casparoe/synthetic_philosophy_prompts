I'm putting together discussion material for an upper-level seminar on environmental ethics, and I want a dialogue that forces students past the usual platitudes about biodiversity. The uncomfortable question at the center: could destroying wild habitat, in principle, reduce animal suffering? On one side stands a conservation biologist who holds that intact, mature ecosystems are on balance good for the animals living in them — that undisturbed populations have stable food, room for play and social behavior, and lower parasite loads than crowded or degraded ones, so that a functioning ecosystem is not just an abstraction but an aggregation of lives that are mostly worth living. On the other side stands a wild-animal-welfare skeptic who presses a harder case: a frog lays thousands of eggs in a season and nearly all of the offspring die within weeks, mostly by predation, starvation, or desiccation; if almost every individual in a lineage's history is a brief prelude to an early violent death, then aggregate welfare across a wild population may be sharply negative, and shrinking such populations — even by destroying the habitat that sustains them — could reduce suffering rather than cause it.

Write a dialogue between these two, with the conversation anchored to a concrete scenario: they are both reviewing an environmental impact assessment for a proposal to drain a large marsh and convert it to farmland, and the skeptic has just argued that the survey's "losses" are miscounted as harms when they may prevent more suffering than they cause. Give both characters real resources and make them argue well: the biologist should engage the r-selection argument directly (e.g., by questioning whether death shortly after hatching subtracts much welfare when there was little positive to subtract, or by pointing out that conversion to farmland creates its own vast populations of short-lived animals in fields and ponds), and the skeptic should in turn concede what is genuinely strong in the opposing view rather than shrugging it off. Neither should be a strawman, they must respond to each other's actual points rather than talk past each other, and small concessions on either side are welcome.

End the dialogue without an artificial resolution, but have the two of them explicitly identify the sharpest point that still divides them — I'd like the closing exchange to make clear what a student would need to decide in order to take a side. Length of roughly 600–900 words for the dialogue itself, with characters' names and a brief scene-setting line at the start.
