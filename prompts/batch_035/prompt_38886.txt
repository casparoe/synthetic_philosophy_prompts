I'm a PhD student in statistics serving a two-year term as the graduate representative on my university's Doctoral Fellowship Committee, which allocates about 120 internal dissertation fellowships a year. A dispute has broken out, and I've been asked to draft the memo that will frame our meeting this Thursday. The committee wants a written recommendation it can act on, and I want the statistical reasoning to be right rather than merely soothing.

Here are the facts. Over the last three years, applicants who entered the PhD with a master's degree earned elsewhere (roughly 40% of the pool) won fellowships at a 27% rate overall, versus 43% for students who entered directly from a bachelor's degree. On the strength of those aggregate numbers, one member — call her Professor A — argues the review process is biased against external master's students and is pushing for anonymized review plus a mandatory bias workshop. But when our provost's office broke the results down by the three divisions — humanities, social sciences, and natural sciences/engineering — the pattern reverses in every single one: within each division, external master's students succeed at a modestly higher rate than direct-entry students. External master's students simply apply disproportionately to humanities, where everyone's success rate is low.

Professor A says the divisional breakdown is a smokescreen and that applicants experience the aggregate world, not a division-adjusted one. A second member, Professor B, says the disaggregated figures are the only honest ones, and that the real problem is that the divisional allocation formulas shortchange humanities — so we should reallocate fellowships across divisions rather than touch the review process. A third member suspects both are wrong but can't articulate why. Meanwhile the Graduate Student Association is preparing a public statement, so whatever we do will be scrutinized.

Please draft the memo from my seat on the committee, recommending a concrete course of action with a time frame the committee could actually follow. It should take Professors A and B each seriously — the strongest version of each position deserves a fair hearing, not a caricature. Pay particular attention to which comparison (aggregate or division-by-division) answers which question, and to whether anything about the students' own choices — for instance, which division someone applies to, and whether that choice is itself shaped by having done an external master's — could make even the disaggregated numbers misleading. Where the memo takes a contested step in its reasoning, it should flag that step rather than paper over it. Finally, it should close with a short section stating explicitly what evidence or argument would lead you to recommend the opposite course, and where the analysis is most likely to fail. Hard limit: 900 words total. I would rather cut prose than cut the counter-considerations.
