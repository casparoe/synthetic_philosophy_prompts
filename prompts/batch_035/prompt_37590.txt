I'm a retired physicist — forty years, mostly instrument design and then control systems late in my career — and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole. Right now I'm deep in Dennett's tower of generate and test, the Darwinian / Skinnerian / Popperian / Gregian creature business from his mid-90s books. I love the tower, but my son and I have cooked up an argument that seems to tear the whole thing down, and it can't be right, because it proves way too much. Here it is:

(1) Darwinian creatures just have fixed routines set by natural selection. Skinnerian creatures revise their behaivor during their own lifetimes by trial and error, driven by what the environment rewards and punishes. Popperian creatures run the trials internally — they let their hypotheses die in their stead. Gregorian creatures go further and import mind-tools (words, maps, notations) from the culture around them.

(2) At any given moment, everything a system can do is fixed by (a) how it was built and (b) everything that has happened to it since, together with the current input. Nothing else is available to act from.

(3) "Everything that has happened to it since" includes all the rewards and punishments it has met — and, for Gregorian creatures, all the words, maps, and tools the culture has poured into them.

(4) A system whose entire behavioral repertoire is fixed by its build plus its history of environmental feedback is, at best, a very elaborate Skinnerian creature. The "environment" doing the shaping is just bigger, and includes teachers and books.

(5) So there is no difference in kind between the levels of the tower, only differences in how rich the feedback channel is. Nobody ever actually gets beyond Skinner, because there's nothing left over to get beyond Skinner *with* — step (2) says the ingredients are always the same two things.

(6) Therefore humans, my grandson's chatbot, and the rat in a Skinner box are all one kind of creature, and the story about Popperian foresight and Gregorian mind-tools is just bookkeeping.

I don't believe (6), and here's what makes me more suspicious: if you squint the same way, even a Darwinian creature is getting "rewarded" by differential survival, so the same reasoning flattens all four floors including the ground floor Dennett starts from. The part that feels fishiest to me is the slide from (4) to (5) — "the environment is just bigger" sounds like it's answering a different question than the tower asks — but I can't pin down exactly what's being equivocated on. And honestly step (2) also looks rock solid to me, which is what scares me: I've spent four decades assuming every system is boundary conditions plus input. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine to someone trained like me, and what rejecting it costs. And if you think the argument is actually valid and I should just eat conclusion (6), say so — but then tell me what eating it costs.
