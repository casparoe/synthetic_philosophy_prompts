Last night my closest friend and I had a long, running argument about the precautionary principle, and she ended it with a summary of the principle that she took from a popular book on risk and decision-making she'd read a few months ago. She put it roughly as follows: the precautionary principle says that whenever an activity carries any possibility of catastrophic harm, regulators must ban it until its proponents can prove it safe; since nothing can ever be proven safe, the principle, taken seriously, forbids everything; so what it really amounts to is plain risk aversion dressed up as a principle, and since ordinary cost-benefit analysis already handles risk aversion by weighting harms by their probability, the precautionary principle adds nothing except an irrational bias in favor of the status quo. She concluded that a rational society should simply drop it and weigh all harms and benefits on a single expected-value scale, as cost-benefit analysis already does.

I'm inclined to think this is too easy a dismissal, but she argued it well and I don't know the literature deeply enough to say exactly where it goes wrong, if it does. Give me a careful audit of her summary. Which of her claims would a serious defender of the principle accept as a fair statement of at least some version of it? Which conflate distinct formulations — I gather there are weaker and stronger versions, and I suspect she has stated one of the strongest as though it were the principle itself? Which of her steps are straightforward misstatements rather than simplifications? And does her conclusion — that we should abandon the principle in favor of ordinary expected-value weighing — actually follow from the parts of her summary that survive scrutiny? I'd especially like to know whether her claim that the principle collapses into risk aversion is something anyone who works on precaution would recognize: my instinct is that defenders are pointing at something about irreversibility or catastrophic downside that ordinary probability weighting doesn't capture, but I can't quite articulate why that would be, or whether I'm right that it matters.
