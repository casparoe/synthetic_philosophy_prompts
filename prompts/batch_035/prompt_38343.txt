so I've been trying to work out where I actually stand on Dutch book arguments and I want someone to audit the whole package, because I suspect it doesn't hang together but I genuinly can't see where the problem is. Here's the list I'd defend at a reading group tomorrow:

(1) The Dutch book argument is basically right and it's the justification for the probability axioms: if your credences violate them, a bookie can offer you a set of bets each of which looks favourable to you individually, and the set as a whole guarantees a loss. And I mean this as a mark of irrationality, not just "you might lose money" — an agent who is bookable is epistemically broken in a way that has nothing to do with how much they care about money.

(2) I accept the diachronic version too: if you update in some way other than conditionalising, you can be booked across time (past-you accepts one bet, future-you accepts another, jointly a guaranteed loss), and that shows conditionalisation is the rational update rule.

(3) But I'm also pretty committed to rationality being a synchronic affair: what makes your credences irrational today has to be something about you today — your current credences and your current evidence. Past-you's betting behaviour can't make current-you irrational, any more than breaking yesterday's promise makes today's beliefs irrational. Rationality doesn't reach across time.

(4) Credences are graded beliefs, full stop — I'm no behaviourist about them. Betting dispositions are at best fallible evidence of credence, and an agent who refuses all bets out of pure risk aversion, while having perfectly coherent credences, is fully rational. I would never say that to have a credence just is to have certain betting dispositions.

(5) I think imprecise credences are fine and sometimes rationally required — real evidence often doesn't pin down a sharp number, and insisting on one anyway can be a kind of false precision. Though I'll admit I have a nagging worry that an agent who can't produce a sharp answer to "what's your credence in p?" is somehow bookable, which would make (5) sit badly with (1).

(6) And, more tentativly, I think countable additivity is a genuine requirement of rationality — e.g. there can't be a rationally permissible uniform credence distribution over the natural numbers — though I've heard the Dutch book machinery can't enforce countable additivity (something about the book needing infinitely many transactions, with no guaranteed loss at any finite stage), and I've never known what to do with that.
