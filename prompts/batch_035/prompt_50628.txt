quick backstory so you know where I'm coming from: I spent 37 years in pharmacology, mostly on antihypertensives, retired last spring, and have since fallen down the philosophy of medicine rabbit hole. I read a bunch on the naturalism vs normativism fight about disease -- Boorse's line that disease is just statistically subnormal part-function relative to the species design, the normativist line that you can't get "dysfunction" without smuggling in values about what we want bodies to do, and Wakefield's hybrid trying to bolt "harm" onto "dysfunction." And honestly I keep picking sides based on whichever paper I read last, which is embarrassing for someone who used to design trials. So before I commit to anything I want the spec first.

What I want from you: the full list of desiderata for an adequate account of disease/health -- what any theory in this area has to actually deliver, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. I'll seed the list with the cases I keep chewing on, you flesh these out and add the ones I'm missing:

- sickle cell trait in a malarial region: the trait is statistically common there AND fitness-enhancing there, so "subnormal species-typical functioning" has to say something non-stupid about environment-relative reference classes
- the threshold problem I lived with my whole career: we moved the hypertension cutoffs and literally created millions of new patients overnight with a committee vote. whatever disease is, a theory has to tell me whether that was discovery or legislation
- asymptomatic stuff like early atherosclerosis: nobody feels sick, plenty of function, still kills you. feels like disease without illness
- the reverse: chronic pain with no finding, or the patient who feels awful and all the labs are clean. illness without any measurable dysfunction?
- menopause and aging: species-typical functioning par excellence, and also the thing half the pharma industry wants to classify as a disease. a theory has to have a principled answer, not just "ugh, hard case"
- the Deaf community folks who say deafness is not a defect but a culture; the theory has to say something about whether that objection even engages with what disease is
- medicalization run amok: shyness becomes social anxiety disorder, grief gets a clock on it, and the answer can't just be "the DSM is bad" -- the theory itself has to be the thing that tells us where the line is

I also want the distinctions a theory must respect and can't be allowed to collapse: disease vs illness vs injury, having a condition vs deserving treatment, dysfunction vs mere statistical rarity (Olympic hearts are rare too). Add whatever else belongs there.

For each desideratum, mark it non-negotiable or negotiable, and say why -- and here's the part I most want: if any two or three of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied (I suspect value-neutrality and the anti-medicalization one are at war, but convince me either way), say so explicitly and say which one you'd give up. Then a SHORT scorecard at the end: Boorsean naturalism, straightforward normativism, and the Wakefield hybrid against your list, no more than a line each on where they bleed.

Format: one table with columns [desideratum (short name) | what it demands | motivating case | non-negotiable? | why / tension with which others], then the joint-unsatisfiability discussion in prose, then a second small table with columns [view | cleanest fail | cleanest pass | one-line verdict]. Keep the scorecard thin, I want the thinking in the criteria, not the grading.
