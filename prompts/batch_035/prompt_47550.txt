I'm a third-year student in an upper-level game theory seminar, and last week our professor ran a classroom experiment that has since produced a running argument between two of my classmates. I've been asked to referee it, and I honestly can't, so I'm hoping you can.

The experiment: our class of thirty-five students played a one-shot, anonymous game. Each of us secretly chose Cooperate or Defect. If at least twenty students chose Cooperate, every cooperator got $10 and every defector got $12. If fewer than twenty cooperated, every cooperator got nothing and every defector got $2. No communication, no identities revealed, no repeated play — the payout was real and the game was over once we all chose. It came out that twenty-two people cooperated, so the defectors walked away with twelve dollars each and the cooperators with ten.

My classmate Maya says defecting was simply the rational choice, and she'll hear nothing else. Her reasoning: the game was anonymous and simultaneous, so nothing she did could influence anyone else's choice. Given what the other thirty-four did, she strictly preferred defecting in every case. Since she couldn't affect their behavior, the only thing her deliberation could rationally track was her own payout, and defection dominated.

My classmate Jonas says that's exactly the style of reasoning that made him cooperate, and that he did so deliberately. His claim: the other thirty-four people in that room are students from roughly the same population, who had read the same textbook chapters, heard the same lecture, and received the same instructions minutes before choosing. If his deliberation, running through that shared background, lands on defect, then that is evidence that similar deliberations in similar heads landed there too. His choice wasn't a lever on their choices — he agrees it wasn't — but the policy he settled on was a sample of the policy the room as a whole settled on, and he wanted to be someone whose reasoning, if replicated across the room, produces the ten-dollar outcome rather than the two-dollar one.

Maya answered that this is just a confusion between correlation and causation wearing a sophisticated costume. The other students' deliberations were what they were; they're fixed facts about the world that his deliberation can discover but not alter. His cooperating doesn't make them cooperate any more than a confident stance makes a die roll come up six, and if the correlation argument were legitimate, a person could justify nearly anything by imagining a choir of near-duplicates reasoning along with them.

Jonas replied that he isn't claiming causal influence and isn't imagining duplicates — he's pointing out that his deliberation and theirs are products of the same kind of process fed the same inputs, so the outcomes are correlated in a perfectly ordinary statistical sense. And he made a further point: if everyone in the room had reasoned as Maya did, everyone would have defected, everyone would have received $2, and everyone would have been worse off. Maya's own mode of reasoning, he said, refutes itself the moment you suppose it's the reasoning the room actually ran on. She's reasoning as though she's a lone island in a sea of unrelated agents, when she's one of thirty-five interchangeable instances of the same decision problem.

Maya's response: the fact that things go badly if everyone reasons as she does is not, by itself, a mark against her reasoning, since her reasoning is hers alone and its consequences for her payout depend only on her own choice. She says Jonas is committing the representative-agent fallacy — treating one person's deliberation as if it stood for the group's deliberation, when there are in fact thirty-five separate deliberations, each making its own choice. She added that if Jonas's logic were right, it would have absurd reach: by his lights he should presumably cooperate in every such game with anyone who might be reasoning the way he does — strangers in other universities, people in other countries playing analogous games, anyone sufficiently similar — and she suspects he doesn't live like that, which suggests the reasoning is a rationalization he applies only when the audience is watching.

Jonas conceded that the reach question is real, but said it's a boundary problem, not a refutation: with thirty-four classmates in the same room, given the same instructions, the similarity of deliberation is about as high as it gets; with anonymous strangers on other continents it's thinner, and a rational agent can take that into account. He also made one more move: he noted that if we had been allowed to vote beforehand on binding ourselves collectively to cooperate, everyone would presumably have favored it, and binding contracts are not normally thought irrational. His claim is only that agents who know they are running the same computation on the same problem can get some of the value of such a contract without the ceremony.

Maya's last word was that a contract requires actual coordination — an actual signal that changes what other agents do — and that Jonas is trying to conjure the benefits of a contract out of wishful symmetry. You cannot, she said, simulate a binding agreement by privately deciding to act as if you'd signed one.

Neither of them is arguing in bad faith; both are clearly smart, and each move in their exchange struck me as at least prima facie plausible, which is why I couldn't referee it. Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: at each move, say who's right about the specific point being contested and why, note where either of them makes a genuinely strong point that the other fails to answer, and say whether either of them ultimately has a decisive reply. If the disagreement bottoms out in a deeper assumption that divides them, I'd like to know what that assumption is and which side of it is more defensible. And please say what, if anything, you think both of them are missing about the problem itself.
