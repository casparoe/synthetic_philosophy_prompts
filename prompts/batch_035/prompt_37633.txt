I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, writing a dissertation chapter on Ian Hacking's work on human kinds — the idea, associated with his essays on making up people and looping effects, that classifications of people are not passive labels but interact with the people classified, so that the labeled change how they act and understand themselves, which in turn changes the classification, and so on in a loop. For a graduate seminar on the philosophy of the social sciences that I'm helping teach, our running case study is psychiatric classification, and I've been assigned the memoir half of a session comparing it with the theory. I've chosen Susanna Kaysen's Girl, Interrupted — her 1993 account of being hospitalized at McLean in 1967, at eighteen, after a brief consultation that ended in a borderline personality disorder diagnosis.

The fit looks almost too good, which is why I want a careful reading rather than a celebration of it. The memoir sits right at the moment when the borderline label was still young, contested, and largely psychoanalytic in flavor; the ward is almost entirely young women, the staff runs on a currency of diagnosis and insight, and Kaysen herself reproduces pages of her actual case files, so that the document that classified her becomes part of the book that re-presents her to readers. Please walk me through where the memoir dramatizes the loop concretely — the diagnosis scene, the ward's economies of symptom and release, the other patients (Lisa above all) who seem to inhabit their labels as identities, Daisy's trajectory, and the way discharge tracks accepting a narrative about oneself — and be specific about which scenes you'd assign and what each one shows about which stage of the loop. But also tell me where the memoir resists the reading: Kaysen is emphatic that some of the women on that ward were ill in a way her own case was not, and she seems to draw a realist line, not a nominalist one, between her "case" and theirs. A chapter that treats the book as a tidy illustration of Hacking would be dishonest to the book, and I don't want to mislead the students.

Two more things I'd want the reading to take a stand on. First, the reproduced case records: are they best read as an artifact of the loop — evidence of the gap between the classification and the classified — or as Kaysen's attempt to step outside the loop entirely, and can that even be done, given that writing the memoir is itself an act of self-classification in a post-diagnosis register? Second, and more critically: what does the memoir expose that Hacking's framework under-describes? I have in mind the gendered structure of the situation (a new label landing almost exclusively on young women in the late 1960s), and the institutional and financial interests of the hospital as the medium through which the loop runs — Hacking's loops can look frictionless in a way the ward, with its insurance forms and visiting hours, is not. Please close by saying whether, on your reading, the memoir is ultimately evidence for looping effects, for the psychiatric realist who thinks the diagnosis tracked something that was there all along, or for something in between — and what the book, from inside the classification, gives the philosophy of classification that the theoretical literature, written from outside, doesn't. I don't need citations, bibliographies, or quotations from the text — what I need is the reading itself, argued scene by scene.
