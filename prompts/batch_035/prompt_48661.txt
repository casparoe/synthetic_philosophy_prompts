Consider the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: for three or more alternatives, any onto, non-dictatorial voting rule is manipulable, in the sense that some voter with known preferences could gain by misreporting them. My political theory seminar has split on what this actually shows. One faction treats it as the voting-theoretic analogue of the second law of thermodynamics — strategy is baked into collective choice, so "sincere voting" is a fantasy and we should design systems that degrade gracefully under manipulation instead of pretending to prevent it. The other faction thinks the theorem is nearly empty in practice: it assumes voters know everyone else's preferences well enough to compute a profitable lie, it assumes ordinal preferences when real voters have cardinal intensities, and it assumes an unrestricted domain when real electorates arguably cluster around structured, single-peaked-like preference profiles where median-voter-style rules are genuinely strategy-proof.

I'd like your credence that real, large-scale elections with three-plus serious alternatives are inescapably manipulable in a way that matters normatively — call that proposition M. Don't give me "it depends on the electorate"; give me a number and a decomposition into the sub-claims that M factors into. As I see it, at minimum there are: (1) the probability that realistic preference profiles in actual democracies violate the domain restrictions that would let a non-dictatorial strategy-proof rule exist; (2) the probability that, even where manipulation is theoretically possible, the information and coordination costs make it rare enough that "manipulable in principle" and "manipulated in practice" come apart; (3) the probability that strategic behavior under uncertainty (e.g., voters heuristically deserting third parties, which is manipulation-adjacent) is itself the normatively relevant phenomenon even if no voter ever computes a lie; and (4) whatever weight you give to the view that Gibbard-style impossibility results for cardinal or randomized rules (Gibbard's own later theorem) close off the escape routes the ordinal theorem seems to leave open. Assign each a rough probability with one line of justification, combine them into a number for M, and tell me which term the number is most sensitive to. Then tell me which of my two seminar factions you think is making the mistake that actually drives their disagreement.
