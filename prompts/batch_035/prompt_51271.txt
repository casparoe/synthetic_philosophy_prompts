I'm part of a year-long working group, half philosophers of science and half social psychologists, that has spent two semesters on the so-called gender-equality paradox — the family of findings, going back to Stoet and Geary's 2018 study of STEM degrees and Falk and Hermle's 2018 study of economic preferences, that between-country differences in various gendered outcomes (occupational and educational choices, self-reported preferences, values) tend to be *larger*, not smaller, in countries that score higher on gender-equality indices. We've reached a genuine impasse, and I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement we're actually having, because I've stopped believing it's the disagreement we think we're having.

Here is how the group has lined up. One faction reads the cross-national pattern as evidence about underlying nature: whatever one thinks about magnitudes, the direction of the gradient is what you'd predict if robust sex-typed dispositions exist and are most fully expressed where constraining social forces are weakest. The second faction reads the same pattern as evidence about culture: affluent, individualistic societies don't simply remove old constraints, they actively manufacture new gendered pressures — through differentiated parenting, occupational cultures, marketing, identity signaling — and the correlation reflects a third variable, wealth plus individualism, doing the work of both. A third faction, smaller but I think important, holds that the whole framing is confused because "gender equality" as measured by composite indices is dominated by formal, institutional indicators (law, representation, education gaps), while occupational self-sorting responds to informal cultural conditions, so the correlation may be an artifact of what the index happens to weight; a fourth member counters that this can't be the whole story, since the pattern shows up with outcome variables measured at the individual level with instruments that have nothing to do with institutional equality.

What has struck me most is how little any of this has moved anybody. My working hypothesis is that at the cross-sectional level the two main explanations are close to empirically indistinguishable — "constraints removed, dispositions express themselves" and "new constraints generated, dispositions manufactured" both predict exactly the gradient we observe — so the group is really arguing about prior probabilities assigned to the two models, and dressing that up as an argument about the data. And I suspect the third faction's point, if pushed, would dissolve into a verbal dispute about what "a more gender-equal society" even refers to, which is a dispute about the extension of a phrase rather than about the world.

So my questions are these. First, is my diagnosis right, or is there a real empirical or conceptual question hidden here that our discussions have simply failed to isolate? Second, if it is a dispute about priors, is that a defect of the debate or a normal and acceptable feature of it — and what kind of evidence could legitimately move priors of this kind? Third, what would actually count as progress: some individual-level or longitudinal design that discriminates the two explanations, a decomposition of the composite indices into institutional and informal components, the within-country replication someone proposed using regions of a single large country as the units of analysis, or a more careful treatment of what the "paradox" is a paradox *of* — since it seems paradoxical only relative to one specific prediction, and maybe that prediction, not the finding, is what deserves scrutiny? Finally, is there a reading on which the philosophical payoff is independent of the empirical resolution — for instance, about whether justice requires gender-similar occupational distributions at all?

I should say upfront that I'd like candor rather than diplomacy. Tell me which of our factions, if any, is asking a well-posed question, and where appropriate say plainly that a disagreement is about taste or ideology rather than evidence. Also indicate how confident you are in each of your claims, and identify the single point in your diagnosis that you're least sure of.
