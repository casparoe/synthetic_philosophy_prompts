I am halfway through a novel whose central family is Hungarian, and a disagreement has broken out between my editor, who has shepherded the manuscript through two drafts, and my first reader, a cousin who lectures in sociology and to whom I send chapters as I finish them. The manuscript leans fairly heavily on national character: the father is written as belonging unmistakably to a certain old-world type — proud, formal, voluble in grief, exacting about soup — and individual scenes are built on the reader recognising the type and then watching it bend. My editor wants that scaffolding kept and strengthened; my cousin wants it struck out and replaced by, in her words, characterisation that happens to be Hungarian the way a person happens to be left-handed. Their argument, as best I can reconstruct it, ran as follows.

My editor opened by saying that fiction has always traded in national types, that Dickens and Trollope built whole comic engines out of them, and that a novel is not a sociology paper: readers arrive knowing that a statement like "the French are sentimental about food" is a generic rather than a quantifier, so no one is deceived, and the generic earns its keep by doing narrative work no list of individual traits could do. My cousin replied that this concedes her point: a generic that survives every counterexample by reclassifying it as an exception is unfalsifiable, and a category whose members cannot falsise it by their behaviour is not a description of anything — it is a picture we hold up to the world and then marvel that the world resembles it, which is precisely how 19th-century travel writing manufactured the national characters my editor now treats as raw material. My editor answered that the same charge lands on ordinary trait talk, and no one wants to abolish it: "she is shy" also admits of exceptions, also came from somewhere, and also gets applied by observers primed to see it — if trait ascription is legitimate at the level of individuals, and cross-cultural psychology does find stable mean differences between populations in things like norms of queuing or directness of speech, it is arbitrary to forbid the same inferential move one level up. My cousin responded that the two levels are not parallel, because a person is a relatively fixed bundle whose dispositions are anchored in a single nervous system, whereas a nation is a shifting political container: the borders moved, the populations intermarried, the institutions were rebuilt, and a claim that fit "the Hungarians" of 1870 is not a claim about anyone now, so there is nothing there for the character to be a character of — the move from population means to national character is an ecological fallacy dressed as a trait. My editor's last word was that even if all that is true, it is true of sociology and not of the novel: characters are individuals in any case, the generalisation is scaffolding for the reader rather than a thesis about the world, and verisimilitude is a reader-expectation, not an assertion. My cousin closed by saying that this was the most dangerous move of the lot, since scaffolding is precisely what gets left standing in the reader's head after the building comes down.

I have deliberately not said who I find more persuasive, and I would rather not imply it here. Please adjudicate their exchange as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: go through the argument in order, say who is right or partly right at each stage, and if there is something both of them are missing, make it one of the five.
