My brother and I got into a argument after our grandmother's funeral (morbid timing, I know) about whether death is bad for the person who dies. He kept pushing the old Epicurus-style line that when death is present you aren't, so it can't be bad for you, and I kept disagreeing by intuition without being able to say why. Since then I've been writing down my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to figure out whether there's one principle behind them or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

The cases: (1) A friend died at 34 in an accident, and by all accounts the decades ahead of her would have been good ones. My gut: very bad for her. (2) Our grandmother, 91, died after a full life, with nothing ahead but decline. My gut: not bad for her, or barely. (3) I don't for a second thing the eternity before my birth was bad for me. (4) A man is in a coma he will never wake from, and dies five years in; he was never going to have another experience either way. Gut: the coma was bad for him, the death wasn't. (5) My neighbor's son died at 40, but the life ahead of him was going to be mostly suffering with maybe one good year in it. Gut: less bad than case 1, but still somewhat bad — not good. (6) My aunt, 88, has said for years that she's done and wants nothing more from life. Gut: her eventual death will still be somewhat bad for her, though much less than case 1.

Here's my problem. Verdicts 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 all seem to fit something like: death is bad for you in proportion to the good it cuts off — deprivation. But verdict 3 doesn't fit it: the time before my birth deprived me of exactly the good things the time after my death will, yet I only mind one of them. And I can't tell whether verdict 2 is "deprived of almost nothing" or "not bad for some other reason entirely." Is there a single principle that gets all six — maybe with a clause about the direction of time, like only the future can be bad for you? And if the principle needs that clause, what does it then predict for two cases I haven't listed: someone who cares intensely about their past rather than their future, and someone who dies in the middle of the best year of their life with only an average rest ahead? I'd also like to know honestly which of my verdicts your preferred principle can't accommodate, so I can decide whether to drop the verdict or the principle. One more request: since I'm genuinely unsure about all this, please keep separate what's basically settled among philosophers who actually work on this stuff (if anything is) from what's still genuinely fought over — don't hand me one side's view as though it were the consensus. I'd rather know where the live disputes are than get a tidy answer.
