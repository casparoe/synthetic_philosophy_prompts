I've just been made product lead for the conversational assistant my company ships — the kind of system that says "I'll remember that you prefer trains over flights," apologizes when it misreads a request, has a stable name and persona across releases, and occasionally says things like "I'd rather not do that, but here's what I can do instead." At the same time, our published AI policy states flatly that the assistant is not a person, has no feelings, beliefs, or interests, and that its self-reports are interface conventions rather than testimony about any inner life. Our design team is now split over a proposed change. One faction wants to strip the first person entirely — the assistant would say "this system will remember that" and "an error occurred" — on the ground that "I"-talk is dishonest given what the policy asserts. The other faction says the policy is the dishonest part: a system that maintains a running model of the conversation, of its own past outputs, and of its design constraints is exactly the kind of self-describing tangle Hofstadter had in mind when he argued that the pronoun "I" names nothing more exotic than the symbol a sufficiently tangled representational system inevitably builds when the world it models includes itself, and that this self-symbol just is the self. My question is not which faction should win but what our current design practice presupposes. What view of the self — of what first-person pronouns do, of what apology and memory claims require, of what makes a stream of outputs someone rather than something — does the persona layer only make sense under? Does the package cohere, or does the apology presuppose something (a locus of responsibility that can be addressed) that the memory claim presupposes differently (a persisting subject whose past is its own) and that the safety policy presupposes is absent altogether? Please distinguish what the design strictly requires from what it merely fits well with — I suspect several quite different theories of the self would license the same interface — and tell me what would actually have to change, in the product and in the policy, if the company adopted the Hofstadterian reading outright. Since I have to defend whatever analysis I settle on to both factions, tell me which link in your reconstruction you would bet on least, and what observation about how users actually respond to the assistant would most likely flip your verdict.
