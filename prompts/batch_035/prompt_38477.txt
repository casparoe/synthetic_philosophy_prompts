You are an expert in classical Islamic philosophy and in contemporary philosophy of mind, asked to reason carefully about a counterfactual. I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the afterlife of Avicenna's flying man argument in current debates about the minimal self, and I want a well-argued prediction, not a survey.

Suppose Avicenna were transported to the present and shown two bodies of experimental work. First, full-body ownership illusions: a subject wearing a head-mounted display sees a virtual body, or a distant physical body, being stroked in synchrony with their own, and afterwards reports having felt located in, or embodied as, that other body. Defenders of these experiments often present them as showing that the bodily self is not given prior to perception but constructed out of multisensory integration. Second, patients who have lost nearly all bodily sensation from the neck down yet report a continuous, vivid sense of existing — a kind of lived confirmation of the flying man's claim that self-awareness does not depend on proprioception or touch.

Take on these tasks:

1. Predict Avicenna's considered reaction to each body of work. For the illusions, the key question is whether they threaten the flying man's conclusion that self-awareness is independent of sense perception, or whether he could accommodate them by distinguishing the bare existence of self-awareness from its contingent, sensory-given content — after all, the subject who is fooled about *where* they are is still aware of the one being fooled. For the patients, ask whether he would treat them as allies or as irrelevant, since they retain memory, interoception, and thought, while his argument needs self-awareness to survive the loss of *all* experiential input.

2. Predict his response to a related objection he never faced: dreamless sleep and anesthesia seem to involve intervals with no self-awareness whatsoever. Would he treat subjects' post-waking reports of an empty interval as evidence of an absence of awareness, or merely of a failure to remember awareness that was present — and does the flying man argument itself give him a principled rather than ad hoc answer to this?

Say where you think his final position would land on the contemporary map — closer to the view that the self is given prior to all perception, or closer to the view that it is a construct of bodily processing — and flag explicitly which parts of your prediction are underdetermined by what we know of his actual commitments, rather than pretending the texts decide everything.
