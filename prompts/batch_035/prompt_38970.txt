I chair the incident review panel at a mid-sized regional hospital network. We've had an autonomous triage and sepsis-alerting system in production for two years, and every time it's implicated in a near-miss or an actual bad outcome, the panel meeting turns into the same fight: legal wants a name for the file, the clinicians point at the engineers, the engineers point at the training data, and the people who curated the training data have left. I read somewhere that this is called a "responsibility gap" — when an autonomous system does something harmful and you can't pin it on any human, because nobody intended it, nobody could reasonably have foreseen it, and the machine isn't the kind of thing you can blame. There's also apparently an older thing called the problem of many hands, where a harm comes out of a pile of individually reasonable decisions by different people. I think our situation is some mix of both, and I want a procedure, not a philosophy seminar.

So: write me a decision procedure the panel can actually run in one two-hour meeting on a single incident. The panel is me (administrator, chair), a senior nurse, the clinical engineer who maintains the system, a quality officer, and someone from legal who mostly wants names. None of us our philosophers. The procedure should:

- Lay out the questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each — especially some way of testing whether responsibility traces back to an identifiable human decision point (a moment where a person had the relevant knowledge, a real choice, and no good excuse) versus dissolving into many hands versus being a genuine gap.
- Keep two questions seperate on purpose: the backward-looking one (who, if anyone, is at fault) and the forward-looking one (who has to remediate, monitor, and make the patient whole regardless of fault). Legal is going to try to collapse these and I need the procedure to make the collapse look wrong, not just declare it wrong.
- Mark the points where the procedure runs out and a human judgment call has to take over — and be honest about which of those are questions of fact we can't settle and which are questions of value we shouldn't dress up as facts.

Then run it on three cases from our last year (anonymized):

1. A patient deteriorated after the system scored her low-risk for about 14 hours. A vital sign had been double-entered during a shift handoff, and everyone in that chain did something locally reasonable — it's the kind of slip a human doing the triage by hand would also have made at 3am.
2. A nurse, with supervisor sign-off, had configured the alerting to snooze repeats after a run of what everyone agreed were nuisance alarms. Three weeks later a genuine escalation got snoozed along with the noise. The nurse did exactly what the policy she was working under told her to do.
3. The system behaved exactly as designed, but the design had been tuned on a population that doesn't include patients like this one — I can't say more, but it's a real case — and nobody in the design chain did anything wrong by the standards of their own job.

For at least one of these I expect the honest output to be "nobody is blameworthy, but here is who owes what going forward." That's the answer the board will hate — they want a name for the file — so if that's where the procedure lands, say so plainly, and tell me what the procedure can and can't do to protect us from the pressure to manufacture fault.

Also: if the procedure has an obvious failure mode — like making it too convenient to declare a gap whenever blame would be awkward — flag that and build in whatever check you can against it.
