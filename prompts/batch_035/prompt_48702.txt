I've read basically everything on gwern and Scott Alexander and the tail end of the LessWrong-adjacent psychiatry discourse, and I keep noticing we argue past each other about DSM categories without ever saying what we're asking of them. So before I read yet another "is depression one thing or five things" thread, I want the spec written down.

Write the desiderata: what an adequate taxonomy of mental disorders has to deliver. I'm not asking for a survey of DSM vs ICD vs HiTOP vs RDoC — I want the requirements themselves, each with the case or phenomenon behind it. Things it has to get right, like the fact that diagnoses predict treatment response only meh, or that comorbidity is the norm rather than the exception, or that two patients with the same label can share basically zero symptoms. Distinctions it can't collapse, like reliability (clinicians agreeing on the label) versus validity (the label carving anything real), or "clinically useful" versus "biologically natural." And the weird one that psychiatric classification doesn't share with, say, mineral classification: Ian Hacking's looping effects. The categories are interactive — people read their diagnosis, identify with it, reorganize their inner life around it, symptom checklists get memorized and fed back, and the classified population literally changes in response to being classified. Whatever else a taxonomy needs, it needs some story about operating on a moving target that it is itself moving.

For each desideratum say what makes it a genuine requirement versus just a preference of one camp (the biologically-minded people want external validators, the pragmatists want clinical actionability, and they keep treating their own preference as a hard constraint). Then the part I actually care about: do you think some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable? Reliability wants crisp checklists and fixed thresholds, but crisp checklists plus looping effects means the crispness itself degrades the validity over time, since people game the threshold and the population migrates. And note that asking for stability in the categories conflicts with the fact that our concept of, e.g., autism has already been reshaped twice by the people it classified.

Don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick — commit. Tell me which single requirement you'd drop first if forced, which one you think does the most work excluding real contenders, and whether you'd build the taxonomy around natural kinds at all or around something like treatment-relevance clusters that accepts it'll be rewritten every decade on purpose. If you think the looping effects point is overrated, say that too, but argue it. A typo or two I'll forgive, hedging I won't.
