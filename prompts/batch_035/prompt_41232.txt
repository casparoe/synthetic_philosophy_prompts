You are an expert epistemologist with a particular interest in the post-Moorean literature on skepticism, and I need your careful, step-by-step help diagnosing an argument. Please think through each step on its own before committing to a verdict.

Context: in my epistemology reading group we just finished Moore's proof of an external world — the one where he holds up his hands, remarks that each of them is a thing outside his mind, and concludes that external things exist. Moore himself granted that the proof, though it starts from premises he knew and proceeds by obvious steps, might not manage to convince the skeptics it was aimed at. That concession is where my friend pounced.

His argument:

(1) Moore's proof is a genuine proof: its premise is something he knew outright, without any inference, and its conclusion follows by steps anyone can verify.
(2) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if you know p, and you know that p entails q, then you know q.
(3) So in raising his hand and noticing the entailment, Moore came to know that he was not a handless brain in a vat being fed hand-experiences.
(4) There is nothing special about hands here. The recipe is fully general: any proposition you know without inference, combined with any of its entailments, yields a proof.
(5) My aunt claims to know that God exists by direct religious experience — no inference, no argument, just something she says she knows in exactly the way Moore knew he had a hand. From the inside, her certainty is subjectively indistinguishable from his.
(6) If (4) is right, her conviction licenses her to prove everything her creed entails — that miracles happen, that naturalism is false — from a premise she knows as directly as Moore knew his premise.
(7) So either Moore's proof was never really a proof, or my aunt can prove that God exists; and since nothing distinguishes her from any other confident non-inferential believer, proof has collapsed into an inflated name for whatever follows from something you feel sure of.

He says the moral is that Moore's proof proves too much: the recipe that was supposed to humble the brain-in-a-vat skeptic also hands a proof-licensing machine to every dogmatist. I can't accept (7), but every step looks defensible, and my attempts to break the argument keep running into the same wall: if I deny that my aunt knows her premise, I can't explain what distinguishes my judgment from Moore's, given that I have only the inside view to go on for either of us.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I would most like to keep (2), closure, and my conviction that Moore really did know he had a hand. If keeping both of those forces me to accept (6), tell me plainly, and tell me exactly what price I pay wherever I finally jump.
