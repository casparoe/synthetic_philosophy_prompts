I'm a novelist, and I'm near the end of a book in which the protagonist's wife dies after a long illness. The chapter I'm drafting is the vigil: he sits beside her body through the night, and I have to decide what happens in him. My editor, who studied classical Chinese philosophy in graduate school before she went into publishing, has been pushing me toward serenity in this scene. Her touchstone is the famous anecdote about Zhuangzi: when Zhuangzi's own wife died, his friend Huizi came to condole and found him drumming on a tub and singing. Challenged about it, Zhuangzi is supposed to have explained that his wife had emerged from the formless, passed through life, and now gone on through death just as the seasons run their sequence; to weep and wail over it, he suggested, would show that he did not understand destiny. My editor takes this as a directive: the widower should move briskly from shock to acceptance, and any sustained grief I portray would read, as she puts it, as a philosophical failure on the character's part.

Before I restructure the chapter around that, I want the analogy itself audited rather than admired. Please lay out what maps onto what: the progression of the seasons is presumably meant to correspond to the progression from formlessness through life to death, and the turn from winter to spring to whatever comes next — but I want to see the correspondence made explicit, including what in the target the parts of the source quietly import. Seasons are cyclical and return; a particular life is not. Seasons are regular, expected, and no one's responsibility; the death of a wife is a singular event in a shared history. The seasons analogy seems to reclassify a person as a phase of an impersonal process — which may be exactly what deflates the death, but I'd like to know whether that reclassification is the argument or merely a picture standing in for one. Then tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for the use my editor wants to make of it: I can see how the analogy might support the claim that death is not an injustice and need not be railed against, but I cannot yet see how it gets her from there to the claim that grief itself is a confusion. If those are two different conclusions, I need to know which one the scene should embody — and whether some better analogy (or none) would carry the weight the seasonal one is being asked to carry.

One further consideration, and then a request about your own reasoning. In the story, Zhuangzi seems to concede that he wept at first, before he steadied himself and saw the matter whole — and I've noticed that my instinct is to make that concession do a lot of work, since it appears to license my widower his night of grief after all. I'm not sure that instinct isn't sentimental special pleading on my part, and I'd like your view on whether the initial weeping is a genuine crack in the analogy or an edifying flourish. More generally: tell me what would change your verdict about whether the analogy can support my editor's conclusion — which reading of Zhuangzi, or which feature of grief or of the seasons, would flip it — and point to where in your reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong. I would rather restructure the chapter on the strength of an argument I can see the stress points of than on the authority of a beautiful story.
