I am a philosopher finishing a position statement on the epistemic foundations of game theory — specifically, on the status of the common prior assumption — for a workshop volume on rational disagreement. Before I circulate the draft, I want an audit of whether the package of views I have settled on actually hangs together. Here is the package:

(1) I reject the Harsanyi doctrine. Differences in prior probabilities are not, in general, to be explained by differences in information. A prior encodes inductive temperament and methodological commitment, and two agents with the same total evidence can rationally hold different priors; at the level of priors, rational permissivism is correct.

(2) Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem is sound, and I take its conclusion to be normatively significant rather than a technical curiosity: it is no accident of the model that agents with a common prior, whose posteriors are common knowledge, end up with identical posteriors.

(3) I accept, as an independent normative datum, that two agents who are fully rational and who know each other to be fully rational cannot agree to disagree — cannot maintain divergent credences while knowing that they do.

(4) Rational credences are fully manifested in betting dispositions. A rational agent with credence p in a proposition will accept some bets whose odds are favorable by the lights of p, provided the stakes are moderate and the counterparty is not exploiting private information the agent lacks.

(5) I take the no-trade results in the tradition of Milgrom and Stokey as substantive claims and not merely as conditional theorems: rational, risk-neutral agents with common knowledge of their situations cannot profitably trade on differences of opinion alone.

(6) I hold a strong conciliationist view about discovered disagreement between epistemic peers: on discovering that a peer with the same evidence assigns a different credence to a proposition, each agent should move substantially toward the other's position.

My request is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the commitments pairwise and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are — I do not want to make revisions I don't need, so please be explicit about which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why. For each genuine tension, rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, say exactly what would have to give, and what accepting the revision would cost me dialectically (e.g., which other commitment or motivation it would undercut, and what view I would then be under pressure to abandon or reframe). Finally, tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up most of the problem, and if so, which one.
