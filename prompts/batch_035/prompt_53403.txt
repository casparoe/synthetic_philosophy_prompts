I teach a module on philosophy of science to fourth-year engineering students, and I've been given a single forty-minute slot to introduce the special composition question — not "what things exist?" but "when do some things add up to, i.e. compose, one further thing?" The students are comfortable with talk of systems, emergence, and holism, which I suspect will cut both ways. Please plan the session for me rather than giving me the content. Specifically, I need: (1) the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what each step depends on — in particular, how to get them to see, before any candidate answer is presented, that the question is neither "do tables exist?" nor "when are we permitted to call something a table?", since they will otherwise hear the whole issue as a verbal bookkeeping dispute about counting; (2) the one carrying example that should anchor the whole session, which must include some things that clearly compose something, some things that clearly do not, and a middle case where engineering intuition runs out — and please also tell me why the Ship of Theseus would be the wrong carrying example, since it tempts persistence-over-time questions rather than composition questions and I expect someone to raise it; (3) the misconceptions they will predictably form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off — I anticipate at least: composition confused with physical attachment or contact (two glued bricks), composition confused with spatial containment (the objects in a room), composition confused with functional integration (the "system" reflex from their engineering training), and a no-artefacts answer such as van Inwagen's being heard as the claim that tables are illusions rather than a substantive thesis about when composition occurs; (4) one question I can pose at the end that would reveal whether they grasp the question itself, not just one answer to it; and (5) what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I assume the formal machinery of mereology and the "composition as identity" view — and why saying so explicitly is better than silently omitting it.
