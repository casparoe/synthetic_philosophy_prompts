My partner and I bought our first house this spring, and the whole process has left me unsure whether my standing views about decision-making still form a coherent package. Before I go around dispensing advice to the next friend who house-hunts, I'd like an audit of whether these five commitments hang together:

1. I accept bounded rationality as the right frame: no one can survey and compare all available options with all their consequences, so the rational procedure for creatures like us is Herbert Simon's satisficing — set an aspiration level, search until an option clears it, take that option, and stop searching. I think this is not merely excusable but often the rational thing to do, not a concession we should apologize for.
2. At the same time, I think there is usually a fact of the matter about which option in a given situation is best, and that if I pass over an available better option and take a worse one, my outcome is worse and I want to be told so. Satisficing, on my view, explains a stopping rule; it doesn't redefine what counts as better.
3. In sequential search where you cannot go back — which is exactly what house-hunting was, since houses come off the market while you deliberate — I accept the optimal-stopping analysis: observe without committing for some initial stretch, then take the first option that beats everything seen so far. Looking back, I think it was this structure, and not any fixed aspiration level of mine, that actually governed when we made an offer. I don't know what to say about whether that makes me a satisficer who got lucky or something else entirely.
4. I treat post-decision regret as information about decision quality. After we closed, I learned that a friend got a marginally better house for less money, three streets over, and my regret about that seemed to me like a rational signal that my aspiration level had been set too low — something to update on, not just an unpleasant feeling to be managed away.
5. I also believe, on the basis of the psychological literature contrasting maximizers with satisficers (Barry Schwartz's line of work), that the standing disposition to keep searching for the best makes choosers less satisfied and more regretful even when they objectively do better, and that this is a reason to cultivate satisficing as a character trait rather than deploy it as a mere tactic.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like it? For each real tension, tell me what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me elsewhere. Then rank them by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and if there's a single revision that would clean up the most, say which one. I'm especially interested in whether my regret, as described in (4), can really be evidence of anything, given that on my own account in (3) a well-designed search policy is one that will sometimes be beaten by luck — and given that if (5) is right, how much one regrets may track a disposition rather than a decision.

A last request about the response itself: as you go, indicate how confident you are in each major claim, and at the end single out the one point you are least sure of and explain why. I'd rather know where the ground is soft than receive the whole thing in the same confident tone.
