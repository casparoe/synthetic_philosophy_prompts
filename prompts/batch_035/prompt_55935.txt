I teach a mid-level philosophy of mind course (15 students, 75-minute seminar sessions), and in two weeks we reach a unit on free will built around what I think of as the engineering question: whether the difference between the digger wasp and us is a difference in kind — a spark of genuine freedom — or only a difference in the machinery, in better self-monitoring, wider repertoires, and subroutines able to notice when other subroutines have failed. The emblem for the unit is the classic wasp anecdote: she drags her paralyzed prey to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it outside, descends to inspect the nest, and returns to haul the prey in. If an experimenter moves the prey a few centimeters while she is below, she re-retrieves it — and then inspects the nest again. Move the prey each time, and by some accounts she loops indefinitely without once revising the routine. Hofstadter's coinage for this profile of condition-triggered, self-resetting rigidity is sphexishness, and I want the students to hit the phenomenon from the inside rather than read about it. One further complication should be in play: philosophers such as Fred Keijzer have noted that the anecdote's empirical basis is shakier than its fame suggests, with some observers finding adaptation after many iterations, and even the suggestion that repeated inspection of a disturbed nest may itself be adaptive — so the students' own experience in the activity may be the best data we have.

Design the in-class activity under these constraints:

- Ordinary seminar room, minimal materials, no advance preparation for the students. No lecture up front, and no use of the terms sphexishness or free will before the debrief.
- The core should be a staged cooperative task in which some students operate under rigid, externally imposed scripts — cards specifying condition-triggered routines that include restart triggers, i.e., events that reset the entire routine from the top. The perturbation schedule must be under the facilitator's control, so the design can reliably produce, in the scripted students, the experience of a loop they can fully see and are nevertheless required to keep executing.
- Flexibility should be introduced in graded tiers: for example, pure script; script plus a small, depletable supply of exception tokens; script plus the right to propose a new standing rule through some ratification procedure; and unconstrained discretion. The point is for students to experience flexibility as something added in increments, and then to have to ask whether each increment really differs in kind from the last.
- A payoff scheme in points, not money, in which rigidity is costly but overrides cost something too — flexibility as a scarce good with a price, so students feel the trade-off rather than simply preferring more of it.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical terrain: the script card as a stimulus-response routine, the restart trigger as the wasp's re-inspection loop, the exception token as something like meta-level monitoring, rule amendment as something like re-describing the situation. The debrief must force the question of whether the top-tier student's behavior differs in kind from the bottom-tier student's or merely in mechanism, and whether calling the top tier free while denying it to the bottom tier draws a principled line or merely hands out a flattering label. It should also return to the disputed wasp data: any students who adapted despite their scripts, and what that does to the anecdote's rhetorical role.
- Predict at least three things that will go wrong on the first run — scripts too confusing to execute, students slipping out of role, one tier dominating the others, and so on — and how the facilitator should handle each.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: scripted students are still humans who know they are in a game, can anticipate the facilitator, and can deliberate about the script even while following it, so the design stipulates rather than produces the absence of meta-monitoring. State plainly which parts of the free-will debate this leaves untouched and must be supplied in discussion.
