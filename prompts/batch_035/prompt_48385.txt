I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I write a small newsletter that uses everyday puzzles to explain probability and decision theory to a general audience. My next issue is on the paradox of voting: the rational-choice observation that a single vote's expected payoff — its minuscule chance of deciding the outcome, multiplied by even enormous stakes — is swamped by the modest cost of showing up, and yet turnout in real elections is high. I'd like to close the issue with a poem.

Write a villanelle — five tercets and a closing quatrain, the two refrains rotating through the ends of the tercets and meeting as the final couplet — from the point of view of someone standing in line at a polling station on election day. One refrain should voice the rational-choice position, and it must be arithmetically honest: the chance of one vote mattering really is astronomically small, so have the line gesture at the multiplication rather than assert anything false. The other refrain should voice the best version of the opposing position — duty, identification with the electorate, or the thought that the queue itself is the answer — not a strawman. The poem must not resolve which side wins. Somewhere in the middle tercets, the speaker should notice that the line is long, and that observation should do structural work as the turn: mass turnout is exactly the datum that keeps the two refrains circling back on each other. A political scientist should be able to read it without wincing.

Since a poem has no single verdict to extract, finish with one plain line after the poem, beginning "Summary:", that in a single sentence names the two refrains and says what the ending does with them.
