I'm a third-year PhD student in intellectual history, and every semester in our reading group on Jefferson someone deploys the "he was a man of his time" move, the room nods, and we move on. Last week it got used about Churchill AND about Columbus in the same afternoon and I realized I genuinely can't say what the sentence is claiming. Like, is it descriptive (essentially everyone in his milieu believed slavery was fine, so it's unsurprising he did too)? Is it normative (it's unfair to judge people by standards that weren't available to them)? Is it some excuse-adjacent thing about blameworthiness needing something like culpable ignorance? Or is it just a prediction dressed up as an excuse — given his environment, what else would he have thought? The annoying part is that the descriptive reading is often just false — abolitionists existed, Quaker petitions, people were literally making the arguments to Jefferson's face — and I can't figure out whether that falsity infects the normative reading or whether they come apart. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in "man of his time," say for each whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or straight-up false, and then tell me which reading is actually doing the work when someone uses the phrase to shut down a moral judgement of, say, Columbus? I want it as a bulleted list, one verdict per reading, that I can bring back to the reading group — one page max, don't pad it.
