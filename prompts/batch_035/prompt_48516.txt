My department has just assigned me to TA our introductory formal epistemology course, and the week on Dutch book arguments has become unexpectedly contentious. The textbook defends probabilism by treating credences as betting prices: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie can offer you a set of bets, each of which looks acceptable to you at your stated prices, but which together guarantee you a net loss; so, the story goes, incoherent credences are irrational for the same reason that knowingly accepting a sure loss is irrational. A student in my section pressed hard on this. She said the argument only works if she would actually accept each individual bet at her credence-price, and she wouldn't: she is risk-averse, money is not utility, and in any case there is no bookie in her life — nobody is ever going to offer her odds on most of the propositions she has credences about. My professor thinks the objection is confused and that it misses the point of the argument; I am honestly not so sure. Could you audit the analogy that is doing the work here? Lay out what maps onto what — credence to fair betting quotient, the agent to a gambler, irrationality to susceptibility to a sure-loss contract — and say where the mapping breaks. Then distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the justification of probabilism from those that merely complicate the presentation, and tell me whether the textbook's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the argument needs to be restricted (say, to idealized agents, or to utility denominated in something other than money) to carry its conclusion at all. If there is a better analogy for what incoherent credences are supposed to be like, I would like to hear it.
