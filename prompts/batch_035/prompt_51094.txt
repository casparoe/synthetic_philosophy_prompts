I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training -- the closest I've come is listening to podcasts where people throw around words like "emergence" and "reductionism" without defining them. But my day job keeps forcing me to make snap judgments about whether something is "really just" its parts or whether it's a genuine new thing sitting on top of them, and I've noticed my judgments don't obviously form a coherent picture. I'd like you to figure out whether they do. Here are the verdicts I find myself giving; tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and be willing to tell me some of them have to go.

1. The temperature of a gas is just the average kinetic energy of its molecules -- nothing over and above. If somebody told me temperature is "emergent," I'd assume they were misusing the word.

2. A phantom traffic jam on a circular track: each driver follows simple rules (brake when the car ahead is close, accelerate when there's room), and a jam forms that travels backward faster than any car moves, persists after every car that was in it has left it, and can be dissolved by one driver changing behavior. I'm confident this counts as emergent, even though in principle every fact about it is deducible from car-level rules plus initial conditions -- you could simulate it. So apparently something can be both deducible and emergent, which already makes me suspicious of my own word "emergent."

3. A glider in Conway's Game of Life moves across the grid, persists, collides with things -- and my gut says it's not emergent, just a convenient label for a pattern of cells. But structurally it looks just like the jam: it moves, it persists through complete turnover of its constituent cells, it interacts with other patterns. I cannot articulate what separates them. My tentative separator -- that the jam has autonomous dynamics you can model without tracking individual cars, the way traffic engineers write continuum equations for jam propagation, whereas the glider has no such macro-theory -- feels like it proves too much, since you can perfectly well give compressed descriptions of glider streams.

4. A Python program runs on ARM, on x86, through an interpreter, conceivably one day on some exotic mechanical contraption. I want to say the program is real and does real causal work, but it seems wrong to call it reducible (reducible to which machine-code listing?) and equally wrong to call it emergent (nothing surprising appears at runtime that isn't just the computation itself). So: real, not reducible, not emergent. I don't know what box that goes in, or whether there's a box.

5. When a cache-coherency race makes a payment system double-charge someone, I say the double-charge has a software-level explanation ("the read wasn't invalidated in time") that's better than the hardware-level one, where "better" means it supports the counterfactuals I care about: if we'd taken a lock, no double charge -- whereas the electron-level story makes counterfactual reasoning hopeless. But this makes me suspect reduction is only ever a claim about explanation, not about what exists -- and then I can't see what I meant in (1) by temperature being "nothing over and above" the molecules. If (1) and (5) can't both be kept, tell me which to keep.

6. The case I keep going back and forth on: if some large trained network ever turned out to have something honestly worth calling pain, I oscillate between "that would be the strongest kind of emergence there is -- no amount of inspecting weights would tell you pain was in there" and "that's just my ignorance of an extremely long deduction, like calling the jam mysterious because I can't track a million cars in my head." I'd genuinely like to be pushed off the fence on this one.

Deliverables: state the principle, or small set of candidate principles, precisely, clause by clause, with the verdict number that motivates each clause; say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of those should be abandoned; and apply it to at least one case I haven't listed -- candidates that occur to me are whether a market price is emergent from individual trades, or whether an ant colony's trail network is emergent from ants following pheromone gradients -- and say what the principle predicts there.

One request about epistemics: I know philosophers have fought about reduction and emergence for a long time without settling it, and I'd guess things like the deducible-yet-emergent move in (2) or the real-but-neither-reducible-nor-emergent move in (4) belong to particular camps rather than to settled consensus. Please mark clearly which parts of your answer reflect broad agreement among people who work on this and which parts are one school's position, and don't present a contested view as the default or as established fact.

Format: please end with a fixed, machine-readable final line, \answer{...}, containing at most about fifteen words naming the best principle (or the label of the best set, if no single one) plus the verdict number, if any, that it overturns -- something like \answer{emergence = autonomous macro-dynamics; overturn verdict 3} -- and put nothing after it.
