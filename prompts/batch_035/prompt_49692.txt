I am preparing teaching materials for a mid-level undergraduate course in philosophy of mind, and I would like your help constructing a diagnostic quiz on the knowledge argument — the family of responses to Frank Jackson's 1982 case of the color scientist confined to a black-and-white room who has mastered all the physical facts about color vision but, upon release, seems to learn something new about what red looks like. The quiz should be titled something like "Which response to the knowledge argument do you actually hold?" and is meant to be completed by students before they have read any of the secondary literature, so that it sorts them by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which label they find congenial. It should contain eight to ten questions, each a short scenario with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the standard positions: the dualist who holds that Mary acquires knowledge of a genuinely non-physical fact; the ability hypothesis associated with Lewis and Nemirow, on which she gains only know-how (imagination, recognition, and the like); Conee's acquaintance hypothesis, on which she gains familiarity rather than either a fact or a skill; the phenomenal-concept or new-mode-of-presentation strategy in the spirit of Loar, on which she learns an old fact under a new concept; Dennett-style deflationism, on which the case is underdescribed or she learns nothing that should surprise a completed physics; and Jackson's own later representationalist recantation. A hard constraint: no question may use any of these labels, and no question may ask directly whether the student is a physicalist — every question must be answerable from intuition about a case alone.

A second constraint concerns the cases themselves. Please do not simply recycle Mary: at most one question may involve the room. Draw instead on adjacent scenarios — a congenitally deaf acoustician who has read everything about audition, a supertaster trying to convey a novel flavor to someone who lacks the relevant receptor, a grandmaster explaining a chess position to someone who knows all the rules, someone who has memorized a complete functional specification of a sensory system and is then asked to imagine the corresponding experience — or invent comparably concrete cases of your own. The point of these is that the positions genuinely come apart on them, so design each question so that at least two of the leaves above would give different answers, and say in the scoring key which answers carry the diagnostic weight.

Finally, please build the quiz from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first fix the two or three questions whose answers jointly determine the tree — whether anything is gained, whether what is gained is propositional, and if propositional whether it concerns a new fact or an old fact under a new presentation — then locate the named positions as leaves in that space. Note any combination of answers that no standard position occupies (for example, someone who insists nothing is gained but that the nothing gained is a new proposition), with a sentence on whether that combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. And please flag honestly any pair of leaves the instrument cannot reliably separate by cases alone — my sense is that the ability and acquaintance hypotheses, and perhaps the phenomenal-concept and old-fact views, tend to collapse under this kind of testing, and I would rather the handout admit that than paper over it. The finished quiz with its key should fit on a single double-sided page.
