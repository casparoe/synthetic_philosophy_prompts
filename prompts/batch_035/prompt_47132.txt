I've been asked to circulate a short position statement ahead of a workshop on the ethics of blame, and I've slowly realized that my views here were assembled case by case over years of seminar arguments rather than chosen as a package, so before I send it around I want an audit of whether the pieces actually fit together. Here is the set. (1) I take standing to be a genuinely distinct normative category from the fittingness of blame: a particular act of blame can be entirely apt — proportionate, responsive to real wrongdoing, nothing overheated about it — and still wrong its target simply because of who is doing the blaming. (2) My explanation for why the hypocrite lacks standing is the fairness story: when I blame you for what I also do, I implicitly demand of you a standard I exempt myself from, and that implicit demand asserts a kind of superiority over you; the assertion is the wrong. (3) I think standing is recoverable in two ways: the hypocrite who first owns and blames her own similar conduct seems to get it back, and so, I think, does the hypocrite whom the target has explicitly licensed — if I tell a friend who also gossips that I know she does it too but she should please call me out when I do, her subsequent blame doesn't wrong me. (4) I hold that blame explained entirely by the blamer's personal stake in the outcome loses standing even without any hypocrisy: the neighbor who is vocally aggrieved when your double-parking blocks her car but has never once cared about double-parking that blocks anyone else's applies perfectly consistent standards to herself, yet something about her complaint strikes me as meddlesome in a way that undermines it. (5) At the same time, I'm fully committed to bystander standing: a total stranger with no stake and no connection has complete standing to blame a genuine wrong he happens to witness. (6) I'm firm that the reply 'you do it too' is a fallacy when offered as a rebuttal of a charge, since it does nothing to show the charge false or unfair — and yet, given (1) and (2), I also hold that the target of a hypocrite's blame is genuinely wronged and can legitimately demand that the blame stop. (7) Finally, I have the sense that standing generalizes well beyond resentment-style blame to criticizing, advising, even warning — but I'm equally firm that the doctor who smokes does not wrong his patient by telling him that smoking will kill him, even though the doctor exempts himself from precisely the standard he presses.

What I'd like is for you to go through this and tell me which pairs or triples are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and how badly. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs me elsewhere in the set — I'd rather not, say, quietly lose bystander standing or the meddlesome-neighbor verdict just to keep the fairness story tidy. And please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally fatal. One thing I'm especially unsure about is whether the two halves of (3) really stand or fall together: it feels like there's a difference between the hypocrite earning back standing by blaming herself and the target simply handing it over, but I can't articulate what grounds that difference, if it's real. I genuinely don't know which commitment I'd surrender first, so don't pull any punches on my behalf.
