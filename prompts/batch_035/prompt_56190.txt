I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and the adjacent rationalist blogs, and I've noticed that questions about causation keep resurfacing there in modern dress — Eliezer's essays on dissolving the question of free will, the simulation argument literature, debates about whether the laws of physics are prescriptive or merely a compressed description of observed regularities. While reading up on the history of these debates, I was surprised to find how much of the territory was already staked out in the eleventh century: Al-Ghazālī, in his Incoherence of the Philosophers, argued against the Aristotelian philosophers that there is no observable necessary connection between cause and effect at all. His famous example is fire touching cotton: nothing in our observation of the fire shows that it must burn the cotton, and the regular co-occurrence we see is a habit or custom of nature, sustained moment by moment by God's direct action, rather than a mechanism the fire carries in itself. What struck me is how close this is, structurally, to later worries: Hume's problem of induction, the modern point that physical laws as we write them down describe rather than govern, and quantum results that have made physicists uneasy about naive local causal pictures.

Here is what I'd like you to do. Please make a serious argument for the following position: that a naturalized Ghazalian occasionalism is a live metaphysical hypothesis rather than an obsolete one. By "naturalized," I mean the theological trappings stripped away and replaced with something like this picture: the universe's state is recomputed at each fundamental time step according to a fixed update rule, and what we call the laws of physics is just our best compressed description of that update rule — not a mechanism that pushes events around. On this view, the "fire" at one step and the "burning cotton" at the next are correlated by the rule but neither produces the other in a robust, trans-stage causal sense. Your argument should engage seriously with the standard objections — that this makes the regularities a staggering, constantly repeated brute fact; that the view is predictionally inert and empirically indistinguishable from ordinary causal realism, and therefore empty; and that it multiplies miracles beyond necessity. It should also say something about why Al-Ghazālī himself took the philosophers' necessary connection to be an assumption smuggled in rather than something demonstrated, and whether that epistemological point survives naturalization.
