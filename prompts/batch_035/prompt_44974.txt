Act as a forecaster and philosopher of science who specializes in diagnosing long-running disputes, not adjudicating them. Think carefully and do a bit of brainstorming before you commit to a diagnosis. My friend and I spent three hours last night re-running the old fast-vs-slow takeoff argument about the intelligence explosion and got exactly no where — which honestly matches how the real debate has gone for over a decade, with the same people having the same argument forever. Roughly: I took the slow side — capability gains come from accumulated R&D spread across the whole economy, growth is fairly smooth, and there's no mechanism for one project to secretly recurse on it's own improvements when everyone reads the same literature, compute gets cheaper for everyone at once, and algorithmic tricks diffuse fast. He took the fast side — the feedback loop is on the thing doing the research itself, so returns can hit a knee in the curve, and the question isn't the average pace of progress but the tail: how far can one system get before anyone catches up, and the usual outside-view smoothing of growth curves doesn't apply when we're inside the loop being improved. We both knew the standard moves going in and neither of us moved an inch. What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a hidden empirical dispute about the steepness of returns and spillovers that we're each filling in with different priors? A verbal one where "takeoff" or "recursive" means slightly different things to each of us? Two different questions — average growth rates versus the timing and variance of the one critical transition — smuggled under one word? Or bedrock intuitions (his: intelligence is qualitatively unlike other tech; mine: nothing in history has ever jumped like that) that neither side can argue for? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims, and be honest about whether anything would count as progress here — a sharper operationalization, a structural argument about feedback loops, or just admitting it's priors all the way down — or whether we should expect this one to stay permanently stuck.
