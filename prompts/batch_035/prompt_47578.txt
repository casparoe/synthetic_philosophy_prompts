I'm taking a graduate seminar on Scanlon's contractualism, and a classmate has put an argument to me that she claims shows the individualist restriction on complaints refutes itself, and I can't find where it goes wrong. Here it is. (1) An act is wrong if and only if it would be disallowed by some principle that no one could reasonably reject. (2) Whether a principle can be reasonably rejected depends only on the strength of the strongest single-person complaint against it, where a person's complaint is the burden that principle would impose on them; complaints do not sum across persons, and this is supposed to be what keeps the separateness of persons in view. (3) A lifeguard can save either one swimmer or five, all of whom will otherwise drown; the strongest complaint against a principle permitting her to let the five die is one death, and the strongest complaint against a principle permitting her to let the one die is also one death. (4) Since the strongest complaints on either side are exactly equal, neither principle can be reasonably rejected, and where the reasonable-rejection test cannot discriminate, contractualism neither requires nor forbids either choice. (5) So the numbers count for nothing even as a tiebreaker, which is just Taurek's view that the difference between one and five is in itself morally irrelevant -- a view almost no one accepts. (6) The same comparison run in the other direction says that a city may not condemn one family's home to supply running water to a million residents, since the family's complaint against that principle is larger than any single resident's complaint against the alternative. (7) So either (2) is false and complaints must be allowed to aggregate after all, in which case contractualism turns into a headcount of burdens and loses exactly what distinguished it from utilitarianism, or contractualism is refuted outright, since it shrugs at the five and forbids the reservoir.

I can't accept (7), but every step looks defensible to me individually. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I'd like to keep (2), since what drew me to the view in the first place is its refusal to trade one person's burden against anyone else's, and I also think the lifeguard who lets the five die does something wrong. Is there a reading of some step that lets me keep both, or am I forced to give one of them up -- and if so, which is the cheaper sacrifice?
