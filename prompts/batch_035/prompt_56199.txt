so I'm the registrar at a regional transport museum (trains, trams, a couple of boats) and we're finally rebuilding the accession database, which has forced the fight we've been dodging for decades: what goes in the "same object as acquired?" field. Every loan agreement and insurance valuation turns on whether the thing in the shed is the same object we accessioned in 1962, and right now the answer depends on which volunteer you ask. During this project we're doing maybe 15-20 of these a week, and a few of them feed a heritage listing bid where the phrase "substantially the original" is doing a lot of unexamined work. Nobody on my team has read a word of philosophy and I have final sign-off, no board involvement.

I've gathered there's an actual literature on this — the "statue and the clay" stuff: the statue and the lump of clay it's made of sit in the same place but seem to survive different things (squash the lump and it's still there, the statue isn't), so some philosophers say constitution is not identity and you really have two objects overlapping in one spot; others say that's a confusion and there's only one thing there with two descriptions; and a third camp holds that only the original matter matters, so swap out any part and it's not the same object anymore — which seems to mean Theseus's ship went out of existence plank by plank. None of it tells a working registrar what to do, which is what I need.

Write me a decision procedure the team can run on a record in a couple of minutes: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it outputs "same object" vs "not the same object," and where it has to stop and punt to me because the question isn't about facts anymore, it's about what the museum wants to count. Then run it on three records: (a) a locomotive whose boiler was replaced in the 70s, the original boiler preserved seperately under its own accession number — both records claim to be the locomotive; (b) a carriage where every plank got swapped piecemeal over sixty years with no single replacement event you can point to; (c) a boat whose hull is original but which sits on entirely new chassis and running gear as a static exhibit, wheels never turning again. One of these should come out "same object" even though the answer will make our heritage people unhappy — tell me which, and don't sugarcoat it. Also be honest about the places where my "procedure" is really just institutional policy wearing a philosophy costume, because I'd rather know that now than find out during the audit.
