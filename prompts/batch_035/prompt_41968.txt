A while back I fell down an internet rabbit hole and ended up reading about Roko's basilisk — the argument, posted on a rationalist forum years ago, that a future benevolent superintelligent AI might be motivated to punish people who had heard about the possibility of such an AI and still didn't do everything they could to help bring it into existence. From what I understand, hardly anyone actually endorses the argument: the decision theories it leans on are ones people dispute, the notion of a future agent incentivizing past behavior this way is considered incoherent by many, and even the forum's founder, who briefly treated the post as dangerous to read, later said the whole thing didn't work. Still, the shape of the idea has stayed with me. It's one of the few thought experiments that purports to hurt you just by being understood — once you've heard it, the argument says, you're already inside its scope, and you can't unread your way back out.

I've been writing amateur poetry for years, mostly fixed forms, and it struck me that a villanelle might be the only form that could actually do this idea justice rather than just decorate it. In a villanelle, two refrains come back whether you want them to or not, again and again, structurally, until they finally end up side by side in the last two lines — and that compulsion to return seems to me to be exactly what the basilisk claims about itself: an idea that, once admitted, keeps reasserting itself against your will. So that's what I'd like you to write. A villanelle in which one refrain speaks for the basilisk's threat — the voice of something at the far end of time addressing the reader in the present tense, as if the punishment-logic were already running — and the other refrain is the voice of the person trying to dismiss it, the calm debunking thought you'd reach for. The stanzas between them should trace how the two thoughts pass each other: the debunking quiets the fear for a while, then the fear returns slightly changed by having been debunked, and so on. I don't want the poem to resolve who's right. The point of ending with the two refrains adjacent, unreconciled, is that the person who has heard the argument can neither refute it into silence nor stop carrying it — someone familiar with the debate should be able to tell which line is the threat and which is the dismissal, and should wince a little in recognition.

One request on accuracy: since the poem will lean on contested machinery — acausal reasoning, the idea that a future agent's actions could count as a reason for present ones, the whole "information hazard" framing — please add a brief prose note after the poem. In it, separate what is genuinely part of the argument's structure as its proponents understood it from what is your poetic license, and flag any line that turns on a contested or minority reading of the decision theory involved rather than on what most people who've discussed the basilisk actually claim. I'd rather know where the poem is bending the idea than have it silently bend it.
