So some context: I've been a research analyst at an effective-altruist-aligned global health funder for about two years, and I got assigned to present at next month's grants comittee meeting the case for carving out maybe 10% of the portfolio for what I'm internally calling "institutional" grants — things like funding tenant unions in low-income housing, litigation capacity for gig workers, community organizing in the factory districts where our other grantees operate — instead of putting it all into the usual bednets/deworming/vitamin A bucket. The committee is six people: four are hardcore measurement types (two ex-GiveWell-style analysts who live and die by cost-per-DALY estimates and want an RCT behind everything, one health economist, one philosphy PhD who's sympathetic to person-affecting stuff), plus two major donors who made their money in tech and think entirely in expected value and "is this legible" terms. Nobody in that room will be moved by vibes, and frankly that's fair.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this specific room — not the generic anti-EA ones you'd get from a sociology department — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a measurement person would put it ("what's the denominator?", "who funds this if we don't?"), with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Cover at minimum: the measurability point (we fund what we can count, and this stuff can't be counted); the funging point, which I confess I always get backwards — if we skip the bednets someone else fills the gap, whereas our marginal dollar on a tenant union wouldn't be replaced, or is it the deworming side that gets crowded out? straighten me out on that; the accountability objection (this is just politics with donor money, and unlike a government we face no electorate); and the nastiest one, that even after you apply a big uncertainty discount, our own numbers still say the deworming dollar buys more welfare than the organizing dollar, so aren't I asking the org to knowingly do less good. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the version of "isn't this just you getting bored of bednets" that's actually hard to answer.

For what it's worth, my sketch of the argument is that the cost-effectiveness ranking between deworming and organizing isn't a fact we discovered but an artifact of what our measurement tools can see, and a portfolio restricted to legible interventions is like a map that only shows paved roads — you can drive it with total confidence and never learn where the villages are. Tell me if that sketch is actually weak, I'd much rather hear it from you than from the health economist mid-meeting.
