Quick background then the ask. I'm a journalist, I write about science and medicine for a mid-size paper, and for the better part of a decade one of my beats has been the national commission that advises hospitals on how to actually decide someone has died. They rule on cases, sort of like a court, and the hospitals mostly follow them. Next month they've got a doozy on the docket and my editor wants a prediction piece.

From my files, here's their track record so far:

- 2016: man on a ventilator, tests show no function anywhere in the brain, but his heart was still beating on its own. They ruled him DEAD.
- 2018: woman in a persistent vegetative state — brainstem working, breathing without help, sleep/wake cycles, but as far as anyone can tell nobody is home upstairs. NOT dead.
- 2019: anencephalic newborn (born with essentially no upper brain, brainstem intact). NOT dead.
- 2021: organ donation case — treatment withdrawn, the heart stopped, they watched five full minutes of flatline, then declared death. DEAD.
- 2022: same setup but the hospital protocol called for only two minutes of flatline; the doctor's note said nothing suggested the heart would kick back on its own. DEAD, no hesitation.
- 2023: an abstract question from a cardiac surgeon — patient on full heart-lung bypass for open-heart surgery, heart deliberately stopped for three hours, no pulse at all, the pump doing the entire circulation. Obviously ALIVE, they said, not even a close call.

Now the new case, which the transplant people are bringing. A donor, treatment withdrawn, the heart stops, two minutes of flatline, death is declared, family says goodbye. Then — declaration already signed — surgeons clamp off the arteries to the head so no blood can reach the brain, hook the body up to a heart-lung machine, and restore circulation everywhere below the clamp to keep the kidneys, liver etc. in good shape until they're recovered. And here's the kicker: during this perfusion the donor's own heart starts beating again. It's riding on the machine's flow, but it is beating, in the donor's chest, and it's slated to be the transplanted heart.

One commitee member says the whole thing exposes the declaration as a fiction — you can't be dead at 2:00 and have a beating heart in your chest at 2:15. The transplant team's line is that the patient died and stays dead, because blood flow to the brain was never coming back and that's what the brain death idea is really tracking. There's also apparently a fight brewing about whether the person was even dead at the moment of declaration, since at 2 minutes in, if they'd perfused the brain instead of clamping it off, they might have saved something.

So help me out. What are the live candidate rules that actually fit all six earlier rulings — not strawmen, rules a serious person on that commission could be running? Which of those rules split apart on this perfusion case, and what does each one predict about it? Where do you think they'll come down, and why? And if I wanted to figure out which rule they're really running — say, for the feature version of this story — what further, realistic case would cleanly tell the surviving rules apart? Be honest about where their record underdetermines things, because I'd rather write "the commission has to pick its principle" than pretend there's an obvious answer.

I'm on deadline friday, so alot of words is fine but no reading list — just the reasoning.
