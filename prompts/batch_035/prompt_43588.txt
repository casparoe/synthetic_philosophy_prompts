I've been going down a decision theory rabbit hole ever since a stats elective I took last semester, and last week I ran into something called the Pasadena paradox, and honestly it's kind of freaking me out. From what I understand, it's basically the St. Petersburg game with a twist: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, the payoff grows the longer you wait, but the payoffs alternate between gains and losses, so when you compute expected value as probability times payoff summed over all outcomes, the terms shrink but keep flipping sign. And because of that the total never settles — depending on how you order or group the terms, you can make the "expected value" of one and the same game come out to whatever number you want. If that's right, then expected value isn't really a property of a gamble at all, it's just an artifact of bookkeeping, and every EV figure I've ever been quoted is one rearrangement away from being a completely different number. (I tried arguing this with a friend who works in finance and she just said "diversify," which felt like dodging the question.)

So here's what I actually want to know. When I'm making ordinary decisions — picking between insurance deductibles, weighing a job with a variable bonus against a flat salary, that kind of thing — what's the standard correction for this? Is there some safety margin you're supposed to apply once a decision involves enough uncertain outcomes, or does that only kick in when the outcomes could be unbounded? Basically, at what point does an everyday decision become Pasadena-like enough that I should stop trusting the EV calcuation entirely, and what am I supposed to use instead?
