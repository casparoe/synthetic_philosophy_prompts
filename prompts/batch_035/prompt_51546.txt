I'm in a second-year ethics course and we've now spent three whole weeks on supererogation — acts that are good to do but not wrong not to do, like donating a kidney to a stranger or diving into a river to save a child at real risk to yourself. The debate in my seminar has completely stalled and I'm after a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict on who's right.

Here's the loop we're stuck in, concretely. One side (mostly the consequentialists, plus some of the readings) says: if doing A does more good than doing B, and there's nothing morally relevant that excuses picking B, then you're obligated to do A — so there just is no room for a category of "admirable but not required". The other side, which traces back to J. O. Urmson's 1958 paper on saints and heroes, says that inference begs the question: the entire point of the supererogatory is that obligation is a threshold notion rather than a maximizing one — you discharge your duty by reaching some level, and going past it is lovely but extra. And then the reply is always "no, YOUR principle begs the question against mine", and around we go. Apparently it's been roughly like this since the fifties, which is a bit depressing.

My working hypothesis (could be rubbish): the two sides aren't really disagreeing about supererogation at all — they're answering two different questions under one name. The consequentialists treat "obligated" as a verdict on which act to pick, whereas the threshold people treat it as a standard you can meet or fail, like a pass mark on an exam. If that's right the dispute is basically verbal and everyone's been shouting past each other for seventy years. But I can also see it being genuinely substantive, because there seems to be a real fight underneath about blame — whether you may blame someone for stopping at the threshold — and that doesn't feel verbal to me.

So please give me exactly five numbered points, each only a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion. Point 1: say what kind of disagreement this actually is (verbal, substantive, different questions under one heading, bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for — whatever you actually think). Point 2: give the strongest piece of evidence that it's substantive, drawn from what the sides actually say. Point 3: ditto for the strongest evidence it's verbal or otherwise not a real clash. Point 4: tell me whether my hypothesis survives, and be blunt if it doesn't. Point 5: say what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a test case, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Throughout, please flag how confident you are in each claim, and explicitly single out the one point you're least sure of. Oh, and answer in British English please (my lecturer is picky and so am I).
