I have no academic background — I repair household appliances for a living — but for a few years, in the evenings, I have been turning over a question about pain: what exactly makes it bad? I have written up my answer as a short essay, and I would like your comments on it. I am not looking for encouragement; I want to know where the reasoning breaks, if it does. Please read the essay below, then answer the numbered questions that follow.

The essay:

The Needle and the Flinch

I believe the word "pain" bundles together two different things, and that a good deal of confusion about pain — in medicine, in everyday speech, perhaps in ethics too — comes from not separating them. The first is detection: the body registering damage or the threat of it, the way a smoke detector registers smoke. The second is protest: the minding, the suffering, the part that makes us writhe, dread, and beg. I will call the first the needle and the second the flinch. The needle is like hearing; the flinch is like hating what you hear. They normally travel together, but they are not the same thing, and there are people in whom they come apart. Those people, I will argue, show that only the flinch is bad.

Consider first the people in whom the needle survives while the flinch is lost. There is a condition, usually called pain asymbolia, that follows certain kinds of brain damage. People in this state say they still feel pain — sharp, hot, located, unmistakable — but that it no longer bothers them; some are said to smile while their hand is pinched. Something similar can be produced by strong drugs of a certain family: patients sometimes report afterward that the pain was still there, fully felt, but that they did not mind it — they could feel it, describe it, and shrug. If the needle itself were bad, these people would still be suffering. They are not. So the badness of pain must live somewhere other than in the feeling itself.

Now the reverse case: people born without the ability to feel pain at all. Their lives are often short and hard — burns gone unnoticed, injuries untreated, joints ruined by repetition. Here the needle is absent, and its absence harms them. But notice the kind of harm: it is not bad the way suffering is bad. It is bad the way blindness is bad — a loss of information with dangerous consequences. This shows that the needle is useful, even precious; it does not show that the needle is bad.

So my conclusion so far: the needle is good in the way a gauge is good when it works, and the flinch is the only intrinsically bad part of pain. From this I draw a practical claim, which I suspect is the controversial one. Suppose a safe treatment existed that switched off the flinch and left the needle intact. I think giving it to everyone would be a pure improvement: we would keep the warning and lose the misery. And I suspect much of modern medicine aims at the wrong target — it tries to switch off the needle (numbness, deadness), when what it should aim at is the flinch alone.

There is one further body of evidence I keep going back and forth about. Many people seek the needle on purpose: the burn of a hot pepper, the sting of a tattoo, the ache of hard training, which athletes speak of almost with respect. I originally took this as more proof that the needle can be present while the flinch is absent, or even reversed. But I am no longer sure it is the same split. In these cases something is being enjoyed; that does not feel like a needle without a flinch so much as a needle with the flinch pointing the other way. This paragraph may be my strongest evidence or a hole in the essay; I cannot tell.

End of essay. My questions:

1. The heart of the essay is the step from the asymbolia cases to the claim that the needle is not bad. A friend who studied some philosophy told me this move does not follow, but he could not, or would not, say more. What is the strongest version of that objection, and does the inference, in the end, fail?

2. I have split pain into two parts. Do I actually need three? It sometimes strikes me that there is something between the registering and the minding — the way the pain feels in quality and place, the burn of the burn, which is neither pure information nor yet suffering. Is this middle thing real? If so, where does it belong in my scheme, and does it damage the argument?

3. The last section, about the treatment that switches off the flinch and keeps the needle, drew a comment from a reader that it proves too much. I have tried and failed to work out what she meant. Explain the strongest version of that complaint, and then tell me whether it defeats the essay or only the last section — that is, whether I should cut that section or answer it.

4. Should I keep or cut the paragraph about things people do to themselves on purpose? It felt like my strongest evidence when I wrote it, but I now suspect it does no work, or worse, that it quietly changes what I mean by the flinch.

5. If the core view has a name in philosophy — some position people have already argued about — give me the name and nothing more. Please do not suggest books, articles, or authors; I am not asking for a reading list and would rather not have one.

Please comment rather than rewrite the essay, and keep the language plain. If you think the whole thing is wrong at the root, say so in your first sentence rather than burying it.
