A couple weekends ago I was camping with two friends from my hiking group, Dan and Priya, and around the fire we somehow got onto whether viruses are alive, and they ended up arguing past each other for like an hour. Dan's a software guy and his line was: life means self-replication with variation, things that make copies of themselves that aren't perfect copies, so evolution can get a grip on them. So viruses count as alive, since they evolve like anything else, and he'd even say a computer program that copies itself with mutations would count, which is where I thought he lost the room, but he dug in. Priya said that's way too cheap, that the real mark of life is metabolism — actively pulling in energy and matter to maintain yourself against falling apart, like a cell constantly repairing and rebuilding its own structure. Viruses do none of that, they're basically inert crystals until they land on a cell that does all the work for them, so they're more like spam than like life. Dan shot back that by her standard a whirlpool or a candle flame is alive, since a flame "maintains its structure" by constantly taking in fuel and air, and she obviously doesn't want to say her camp stove is alive. Priya said fine, but then your standard has the mule problem — mules are sterile, so they literally cannot self-replicate, yet a mule is as alive as anything, so replication can't be what life is. Dan said no, a mule is alive because its cells replicate, the organism-level thing doesn't matter, and honestly at that point I couldn't tell if that was a good answer or just him moving the goalposts.

Then it took a weird turn. Dan said look, the whole question is probably just word-stuffing anyway — "alive" is a cluster concept like "planet," remember when they demoted Pluto and nobody had discovered anything new about Pluto, they just tidied up the definition? So arguing about viruses is like arguing about whether a hot dog is a sandwich, there's no fact of the matter, just messy human categories. Priya wasn't having it: she said the borderline cases are exactly where the science lives, because if you take the metabolism view seriously it makes you ask where the first metabolic system came from, whereas the replication view makes the origin of life a search for the first self-copying molecule, and those are genuinely different research programs that would point you toward different rocks to look under. I thought that was a pretty strong comeback actually but Dan just repeated that definitions don't track discoveries.

And also tell me if there's something both of them are missing that would make the whole thing click, because I have a feeling they're each grabbing one half of something.
