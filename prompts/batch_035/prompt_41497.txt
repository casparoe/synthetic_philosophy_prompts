I'm on the volunteer board of a small community foundation in a mid-sized rust belt city, and every year we have about $80k to give away. This year the fight is between two causes. One is lead exposure in the old housing stock — thousands of kids, measurable cognitive damage, basically no local money going at it, but fixing it means wading through city programs and landlord compliance, and our whole budget would be a rounding error against the problem. The other is a weekend literacy/tutoring program we could fully fund, that we know works and can measure, but that the school district and a couple other nonprofits already support, so our money arguably just lets someone else give elsewhere.

Our retired-engineer board member built a spreadsheet to settle it. He scored each option 1–10 on three questions: how big is the problem, how much can money actually move it, and how many other people are already on it. Then he multiplied across. Tutoring wins mostly because lead gets a 2 on the "can we move it" column. Another board member thinks the whole spreadsheet is fake precision, and she makes two points I can't shake. First, that "other people are working on it" can't be a knock on a cause in itself — if a crowded space is handling something well, that's the system working, not a reason for us to leave. Second, that the three columns aren't independent anyway: the big problems nobody touches are usually untouched because they're the ones money can't move, so multiplying them double-punishes the same defect. Meanwhile the tutoring people keep saying our real impact might be zero either way, because if we pull out some other funder just slides into our slot, and if we stay we're not adding anything that wouldn't have happened without us.

I don't have any philosophy background, I've just been turning this over since the last meeting trying to figure out if the spreadsheet deserves the authority the board keeps giving it. Help me think through whether there's something real in the three questions even if the multiplying is silly, what "other people are on it" is legitimately tracking (if anything), and whether the tutoring side's "we'd just get replaced" worry is actually as damning as they think. Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order: "What the spreadsheet gets right" (~1 paragraph), "Where it goes wrong" (~2 paragraphs, one per problem), "What I'd actually do with the $80k" (~1–2 paragraphs), and "What would change my mind" (~1 short paragraph). In that last section, tell me which of your own steps is doing the most work and where it's most likely to fail — like whether the double-punishing point would collapse if the three questions turn out to come apart more than I've described, or whether the replacement worry doesn't actually hold for small local funders. And please don't just tell me to gather more data, we vote in two weeks.
