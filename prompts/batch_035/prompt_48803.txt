My oldest friend and I spent half the night arguing about when a theory's repairs are legitimate science and when they're just fudging, and I ended the argument with a page of my own verdicts that I trust more than anything either of us said in the abstract. He kept invoking Lakatos, the Hungarian philosopher of science, talking about progressive and degenerating research programmes and protective belts, and I suspect he was half-remembering it and applying it however suited him. So here is what I actually believe about the cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern, whether my friend's Lakatos story delivers it, and what the principle says about the cases I haven't listed.

When Uranus refused to move as Newtonian celestial mechanics said it should, and astronomers posited an unseen perturbing planet, which turned out to be Neptune, I think that was good science, full stop. When Mercury's perihelion refused to move as Newtonian celestial mechanics said it should, and Le Verrier posited another unseen planet, Vulcan, which nobody ever found, I think that was fudging, even though structurally the two moves look identical to me: in both cases you save the theory by pointing at an unobserved body. When the Cartesians accused Newton's gravity of being an occult quality, an action at a distance with no mechanism behind it, and Newtonians pushed ahead anyway, I think the Newtonians were right to push ahead, and that this was the best science of its day, not a scam papering over a missing explanation. When Ptolemaic astronomers responded to each new planetary observation by adding another epicycle to accommodate it, I think that was degeneration, and yet I also think the Ptolemaic system was doing serious, creditable predictive work for centuries before it collapsed, which I find hard to hold together with the first half of that verdict. When Freudians treated a patient's agreement as confirmation and the same patient's disagreement as resistance, thereby confirming the diagnosis either way, I think that is the paradigm of what science must never do. And when Bohr's atomic model first accommodated the already-known Balmer series of hydrogen spectral lines and then went on to predict lines in the ultraviolet that were subsequently found, I think that was progressive even though the model was, in the end, false in its core claims about electrons in fixed little orbits, which is a fact I keep tripping over. On top of all that, I confess I go back and forth about the phlogiston theorists: I want to say their programme was respectable science even though the move of assigning phlogiston negative weight to escape an apparent refutation looks preposterous to me, and I cannot decide whether that move killed the programme or was a reasonable price for its other successes.

My tentative principle, before my friend got me confused, was that a repair is legitimate when it issues in risky new predictions and illegitimate when it merely accommodates what is already known. I can see this handles some of my verdicts and stumbles on others, and I would rather be told honestly which of my verdicts has to go than be handed a principle so baroque it saves them all.

So: what principle captures these verdicts, or close to them? Does the Lakatosian machinery my friend was waving around actually do the job, and if so, is he even applying it correctly, or does he have the progressive-degenerating distinction bent out of shape? State whatever principle you settle on precisely, motivate each clause by the case that demands it, tell me its verdict on a case I have not listed (string theory is the one my friend and I circled for an hour without resolving, but pick anything), and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and why I should give it up. One more thing: I want to be able to distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know this literature. Historians of science, for instance, dispute the tidy textbook versions of some of these episodes, and philosophers of science disagree about whether novel prediction is really necessary at all, so do not sell me a contested reading as if it were consensus; flag the load-bearing judgment calls. Please write in continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
