I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month I published a long feature arguing that effective altruism's measurability bias is not a fixable bug but constitutive of the whole project: once you set an evidence bar that only RCT-style interventions can clear, unmeasurable work can never pass it, so the bias isn't creeping INTO the method, it IS the method. The second half of the piece is nastier — I claim earning-to-give functions as a sorting mechanism, selecting for people comfortable treating systemic problems as donation targets rather than things you fight, which explains the movement's rightward drift better than any story about individual leaders. Next week I've been invited to defend the piece at an event hosted by a big university EA chapter, and I want to be ready for the Q&A. The room will be mostly earnest undergrads who've taken the Giving What We Can pledge and will feel accused, a couple of philosophy PhD students who've read the institutional critique literature already and will be annoyed I'm getting attention for their points, and at least one econ postdoc who runs impact evaluations for a living. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each group would phrase them, flag which ones have real teeth and which are just discomfort, tell me what I should concede up front so the room stays with me, and identify the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. Be honest that my sorting-mechanism claim rests on a correlation I've been dressing up as a mechanism — I'd rather know now exactly how a sharp econ person would take it apart than find out on stage.
