I retired a few months ago after 38 years as a structural engineer, and it turns out the question of what my life is actually FOR stops being a dorm-room question pretty fast once nobody's waiting for me at the office. So I've been reading philosophy of life stuff, mostly badly, and two pieces have grabbed me and I can't decide whether they're saying the same thing or the opposite thing, and it's starting to feel personally urgent.

The first is Moritz Schlick's little essay on the meaning of life (yes, the Vienna Circle guy, which surprised me too). As I read him, meaning is never located in achieving goals — it lives in something like play, in treating the activity itself as the point, the way a kid does. Striving and deferring satisfaction toward a goal is precisely what drains a life of meaning. He even frames it as the difference between the attitudes of youth and old age, not between young and old people. The second is Bernard Williams' argument about immortality, the Makropulos one about the opera singer who's been alive for centuries: an endless life would necessarily go tedious and hollow, because your categorical desires — the ones that keep you going for their own sake rather than for the sake of something else — eventually just run out, while you keep going anyway.

Here's what's bugging me. At first Williams looked like an argument FOR Schlick: both of them deny that meaning can be banked in completed goals, and both put it in present-tense engagement that has its point in itself. But then they seem to split. Schlick seems committed to a person who really has the playful attitude just continuing — new games forever, no reason the show ever has to close. Williams insists that no, any particular person runs dry eventually, playful or not. So which is it: are they allies with a modest disagreement about shelf life, or is the tension deeper? Is Schlickian play a genuine counterexample to the tedium argument — would Williams have to say that play is just another categorical desire that gets used up like everything else — or can Williams bite back somehow? And the version I actually care about: I'm 65, hopefully with a couple of decades left. Does either view imply that whatever meaning I've got left depends on the deadline, or is the deadline orthogonal? I'd like a real comparison — where exactly they agree, where exactly they pull apart, and who has the better of it — not just two summaries next to each other.
