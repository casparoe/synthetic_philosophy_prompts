My flatmate (a controls engineer) and a friend of mine who did her thesis on this stuff keep arguing about responsibility gaps in autonomous systems, and I want to put a number on where I actually stand. The claim on the table: there is at least one realistic deployment scenario — current-gen autonomous vehicles, or triage software in a hospital, whatever — in which a serious harm occurs, the system operated exactly as designed, and yet no human agent anywhere in the chain is morally responsible in the backward-looking, blame sense. Not the programmers upstream, not the managers who shipped it, not the operator who deployed it, not the regulator. Full information allowed: imagine after the fact we know everything about how the system was built and why. The engineer says this basically never happens — with enough forensic digging you can always find some human whose negligence or quality of will explains the harm, and where you genuinely can't, it's because the harm was unforeseeable, which dissolves responsibility anyway. So he puts it around 0.05. My other friend is at like 0.8: she thinks once systems select actions through processes no human can reconstruct in relevant detail, human responsibility just fails, full stop, whether or not anyone was sloppy on the way there.

Give me your own credence, but honestly the number is the least of it. I want the decomposition. How much of the answer rides on what backward-looking responsibility actually requires (control? foreseeability? the capacity to have done otherwise? some quality of will?), how much on whether distributed responsibility across many hands is a real solution or a rhetorical trick that sounds like it covers the gap but doesn't, how much on whether foreseeability should be judged case-by-case or at the statistical level (the system was known to cause N serious harms per year, just not to whom), and how much on whether no-fault compensation schemes and strict liability count as closing the gap or as admitting it exists and socializing the cost onto nobody in particular. A rough probability for each term with one line of justification, then however you combine them, then tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move it most.

Last thing: tell me where you think the engineer and my friend actually disagree. My suspicion is its not really about how smart machines will get — it's about something older, like whether responsibility can survive without anyone knowing, or whether blaming someone is something we do instead of describing the causal chain rather than on top of it. But I'd like to hear your version.
