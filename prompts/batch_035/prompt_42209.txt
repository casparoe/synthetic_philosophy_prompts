I'm teaching a summer intro course on political philosophy (about 22 students, one 75-minute session) and next week is the Nozick entitlement theory section from Anarchy, State, and Utopia. The reading went fine but discussion keeps stalling at the Wilt Chamberlain argument — half the class just hears "so inequality is fine??" and the other half immediately objects that D1 was never legitimately obtained in the first place, so nobody actually feels the tension Nozick is pointing at between a patterned distributional principle and people being allowed to make voluntary transfers. I want them to live through that tension instead of arguing past it. So design me an in-class game: a token economy with some real-ish stakes (points toward skipping a homework problem or something, no money), an initial distribution, trading rounds where people actually have reasons to trade, one student designated as the "Wilt" whose special skill everyone wants to pay for, and then a redistribution round imposed by majority vote after each trading round — so the redistribution has to keep happening, over and over, which is the part I most want them to feel viscerally. Also include one wrinkle I've been toying with: I want a few of the starting tokens to have deliberately sketchy provenance (some were lifted from another section's supply box before class), so the students hit the justice-in-acquisition problem themselves mid-game rather than me raising it in lecture like an objection I'm handing them.

Give me the design as exactly five numbered sections: (1) materials and setup, including how to seed the initial distribution and the dirty tokens; (2) the rules of the trading rounds and how the Wilt-analog student gets set up so the payments to them feel voluntary and not staged; (3) the redistribution mechanic — how the vote works, what happens to people who voted against it, and how often it recurs; (4) a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto D1/D2, the notion of a distributional pattern, and the historical vs. end-state distinction, without me lecturing for more than five minutes; and (5) an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, plus which parts of Nozick's argument the game just cannot reproduce no matter how I run it (the Lockean proviso, obviously, and the fact that nobody's life prospects are actually at stake over tokens). Keep each section to a tight paragraph, no intro or conclusion.
