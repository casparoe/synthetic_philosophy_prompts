I'm my dad's healthcare proxy. He's mid-stage Alzheimer's, and I'm the one who gets called when a decision has to be made — which is every couple of months lately, sometimes with a day or two to decide, sometimes with the hospital wanting an answer in an hour. Before the disease he was a trial lawyer, extremely sharp, and left very definite opinions behind: no feeding tubes, don't keep him alive "in some warehouse with no idea who anyone is," that kind of thing. But current-him is calmer and sweeter than pre-Alzheimer's him ever was, and honestly he seems pretty content most days, while his past self's whole fear was of exactly the condition he's now in and doesn't seem to mind.

Everyone gives me a different theory. His doctor says the advance directive governs. My sister says just keep him comfortable and happy now, that's the whole job. And I keep reading about this debate in the literature — Dworkin's split between experiential interests and critical interests, the whole advance-directive fight about whether past autonomous preferences should beat a now-happy present person, and also the AI-alignment people's idea of "coherent extrapolated volition," asking what someone would want if they knew more, thought faster, were more the person they wished they were. It struck me these are all basically the same question I'm facing with my dad.

So what I want is a procedure, not a philosophy lecture. Something I can actually run each time the phone rings: the questions in order, when his past stated preferences bind and when they don't, how to weigh what current-him seems to want when he can't really explain himself anymore, and what to do in the case I find hardest — when present-him clearly wants something (like still wanting his "going to the office" routine at the day center, which is a fiction but keeps him calm and oriented) that past-him would have found undignified. Tell me where the procedure just can't decide and I have to make a judgment call, and mark those spots honestly.

Then run it on three real-ish cases: (1) he stops eating enough and they ask about a temporary feeding tube, (2) a UTI that could turn septic — antibiotics or comfort care only, (3) whether to keep going along with his belief that he still has cases to prepare, including letting him "work" in his notebook for hours. No reading lists please, I've had enough of those — I need the decision rules.
