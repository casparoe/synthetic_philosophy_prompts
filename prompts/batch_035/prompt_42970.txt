Write an Onion-style satirical news article — headline plus 450–550 words of body text — reporting that a local man has adopted full Kelly-optimal sizing across every domain of his life after reading a popular book on the mathematics of betting, and can no longer take a fixed-size risk of any kind. He tips at restaurants as a fraction of his net worth rather than a fraction of the check; he sizes his portions, his sleep, and his weekend plans by expected logarithmic growth; he has done the arithmetic and discovered that a single career gamble, being unrepeatable, gets no Kelly allocation at all, which is why he hasn't changed jobs in eleven years.

The humor must come from getting the actual mathematics right, not from mocking a caricature of it. In particular, the piece should dramatize, without ever lecturing about: the difference between arithmetic and geometric growth rates, and the fact that variance drags the geometric mean below the arithmetic mean; the fact that betting above the Kelly fraction turns a positive-edge bet into near-certain ruin; fractional Kelly as a hedge against the possibility that your estimated edge is wrong; the risk-aversion built into log utility, which makes the man an enthusiastic buyer of insurance on things an expected-value maximizer would happily self-insure; and the awkward point that the Kelly argument assumes an indefinitely repeated sequence of bets, so it is silent — not merely cautious — about one-off decisions. At least one joke should turn on a number or calculation the reader can mentally check.

Include at least two quotes from invented sources: one from the man himself, serene and ruined-adjacent, explaining his sizing in fluent jargon; and one from his financial advisor, an unrepentant arithmetic-mean maximalist who keeps pointing at the expected value of the man's portfolio and who should be genuinely wrong about something rather than a mere foil — his blindness to drawdown risk should be played straight even as the article subtly sides against him. A third quote, from the man's spouse or book club, should raise a worry that is actually deep — e.g., that a person is not a bankroll — without resolving it.

Keep the satire even-handed: mock the man's overextension of a genuine theorem into a life philosophy, but also let the advisor's expected-value myopia look at least as foolish. Deadpan journalistic register throughout; no winking at the reader, and no character may be allowed to say anything mathematically false unless the article's framing makes clear that this is the error being satirized.

End with a single line of the form

Moral: <one sentence>

where the moral is itself a correct, self-contained statement about geometric rationality — for instance about why the same bet can be simultaneously positive in expectation and ruinous in sizing, or about what a growth rate does and does not tell you to do when you only get one pass at the bet. The moral must not itself commit the error the article satirizes.
