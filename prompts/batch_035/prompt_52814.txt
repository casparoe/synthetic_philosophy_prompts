I'm one of the TAs for a big intro modern philosophy survey at a state school (I mention this in half my posts, but for context: I got drafted into it after running a LessWrong-adjacent reading group for years, and apparently being able to explain the map/territory distinction to freshmen makes you employable). We just covered the Meditations and the midterm had one question I now have 80 copies of to grade, and one of them is bugging me enough that I want a second opinion before I commit to a number. The exam question read: "The cogito is often summarized as the claim that one's own thinking guarantees one's own existence. At the point in the Second Meditation where Descartes reaches this conclusion, what exactly is it supposed to establish, and what is it not yet in a position to establish? In your answer, address (a) whether the cogito works as an inference from a more general premise, and (b) whether it establishes that the I is a thinking substance."

Here's the student's answer, typed out verbatim (minus their handwriting, thank god): "The cogito is the one belief that survives the doubt of the First Meditation. Even if an evil demon deceives me about everything, in order to be deceived I must exist, so the demon's very efforts confirm my existence. This makes the cogito self-verifying: any attempt to doubt it is itself a case of thinking, which just re-affirms it. Note that it's essentially first-person — the belief that I exist is certain only when actually thought or asserted, and Descartes isn't claiming to have shown that any other mind exists. The reason the cogito is certain is its logical form: it is a valid syllogism whose major premise, that whatever thinks exists, cannot be doubted. Descartes stresses that this argument form, in contrast to the sense-based beliefs that came before, is what guarantees the conclusion. And the conclusion is precisely that I am a thinking substance whose essence is thinking — that is the whole payoff of the Second Meditation, that mind is better known than body. From there Descartes will use clear and distinct perception to prove God's existence and thereby escape the demon worry once and for all."

My problem is that it reads as solid on the visible stuff — the demon reversal, the first-person indexicality, all fine — but I keep going back and forth on two or three moves deeper in, and I don't want to dock points for something that's actually defensible or let slide something that's a real conflation just because the prose sounds confident (my study group had a whole thread about how graders Goodhart on confident-sounding essays). So: give me the grade you'd assign out of 10, then walk through the answer point by point with specific comments justifying the grade, flagging exactly which claims are right, which are subtly off and why, and what partial credit they'd deserve. Lead with the bottom line — grade first sentence — then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question back at me. And if you think one of the moves I'm suspicious of is actually fine by mainstream Descartes scholarship, say that plainly rather than hedging, I'd rather be corrected than pass on my own confusion to a freshman.
