For my junior-year extended essay on emergence I have chosen the "Aunt Hillary" conceit from Gödel, Escher, Bach: the ant colony that converses and reflects even though no individual ant understands anything. My advisor approved the topic, but she also warned me that "could a colony have a mind?" is too loose a question to answer, so let me state the claim as precisely as I can. I do not mean "is it useful to describe colonies as if they had goals," the way one might describe a market as nervous. I mean: there is a physically possible ant colony whose behavior is best explained by attributing beliefs, desires, and something like a point of view to the colony as a whole, in whatever sense those attributions are true of you or me rather than merely useful fictions.

My friend, who has read more philosophy of mind than I have, puts her credence in that claim at about 0.7. I put mine at about 0.25, and I would like yours — but the number alone is useless to me. Please decompose it. At minimum I can see these sub-questions, and I suspect you will find others: (1) the probability that some functionalist or organizational theory of mind is correct, since on a strongly biological view the claim is dead on arrival; (2) given functionalism, the probability that colony-level processes can realize the right kind of structure — my worry here is that pheromone trails seem to carry aggregate gradients rather than anything with compositional structure, and that a colony's internal signaling operates on a timescale of hours, which feels too slow for anything deserving the name of thought, though I am honestly unsure why I think slowness should matter; (3) the probability that mentality requires a unified subject of experience that a swarm architecture simply cannot provide, independent of any particular theory; and (4) the probability that the whole issue is verbal — that once we learn to describe colony dynamics precisely, the question of whether there is "really" a mind there dissolves, so that neither my friend's 0.7 nor my 0.25 answers anything.

Assign a rough probability to each of these (and to any additional terms you think belong in the decomposition), give one line of justification per term, show how they combine into your overall credence, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to.

One more thing, because it is the part of my essay I am least sure about. Imagine biologists engineered a colony that answers typed questions by laying scent-trail patterns that decode into grammatical English answers, and suppose the setup is honest — no human in the loop, the decoding fixed in advance, the colony's answers novel and coherent over weeks of conversation. Does imagining that scenario move any of your terms, and if so, which one and by how much? Finally, given your decomposition, tell me where you think my friend's 0.7 and my 0.25 most likely diverge: do we disagree about functionalism itself, about what colonies can do, or about what the word "mind" is for?
