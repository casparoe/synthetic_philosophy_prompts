I write a little decision-theory-ish blog and I want to do one of those "which position do you actually hold?" quizzes, this one on sunk costs. The pop answer is always "sunk costs are irrelevant, just forget them and optimize from here" — but there are real defenses of honoring them, and I want the quiz to catch people who nod along to the pop answer but don't actually believe it. The positions that should show up as leaves: (a) the pure forward-looking/economist view that sunk costs never count, period; (b) resolute choice — you treat your past self's plan as binding so your future self can't wiggle out of things through myopia; (c) sunk costs as evidence — the fact that you've already invested a lot tells you something, either about the option's quality or about how much you value it; (d) waste-aversion as a genuine reason — letting resources be wasted is itself bad, not just instrumentally; (e) the view that past investment changes what outcomes *mean* to you (the summit means more because you trained for years; a medal won off a lifetime of effort doesn't feel like the same outcome as one handed to you); and (f) the strategic/commitment view, where honoring sunk costs makes you credibly stubborn and that reputation is worth the occasional loss.

Give me about eight questions, each a concrete case — finishing a bad movie you paid for, Concorde-style national projects, staying in a mediocre relationship or PhD, standing in line longer than the thing is worth — with two or three answer options apiece, phrased so a reader can answer from the case alone without knowing the labels. Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the positions. Some things I care about: the evidence leaf and the meaning leaf should come apart somewhere in the quiz (a case where "my investment shows I care" and "my investment makes the outcome more valuable" give different verdicts would be great), and you should flag any pair of positions the key can't really seperate. If some region of the logical space — like waste-aversion about your own money but not about other people's — is basically unoccupied by actual thinkers, say so and take a quick guess at why (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?). My readers are smart laypeople, so no jargon in the questions themselves, and dont ask anything that requires them to already know what "resolute choice" means.
