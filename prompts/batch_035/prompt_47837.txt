I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I'm at work on a long-form feature marking roughly six decades since the independence of the continuum hypothesis — the fact, established through Gödel's constructible universe in 1940 and Cohen's forcing in 1963, that ZFC can neither prove nor refute the claim that there is no size of infinity strictly between the integers and the reals. My piece is about what, if anything, has happened since: the people I've interviewed divide in ways that have surprised me. One camp, around Woodin's program of inner model theory and the hoped-for Ultimate-L, holds that the question has a definite answer and that new axioms may yet deliver it. Joel David Hamkins and others argue for a set-theoretic multiverse in which CH holds in some universes, fails in others, and was never a well-posed question with a singular answer. A third group points out that forcing axioms like the proper forcing axiom already decide CH negatively, on the strength of their other consequences, and considers the matter effectively resolved by that route. And a deflationary contingent — some philosophers, a few mathematicians — thinks there is simply no fact of the matter to settle. The problem is that I, as a non-set-theorist writing for readers with no set theory at all, have no principled way to evaluate claims of the form "this settles it," and I refuse to adjudicate the dispute by whoever gives the best interview.

So before I write a word of the feature, I want the specification. Write the desiderata: what a proposed resolution of CH would have to deliver to count as settling it rather than merely taking a side. I want each requirement stated with the case or consideration behind it, and I want you to take seriously the possibility that some are genuine desiderata and some are prejudices of one camp dressed as neutral standards. The ones I can see from the outside, at least: a resolution owes us some account of why the standard axioms are silent (adding CH as an axiom by fiat seems too cheap, and if it isn't too cheap, I need to know why); Gödel's distinction between justifying new axioms intrinsically, by their self-evidence, versus extrinsically, by their fruitful consequences, and whether only one kind can bear the weight; a demand that the deciding axiom generalize, also settling other independent statements like Souslin's hypothesis or the cardinal characteristics of the continuum, rather than being reverse-engineered for CH; compatibility with large cardinals, which seem to leave CH entirely untouched; and a cluster of considerations about the universe of sets being "maximal" rather than thin — which I understand cuts against Gödel's constructible universe and yet sits awkwardly with an Ultimate-L that resembles it. Mark which requirements you take as non-negotiable and which as camp preference, and tell me plainly if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — whether, that is, the list itself is quietly incoherent, such that no resolution could meet it and the real news is that the disputants are demanding different things of the word settled.

Then two shorter things, to make the specification usable to me. First, a brief scoring of the four main lines I described — the Ultimate-L program, the multiverse view, the forcing-axiom route, and the no-fact-of-the-matter position — against the list, keeping it compact because the criteria are the story, not the scoreboard. Second, a short checklist I can actually run as a journalist when a press release or preprint claims a resolution: questions I can answer from the abstract and from asking the author one or two things, with an indication of which answers are merely alarming versus genuinely telling. Throughout, flag which of the desiderata are philosophy rather than mathematics — where mathematicians themselves would disagree about the standard being applied — because part of what I want to convey to readers is that this community lacks even a shared notion of what would count as an answer, and I'd rather show that precisely than assert it loosely.
