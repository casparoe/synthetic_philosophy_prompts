As a teaching assistant for a large undergraduate applied-ethics course, I have a recurring problem: every year, at the end of the unit on the doctrine of double effect, students exit with labels rather than positions — they announce "I'm a consequentialist" or "I follow Aquinas," but their actual judgments about cases don't match the label they've adopted, and often don't even match each other. This semester I want to hand out a one-page diagnostic decision tree that sorts students by what they say about cases, before they know which position the cases are supposed to feed into. Please act as a professional moral philosopher with experience designing diagnostic instruments for teaching. Before writing anything, take some time to brainstorm which questions could actually do the sorting work — the value of a tree like this lives entirely in the choice of branch points, so justify briefly, in a short preamble, why you selected the ones you did.

The tree should take a student from a fixed starting commitment — roughly, "it can sometimes be permissible to cause serious harm in pursuit of a proportionately greater good, though not always" — down to a leaf naming the position their combination of answers genuinely supports. The branch nodes should be concrete cases that the student answers, never questions about terminology: the standard trolley case of redirecting a runaway tram from five to one; the footbridge case of pushing someone in front of it; a strategic bomber who destroys a munitions factory, foreseeing but not aiming at civilian deaths; a terror bomber who kills the same civilians precisely to break the enemy's morale; a craniotomy-type case where the death of the one is the instrument of saving the others; and at least one case engineered to make the classification genuinely contested — for example, a "loop" variant in which the tram is stopped by the body of the person on the side track, so that the death is causally necessary for the rescue even though the agent does nothing but redirect an existing threat, or a case of double prevention where the good outcome is secured by preventing something that would have prevented it. Every node must be answerable by a student who has never heard the names of any of the positions.

The leaves should include at least: unrestricted consequentialism; the doctrine of double effect in its plain form; a refined version adding something like a requirement of double intention — that the agent not rely on or welcome the harm even when she doesn't aim at it; and a view that forbids using people as means but has no use for the intended/foreseen distinction at all. Where a combination of answers has no natural home, say so rather than forcing it. Most importantly, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — I expect the loop case, or something like it, to make trouble — and for each such flag say whether it reflects a genuine indeterminacy in the philosophical positions themselves or a defect in the instrument, and what you would tell a student who lands on the boundary. Keep the deliverable to a single page as it will appear in the handout: the tree, a brief answer key, and a short paragraph of flags and caveats. No citations or reading list — I'll add references myself if I decide to include any.
