Here is the pattern I want named. A theory built on a mechanism makes a prediction with an implied timeline: Malthus's arithmetic of food against the geometric ratio of population implied that England should run up against a subsistence ceiling within a foreseeable number of generations. The ceiling did not arrive; wages and life expectancy rose for two centuries straight. Yet the theory was not so much abandoned as re-issued. Each escape from it — mechanized agriculture, chemical fertilizer, the opening of new continents to grain, and eventually the demographic transition itself — was absorbed as a temporary reprieve, and the ceiling was re-derived a bit further out, with the mechanism intact. The same structure recurred in the twentieth century with the neo-Malthusian bestsellers that predicted famines in the 1970s: the decade passed without the predicted mass famines, and the standard defense was that the mechanism was sound and only the timing was wrong. I have even seen the original Malthus defended in exactly these words — that he was right but early.

Now, the reason I can't just file this under familiar headings: it is not ordinary ad hoc rescue, because each adjustment (trade, land-saving innovation, falling fertility as prosperity rises) was independently motivated by respectable evidence, not invented solely to save the prediction. It does not look like simple unfalsifiability, because the timing claims were falsifiable and were in fact falsified. Whether it counts as a degenerating research programme in the Lakatosian sense seems to me to depend on whether the post-rescue versions made any successful novel predictions, which is itself contested. And the pattern is not confined to demographics: I see the same split whenever someone defends a failed deadline by saying the underlying forces are real and merely slower than expected.

So the thing I want you to identify is, more precisely, this move: taking a prediction that bundled together a mechanism and a timeline, treating the falsification of the timeline as leaving the mechanism untouched, and then presenting the survival of the mechanism as vindication of the original prediction. Does that move, or the general practice of re-issuing failed predictions with deferred deadlines while calling it vindication, have an established name in the philosophy of science or elsewhere?
