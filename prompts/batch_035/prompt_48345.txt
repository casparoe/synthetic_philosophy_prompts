I've spent a fair amount of time on the analytical-Marxism and value-form literatures, and there is a dispute between those two camps that I've come to think is structurally stuck, and I'd like a diagnosis of it rather than a verdict on who is right. The subject is what Marxian exploitation is supposed to be a theory of. On one side sit the analytical Marxists, following John Roemer's work of the early 1980s: exploitation, on their reconstruction, is a fact about property relations and distribution — a coalition is capitalistically exploited when its members would be better off withdrawing from the economy with their per capita share of society's alienable productive assets — and a theory of exploitation needs nothing beyond that; the labour theory of value drops out as an unnecessary appendage, retained by traditional Marxism only out of piety. On the other side sit the value-form theorists, in the lineage of Isaak Rubin and the German New Reading of Marx that formed around Backhaus and Reichelt in the late 1960s: for them exploitation is not primarily distributive at all but a fact about the social form of labour — under commodity production, private labours count as social only through the exchange of their products, and it is this form, in which the market validates labour post hoc, that the concept is meant to pick out. The two camps barely cite one another, and where they do meet it is mutual dismissal: the analytics find the value-form talk mystical and unreadable, while the value-form people regard the withdrawal criterion as a liberal theory of fair shares wearing a Marxian hat.

My working hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis, is that this is two questions travelling under one heading. Roemer's apparatus answers something like: under what property arrangements does a distribution count as unjustly extracted from one class for the benefit of another? The value-form apparatus answers: what social relation makes wage labour under capital distinctive, such that the wage relation itself is a site of domination regardless of how the pie is divided? If that is right, the impasse is not a hard substantive disagreement at all but a failure to notice that "exploitation" is doing double duty, and progress would consist not in new arguments for either side but in an agreed distinction — perhaps renaming one of the notions, as one might split "exploitation-as-injustice" from "exploitation-as-domination" — after which whatever dispute remains could actually be joined. But I can see a rival diagnosis: that there is a genuine substantive conflict about whether any distributional fact could suffice for exploitation, the value-form side holding that certain social forms are constitutive of exploitation in a way no allocation-based description could capture, and the analytics denying that this amounts to anything more than insistent rhetoric. I confess I ask partly because I've watched this exact failure mode — debates that turn out to be category confusions — flagged on the rationalist blogs I've read for years, and I want to know whether this is one of those or a real fight that I am underselling.

So: diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, correcting my sketch where it is off; classify it — verbal, two different questions under one name, a hidden normative disagreement about what makes an arrangement unjust, or a substantive dispute in disguise; and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether that is a distinction, a reformulation, a case both camps would treat as decisive, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Throughout, please indicate how confident you are in each claim you make, and explicitly single out the point in your diagnosis that you are least sure of — I would rather have the uncertainty flagged than receive a polished line.
