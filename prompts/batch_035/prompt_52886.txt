I teach an introductory epistemology course for philosophy majors — 14 students, one 75-minute session. We have just covered the Agrippan regress, and my students can now recite the trilemma as a taxonomy: foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, each with its stock objections filed under it. None of it is felt. I want an in-class game that makes them experience the regress as a decision they have to keep making, not a diagram they can label.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should improve or replace wherever it is weak. Students are divided into advocates, who must get claims certified, and registrars, who will stamp a claim only if handed a reason for it. But any reason offered is itself a claim requiring a stamp, from a different registrar, and so on down. Every additional layer of certification costs the advocate something — effort tokens drawn from a fixed budget, or minutes off a clock — while an unstamped claim earns nothing. So there is a real trade-off between stopping the chain somewhere (with the stopping point's status left contestable), looping back to a claim already in the chain (the rules should permit this but price it in a way that makes its cheapness disturbing rather than reassuring), and simply continuing forever, so that both the appeal and the burden of never finishing are felt rather than described.

Requirements: rules that fit on one handout and can be explained in under five minutes; materials limited to what a seminar room already contains; a payoff scheme with no real money that nonetheless generates real pressure; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the three positions — which moment corresponded to which horn, and where the students' actual behavior diverged from what the textbook says they should have felt at that point. I also want at least one place where the rules are deliberately silent and the players must decide what to do; that gap is where I expect the philosophy to actually happen.

Two further deliverables. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students gaming the token economy, chains dying of boredom rather than of principle, loops being embraced enthusiastically rather than tolerated, advocates colluding, and whatever else you foresee — and how to handle each case, distinguishing fixes by rule change from fixes by facilitation. Second, be frank about what the game cannot reproduce. I suspect the economic pressure is a poor stand-in for epistemic normativity at some point — presumably where a student stops the chain and the game treats this as a refusal to pay, while the epistemology treats it as a substantive claim about entitlement. Tell me exactly where the analogy breaks, and what I must supply myself in the debrief rather than trusting the activity to have shown it.
