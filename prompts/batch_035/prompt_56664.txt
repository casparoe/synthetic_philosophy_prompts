I am a recently retired immunologist — thirty-six years in the laboratory, the last twenty of them studying lymphocyte turnover — and since retiring I have been working through Parfit's Reasons and Persons with a small reading group. The teletransporter material appeals to my prejudices: I have spent a career watching bodies replace their constituents on every scale, and I came to the book already sympathetic to the thought that a person is nothing over and above a pattern of physical and psychological continuity, with no further fact required. But I want to be sure I am not merely endorsing whatever I already believed, so I would value a careful audit.

At our last meeting, a member of the group — a retired solicitor, fond of confident summaries — presented Parfit's position roughly as follows: Parfit showed that personal identity is not what matters. The teletransporter replicates your psychology in a new body, and since that gives exactly the same psychological continuity as ordinary survival provides, going through it is just as good as ordinary survival. Because ordinary survival is itself nothing but psychological connectedness, held together by a brain whose matter is being constantly replaced, there is no deep difference between the two cases anyway. And once you see this, the conclusion follows that death is not so bad: what we fear in death presupposes a deep further fact about identity, and there is no such fact, so the fear rests on a mistake. I have a feeling this is a mixture of genuine Parfit and of my friend's own editorialising, but I do not yet know the text well enough to say where the joins are. I would add that I found myself nodding along to all of it, which is exactly why I am suspicious.

Please go through the summary part by part and tell me which claims a Parfit scholar would accept as stated, which are distortions of subtler claims (for instance, whether Parfit holds that the teletransporter is exactly as good as ordinary survival, or something more hedged; and whether his reductionism is the claim that there is no deep further fact or the stronger claim some readers attribute to him that persons do not exist), and whether the final inference about death is one Parfit draws or one that my friend has bolted on. Where the summary conflates claims Parfit keeps distinct, I would like that flagged explicitly, since I want to know what I am actually endorsing. Finally, if my framing is itself off — if, say, the question of whether the summary is fair is confused, or if you think my biologist's reductionism is doing the work my reading of Parfit only seems to be doing — please say so plainly rather than giving me a politely balanced audit; I would rather be corrected than reassured.
