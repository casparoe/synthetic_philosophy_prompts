I'd like your help auditing a characterization I came across, because I can't tell how much of it to trust.

I was listening to a documentary-style podcast on the history of science, and the presenter gave an account of Kepler's relationship to astrology. It went, roughly, as follows. Kepler was by temperament the first modern scientific skeptic: he despised the superstition of horoscope-mongering but had to keep casting nativities because astronomy paid nothing, and he complained about the drudgery bitterly in his letters. His own discoveries put the whole edifice out of business: once he showed that the planets sweep through ellipses at varying speeds rather than riding perfect circles, the celestial harmonies that astrology depended on were physically bankrupt, and he knew it. He drew one strict line, conceding that the heavens can influence the weather down here but insisting that they never touch human lives. He joked, in a single memorable line, that if the foolish daughter did not earn the bread, the mother astronomy would starve — though he kept his real opinion, that nothing in the sky influences the earth beyond light and the tides, largely to himself for professional reasons. The episode closed by calling Kepler, in effect, the first scientific opponent of astrology, one who had to stay quiet about it to keep his position.

I have one reason to hesitate. Elsewhere I have read that Kepler gave the aspects — conjunctions, squares, trines — genuine, non-economic weight, holding that the human soul, at the moment of birth, responds to geometrical configurations in the heavens as a matter of archetypal resonance, and that his work on celestial harmonies was meant to deepen the foundations of such correspondences rather than demolish them. That sounds close to the opposite of the podcast's picture, but the presenter seemed otherwise well informed about the period.

Please take the presenter's account apart claim by claim. For each part, tell me (a) what a historian of early modern science would accept as correct, (b) what such a historian would wince at as an oversimplification or conflation, and (c) what they would simply reject as false. In particular, I'd like your judgment on these specific points: Was Kepler's motive for casting horoscopes purely financial, or did he distinguish between a corrupt popular astrology and a reformed natural one that he defended on principle? Did his elliptical astronomy, in his own view, refute astrology's physical basis, or did he see the geometry of the heavens as strengthening a purer version of it? Did he deny celestial influence on human affairs outright, or only deny the cruder claims about particular fates? And finally, does the closing label survive the corrections — was Kepler in any meaningful sense the first scientific skeptic of astrology — or does the truth, whatever it turns out to be, fit neither the podcast's picture nor the alternative I encountered?
