I have no background in philosophy — I'm a retired bookkeeper who has spent a lot of time thinking about emotions, on my own, from my own experience, from watching my family, and from reading whatever I could follow without a dictionary. Over the years I've settled into a set of views about what an emotion actually is, and lately I've begun to suspect that they don't all fit together, though I honestly can't see where the problem is. I'd like an audit: tell me which of my views genuinely conflict, which only seem to, and what the least costly repair would be for each real conflict. Please don't lecture me on what the famous positions are; just work with what I've written.

Here are my views, in no particular order:

1. Every emotion is about something. There is no such thing as anger with no object — if I am angry, I am angry at someone or about something. Even moods that feel diffuse turn out, on inspection, to be about something (usually myself).
2. What an emotion is about can be put into words as a judgment. My fear of the loose dog is, at bottom, the judgment that the dog is dangerous; my anger at my brother is the judgment that he wronged me. I believe this is why emotions can be appropriate or inappropriate — the judgment can be true or false, well or badly supported.
3. The body is not incidental to an emotion. Fear has a particular physical character — the stomach drop, the heartbeat — and sometimes the body moves first: I've felt the surge before I could name any thought that accompanied it.
4. Emotions can dig in against my better judgment. A coiled garden hose that I know perfectly well is a hose still produces snake-fear; my settled conviction that a remark was not aimed at me does not dissolve the humiliation. Since a belief can't straightforwardly contradict itself, I take this to show that emotions are not simply beliefs or judgments.
5. So I've come to think of emotions as perceptions — not of colors and shapes, but of what matters. The insult presents itself to me as an offense the way a half-submerged stick presents itself as bent. I don't infer that I've been wronged; I see the wrong, and the seeing can be accurate or distorted.
6. Animals and very small children plainly have emotions, and I am not willing to give that up.
7. Emotions are trainable in a way perception is not. Through years of effort — therapy, practice, deliberate reframing — I have genuinely changed what I feel in situations that used to floor me. I can't teach myself to see the stick as straight by practicing, but I have taught myself to feel calm where I used to feel panic.
8. I think an emotion can be fitting even when it rests on a falsehood: the fear of the hose-taken-for-a-snake is a reasonable fear given how things appeared to me, even though there is no snake. Something can be appropriate relative to appearances and still be wrong about the world.

Go through the list and tell me which commitments pull against each other, ranked by how serious the conflict is rather than treating all tensions as equal. For each genuine conflict, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere on the list. For anything that looks like a conflict but isn't, show me why it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Finally — and this matters to me — I'd like you to say where your own audit is most likely to be wrong: which of your verdicts about my list rests on the shakiest reasoning, and what consideration, if it turned out to be true, would change your ranking of the conflicts. I want to know how much weight I should put on your answer, not just what it is.
