I am writing a novel whose protagonist loses, to digital vandals, a cathedral she spent two years building in a shared virtual world, and I cannot dismiss an argument my son pressed on me over dinner. (1) To be wronged by the destruction of a thing, the thing must have been yours in some real sense; one cannot be robbed of what one does not own. (2) What made the cathedral hers was not law or code but her two thousand hours of labor; where no contract exists, investment alone constitutes ownership. (3) When the vandals razed it she was genuinely wronged: her grief was real, her plans were really thwarted, and her life's work was really gone. (4) So virtual things built through labor are owned in a sense robust enough to be destroyed by wrong. (5) My readers labor over my characters for years — rereading, annotating, corresponding with me about them — investing more effort than any cathedral demands. (6) By the same reasoning, the characters belong to them, and I would wrong several thousand strangers if my final chapter treated the characters as anything other than common property. (7) That I cannot accept: the characters are mine, and the ending is already written. Yet each step still looks defensible to me, which is precisely what unsettles me. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost — for my protagonist, and for my readers?
