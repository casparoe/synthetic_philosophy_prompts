Six months ago I agreed to serve as a lay juror for my mid-sized city's arts commission, and I have discovered that the job is mostly applied philosophy disguised as paperwork. Our founding ordinance limits the annual open-call exhibition to "original works of visual art," and every cycle we receive around seventy submissions that must be sorted into eligible and ineligible within a two-week window. I am the only one of the five jurors without an art background, and each decision requires a one-paragraph public rationale; rejected artists can appeal to the full commission, and the local paper covers our more controversial calls. In the last two cycles we managed to be accused of snobbery for rejecting a quilting guild's submission and of wasting taxpayer money for accepting an AI-generated image, so the stakes are modest in money but not in goodwill.

The problem is that we have no shared method, and our discussions reveal that we are constantly sliding between three different questions: whether something is art at all, whether it is good art, and whether it is the kind of art our exhibition exists to show. I have read enough to know that philosophers have been circling this for decades — the anti-essentialist claim that art has no definition and only family resemblances, the institutional theories that make art-hood depend on presentation to an artworld, the historical definitions that make it depend on relation to prior works, and the view that art-hood requires interpretation and aboutness rather than mere resemblance. But I cannot turn these into anything we can actually run in ten minutes per submission.

Please write a decision procedure for our jury. Specifically:

1. A short ordered set of questions a juror with no philosophical training can ask about a submission, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with "eligible," where it terminates with "ineligible," and where it explicitly stops and hands the question to judgment because it is a matter of values rather than facts.
2. A statement of which philosophical distinctions had to survive the translation and which you deliberately dropped, and where you expect the simplified procedure to misfire.
3. Worked applications to three of our actual recurring cases: (a) an AI-generated image whose submitter wrote a long, carefully crafted prompt and claims the prompt, not the output, is the artwork; (b) the quilting guild's hand-stitched narrative quilt, which I personally admire; and (c) a submission consisting of a typed certificate and instructions directing whoever buys it to spend one hour in the gallery with the lights off. For at least one of these, the procedure should reach a conclusion I will not like — I want to see it do that rather than curve toward my sympathies.
4. A note on what the procedure cannot do: which disputes before our jury it will leave unresolved no matter how carefully it is followed.

Two epistemic requests. First, be explicit about which step of the procedure you are least confident in, and describe the kind of case that would show that step is failing. Second, tell me what would change your mind about the overall design — for instance, whether accumulating a record of appeals overturning the procedure's verdicts should be treated as evidence against the procedure or as evidence that our appellate body is measuring something other than art-hood. I would rather have a procedure with known failure modes than one whose confidence outruns its foundations.
