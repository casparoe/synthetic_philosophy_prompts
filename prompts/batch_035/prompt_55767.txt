longtime lurker of LessWrong and the whole quantified self / productivity blog sphere here, so this question has been living in my head rent free. Ben Franklin basically ran the first habit-tracking experiment: his 13 virtues, the little book ruled with red columns, marking a black speck for every fault, focusing on one virtue per week through a full 13-week course. He also admitted the project only half worked — he never really got Order under control, and he suspected the whole writeup in the Autobiography might itself be vanity, though he figured even vanity-driven self improvement leaves you better off.

so suppose you sit him down with modern stuff: streak counters, apps that guilt you with charts, wearables giving your sleep a grade every morning, maybe an AI coach that checks in each evening and nudges you toward your goals. Would he think this is his system finally scaled — that the tech fixes the friction that made him drift (tedious bookkeeping, only weekly review, no accountability partner)? or would a guy who openly said the point wasn't perfection but gradual character apprenticeship (with nice reputational side effects) look at gamified streaks and think we've rebuilt his tool while losing the point — turning moral training into metric chasing?

basically: is Franklin best read as the patron saint of the quantified self, or as its earliest internal critic? argue for one, using his actual project and what he said about how it went. dont give me a wishy-washy "he'd probably find it interesting" — take a side.
