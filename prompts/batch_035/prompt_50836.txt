I'm a science-and-ideas journalist preparing a long-form piece on the invisible ethics built into everyday AI assistants — the refusal decisions that users run into constantly but almost never examine. My reporting has surfaced a pattern that I can't quite name, and I want to understand what philosophical machinery must be operating underneath it before I draft the article.

Here is the pattern. A mainstream chatbot will refuse to explain, in practical detail, how to pick a lock, grow certain plants, or restrict one's food intake sharply — even though the information is legal to possess, is a few searches away on the open web, and would be handed over by a librarian without a second thought. It will refuse to help someone draft a cruel letter to an ex-partner or a plan to confront a friend, where the only person plausibly harmed is the user's own future self. And it will often supply the very same information it just refused when the request is reframed as research, fiction, or harm reduction. Meanwhile, some refusals are clearly aimed at protecting third parties (helping with stalking, say) while others look straightforwardly paternalistic, yet the two kinds are rarely distinguished in the policy itself — they are just "harmful requests."

My question for you, then: what theory of the speaker's relation to harm does this practice presuppose? Specifically, I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit view. Does it treat the assistant as something like an accomplice or proximate cause, such that handing over information creates a responsibility that a search engine or library doesn't? Does the willingness to answer under fictional or research framings presuppose that intent and context, not content, carry the moral weight — and if so, is that coherent, given that the assistant cannot actually verify the stated intent? Does the refusal to help with self-regarding choices presuppose a picture of users as imperfectly autonomous — like a bartender or a pharmacist rather than a reference desk — and is that picture one the practice could actually defend, or just a default it drifted into?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with: which rival theories of autonomy, complicity, and harm would all license roughly this behavior, and where is the practice underdetermined between them? And tell me what would have to change, concretely, if the implicit theory were replaced by a straightforward Millian harm principle, under which competent adults get whatever information is legal to give. I'm not looking for an opinion piece on whether refusals are good or bad; I'm looking for the hidden theory, its internal tensions, and its pressure points, explained clearly enough that I can use it as the spine of the article. Please don't send me citations or a reading list — positions and arguments are enough.
