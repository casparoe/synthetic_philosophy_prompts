I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on distributive justice, and something about the design of social insurance programs has been bothering me. Unemployment insurance in most countries pays out fully when a worker is laid off but is denied or sharply reduced when a worker is fired for cause — the same income loss, treated differently depending on whether it traces to the worker's own choices. Disability insurance and public health systems, by contrast, typically pay out in full even when the disability or illness plainly resulted from voluntary risk-taking: the skier who ignored the avalanche warning, the lifelong smoker, the driver who refused to wear a seatbelt. Reconstruct the implicit theory of justice this patchwork presupposes. Is it luck egalitarianism, with its distinction between option luck and brute luck, or something else entirely — deterrence, desert, actuarial fairness, an administrative rule of thumb about what is cheap to verify? Then tell me whether the two halves of the practice are genuinely in tension or can be reconciled under a single theory, and sketch what would have to change in program design if the implicit view were replaced by a thoroughgoing luck egalitarianism — and again what would change if it were replaced by a Rawlsian view on which responsibility for outcomes never justifies unequal provision of basic goods. Please commit to a verdict about which theory best explains the practice as it actually is, and state explicitly what would change your mind: which consideration, if it held up, would push you toward a rival reconstruction, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
