The exam question (as it appeared on the exam):

"Nel Noddings's care ethics is sometimes charged with parochialism: that it offers little guidance for, and perhaps assigns no obligations regarding, people we stand in no caring relation with. In your answer, (a) explain the machinery of Noddings's view that generates the charge — in particular her distinction between caring-for and caring-about, and the roles of engrossment and motivational displacement in the caring relation; (b) describe how Virginia Held's The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global responds to this challenge, and explain why that response arguably changes the theory's extension beyond what Noddings herself would allow; and (c) briefly discuss the state of the empirical evidence regarding the original empirical motivation for the care perspective — the claim, associated with Carol Gilligan's work in the 1980s, that Kohlberg's stages systematically under-scored women's moral reasoning."

The student's answer:

"(a) Noddings distinguishes two modes of caring. Caring-for is direct, face-to-face care: the one-caring receives the cared-for through engrossment — a receptive attention to the other's subjective reality — and this engrossment produces motivational displacement, in which the one-caring's motivational energy flows toward the needs of the cared-for rather than toward her own projects. Because the one-caring responds to a concrete person in an actual relation, the cared-for must be able to receive the caring; completion of the relation depends on the response of the cared-for, and this completion is partly what distinguishes ethical caring from mere sentiment. Noddings holds that genuine obligations arise from this relation. Caring-about is her term for a weaker, generalized concern for distant others and for institutions — she calls it a form of caring that shares the phenomenology of caring-for, so that we can feel real concern for people we have never met. Since we cannot have engrossment with strangers, obligations to them are much thinner: for strangers, we have at most a duty of universal caring-about that constrains our conduct but does not generate positive duties to seek out the needy.

(b) Held accepts that care begins in particular relations but denies that it must end there. In The Ethics of Care she argues that the practices and values of care can be extended by stages outward from intimate relations: the family, and then the local community, and finally the global level, where caring institutions rather than caring individuals carry the moral load. So on Held's view the objection misfires: an adequate care ethics must address distant strangers, and can, because whole societies can be organized around caring values just as they are currently organized around, in her view, contract and self-interest. Her example is that care ethics would evaluate war and international conduct by the standards applicable to the behavior of individuals toward one another. But notice that this extension trades on shifting the bearer of care from persons to institutions and practices. Once the one-caring is a government or a distribution system, engrossment and motivational displacement have no evident application, since institutions lack the receptive attention that Noddings builds her account on. So the extension works only by leaving behind the very relation that, on Noddings's own account, grounds the value of caring in the first place.

(c) The empirical case for a distinct 'different voice' was undermined early. Gilligan's claim that Kohlberg's stages scored women as morally immature rested on studies whose sex differences disappeared once education and occupation were controlled; a meta-analysis of over a hundred samples, published in 1984, found no significant sex differences in moral reasoning, and the consensus since has been that Gilligan's original finding is one of psychology's best-known failed replications, comparable in status to the bystander-apathy studies. But Gilligan's point was a claim about theme rather than about gender, and the empirical weakness of the gender claim does not by itself decide whether the voice-of-care perspective is a legitimate alternative to the justice perspective."
