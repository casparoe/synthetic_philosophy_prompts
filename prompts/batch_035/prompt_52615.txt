I am halfway through a trilogy about a civilisation that fails, and I have begun to suspect that I do not know what the word 'collapse' means well enough to write its ending. Rather than read another shelf of archaeology, I would like to set out my verdicts on the historical cases and see whether they add up to a principle, and I would be grateful if you would do the sifting.

My verdicts, stated plainly. The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean is a collapse in the fullest sense: the palaces burn, the administrative scripts vanish, the trade in tin and copper comes apart, and it takes centuries for anything comparable to re-emerge — that the villages carried on and the peasants barely noticed has never struck me as counting against the verdict. The fall of the western Roman Empire is likewise a collapse, even though the eastern half flourished for another thousand years and the Latin of the Gaulish countryside simply wore down into French. The Black Death, by contrast, is not a collapse, even though it killed a far larger share of its population than the fall of Rome did: half of Europe dies and within a few generations the same institutions, the same liturgy, the same jokes more or less continue. Easter Island I count as a collapse, and a paradigmatic one, because the losses were irreversible — the forests, the canoes they made possible, the whole moai-building way of life — even though the people themselves survived, adapted, and are very much still there. The Classic Maya I cannot make up my mind about: the southern lowlands empty, the monuments stop, kingship ends, and yet I keep feeling that 'collapse' is the wrong word, since the northern cities went on to considerable splendour and the Maya are not a vanished people but a living one — it feels more like the fall of a particular political order than the failure of a society. And the Highland Clearances, which emptied a whole country of its way of life without war or famine or pestilence, strike me as something other than a collapse, though I cannot say why without sullying the verdict with special pleading.

Here is the tentative principle I have been nursing, such as it is: a society collapses when it irreversibly loses capacities it once took for granted — and the more central the capacity, the more complete the collapse. It seems to explain Easter Island and the Black Death neatly enough, and I can live with what it says about Rome. But I notice that it sits badly with my Bronze Age verdict, since literacy and long-distance trade did come back, in altered form, within a few centuries; on my own principle the burning of the palaces ought to come out as a transformation rather than a collapse, and I do not want to say that. And I cannot see that it delivers the Maya verdict at all, given how structurally similar the Maya case is to the Roman one — a half falls, a half endures, the language persists — where my judgements run in opposite directions.

So: is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or a small set of candidates, with the case that motivates each clause? Where my tentative principle fails, I would like to know how to repair it or what to replace it with. I would also like its verdict on two cases I have not decided. The first is Angkor, whose hydraulic city was abandoned to the forest while the Khmer people, language and kingship carried on — collapse or mere change? The second is the ending of my own third volume, in which the cities empty and the script is lost, but the literature survives in oral form and the farmers keep the old calendar: I want to know whether I have written a collapse or only a metamorphosis, and whether any honest principle can call it the former without also calling the Black Death one. Finally, tell me plainly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and which I ought simply to give up.
