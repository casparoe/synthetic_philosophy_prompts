A friend in my philosophy of mind reading group gave me the following account of the Dennett–Searle dispute over original versus derived intentionality, and I'd like to know how much of it I can trust before I lean on it in discussion:

"Searle's Chinese Room argument was meant to show that running the right program is never enough for understanding: whatever meaning a computer's symbols have is derived intentionality — borrowed from the programmers and users, the way a stop sign means stop only because we interpret it that way. Brains are different: they have original intentionality, Searle's term for the creative, self-generated kind of meaning that invents content rather than receiving it from outside, the way an author coins a new word. Dennett tried to break this distinction with his two-bitser: an Australian vending machine built to accept a particular two-bit coin. Ship it to a country where a different but physically indistinguishable coin circulates and it will happily take the foreign coin — so, Dennett says, the machine is literally wrong, mistaken about what it has been fed. That shows the machine really does have intentional states, and Dennett then turned the screw on Searle: your own brain's intentionality is derived too — handed down by evolution and training, just as the machine's is derived from its designers. So the original/derived distinction collapses, and the moral is that nobody has real, intrinsic understanding: all intentionality is as-if, all the way down. Most philosophers of mind now read this exchange as a clean win for Dennett."

I'm inclined to side with Dennett, so I worry I'll swallow this too uncritically. In particular, I suspect the summary equivocates between saying the two-bitser has derived intentionality and saying it has none at all, so I'd like you to be precise about where Dennett himself stands on the machine's status, and about what Searle's "original" is actually meant to contrast with.
