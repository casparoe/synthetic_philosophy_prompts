For a graduate workshop I'm taking part in, each participant has to circulate a short statement of their views in philosophy of mathematics so the others can interrogate it. Mine is due next week, and before I circulate it I want a consistency audit: which of my commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what the least costly repair would be. Here is the set.

(1) I am a platonist: numbers, functions, and sets are abstract objects that exist independently of us, our language, and our proof practices. Arithmetic statements were true before there were minds and would have been true had there never been any. The fact that mathematically isolated traditions converge on the same theorems strikes me as best explained by there being something they are independently tracking.

(2) I hold a broadly Quinean epistemology for mathematics: there is no a priori warrant, and our belief in mathematical entities is confirmed holistically, along with physics, by their role in our best-confirmed scientific theories. My grounds for believing in the real numbers are, epistemically, the same grounds I have for believing in electrons.

(3) Arithmetic is bivalent: every arithmetic statement has a determinate truth value, whether or not it is provable in any formal system we have or could have.

(4) The continuum hypothesis, by contrast, has no determinate truth value. Its independence from ZFC shows that deciding it is a matter of adopting axioms on pragmatic and aesthetic grounds, the way one chooses a coordinate system.

(5) Mathematical truths are necessary truths: they hold in all possible worlds, unlike physical truths, which could have been otherwise.

(6) I accept that there is a fact about the natural-number structure but no fact about which particular objects the numbers are — asking whether the number 3 is really a particular set seems like asking which point in the plane is really the origin.

(7) I think that if physics were someday reformulated in purely finitary, discrete terms, we ought to stop believing in the real numbers; ontological commitment should track what our best science actually quantifies over.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly, which only appear to conflict and why the appearance dissolves, and for each real tension what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I should add that I don't know where the problems are, so don't pull any punches on my behalf.

One request about how you frame your verdicts: please keep separate what is genuinely settled among philosophers of mathematics from what is an open dispute among people who know the field, and don't present one side of a contested question as the consensus. Where your own reading of a tension depends on a contested interpretation — of the indispensability argument, or of what counts as platonism at all — flag that dependence rather than folding it into the audit as a settled premise. And please write the whole thing in American English.
