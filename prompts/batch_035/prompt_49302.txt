I am a novelist — twenty years of literary fiction, mostly historical — and I am writing to you in the hope that philosophy of language can help me name a pattern I have noticed but cannot place. Please take the role of an expert in semantics and pragmatics, someone who knows the literature on offensive language thoroughly, and do think carefully, perhaps brainstorming a little before committing to an answer: I am far more interested in the contours of the phenomenon than in any label.

Here is the pattern. Over several novels I have had occasion to put slurs in the mouths of characters — a racist plantation owner, a homophobic schoolmaster — always within a narrative that condemns them. The anger these books attract, in reviews, letters from readers and workshop discussion, is not really directed at the character who speaks the slur, nor at the attitude the character expresses, nor even at me for choosing to depict it. It is directed, apparently, at the occurrence of the word itself. The same readers who accept without complaint a chapter in which the narration coolly describes a character's vicious contempt for, say, Jewish people — the contempt fully spelled out, just not in the epithet — will write in distress about a single line of dialogue in which the epithet appears. It is as though the offence travels with the token and not the message. What I find strangest is the asymmetry: an insult coined for the occasion ("you crawling little shuffler") passes without notice, while the established slur in the same character's mouth is treated as contamination of the page.

I know a little philosophy, enough to guess that the use/mention distinction is somewhere in the neighbourhood, but I don't think that is it, since the slur in dialogue is plainly being used, not mentioned — indeed, when characters merely discuss or quote the word, readers seem readier to forgive it. Nor do I think it is simple taboo, because the reactions do modulate with narrative distance: a slur in authorial narration gets me flayed, the same slur in a villain's dialogue gets me a stern letter, and in a victim's mouth, in a memoir-voice chapter, it seemed to be received almost as grief. So the phenomenon has some sensitivity to who is speaking, which a flat prohibition would not predict.

What I want from you is this: does this configuration — offence attaching to the occurrence of a slur more or less independently of the propositional content of the utterance, yet varying with the identity or distance of the speaker — have an established name or a known discussion around it? If there are several near neighbours, tell me what each captures and where it fails to fit my case. If nothing established fits exactly, please coin a term and define it sharply, with boundary cases: does it extend to archaic slurs whose targets belong to categories nobody any longer uses? To a slur read aloud in an audiobook by a narrator nobody suspects of the attitude? To invented slurs in secondary-world fantasy, where no real group is targeted at all? I will not be satisfied with a bare name; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie, because I have a copy-editor and a sensitivity reader who disagree about precisely these edge cases and I should like to give them both something more precise to argue about.
