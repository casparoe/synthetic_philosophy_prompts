Write a philosophical dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about how practical stakes interact with knowledge claims.

The two characters:

- Ada defends epistemic contextualism. She holds that the truth of an utterance of the form "S knows that p" depends partly on the conversational context of the person making the attribution, so that the same sentence can be true as uttered by one speaker and false as uttered by another, even when both are talking about the same subject at the same time.
- Ben defends subject-sensitive invariantism, a form of pragmatic encroachment. He holds that whether S knows that p depends on S's own practical situation — on how much turns on S's being right about p — and not on the context of whoever happens to be talking about S. On his view, a given knowledge attribution has a single, context-invariant truth value.

The dialogue takes place over coffee after a colloquium, and centers on two cases:

Case 1 (varying stakes for the subject). Mei has checked her bank's website once and seen that the branch is open on Saturdays. In the low-stakes version of the case, she and her partner are depositing a paycheck on Friday afternoon; if the branch were unexpectedly closed, they could simply return on Monday with no real consequences. In the high-stakes version, a payment must clear by Sunday or their mortgage goes into default. In both versions, her partner asserts, "Mei knows the bank will be open tomorrow."

Case 2 (varying stakes for the attributor). A structural engineer, Priya, has checked her load-bearing calculation once and is fully confident that the bridge design is safe. Her manager, who must decide whether to sign off on the design, says "Priya knows the design is safe" while weighing whether to demand a second, costly round of review. Later, at a party, with nothing riding on the question, the same manager says the same thing about the same once-checked calculation.

Requirements for the dialogue:

- Both characters should be equally competent philosophers: neither should be a strawman, and neither should capitulate for no good reason.
- The conversation should involve genuine back-and-forth. In particular, each character should press the other with the case that most favors their own view (Ben with Case 1, Ada with Case 2) and then respond to the other's actual reply, rather than talking past each other.
- Each character should articulate the strongest available response to the case that initially seems to favor their opponent — for example, how Ben handles the fact that the manager's two utterances in Case 2 can seem to differ in truth value, and how Ada handles the fact that in Case 1 it is Mei's situation, not the partner's, that seems to drive the intuitive difference.
- The dialogue may end unresolved, but it should end with the two of them pinpointing, as precisely as possible, the further question whose answer would decide the matter between them.
- Keep the register conversational but philosophically careful: the characters should be allowed to use examples, reformulations, and occasional concessions, not just exchange position statements.
