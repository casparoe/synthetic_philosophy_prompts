One exam question concerned the problem of logical omniscience, and I've reproduced the question and one student's answer below. The student wrote the answer in ninety minutes, without notes; the seminar covered possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, the standard closure principles, and several proposed remedies. Please grade the answer on a conventional letter or percentage scale, and then give me point-by-point comments that justify the grade: for each substantive claim, definition, or inferential move in the answer, tell me whether it is correct, and where it is not, say precisely what is wrong and what would have to change for the claim to be right. I'm especially interested in anything a hasty grader might wave through. Don't grade on style or organization; grade on philosophical and technical accuracy.

The exam question was:

"Philosophers and computer scientists often describe logical omniscience as a defect of standard epistemic logic. (a) Explain exactly how the problem arises from the formal machinery of possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, identifying the specific principles responsible. (b) Explain under what assumptions the problem is a genuine defect rather than a harmless idealization. (c) Critically discuss one or more proposed remedies, including what each costs us."

And the student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

"(a) Epistemic logic represents knowledge with Kripke's possible worlds semantics: each agent is assigned an accessibility relation R on a set of worlds, and the agent knows φ just in case φ is true at every world w' such that wRw'. On this semantics two principles come out valid. The first is closure under known implication: if the agent knows φ → ψ and knows φ, the agent knows ψ. The second is necessitation: if φ is a theorem of the logic, then the agent knows φ. Applying these repeatedly, every logical consequence of anything the agent knows is itself known, and every tautology is known outright. So someone who knows the axioms of arithmetic knows every theorem of arithmetic, however long its proof, and someone who knows where their keys are also knows each of the infinitely many tautologies conjoined with that fact. Since first-order theoremhood is undecidable, no finite agent, biological or mechanical, could meet these demands even in principle.

(b) Whether this matters depends on what the formalism is for. If epistemic logic is a theory of implicit knowledge — of what follows from what an agent has at its disposal — the closure principles are analytic and the idealization is harmless. This was essentially Hintikka's reaction: distinguish implicit from explicit knowledge, concede closure for the former, and relocate the modeling burden to the latter. But when the formalism is used descriptively — in game theory to model players' strategic reasoning, or in distributed computing to reason about what processors can infer from received messages — the gap between the ideal agent and the real one becomes a genuine defect, because theorems are then proved about agents that no actual player or processor resembles.

(c) Among the remedies, Fagin and Halpern's awareness logic is the most conservative. It supplements the model with awareness sets: the agent counts as knowing φ only if φ is true at all accessible worlds and φ is built only from vocabulary the agent is aware of at the world in question. An agent with no awareness of a predicate cannot be credited with knowledge of sentences involving it, however immediate their consequence of what it already knows. The cost is that awareness is a primitive: the framework stipulates what the agent can entertain but says nothing about what fixes the set, so it records bounded rationality rather than explaining it.

A more radical remedy is impossible worlds, due to Kripke in the very 1963 paper that introduced possible worlds semantics for modal logic: admit worlds at which the laws of logic themselves fail — worlds where φ and ¬φ can both hold — and let some of them be accessible from the actual world. A tautology can then fail at an accessible world, so the agent is no longer stuck knowing it. The cost is that one must somehow constrain the impossible worlds, or inference everywhere collapses.

Finally, one might suspect the whole problem evaporates for the Bayesian. Probabilities are graded rather than all-or-nothing, and the probability axioms impose no closure under known implication: an agent can coherently assign 0.6 to a long tautology it has not yet worked through, and revise upward once it sees the proof. Logical omniscience is thus an artifact of the modal logician's ideal agent, not of probabilism."
