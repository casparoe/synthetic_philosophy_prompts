Hilary Putnam argued in the 1960s that mental state types are multiply realizable. (a) Explain the multiple realizability argument against the type-identity theory of mind. (b) Evaluate the following inference: because mental state types are multiply realizable, the laws of a mature psychology will share no kinds with the laws of a mature neuroscience, and therefore psychology is an autonomous science whose generalizations can never be revised or disconfirmed by neuroscientific findings. Does multiple realizability, by itself, warrant this inference? Defend your answer with reference to at least one objection or complication discussed in the literature.

And here is the student's answer, transcribed in full:

Putnam's multiple realizability argument targets the type-identity theory, defended by U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart, according to which each mental state type is identical to some physical state type — roughly, pain is the firing of C-fibers. Putnam observed in his 1967 paper "The Nature of Mental States" that this cannot be right, because mental states are quite variable across species: humans, octopuses, and hypothetical silicon-based creatures could all be in pain despite having radically different internal constitutions. If pain were identical to a single physical type, then either these creatures would not really be in pain, or the identity theory would have to be abandoned. Since it is more plausible that octopuses feel pain than that they share our neural states, the identity theory is refuted.

The correct view is functionalism: what makes something a pain is its causal role — its typical causes, such as tissue damage, and its effects, such as withdrawal and distress, together with its relations to other mental states. A defender of the identity theory could reply that pain is identical to the disjunction of all its physical realizers, but this disjunctive move is unattractive, since disjunctive kinds do not support genuine laws and counterfactuals.

Because each mental type can be realized by many — perhaps indefinitely many — physical types, the kinds appearing in psychological laws will be functional kinds, and the kinds appearing in neurological laws will be physical kinds. The two sciences therefore share no kinds, and psychology is autonomous: neuroscience can describe the realizers of mental states, but it can never revise or disconfirm a psychological generalization, since psychological generalizations concern functional organization, not hardware.

Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument confirms this picture. Kim argues that since the physical realizer of a mental state is by itself causally sufficient for the behavior, the mental state, if distinct from its realizer, is causally idle — it is preempted. Kim concludes from this that functionalism is false and that we should be eliminativists, denying that mental states exist at all. Block's China-brain thought experiment further supports the functionalist: it demonstrates that any system implementing the right functional organization must thereby have a mind, which is why functionalists can say that even the population of China, if wired to implement the right causal organization, would literally have a mind. So the inference in the question is warranted: multiple realizability establishes the autonomy of psychology, and the arguments of Kim and Block reinforce the functionalist consensus.
