I sit on a working group reviewing early-childhood policy, and a colleague keeps pressing an argument I am not equipped to adjudicate. His reasoning, in brief: twin studies put the heritability of cognitive performance and educational attainment at roughly 0.6 in modern welfare states; heritability of such traits tends to be higher where environmental inequality is lower; therefore egalitarian interventions have already absorbed most of the accessible environmental variance, and further equalisation of children's environments will yield only ever-smaller reductions in inequality of outcomes. Let us call the conclusion C: in a present-day welfare state, additional large-scale equalisation of children's environments cannot reduce inequality in educational outcomes by more than a small fraction. My instinct is that C is wrong, and I would put my credence somewhere around 0.3, but I could not defend that number under questioning.

Please give me your own credence that C is true, decomposed rather than delivered bare. At minimum, assign a rough probability to each of the following, with one line of justification apiece:

1. The statistical claim: that a high within-population heritability actually constrains the achievable effect of interventions. My understanding is that heritability is a variance ratio tied to a particular population and its particular environmental spread, and that a trait can be highly heritable in one population yet shift dramatically across generations or settings — average height in the Netherlands being the stock example. But say whether the colleague's argument needs only the weaker claim that the environmental variance currently present in a welfare state is largely exhausted, and how defensible that weaker claim is.
2. The empirical premise: that heritability of these traits genuinely rises as environments equalise, and — crucially — that modern welfare states sit near the plateau rather than merely at the point in the curve that existing twin studies happen to have sampled.
3. The measurement question: whether twin-study estimates in the region of 0.6 can be taken at face value, given the equal-environments assumption, gene–environment correlation, assortative mating, and any gap between twin-based and molecularly derived estimates.
4. Whatever further terms you think belong in the decomposition — for instance, whether 'a small fraction' in the statement of C is doing the work of an unfalsifiable escape hatch, or whether the argument quietly slides between reducing variance and shifting the mean.

Then show how you combine these into your final credence for C, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move that term the most. Do not hedge into a refusal to give numbers; if a term resists quantification, give it anyway with a wide interval and say why. Please write your answer in British English.
