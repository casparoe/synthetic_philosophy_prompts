You are an experienced designer of classroom experiments in behavioural economics and social epistemology — someone who has run dozens of these sessions and knows precisely which ones collapse in front of a seminar. Design a 90-minute session for a university seminar of 20 students on social epistemology. The target concept is the information cascade: the phenomenon whereby rational agents, choosing sequentially after observing their predecessors' public choices but not their private signals, can end up herding on a belief that is probably wrong, with all subsequent private information being discarded.

The session plan must include:

1. **The core game.** An urn-guessing task: sequential public choice from private signals of a stated accuracy. Specify the exact signal accuracy you would use, and defend the choice against the obvious alternatives (a parameter that makes cascades too rare, or so certain that the phenomenon feels trivial). Use point scores rather than real money. Work out the Bayesian arithmetic for the first several decisions explicitly, so that the facilitator can show in the debrief the exact decision point at which the public history became self-sustaining — a miscalculated posterior is the classic way these sessions go wrong.

2. **A variant phase distinguishing informational herding from reputational herding.** Arrange conditions under which some participants are rewarded purely for accuracy and others for matching the majority, or for appearing competent to observers. Students should come away seeing that the two accounts of herding make different predictions and being able to say which one the class's own behaviour fits.

3. **A fragility demonstration.** Something arranged in advance that shatters a running cascade — for example, a single public revelation of a private signal, or a new entrant with no reputational stake — so that students feel the brittleness that the formal models predict alongside the apparent solidity of the herd.

4. **The debrief.** Map each phase onto the formal results: why a cascade can become self-sustaining after very few identical signals, why choices made inside a cascade carry no further information, and why the cascade can nevertheless lock the group onto the wrong option with high probability. Include an honest note on which features of real-world cascades the classroom cannot reproduce — genuine financial ruin, truly private information, thousands of predecessors — and which the facilitator must supply by assertion.

5. **A section on predicted failure modes on first run**, each with a remedy: students deliberating aloud and contaminating the signal structure, misunderstanding the payoff scheme, a cascade failing to form because early choosers second-guess themselves, and so on.

Before drafting, think carefully through the payoff design and the posterior calculations, and check that each phase's incentive structure actually pushes participants where you claim it does. Present only the finished session plan, beginning directly with the activity itself: no preamble, no restating of this brief, and no meta-commentary.
