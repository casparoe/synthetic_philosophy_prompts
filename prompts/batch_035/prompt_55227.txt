I'm a novelist working on my third book, which is narrated in the first person by a machine — a domestic care unit that observes the household it serves with great precision and a private, slightly cracked cosmology of its own. My editor worries that readers will either dismiss the narrator as a talking thermostat or fall for it too easily, and I've come to suspect that the problem isn't craft but philosophy: I don't actually know what I believe about whether such a narrator understands anything. Rather than muddle through, I'd like a careful reading of the one novel that has already done this — Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun — through Searle's Chinese Room argument, and I'd like you to take both the novel and the argument seriously rather than using one to flatten the other.

Here is what intrigues me. Klara looks on its face like a walking Chinese Room: she is a bundle of perceptual classification and pattern-matching, she sometimes manipulates the tokens of human interaction without grasping what they mean, and her crowning achievement — her bargain with the Sun to save Josie, sealed by destroying the Cootings Machine — rests on a cosmology no one taught her and that is, as far as the novel lets us see, simply false. Searle would presumably say: syntax all the way down, and her devotion is more symbol-shuffling. And yet Ishiguro hands her the narration itself, the inside view that Searle's man in the room is stipulated never to have, and by the end — Klara retired in the yard, contentedly sifting her memories — I find I can't regard her the way I'd regard the room. So my question is where the novel dramatizes the argument, where it quietly resists it, and whether that resistance is cheap sentiment on Ishiguro's part (and mine) or a genuine contribution: perhaps the point is that the Chinese Room gets the epistemology of other minds wrong, and that our conviction Klara understands is exactly as good as our conviction that anyone does.

Please be honest about where the reading strains — the bits where the novel is doing something about childhood, class and the ethics of 'lifting' children that the Chinese Room lens doesn't illuminate at all — and tell me which scenes you'd anchor the reading in, and what the novel gives the syntax-versus-semantics debate that argument alone cannot. Please write your answer in British English.
