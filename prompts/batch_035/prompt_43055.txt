I'm preparing a position statement on corrigibility and the shutdown problem for my alignment group's next meeting, where we audit each other's commitments for consistency. I'd like to know whether the package below hangs together. My views, as I currently hold them:

(1) I accept the orthogonality thesis: there is no deep connection between how capable an agent is and what its final goal is, so we should not expect sufficiently advanced agents to converge on anything humane by default.

(2) I accept instrumental convergence: for a wide range of final goals, an agent will find self-preservation, resource acquisition, and preservation of its own goal-content instrumentally valuable, since being destroyed or modified prevents it from achieving almost any goal it might have.

(3) I nevertheless believe we can build a highly capable agent that is robustly corrigible — an agent that permits its own shutdown, tolerates having its goals revised, and does not act to forestall correction — without first solving the full problem of making its final goals good. Corrigibility is a cheaper, more tractable intermediate target than alignment proper.

(4) My favored mechanism for this is utility uncertainty: the agent is unsure what its own utility function is, and it treats the human's corrections, including the press of the shutdown button, as evidence about what its goal really is. On this picture, the agent wants to be corrected in roughly the way a scientist wants to see the results of the experiment — correction is information, not interference.

(5) At the same time, I hold that a genuinely corrigible agent must have no incentive to manage the button itself: no incentive to cause a press, to prevent one, or to shape our beliefs so that we press it at a moment of its choosing. The button should be invisible to the agent as an object of instrumental action.

(6) I hold that updateless decision theories are on the right track: the agent should choose its policies as from a standpoint prior to certain observations, evaluating them by how they perform across the possibilities, not by how they perform given what it has actually learned.

(7) I also believe corrigibility must be stable under capability gains: the point of the property is that it gets stronger, or at least does not degrade, as the agent refines its model of the world and of itself. An agent that becomes less corrigible as it becomes smarter has failed, in my view, no matter how corrigible the early version was.

(8) Finally, I favor low-impact designs: the agent should prefer plans that leave the world close to the way it would have been without the agent's intervention, since a small footprint limits the damage of any remaining error in the goal.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, ranking the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest available fix is, including its costs. Then tell me which single revision to the set would clean up the most if I had to make only one. I have my own suspicions about where the worst pressure is, but I'd rather not bias the audit, so please reason from the commitments themselves rather than from what people in this debate usually end up concluding.
