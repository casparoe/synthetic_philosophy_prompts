As part of a mathematics elective at my school, we have spent the past several weeks on game theory: the Prisoner's Dilemma and Chicken, Nash equilibria, and the folk theorem. Our teacher has repeatedly emphasized that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between rational players, mutual defection is the outcome, and that getting cooperation out of such a situation requires either repeated play with a future shadow, or an enforceable contract that changes the payoffs. Last week an older cousin who works in AI sent me a link to a blog post about a concept called "safe Pareto improvements," which he says is used in that literature to reason about standoff situations between artificial agents. The post's central paragraph reads as follows: "A safe Pareto improvement is a trick for making everyone in a standoff better off without anyone having to trust anyone. The two players sign a binding agreement to replace their game with a new one whose payoffs are, in every cell of the matrix, at least as good for both players as before. Since every possible outcome is improved for both sides, agreeing costs nothing, and because the agreement is enforced, neither player can be exploited by a partner who reneges — that is what the 'safe' means. Applied to the Prisoner's Dilemma, this lets the players escape the dilemma entirely: instead of the both-defect equilibrium, the new game locks in mutual cooperation, all for the cost of putting the deal on paper. Better still, the trick requires no assumptions about how the players would have played or what they believe — it works for any game."

I don't have the background to evaluate this, and something about it strikes me as too good to be true: if a piece of paper could turn mutual defection into mutual cooperation in a one-shot dilemma, I am not sure why we would have spent so long on the folk theorem and on institutional design for enforcing agreements. At the same time, I am inclined to believe the "no trust needed" part, since it matches what our teacher said about contracts being what allows cooperation in one-shot games. Would you please go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me which parts a game theorist would accept, which parts conflate things the actual proposal keeps apart, and which parts are simply wrong? In particular, I would like to know whether the definition of the "safe" part is being glossed correctly, whether the Prisoner's Dilemma really is the showcase case in which this device delivers the cooperative outcome, and whether the closing claim that the trick is assumption-free and works for any game follows from anything true in the summary. Please finish by saying whether the post's overall conclusion — that dilemma-type situations are basically solved by signing the right agreement — survives once the misrepresentations are corrected.
