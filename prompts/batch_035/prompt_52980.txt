I recently joined a small working group drafting a communication charter for highly capable conversational AI systems, and a dispute has split the group in a way I'm finding hard to adjudiate. The clause under contention — internally we've been calling it the persuasive-overflow clause — would require that such a system never deploy an argument whose persuasive force on a typical user exceeds its evidential force: even when the balance of evidence genuinely supports a conclusion, the system should not move users to that conclusion more confidently than the evidence warrants. One faction, led by our most senior member, holds that the only clean way to satisfy this is a strictly minimal register: no vivid examples, no concessive framing, no appeals to what the user already cares about, since with a sufficiently capable system even entirely true statements, arranged skillfully enough, function as manipulation — the asymmetry of skill does the work that deception would otherwise do. The other faction holds that this misunderstands how humans absorb information: a deliberately unengaging delivery near-guarantees that users will misread probabilistic and comparative claims, so a system that knows this and communicates flatly anyway is being knowingly opaque — paternalism dressed up as restraint. Their rejoinder to the neutrality ideal is that there is no neutral register to retreat to: every choice of framing is a choice, including deliberately flat ones, and a flat register is itself a rhetorical posture that signals (often falsely) that nothing much is at stake.

Since I've been tasked with writing the commentary that will accompany the final clause, I want to see the two positions pressed against each other properly rather than merely summarized side by side. Write a dialogue between a defender of the minimal-register view (call her Ines, a decision theorist by training) and a defender of what he calls audience-aware honesty (call him Marek, who came to the field from medicine, where he watched clinicians learn that the truth, said badly, can mislead as reliably as a lie). Make both of them genuinely competent: neither should concede too easily, and each should respond to the other's actual points — for instance, Ines should have to face the observation that she cannot specify the flat register without smuggling in judgments about her audience, and Marek should have to face that his position seems to license tailoring candor to the user, which is uncomfortably close to the thing the charter exists to forbid. At some point, have the dialogue engage the following worry, which none of us has resolved: if the system can predict how any phrasing will land on any given user, then the distinction between informing and persuading may collapse entirely — every utterance becomes an intervention on the user's beliefs, selected partly for its predicted effects, and Ines and Marek may be arguing about where to draw a line on a continuum with no natural joints. End the dialogue not with a winner but with the two of them agreeing on what they take to be the sharpest question whose answer would most help their case — and let them disagree about which of them it favors.
