Longtime reader here — SSC since like 2015, ACX now, LessWrong when I'm feeling brave — and somewhere along the way I picked up a YIMBY arc and started actually going to my city council's zoning meetings. I need help predicting a vote, and I suspect the interesting part is figuring out what rule the council is even running, because their track record so far doesn't pin it down.

Their verdicts, in rough order:

1. Approved upzoning a commercial arterial from 2 stories to 6, over the business association's objections, because it's next to the light rail station.
2. Approved backyard cottages / ADUs (up to 2 units) citywide with basically zero fight.
3. Rejected a 5-story, 80-unit apartment building that would've replaced two houses at the edge of a single-family neighborhood. Reasons given: parking, shadows, "character of the area." Organized neighbor opposition, like 40 people in matching shirts.
4. Approved a 200-unit mixed-use tower downtown after the developer agreed to 15% below-market units and funded a traffic study or crosswalk or whatever.
5. Rejected converting the municipal golf course to housing, 6-0. Open space concerns, and notably there weren't even organized neighbors on this one, just the parks commission being mad.
6. Approved a 60-bed shelter in a light industrial zone, over scattered opposition, because "there's nowhere else zoned for it."

The new case: a church wants to build 70 units of income-restricted affordable housing on its own surface parking lot. The lot is technically in a single-family zone (churches get weird zoning), one block off the arterial, no transit station nearby. Church leadership is fully on board, the units are at 60% of area median income, and the neighbors have organized — yard signs, a petition, the whole thing — arguing it's out of character and will eat street parking.

So what's the council going to do, and more importantly, what rule are they running? I can see at least three that fit the record: (a) the proximity principle — density goes where the stations and arterials are, single-family interiors are off limits; (b) the incumbent veto principle — they approve whatever organized residents don't block (with the golf course as maybe a different thing entirely, and the shelter as a counterexample I can't square); (c) the concessions principle — big projects get approved when they can extract something, affordability or studies or shiny crosswalks. The church case is affordable AND in a single-family interior AND faces organized opposition, so the rules point different directions. Give me your best guess with your reasoning, tell me which prior case is the closes analog and why, and then design the hypothetical next case that would best distinguish between the rules — i.e., if they approved a market-rate building in a single-family interior I'd learn one thing, but what single case would carry the most information?

Also, and this is optional but I'd genuinely like your take: I've read enough of the "who has a right to the city" discourse that I can't unsee it. If the real rule is the incumbent veto, is that just democracy working as intended — the affected parties show up and vote — or is it a structural bias toward whoever already showed up, given that the 70 hypothetical future tenants of that church lot get no say at all in the meeting about their homes? Doesn't need to dominate your answer, but I want to know where you land.

(For context if it matters: medium-sized city, 7 council members, no obvious landlord bloc, and a mayor who campaigned on "smart growth" and then... this.)
