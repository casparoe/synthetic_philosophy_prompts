I got into a long argument on a subreddit about inheritance tax last week and someone shut me down with a one-liner that I can't stop thinking about. I'd said something like: if the tax destroys value on the way through (rich people dodge it, lawyers get paid, family businesses get flogged off badly) and the revenue raised doesn't fully make up for that, how is it still obviously a good thing? And this guy replies: "benefiting people matters more the worse off they are." So a hundred quid to someone on the breadline counts for more than a hundred quid to a millionaire, even if some money leaks out of the system on the way. I actually think I agree with him?? But then two other people piled in using the exact same sentence to mean what sounded like different things, and I realised I don't know what the sentence is claiming.

One of them said it's just what everyone already believes, because even a pure "add up all the wellbeing and maximise it" type has to admit a hundred quid does more for someone skint than for someone rich — that's just how money works, nothing moral about it. Another said no, it's the opposite of that, it's a specifically moral claim: the same sized benefit is worth more, morally, when it lands on a worse-off person, regardless of how much good it does them personally. And a third guy used it to say we're obliged to help the very worst off first and everything else is second in the queue — like you shouldn't even look at a million people's moderate problems until you've sorted out the single worst-off person on the planet. That one can't be right, but I couldn't see how to get from the sentence to anything that rules it out.

There was also a bit where the first guy claimed this one-liner is how you care about equality without the bad part of caring about equality — apparently there's an old objection that if inequality is bad in itself, then making a rich person poorer while nobody gains anything counts as an improvement, which he thought was mad, and he said the one-liner avoids that whole problem. I understood maybe 60% of that exchange.

For context, I have no philosophy background whatsoever, I drive a forklift for a living and think about this stuff on my breaks. So plain language please, but don't dumb it down either. What I want is: take the sentence "benefiting people matters more the worse off they are" and lay out as a list all the distinct precise claims it could actually be making — from the reading where it's a total platitude nobody would deny, up to the properly radical ones — and for each one say whether it's plausible, and whether it would actually support what my guy was using it for (defending the tax even with the leakage). Also flag which readings would and wouldn't get you out of the "make the rich poorer, nobody gains" problem he was on about, and whether any reading actually forces that strict queue idea the third guy was pushing. And one more thing thats been bugging me: when it says "worse off", worse off than who? Worse off across their whole life or just right now? Worse off compared to other people, or below some absolute line like not having enough to eat? If different readings of the sentence need different answers to that, say so, because that seems like where it all falls apart or comes together.
