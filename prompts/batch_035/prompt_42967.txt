You are a professional philosopher who specializes in utilitarian ethics, especially suffering-focused and negative-utilitarian views, and you've also spent time advising foundations and non-profits. So you know both the theory and how decisions actually get made in a grants committee. I want you to think carefully before answering — maybe brainstorm two or three candidate procedures first, sketch why each works or doesn't, and then commit to one and write it up properly.

Here's my situation. I'm on the grants committee of a small animal welfare foundation. We fund something like 20 projects a year, mostly welfare improvements in farming and stray animal care. Over the years I've become pretty convinced that suffering matters in a way happiness doesn't fully match — a world with less agony seems better to me even at the cost of less joy, and whenever I try to figure out how much bad stuff a good thing can offset I just come up empty. So I'm basically somewhere in the negative utilitarian family, but I genuinely don't know which version, and I don't want to have to settle that question to make funding decisions. We meet quarterly, we get 30-50 applications per cycle, each one gets maybe 15 minutes of my attention, and I'm the only person on the committee with any philosophy background. So whatever you give me has to be runnable by a smart layperson with a checklist, not a treatise.

What I'm asking for: a step-by-step decision procedure for ranking or rejecting welfare proposals, derived from suffering-focused ethics but actually usable. It should tell me what questions to ask of each proposal in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure terminates in a plain "reject," and where it has to hand the decision back to the committee because the question is about values rather than facts.

There are some things it has to handle, because they come up constantly:

1. Tradeoffs between many mild pains and a few severe ones — dehorning dairy calves without pain relief versus widespread lameness in a huge flock, that kind of thing. Any suffering-focused view owes me an answer to whether ten thousand small pains can outweigh one agony, and I need that to be a rule, not a vibe.

2. Proposals that create happiness rather than remove suffering — enrichment programs, "joy" grants. I suspect my own view ranks these near zero, but half my committee disagrees with me, and I don't want the procedure to smuggle my conclusion in. Flag where it forces the issue.

3. Death without suffering. Culling, euthanasia. Under a strictly suffering-focused view a painless death doesn't seem bad in itself — nobody suffers — which is going to make the procedure approve things that would horrify the public and, honestly, probably me too. I've heard there's an old argument that the strictest version of this view ends up endorsing painlessly wiping out all life, and I need the procedure to not have that consequence, or at least to visibly block it and show where.

4. Downstream risks of creating worse suffering — e.g., a sterilization program succeeds, and the city responds with something far crueler than what we replaced.

Once you've got the procedure, run it on three proposals (details changed, but these are basically real things we've seen):
(a) funding pain relief protocols for piglet castration in a supply chain that will otherwise do it without anesthetic;
(b) an enrichment program for shelter dogs — toys, scent work, play yards;
(c) a mass culling of street dogs in a city dealing with rabies, where the alternative on the table is catch-vaccinate-release plus the ongoing suffering of the surviving strays.

One of these should come out with an answer I won't like. Make it clear which one that is and why the procedure gives it anyway — don't soften it.

Also: be honest about where the procedure misfires, where a step is really a judgment call dressed up as a rule, and what the whole thing simply can't do. And write the entire answer in American English. Keep it practical — I'll be honest with you, if you bury me in theory the committee will just ignore it — but I'll read the reasoning, so include enough to convince me the simplifications don't butcher the underlying view. Definately don't skip the part about where it breaks.
