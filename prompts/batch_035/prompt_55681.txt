econ phd student here (mechanism design, so my rawls is admittedly secondhand). over the last year i've basically committed to a version of the difference principle that i think is the actually interesting one: not as a test for judging individual transactions or tax policies one at a time, but as a selection rule over whole institutional designs — pick the feasible basic structure whose worst-off representative position is best off, and stop asking whether any particular inequality inside it is 'justified' piecemeal. i like this because it turns a vague moral intuition into something you could almost operationalize, which is the kind of thing that appeals to me professionally.

what i want to know is what actually counts against it. not the standard nozick stuff about patterns and liberty that everyone has heard a million times — i want the pressures a serious rawlsian would themselves concede. some things i half see already and want sorted properly: the 'feasible' qualifier seems to do a LOT of work, and i worry that defenders just redraw the feasible set whenever the principle seems to bite, so that it never risks anyting — has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that fatal for a view of this kind, or just what you should expect from a political principle as opposed to an empirical theory? also: how do you even identify the least advantaged representative person when primary goods trade off against each other — someone with more income but fewer powers and prerogatives of office, how do you rank them — and does the answer to that index problem smuggle in prior commitments that quietly do all the real work? and intergenerationally: if you can only benefit the worst-off living people by consuming capital that immiserates the future worst-off, what does the principle even say — is the just-savings qualification a genuine repair, or a patch that concedes the principle isn't actually fundamental?

rank the defeaters for me from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones rawlsians bite bullets on versus which they wave off, and tell me whether there's any case where this view commits to something checkable that its rivals deny — some place it genuinely sticks its neck out. my advisor thinks the whole thing is just leximin with institutions doing the hand-waving, and honestly some days i can't argue with her.
