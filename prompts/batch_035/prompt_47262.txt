I have no academic background — my training is in plumbing, and I run a small shop — but free will is the one topic I have never stopped chewing on since my twenties, mostly on my own, and I have finally managed to write down where I actually stand. What I want is not an essay on free will; I want an audit of my own position. Please tell me which of my commitments genuinely conflict, which only look like they conflict, how severe each real conflict is, and what it would cost me to fix each one. Here is the set, numbered so you can refer to them:

1. Neither determinism nor indeterminism seems capable of giving us the deep kind of freedom. If my choices flow from my character and circumstances, I don't see how I am the true origin of them; and if some quantum dice-roll occasionally tips the scales, a choice settled by chance is no more mine than one settled by prior causes.

2. I cannot see how anyone could be ultimately responsible, in the deepest sense of deserving what they get. To be responsible that way, you would have had to have chosen the character that does your choosing, and the regress never bottoms out: your genes, your upbringing, your earliest decisions — none of it was up to you.

3. I accept the force of the thought experiment where a neuroscientist would have stepped in and forced your decision had you been about to decide otherwise, but never had to, because you decided on your own. In that situation you seem fully responsible despite having no genuine alternative. So I no longer believe that being able to do otherwise is what responsibility requires.

4. And yet I believe, firmly, in differences in desert. The cold, calculating murderer deserves far worse than the man who snapped after a lifetime of abuse; and the drunk driver who kills a child deserves far worse than the equally drunk driver who happens to make it home without incident.

5. I think Daniel Dennett was right that the productive question is which kinds of free will are worth wanting, and that the practical kind — being the sort of agent who can take advice, respond to threats and incentives, and be governed by reasons — is the one that morality and the law actually run on. I don't think that is changing the subject.

6. I believe addiction and mental illness reduce responsibility by degrees, and that the degree tracks how much the person could still respond to reasons at the moment of acting.

7. I hold that the real justification of punishment is protection and reform, not giving people what they deserve — and yet when I imagine the calculating murderer sincerely reformed and quietly released after two years, something in me insists that justice has not been done.

Go through the set pair by pair where it matters. Tell me which tensions are real and which dissolve once you look closely, rank the real ones by severity rather than treating them all the same, and tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence for the least cost. I honestly do not know where the trouble is, so don't go easy on any of them.
