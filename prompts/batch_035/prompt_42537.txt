My two coworkers got into a huge argument at our team dinner about whether the app we work on is still "the same app" it was at launch. Some background first: it launched 8 years ago as a Rails monolith for sharing photos. Since then it pivoted to payments, the backend got rewritten twice (Rails -> Java services -> Go), the frontend was rebuilt three times, and literally zero original lines of code survive. But it's the same Postgres database (schema migrated in place, never restored from scratch), same user accounts, same product name, and an unbroken commit history if you count the repo moves.

Round 1: Dana said obviously it's the same app — nothing ever stopped and got swapped for a new thing, it just changed gradually. It's like a person whose cells and opinions get replaced over a decade. Sameness = unbroken lineage.

Round 2: Miguel said lineage is way too cheap. The build server produces a new binary every night and nobody thinks a new app pops into existence at each deploy. And if a competitor shipped a byte-identical copy of our current build, we'd call it a knockoff, not the same app, even though it's a perfect match. So sameness can't be tracked by the artifact at all — the app is more like a plan or a project, and "same app" means same project executing over time.

Round 3: Dana hit back with our pivot. When we went from photos to payments, the "plan" flipped almost entirely — different customers, different features, half the current team has never even seen the original product. If sameness were the plan, that should've been the moment the old app died and a new one started, but everyone (including Miguel at the time) kept saying "the app". So Miguel's rule fails on our own history.

Round 4: Miguel said fine, but then Dana's rule has the fork problem: if some disgruntled engineer had forked the code at the pivot and kept the photo-sharing app alive under a new name, Dana's unbroken-lineage rule would now have two equally good candidates for "the same app" — and identity can't just split like that. And if sameness is only "whatever we keep calling the same", then the marketing line "the same app since 2016" is vacuous, which it clearly isn't — investors treat it as a real fact about the company, not a naming convention.

They gave up eventually and someone muttered that this is the endurantism vs perdurantism thing philosophers argue about, but none of us actually know what those words mean, which was definately not helping.

Can you go through their four moves and rate who's right at each step? And please actually commit to a verdict, don't give me a menu of options and make me pick: is our app the same app or not, and in the fork case (both still running today), which of the two is the real continuation of the original? Also tell me whether those philosophy labels genuinely apply here or whether the guy just made them up to sound smart.
