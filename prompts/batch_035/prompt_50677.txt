For my philosophy elective our teacher handed out a photocopy of this essay by Moritz Schlick (the Vienna Circle guy, normally all about science and verification, so everyone was surprised he wrote about the meaning of life) called On the Meaning of Life, and earlier this semester we read chunks of Tolstoy's A Confession, and the two basically hate each other. Our final assignment is to write a dialogue between two thinkers we covered, and the rule is neither guy can be a pushover, so I picked these two, but I'm kind of stuck on making the debate feel real instead of them talking past each other.

Quick summary of where each of them is at, from my notes (sorry if I mangle anything): Schlick argues that if you ask what life is FOR, like what final goal it all serves, you never get an answer, because whatever goal you name, you can ask again why that goal matters, and so on forever. So meaning can't come from life being a means to some further end. Instead it shows up when an activity is an end in itself: work is valuable only as a means, play is valuable for its own sake, and the fullest version of that is the youthful attitude of enjoying the thing itself instead of treating it as preparation for something later. Meaning in life, not of life, and it lives in the present. Tolstoy meanwhile went through this crisis where he realized that reasoning honestly about life ends in absurdity -- everything you achieve gets erased, humanity itself gets erased, so nothing finite can ground anything. He goes through the ways people cope (just not thinking about it, grabbing pleasures while you can, toughing it out anyway, or checking out entirely) and finds them all empty, and what saved him was the irrational faith of the laboring peasants that life has meaning through God, which reason can't justify but without which nothing means anything. So for him meaning has to come from somewhere beyond finite life, and he would 100% look at Schlick's "play in the present" and call it the epicurean escape, a deception that works fine untill you remember death.

Write the dialogue where they actually engage each other's points. Tolstoy should press Schlick on whether play is parasitic on seriousness (a game only feels like it matters because something real is at stake, and that mattering can't itself just be a game) and on whether enjoying the moment can survive honest reflection about the fact that you'll die and everything gets erased. Schlick should press back on the regress -- why must value eventually point outside all experience, isn't faith just naming the thing it was supposed to explain -- and on whether a meaning that only kicks in after death or in eternity is even meaningful to a life being lived now. Both get their best shots in, no one converts, and it should end with each of them saying exactly what they think the other's fatal blind spot is.

Also since part of my grade is fairness to both thinkers: after the dialogue, add a short honest note telling me (a) where you gave one of them a move the real thinker didn't actually have or that's a contested reading of them, and (b) what the strongest reply to each would be that the OTHER guy never got to use in the dialogue -- basically the places my dialogue is most likely to have gone wrong. Don't sugarcoat it, my teacher will notice if a side got rigged.
