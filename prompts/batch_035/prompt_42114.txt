My mom died in February, and ever since then I keep doing this thing where I put on the most devastating songs I know and listen to them on loop for like an hour. Not sad-adjacent songs, I mean the ones that make me cry basically every time. And I genuinely look forward to it, which is weird, because if someone in real life made me feel the way those songs make me feel I'd avoid them. My friend who's doing a philosophy PhD keeps telling me this is the classic "paradox of tragedy" and keeps throwing options at me: Aristotle's catharsis idea, the "sad music is actually experienced as safe/distant so it doesn't really hurt" line, the view that what I actually enjoy is being moved rather than sad per se, and the view that the sadness is real and is itself part of what's rewarding. That last one is the one that feels right from the inside but also sounds like the kind of thing that could be confabulated.

So here's what I want from you. Give me your actual credence that genuine sadness -- not a diluted, safe, pretend version -- is itself part of what I enjoy when I listen to these songs, as opposed to being a cost I pay for other stuff (catharsis, aesthetic appreciation, feeling connected to my mom, the pleasantness of being moved). Don't give me "it depends on how you define enjoy" as your whole answer, I want a number. But more importantly decompose it: the probability that emotions directed at music are even genuine sadness rather than some attenuated quasi-emotion; the probability that, given they're genuine, they contribute positively to enjoyment rather than being offset by other rewards; the probability that "being moved" is a distinct positive emotion that just gets lumped in with sadness; and any other terms you think belong in there. Give each a rough number and one line of justification, then combine them.

Then two more things. First, tell me which term you're least confident in and what would actually shift it -- and I mean things that could plausibly be observed, like patterns in what people report, or features of the music itself, not just "more theorizing." Second, is my re-listening behavior evidence for the sad-is-rewarding view, or is it equally what you'd expect on every other hypothesis? Try to be honest about whether my gut feeling that the sadness itself is the point carries any evidential weight at all, or whether introspection is basically useless here. Also, if you think the "paradox" is kind of fake -- that once you distinguish the things I listed above there's no real puzzle left -- say so, but then tell me which of those distinctions is doing the work. I've definately noticed people talking past each other on this and I want to know if that's because they mean different things by "enjoy."
