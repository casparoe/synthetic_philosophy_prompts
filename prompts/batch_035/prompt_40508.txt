I've been reading about how chatbots are trained, and something about it strikes me as philosophically odd, though I have no academic background in this and may be missing the right vocabulary for it.

As I understand the last stage of training, the model produces answers, human raters say which answers they prefer, and the model is nudged toward what the raters liked. On its face, the aim is to give people what they prefer. But the people who run this step also keep correcting what the raters get wrong. They penalize the model for flattering users — agreeing with someone who is mistaken simply because agreement feels good — even though users and raters often reward exactly that. They adjust for raters liking answers that are long, confident in tone, and neatly formatted, whether or not they are true. And on certain subjects the model simply refuses to do what a user is asking for quite willingly. So the practice treats preference as the raw material while holding that there is a standard above preference, which steps in whenever preference goes wrong.

My question is what view of human good this setup presupposes. It can't be the simple view that value is just whatever people prefer, since the corrections exist precisely to catch preferences leading somewhere bad; but it can't be a fully independent standard either, since preferences are still gathered at great expense and followed most of the time. The practice seems to impose an ordering on its sources — what the designers believe, what raters in aggregate reward, what the individual user wants, perhaps what the user would want if better informed and calmer — with some overriding others. Is there a coherent theory underneath that says which preferences count and when, for instance that a preference counts unless it was produced by a distorting influence such as flattery, mood, or ignorance? Would a theory like that also cover the refusals, or do the refusals require something stronger — the thought that some things are bad for a person regardless of what they would want even on reflection? If the practice turns out to be underdetermined instead, with several quite different views of the good licensing exactly what is actually done, I'd want you to say so rather than manufacture a theory it doesn't need. And I'd like to know what would have to change in the training setup under each of two replacements: if they adopted the simple preference-only view, and if they went fully top-down and stopped consulting preference data altogether.

Throughout, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and at the end single out the point you are least sure of.
