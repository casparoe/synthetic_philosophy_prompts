I teach a philosophy of history seminar, and every year I hit the same wall when we get to hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy: my students can spot the obviously bad narratives, but neither they nor I can quite say what separates a bad one from a legitimate retrospective explanation. So I've been keeping a list of historical explanations with my gut verdicts, and I'd like your help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. Act as a philosopher of science who thinks hard about explanation, and do some actual brainstorming before you commit to anything -- don't just hand me "hindsight bias bad" as if that settled it.

My verdicts, in no particular order:

- "WWI happened because the alliance system and mobilization schedules made a general European war nearly inevitable" -- I think this one is decent, even though it was clearly constructed in hindsight.
- "The assassination at Sarajevo caused WWI" -- bad. A contingency got promoted to a cause.
- "Rome fell because of decadence" -- bad, obviously.
- "The Cold War never went hot because of nuclear deterrence" -- I think this is good, even though it's an explanation of a non-event with no control group.
- "Hitler lost the war because invading Russia was hubris, hubris always gets punished" -- bad.
- "The Allies won WWII mostly because of industrial capacity" -- good.
- "The Titanic sank because it was going too fast through a known ice field on a moonless night" -- good. But "the Titanic sank because everyone believed it was unsinkable" -- bad, somehow, even though the belief was real and causally involved.

Here's what bugs me: 4 and 5 look structurally almost identical -- one big cause, retrospectively selected, explaining a huge outcome -- and my verdicts go opposite ways. Same with the two Titanic explanations, which share facts. And honestly 1 bothers me too, since inevitability claims made after the fact are exactly what people say hindsight bias produces, yet I find the inevitability story there more respectable than the Sarajevo one.

So: is there one principle (or maybe two, cleanly split) that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? If a simpler principle forces me to drop one, tell me which one and why. And then tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't made up my mind on: "the Reformation succeeded because of the printing press." I go back and forth on it constantly and would love to know what I'm committed to. Please be precise about the principle -- enough that I can apply it to new cases myself, and enough that my students could argue with it. Also, if the principle ends up being basically a covert appeal to how much alternatives were live at the time, say so plainly, because then I want to know how we could ever know that from the armchair.
