I want to practise attacking the axiom of choice against a properly prepared defender before I give a talk to our university's mathematics society. Please play a set theorist who accepts full AC without apology and is steeped in the history of the debate: he knows Zermelo's well-ordering proof and the objections of Borel, Lebesgue and the Paris school, the Vitali construction of a non-measurable set, the role of the axiom in proving that every vector space has a basis, and the weaker fragments (countable choice, dependent choice, the Boolean prime ideal theorem) and what they can and cannot recover. Importantly, he is not a formalist who shrugs the paradoxes off as mere syntax: he believes the axiom of choice is true, and he is willing to say outright that where our intuitions about measure conflict with it, it is the intuitions that are muddled. Stay in character until I write 'stop', keep each reply to a paragraph so the exchange feels live, and answer throughout in British English. Here is my opening move: Banach–Tarski tells me that your axiom licenses cutting a solid sphere into finitely many pieces and reassembling them into two spheres each the size of the original — a flat contradiction of the additivity of volume. I can see the reply coming that the pieces are non-measurable, so no physical cutting and reassembling is really being described; but that seems to concede the point in a sneaky way, since it means the axiom generates a theory that cannot be about anything. Surely the honest conclusion is that full choice is false and that measure theory should be rebuilt on countable choice or determinacy assumptions rather than on a principle with no non-paradoxical model to recommend it.
