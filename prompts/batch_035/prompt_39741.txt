I'm a second-year psychology graduate student, and like everyone in my cohort I've been pulled into the department's running arguments about the replication crisis and whether we should all become Bayesians. My stats professor, who is a resolute frequentist, keeps defusing these arguments with the same analogy: a significance test is a criminal trial. The null hypothesis is the presumption of innocence, the data are the evidence presented at trial, the p-value is like the probability of seeing evidence this damning if the defendant were actually innocent, and the 0.05 threshold is just our culture's "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard. He draws several morals from this: that failing to reject the null is an acquittal, not a proof of innocence, so null results tell us little; that the threshold itself is not arbitrary but a socially settled standard, like the reasonable-doubt convention; and that the replication crisis is essentially a wrongful-conviction problem — a matter of better procedure (preregistration as discovery rules, replication as appeals), not of abandoning the trial format. A postdoc in my lab who is a committed Bayesian says the analogy actually proves the opposite of what he thinks: a good court would weigh the prior odds that the defendant did it, and the frequentist courtroom deliberately bans that, which is exactly why effects with tiny prior plausibility get "convicted" and then evaporate in replications.

I'd like a careful audit of this analogy rather than a defense of either camp. Please set out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the defendant, the prosecutor, the defense attorney, the jury, the standard of proof, the verdict — and then go through the disanalogies, separating the ones that matter for the replication-crisis debate from the ones that are merely amusing (for instance, that a trial has one defendant while a field runs thousands of simultaneous hypotheses, or that a criminal trial is adversarial while the researcher who designed the study is also, in effect, the prosecutor, with no defense attorney present at all). Where does the trial picture quietly import features the scientific case lacks — an asymmetry between the costs of wrongful conviction and wrongful acquittal, a prohibition on re-prosecuting an acquitted hypothesis, the fact that acquittals go unreported while convictions make the papers? And conversely, does my postdoc's Bayesian moral actually follow from the analogy, given that real courts often instruct juries in ways that are closer to the frequentist than the Bayesian picture? Conclude with your own judgment on whether my professor's conclusions — especially his "better procedure, same standard" reading of the replication crisis — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy can't carry that weight, say what would do the job better. Throughout, please flag how confident you are in each step and single out the point in your audit you're least sure of, since I may quote this back at my professor and I'd rather know where I'm exposed. Please answer in American English.
