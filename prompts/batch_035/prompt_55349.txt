I write novels for a living, and somehow that got me onto the board of a writers' emergency fund — a small charity that hands out grants (think a month's rent, not life-changing money) to writers hit by sudden crises. Our applications have tripled this year and the board is split. One faction wants what they call "basic diligence": proof of publication or a contract in progress, a signed statement that writing is your main occupation, a review of anyone who applies two years running, and clawback provisions if it turns out someone had a job they didn't declare. The other faction thinks all of that is just workfare for people who chose a badly paid profession, and that we'd be spending our tiny staff budget interrogating the desperate to catch, at most, one or two chancers. There's also a trustee who keeps saying the fund exists for "genuine writers" in a way I don't love but can't quite put my finger on why. Draft the memo I'd circulate before next month's meeting: recommend a concrete policy — which conditions to keep, which to drop, what to do about the repeat applicants and the undeclared-job worry — and make it engage the diligence faction's strongest point honestly rather than waving it off as cruelty, since a fund that's bankrupted by the next grantee's honesty helps nobody either. I want a bottom line the board can actually vote on, not a both-sides essay. If it helps, my instinct is that the fraud question and the deservingness question are getting muddled together in our meetings, but I write fiction, so check that instinct rather than trusting it.
