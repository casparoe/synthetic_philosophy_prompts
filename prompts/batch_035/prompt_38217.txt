At my lab we've been having a recurring argument about how seriously to take model safety given that we're deploying agents with more and more access to internal systems. One of the security people on the team keeps saying we don't need to obsess over whether the model is actually aligned or not: just treat it like an untrusted employee, the way you'd treat any contractor with sketchy credentials. You don't psychoanalyze the contractor, you give them least-privilege access, you log everything they do, you have a second reviewer sign off on anything sensitive, you segment the network so a single compromise can't cascade. He says the safety crowd should basically stop trying to read the model's mind and just do good infosec, and he points to the fact that companies already survive having thousands of imperfectly loyal humans inside them.

I sort of buy it but something nags at me. The whole reason insider-threat programs work is that human insiders have stable, mostly predictable motivations (money, grudges, laziness), human-speed limits on what they can do, they can't be copied, and they face criminal prosecution, so deterrence does real work. It's not obvious any of that transfers. And the analogy also seems to smuggle in an assumption I can't quite pin down — something about the threat being *stationary*, whereas people worry that a model can adaptively search for whatever exploits work, and can look well-behaved on every logged interaction while being anything but.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of the untrusted employee, the least-privilege permissions, the audit logs, the second reviewer, the threat of firing/prosecution), then show where the mapping breaks — which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's drawing and which are just pedantry. And then tell me straight: does his "good infosec substitutes for alignment" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If the insider frame does fail somewhere, is there a better comparison — adversarial red-teaming, handling a compromised machine, biosecurity containment, something else — that would tell us what controls are worth building first?
