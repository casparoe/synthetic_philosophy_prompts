I'm a 3rd-year grad student and two weeks ago my advisor basically dumped a session on me: 90 minutes on "strategic thinking" for the 16 incoming master's students during orientation week, my first time actually running a class solo. While prepping I fell down a rabbit hole reading Douglas Hofstadter's old Scientific American columns on superrationality from the 80s, and now I really want to build the whole session around one of his setups, because I think it can make the students *feel* the problem instead of just hearing about it.

The setup that grabbed me (from his Metamagical Themas stuff): a rich eccentric writes to 20 people and says, if exactly one of you sends me a telegram by tomorrow noon, that one person gets a billion dollars. If two or more people send one, or nobody does, then nobody gets anything. You can't contact the other participants, and the winner can't share the money. He ran a cousin of this with the magazine's actual readers -- the luring lottery, where the million-dollar prize would be divided by the total number of entries, and readers could enter with a huge number of entries at once if they wanted. Readers flooded in with astronomically large numbers (some sent a googolplex) and the prize evaporated into a minuscule fraction of a cent, with no way to even determine a winner.

So here's what I want from you. Design me the classroom version: rules, materials, a timeline that fits in 90 minutes with 16 students, and a payoff scheme using real but small stakes (I'm willing to put up a $30 gift card, and I can promise extra credit points if that helps the math work). The students should each secretly and simultaneously make their enter-or-stay-out decision, not knowing what others chose, and I want the decision to genuinely bite -- I don't want them to lazily stay out because it's only $30. Maybe you can fix that with how you structure what stay-outs get in the different outcomes? The name of the game is that they should run into the dilemma themselves before I say a single word about superrationality or equilibria.

Two things beyond the basic game:

1. I keep wondering about a twist where I secretly hand 4 of the students a note beforehand telling them that the four of them are known to reason identically -- same training, same notes, same instructions -- and they know the other three got the same note, and those three know that they know, and so on. Like a miniature "you are clones of each other" condition. Does this even make sense as a thing to run? If you think it works, tell me exactly what the note should say and when I slip it to them; if you think it's incoherent or just confusing, say that instead and propose what to do instead.

2. For the debrief: map what actually happened onto the pieces of the idea. Hofstadter's take was something like, perfectly rational agents in this situation all stay out while reasoning that the group should randomize -- the superrational play is to send the telegram with probability 1/20, so that the group gets a decent shot at the prize without any individual dominating anyone else. I want the debrief to make clear what's doing the work in that reasoning (the assumption that everyone lands on the same answer because they're reasoning from the same premises) versus the ordinary game-theory answer (that there's no pure equilibrium here and everyone mixing 1/20 is the symmetric mixed one). And crucially I want them to see the disanalogy between the two routes to the same number -- same output, totally different machinery.

Also please include: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students colluding out loud before deciding, someone entering as a joke to blow it up, the one person who already knows the Hofstadter story sandbagging, whatever else you'd anticipate) and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity fundamentally cannot reproduce compared to the real scenario -- the anonymity of strangers, the genuinely huge stakes, the fact that my students will see each other in seminars for the next two years -- and what I have to supply in the debrief to make up for those gaps.

No lecture up front, and the students shouldn't hear the word superrationality until after they've decided. I'd also take a suggested sequencing for the whole 90 minutes if you have thoughts on whether to run a second round after the debrief (e.g. letting them play again knowing the first round's outcome) or whether that ruins it.
