I'm a graduate teaching assistant putting together a seminar session on material constitution — the statue-and-clay family of puzzles. For the closing part of the session, I'd like a tier list of the main responses to the puzzle, ranked not by general plausibility in the abstract but by how well each one handles a small battery of test cases I've assembled. The responses I have in mind are: (a) the coincidence view, on which the statue and the clay are two numerically distinct objects that temporarily occupy exactly the same region; (b) constitution-without-identity, on which they are distinct but related by a constitution relation that isn't identity; (c) relative identity, on which there is no unqualified fact of the matter about identity, only identity relative to a sortal; (d) deflationism about artifacts, on which there is no statue over and above the clay (or no statue at all, strictly speaking); and (e) the temporal-parts treatment, on which the statue and the clay coincide only during an overlapping stretch of their four-dimensional careers. If you think there's a serious position I've left out, you may add it, but please rank the five above.

The battery of cases is as follows. (1) The flattening case: the sculptor smashes the statue, and while the clay survives, the statue does not. (2) The unbroken gesture: the sculptor works the lump into the figure in one continuous motion, never letting go, so that there is no obvious moment at which the statue first exists — the view has to say something about where in the gesture it appears. (3) The bequest: a will bequeaths "the clay" to one heir and "the statue" to another, and the family wants to know whether the two heirs have been left the same thing, or two things, or one thing described twice. (4) The restoration case: conservators replace every last gram of the original clay over decades of repairs, yet the museum still advertises the object as the very statue modeled at its founding, and a donor is threatening to sue over the phrase "the original work".

Please sort the five positions into tiers by how cleanly they resolve these cases — the more ad hoc save-the-view maneuvers a case demands from a position, the lower it should sit — with a sentence per position explaining why it lands where it does. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the ordering overall, and single out the one tier assignment you are least sure of.
