I teach a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and every year the campaign-finance unit collapses into the same wall. Someone cites Citizens United, someone else snorts that money is not speech, and within ten minutes the room has divided into people who think the slogan "money is speech" is obviously true and people who think it is obviously false — with neither side able to say what the sentence even asserts. Last week a law colleague, guest-lecturing on election law, used it to wave off an entire line of argument about contribution caps: he said that since money is speech, limits on spending are limits on speech, and since we don't license speech, we shouldn't license spending on it. Several students accepted the move; several were certain it was sophistry but could only sputter. I suspect both the slogan and the standard objections to it are each bundles of several distinct theses that have been fused together by repetition, and that my colleague's argument needs a specific one of those theses while the students were refuting a different, weaker one.

I would like a careful disambiguation I can distribute as a handout. Lay out the distinct precise claims "money is speech" could be expressing — I want the full menu, not your favorite. As best I can tell, at least these families of readings are in circulation, and there may be more you can identify: (a) a mere causal-enabling platitude, that money buys the means of communication and therefore restrictions on spending de facto restrict communication; (b) a constitutive claim, that certain expenditures — a political donation, buying a billboard, funding a film — are themselves expressive acts rather than mere instruments to expression, so that regulating them is not regulating the amplifiers of speech but the speech; (c) an anti-differentiation claim in the neighborhood of viewpoint or content neutrality, namely that once an expenditure is expressive, the state may not restrict it because it is an expenditure when it could not restrict the underlying message directly; (d) a claim about the identity of the speaker, e.g. that associations of persons speak through pooled funds and so limits on their spending are limits on their members' collective expression; and (e) whatever is being claimed when the slogan is wielded against arguments from corruption or political equality, where the implicit move seems to be that unequal capacity to be heard is not a defect the state may correct. Distinguish carefully within each family: say what exactly the thesis asserts, what would make it true or false, whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and — this is the part my students most need — whether accepting it would actually license the inference my colleague drew, that campaign-finance limits are impermissible restrictions on speech. I want you to be especially attentive to readings that are obviously true but do no argumentative work, and readings that would do the work but that no one should accept; the interesting cases are readings that do some work at some cost.

Please also treat the negative slogan, "money is not speech," symmetrically: what precise claims does it express, and are any of them more than the observation that banknotes are not words? Consider explicitly whether the standard rhetorical counterexamples — bribery laws, laws against soliciting a hit man for pay, disclosure requirements — refute any of the positive readings, and if so which; and whether the corruption rationale for regulation can be stated in a way that concedes the enabling platitude while still justifying limits. I'd like the whole thing structured as a numbered list of readings, each with a verdict, ending with your assessment of which reading the dismissive use of the slogan actually depends on and whether, on your view, the argument it was used for stands. Please do not pad the handout with the case history of American campaign-finance litigation; the analysis should be a philosopher's, not a constitutional lawyer's, and should remain intelligible to someone who knows none of the cases.
