I'm presenting at a departmental colloquium next month, and I'd like help preparing for the question period. Please draft a Q&A prep sheet for me, written in American English.

My thesis, in brief: consequentialist cluelessness is not a curiosity about far-future effects but a structural feature of almost every ordinary moral choice, and the standard dismissals of it fail. The classic response says that our ignorance about the ripple effects of our actions is symmetric across the available options, so it cancels out and expected-value reasoning proceeds as usual. I argue this cancellation claim is generally false: for many real choices, our uncertainty is not even approximately symmetric across options, and in the cases that matter most — career choice, donations, political action — it systematically disfavors the options with the largest and most diffuse effect chains, which is the opposite of what naive expected-value reasoning tells us to do. My positive proposal is a two-tier view: consequentialism survives as the criterion of rightness, but the decision procedure we're entitled to use includes what I call a legibility constraint — a threshold of predictive confidence below which diffuse, high-variance, feedback-resistant effect chains are treated as noise, not as candidates for cancellation or summation. On my view, the long-run effects of giving to a deworming charity are, for decision-making purposes, not merely unknown but in a principled sense unavailable, and acting as if they were available is a kind of epistemic trespass dressed up as humility.

The audience is a mid-sized philosophy department with, relevantly: one senior act-utilitarian who thinks the whole worry is overblown and that expected-value maximization under the cancellation assumption is fine unless someone proves an asymmetry; two effective-altruist-leaning graduate students who run cost-effectiveness estimates for a living and will treat any discounting of diffuse effects as motivated reasoning aimed at excusing people from giving; a Kantian who will suspect that my legibility constraint is just Kantian ethics with the serial numbers filed off, and that once I've bolted it on, nothing distinctively consequentialist remains; and a decision theorist who knows the formal literature on decision-making under ambiguity inside out and will press me on whether my legibility threshold is anything more than an ad hoc tie-breaker with no principled place between maximizing expected value and going Berkeley-wild on imprecise credences. Nobody in the room is a specialist on cluelessness itself, though several will have read Lenman's paper and Greaves's follow-up.

Please give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience — not the generic ones, but the ones these particular people will raise, phrased the way each of them would put it — ordered by how much damage each would do to my position. For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where the reply is soft or where I should concede something rather than fight. Then, separately: identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks, tell me why I'm right to fear it, and give me the most honest thing I can say if it comes anyway. Please write the whole prep sheet in American English.
