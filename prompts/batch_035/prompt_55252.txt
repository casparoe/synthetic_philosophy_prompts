I've been trying to work out what I actually think about whether conceivability is a guide to possibility, so I've just been going through cases and noting my gut verdicts. Here they are, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures them all or close to it.

Physical zombies — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me with no inner life at all — I can conceive of that, and my gut says that conceiving is real evidence that they're possible. This one feels like the anchor case.

Water turning out to be XYZ rather than H2O: I can't conceive of it, and I think it's impossible. Once you know water is H2O, that seems just necessary.

Gold having atomic number 41 rather than 79: same, can't concieve it, don't think it's possible.

But a chemist in 1820 could perfectly well conceive of water being some other stuff, and I don't want to say they were wrong about what they could conceive. Somehow their conceiving just wasn't evidence of possibility, even though mine is, in the zombie case, from exactly the same kind of act.

Goldbach's conjecture turning out false: I can picture the press conference, some enormous even number that no two primes sum to. But I don't take this as evidence of possibility — if it's true, it's necessarily true, and my little mental movie counts for nothing.

A unicorn existing: conceiving it feels like solid evidence it's possible. But it also seems like whether my horse-with-a-horn image corresponds to a genuine possibility depends on what actual horses are like deep down. Like if actual horses have some hidden essence, I might be conceiving of an impostor-horse with a horn. But a zombie is NOT an impostor-person in the same way — a zombie is a total physical duplicate — so the unicorn case and the zombie case feel different even though they look structurally alike?

One physical law being violated once, tomorrow, by nothing in particular — a single particle just teleporting across the room: I can vividly imagine this, and honestly my gut leans toward "possible, just insanely improbable," though I notice that if the laws are somehow necessary, this shouldn't be possible.

A perfect duplicate of our entire universe existing somewhere, causally disconnected from ours: conceivable, and feels possible. Though someone once told me there's a worry that if all the laws of nature are purely qualitative, a perfect duplicate of the whole universe just IS this universe again, and I genuinely don't know what to say about that.

So that's the set. My instinct is the principle will have to distinguish prima facie from ideal conceivability, and maybe positive from negative conceiving, but I want you to actually state it precisely rather than hand-wave. Tell me which of my verdicts it saves, which it forces me to give up (I suspect the 1820 chemist and the zombie can't both be kept, but I'd love to be wrong), and then tell me what the principle predicts on two cases I haven't listed: (a) Mary, who knows every physical fact there is but has never seen red — is her not knowing what red looks like a genuine possibility the way zombies are, or is she like the 1820 chemist, whose ignorance is compatible with the thing being necessary? and (b) a universe containing nothing but a single electron — I can conceive of that, but is it possible, and does the principle give a clean answer?
