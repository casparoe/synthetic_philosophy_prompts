I write novels for a living, which mostly means spending years inventing people and then being unable to believe they aren't real. Somewhere around my third book I noticed something craft-wise: when a minor character's sister dies, the protagonist literally gets less vivid on the page. Her dialogue goes flat. And it wasn't that she was sad -- it was that half of who she was, for the reader, was the sister listening to her. Around the same time I was reading about ubuntu, the southern African idea that a person becomes a person through other persons, and it landed less like ethics and more like craft advice. Now I hold a fairly strong version of it and I'd like to know what would count against it.

So we're clear on the version: not the weak reading where relationships scaffold or support personhood the way a trellis supports a vine -- that's just developmental psychology plus sociology and nobody's offended by it. I mean constitutive: the relations are parts of you the way organs are, not inputs you once recieved. Consequences I'm willing to swallow: the widow is a partial person in a non-metaphorical sense, mourning is partly metaphysical repair, and what solitary confinement destroys isn't a mood but parts.

Here's my immediate problem, and part of why I'm asking. My current novel has a lighthouse keeper who ends up alone for nine years, and I cannot make the middle chapters work if I believe what I claim to believe. Either he's thinning out as a person -- which my constitutive view seems committed to and which the prose absolutely refuses to do, he gets more interior and more himself, it's honestly the best writing I've ever done -- or the view is wrong. And I can feel the qualification coming: well, he carries his dead relations with him, he's talking to them, he's internalized the lighthouse community of his past. But if what constitutes him is now memories and habits, those are internal states, and I've quietly walked back to an inner self with a relational biography -- the trellis view wearing a costume. Every escape hatch seems to lead to the rival camp.

So tell me what would actually count against constitutive relational personhood, and rank it. I want the full map: the feral-child / developmental stuff, the autistic writers who describe being most fully themselves precisely when attunement demands drop off (does the view slander them?), the last-woman-on-earth case after a plague -- if we say she's still a person, did the constituting relations die with everyone else, and what exactly survived if they did? Also whether there's anything the view predicts that atomistic liberalism doesn't -- I'd have thought grief as literal diminishment was one, but maybe the atomist can just say the lost person was instrumentally valuable and match the predictions. Which of these defeaters would defenders of the view themselves concede as damage, which would they dismiss, and -- the thing I most want an honest read on -- has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a fatal problem for a metaphysics of persons or just the ordinary cost of doing metaphysics at all? My lighthouse keeper is waiting on the answer, a bit smugly, alone.
