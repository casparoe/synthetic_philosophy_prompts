I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar, and we've spent three weeks on Goodman's new riddle of induction. My friend Dara has become the class's fastest and most confident judge of what a body of evidence actually supports, though she's terrible at articulating her reasons — when pressed she just says a bad hypothesis feels rigged, or that it cheats by sneaking in a fact about when or where we happen to have looked. Her track record so far: the black ravens everyone has observed support the hypothesis that all ravens are black, but do not support the hypothesis that all ravens are black or white, even though every black raven is also black-or-white; the green emeralds we've observed support the hypothesis that all emeralds are green, but not the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue, where an emerald counts as grue if it is green when examined before some fixed future date and blue otherwise; repeated tests support the hypothesis that all samples of pure gold melt below 1100 degrees Celsius, but do not support the hypothesis that everything in her lab cabinet melts below 1100 degrees, even though every sample drawn from the cabinet so far has — she says that would only be a fact about whoever stocked the cabinet; and she says the fact that everyone in our seminar drinks coffee supports nothing about the next person who enrolls.

Two new cases come up at tomorrow's meeting, and I've been asked to predict her rulings. First, the numismatics case: we've handled a few hundred United States quarters minted before 1965, and every one of them was 90 percent silver. She'll be asked whether this supports that an uncatalogued roll of supposed pre-1965 quarters sitting in a collector's vault is silver throughout, and whether it supports anything at all about a quarter minted in 1968. Second, the particle case: a newly discovered kind of particle has been observed five times, and all five decays happened within a nanosecond; she'll be asked whether that supports the hypothesis that every particle of this kind decays within a nanosecond. One complication someone raised in seminar: you can apparently define green itself in terms of grue and bleen in a way that makes green look just as positional as grue, and Dara hasn't yet said what she makes of that.

Please predict Dara's verdicts on the coin questions and the particle question, and more importantly, lay out the candidate rules that fit her past rulings equally well — I can see at least the idea that a predicate is unprojectible if its definition references examination times or places, the idea that a predicate is projectible only if it's entrenched by a long history of past projections, and the idea that support flows only toward lawlike or natural-kind hypotheses — and tell me where those rules agree and disagree on the new cases. Note that the pre-1965 case and the brand-new-particle case seem to pull in opposite directions, so her verdicts can't simply favor one rule across the board. Give your best guess about what she'll say and why, and then propose the single further case that would best discriminate between the candidate rules, explaining what each rule predicts about it. Please answer in continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — write it the way you'd write a long letter.
