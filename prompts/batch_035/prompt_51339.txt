I'm taking a course on later Wittgenstein this semester and we just did the private language argument. In the seminar the diarist case gets treated as basically settled: a lone person records a recurring inner experience in a diary under some invented sign, and the objection is supposed to be that there's no available standard of correctness, so her using the sign right is in principle indistinguishable from her merely taking herself to use it right — and hence there's nothing that counts as having got it right. Fine. But a friend of mine made an objection I can't quite shake, and instead of hand-waving I want it made precise and then properly answered. So here's the job.

Construct the strongest version of her case as a concrete thought experiment, to spec. A single person, alone in every relevant sense (no community of users, nobody who could ever check her usage), keeps a diary for decades with one invented sign for a recurring inner experience. The episodes must be genuinely inner — no behavioral or physiological proxy she or anyone could measure, no doctor, no instrument, nothing public that the sign could be secretly tracking. And the diary has to be a success on its face: her entries stay consistent, she reidentifies the episodes across the years, and by her own later lights the record seems to have tracked them. The pressure should come entirely from the fact that, intuitively, it all just works. Two rules about cheating, since that's what fixes the point of the exercise: don't stipulate at the meta-level that she is in fact correct (that would just assume the verdict against Wittgenstein rather than generate the appearance one), and don't smuggle correctness in through a hidden public criterion.

Third, tell me what happens if we strip that feature out: does the case collapse back into the ordinary one, or does something else have to be added?
