My closest friend is a classically trained calligrapher, and we recently spent an entire evening arguing about what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Her position is that every well-formed 'a' shares a common skeleton — an invariant core structure that different styles merely dress in different weights, proportions, and flourishes. My counter was that there is no such invariant core: what makes an 'a' an 'a' is its role in a system of alternatives, something more like family resemblance spread across instances and styles, so that category and style are two independent dimensions along which any letterform can vary. We did not settle it, and I now teach a college typography studio where I would like my students to run into the question themselves rather than hear me lecture about it. I have about fourteen design students and one three-hour session.

Design an in-class activity in which students produce and then cross-classify letterforms so that the separability of category and style arises from what they do, not from anything I say. The idea they should encounter — ideally only after it has already happened to them — is roughly the one behind Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project: a letterform is doubly constrained, vertically by membership in a category (this glyph must be recognizable as an 'a' against its twenty-five rivals, no matter the style) and horizontally by membership in a style (this 'a' must visibly belong with the 'g' and 'e' beside it), and these constraints can pull in opposite directions. The activity should include a recognition or forced-choice element in which students discover they can judge 'a'-ness and judge style-ness as separate calls, and a production element — inventing the missing entries of a grid of letters in invented styles — where the two constraints collide and each student must decide which to sacrifice. Somewhere in the activity there should also be a glyph deliberately made to sit on the boundary between two letters, so students can feel that category judgments are not simply template matching.

Please give me rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the skeleton-versus-family-resemblance argument my friend and I could not resolve. Include an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and a frank note on which feature of the real question no in-class activity can reproduce — for instance, the lifetime of immersion in an alphabet that actually grounds our letter judgments. Please do not tell me who wins the argument. Kindly write your answer in American English.
