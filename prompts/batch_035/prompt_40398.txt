I am a novelist, and I have talked myself into a corner from which I would like to be argued either out or further in.

My current book is narrated in the first person by a man who believes he is writing the very novel in which he appears: each chapter is presented as his manuscript, and the final chapter — the part I keep redrafting — would have him describe, in the present tense, the hand that is writing the final sentence of the final chapter. My editor, who liked the book better when it was quieter, waved the device away with a reference to Escher's 1948 lithograph Drawing Hands: two hands rise from a sheet of paper, each drawing the other's wrist into existence, and nobody thinks the picture is impossible, so the device is a harmless, familiar trompe l'oeil to which the reader consents. Write it and stop worrying.

A philosopher friend of mine, to whom I described the ending over dinner, thinks the lithograph does not say what my editor thinks it says. His reading, which he traces to Hofstadter's use of the image in Gödel, Escher, Bach, goes roughly like this: the visible loop in the picture is fake in a precise way. Both drawn hands were drawn by Escher, whose real hand and real pencil are not depicted; the tangled hierarchy inside the frame is hosted by a level outside the frame that nothing inside can touch — call it the inviolate level. On that reading the picture is not an image of self-creation but of self-creation's illusion, and applied to my novel it cuts against me: whatever my narrator says about the hand writing him, there is a hand that wrote him, namely mine, and his loop is a loop only because I drew it that way. My friend draws a deflationary moral — the device can be charming, but it cannot be about what I want it to be about.

Here is what unsettles me. In the same conversation, the same friend used the same picture to explain consciousness: the self as a pattern that models itself modeling itself, a loop not underwritten from outside because there is no outside — and I could not get him to see that the two uses pull in opposite directions. Either Drawing Hands shows that every apparent self-creation is secretly hosted by an untouched external level, in which case minds are not like the picture and my narrator's loop is a cheat; or it shows a genuine loop with no host, in which case my editor is wrong that the device is a harmless familiar trick, and something real is being claimed. I suspect the answer turns on whether fiction is more like a picture — with a frame, and an artist standing outside it — or more like whatever minds are.

So: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table first — in the fiction case, what plays the role of the two hands, the paper, the drawing board, Escher's real hand, and the viewer standing in the museum; and in the mind case, what plays each of those roles, and which roles go unfilled. Then show me where the mapping breaks in each case, and, crucially, which of the breaks matter: my editor's conclusion (it is just a picture; the reader is never fooled; nothing is at stake) and my friend's (nothing genuinely loop-like can happen in fiction, because the author is always an inviolate level outside the text) cannot both be right, and I want to know whether either survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Finally, since I am tempted to make the claim explicit in an author's note — that my narrator's loop is real in something like the way the self's loop is real — tell me whether the analogy can carry that weight at all, or whether it was doing all of the work all along, and whether there is a better picture I should be reaching for instead.
