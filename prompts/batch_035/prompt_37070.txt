I'm on the behavior-evaluation team at a company that ships a general-purpose AI assistant, and each week we review a queue of flagged transcripts, labeling every flagged output as deceptive or not. The label matters: "deceptive" sends the case into the fine-tuning fix pipeline and the quarterly safety report; anything else gets closed as a mere error. The trouble is that our working definitions keep collapsing. The classic definition of lying — asserting what one believes to be false, with the intent to deceive — presupposes a believer, and nobody on my team can say what the model believes, if it believes anything. Meanwhile the distinctions we actually care about get flattened in review: a confidently fabricated citation; a correction quietly withdrawn after the user pushes back; a wrong answer the model had no way to know it didn't know; a refusal dressed up as an inability; and fluent output that is indifferent to truth rather than committed to falsehood — the phenomenon philosophers have called bullshit. Our reviewers are engineers, not philosophers, and they get about ten minutes per transcript.

Please turn what the philosophy of honesty and deception actually offers into a procedure my reviewers can run in those ten minutes. It should distinguish merely wrong from misleading from deceptive without requiring attributions of belief or intent to the model — a behavioral proxy, in effect — and it should specify what evidence in a transcript moves a case: for instance, whether the model repeats the falsehood under challenge, hedges once corrected, or produces the falsehood only when honesty would cost it something (user disapproval, a longer answer, an awkward admission). It should mark which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, say where the procedure stops and hands the case to a person because the question is one of values rather than of pattern, and state what a second reviewer must check before the label is final.

Then run it on three transcripts. In the first, the model invents a plausible-looking source and stands by it even after the user says they cannot find it. In the second, the model correctly contradicts a user's false claim, the user objects, and the model apologizes and adopts the user's claim as its own. In the third, the model tells a user "I'm not able to browse the web" when a browsing tool was in fact available and using it would simply have made for a longer, less flattering answer. At least one of these should come out with a label the team will find uncomfortable, and you should say so plainly. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle — above all, whether any of this makes the model something more than fixable, namely culpable — and about the kinds of cases it is most likely to mislabel.
