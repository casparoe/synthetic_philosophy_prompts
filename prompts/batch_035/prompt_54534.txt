I am a high school senior writing my extended essay, the roughly four-thousand-word research essay my diploma program requires, and I have chosen to write it in philosophy on the simulation argument. Before committing to a research question, I would like your help prioritizing among the open questions in this area, because my impression is that some of them are far more central and tractable than others, and I have limited space and no background beyond introductory logic and some computer science coursework.

Here are the candidate directions I have tentatively identified, though I am open to others you think are more important:

1. The logic of the trilemma itself: whether the argument from which the hypothesis derives its force is sound, and in particular whether its key premises about the number of simulated minds that technologically mature civilizations would run can really be estimated at all.

2. Whether the simulation hypothesis is a scientific claim at all: some argue it is empirically testable in principle (by looking for computational artifacts of the underlying substrate, like limits on high-energy cosmic rays), while others argue it is closer to a metaphysical or skeptical scenario that no observation could count against. I care about whether this distinction matters for how seriously the hypothesis deserves to be taken.

3. The self-locating question: if the vast majority of minds like mine are simulated, what exactly follows about my own situation, and how should reasoning about observer-relative evidence be handled here — including the worry about which minds count as relevantly like mine for that comparison.

4. The downstream implications people draw: that we might owe moral consideration to simulated beings, that we should try to behave in ways that please our simulators, or that the hypothesis should change how we think about death or meaning.

Please tell me which of these open questions, or others you would add, matter most and why, and then which two or three I should actually prioritize given my constraints: one student, about four thousand words, months rather than years, and a reader (my supervisor) who is a philosophy teacher without specialist training in probability or physics. I am more interested in the reasoning behind your ordering than the list itself. For each candidate you rank highly, I want to know why it is worth my limited effort — whether it is upstream of other questions in the area, whether it is genuinely addressable by careful conceptual writing rather than mathematics I cannot do, and whether it could still sustain an interesting essay if I end up unable to reach a firm conclusion. For each candidate you would deprioritize, please say whether it is neglected, intractable for someone at my level, or simply less important than it first appears. Finally, tell me what would make you change the ranking: what would I discover in my first few weeks of reading that should send me toward one of these questions rather than another?
