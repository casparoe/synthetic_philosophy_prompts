I'd like a case analysis of the American "New Math" reform of the 1960s and early 1970s as a test of Whitehead's theory of educational rhythm, one that runs in both directions: what the theory explains about the episode and what the episode does to the theory. The salient facts of the episode, in brief: after Sputnik in 1957, federal funding and research mathematicians (notably through the School Mathematics Study Group) drove a wholesale redesign of school mathematics; elementary pupils met the vocabulary of sets, unions, and complements, and did arithmetic in bases other than ten; parents and teachers were baffled, critics like Morris Kline attacked the program's abstraction, test scores sagged, and by the mid-1970s the schools had retreated to "back to basics." On the theory side: Whitehead's cycle of romance, precision, and generalisation, with the cycle recurring within subjects and even within single lessons; his insistence that the whole point is the pupil's self-development; and his famous attack on "inert ideas," which he defines as "ideas that are merely received into the mind without being utilised, or tested, or thrown into fresh combinations."

The obvious reading is that New Math imposed premature precision on children still owed their romance with number, and I expect you to make that mapping carefully — but I don't want it to stop there, because it is too easy. Note the complication: Whitehead's own writings on the mathematics curriculum attack traditional drill in arithmetic precisely as the paradigm of inert ideas and praise the power of abstract, general thinking, so it is not obvious he would have sided with the backlash; a theory that condemns both the reform and the reaction risks never being able to lose. Also test a hypothesis I find interesting but can't yet evaluate: that the rhythm cycle applies fractally, so that the reform movement itself can be read as a cycle — the mathematicians' romance with axiomatic structure in the 1950s, SMSG's codification as its precision — with the question of what, if anything, in the episode counts as its generalisation stage. Say plainly whether that fractal application illuminates or is overextension. Finally, be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak: does the rhythm theory have anything to say about teacher retraining, federal funding, or Cold War panic, or were those simply the operative causes?

After the analysis, give me a structured summary as a table with three columns: "Feature of the episode" (e.g., set-theoretic vocabulary in elementary grades, mathematicians writing schoolbooks, parental backlash, the retreat to drill); "Whiteheadian reading" (which stage, concept, or verdict the theory applies); and "Verdict on the theory" — explained, strained, or silent — with one sentence of justification in each cell. Aim for six to eight rows covering the most diagnostic features, not an exhaustive inventory.
