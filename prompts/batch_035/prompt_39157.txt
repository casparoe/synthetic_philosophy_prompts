A friend and I spent an evening arguing about the dollar auction — Shubik's game where the highest bidder takes the prize but the runner-up also pays — and about escalation of commitment more generally. We ended up disagreeing over a specific claim. Her position was the standard one from the behavioral literature: whenever someone persists with a course of action past the point of negative expected return, this is diagnosable from the outside as bias, driven by sunk costs, face-saving, and the desire to justify one's earlier decisions. My position was that there must be cases where the outside observer, applying exactly that diagnostic checklist, wrongly flags the persistence — cases where the right call is to persist, and where this is visible on a proper forward-looking analysis rather than being something only the agent could know.

Please construct a thought experiment that adjudicates between us, built to the following specifications. The scenario should be realistic — a workplace or contract setting, not a war room or a poker table. All decision-relevant information should be equally available to the agent and to a hypothetical outside auditor, so that the case cannot be won by private knowledge: if the persistence is vindicated, it must be vindicated by analysis, not by the agent happening to know something. The justification for persisting must give no weight whatsoever to unrecoverable prior investment; only forward-looking reasons may appear. Yet the auditor, applying the usual escalation red flags (large sunk costs, the agent's personal responsibility for the original decision, prior public commitment), must flag the behavior as textbook escalation. After the scenario, walk through the auditor's diagnosis and the proper forward-looking analysis, and tell me which feature of the case is doing the work in making the two come apart. Finally, give my friend's best response — whether she should say the case was misclassified rather than that the diagnosis misfires — and whether removing the feature you identified would collapse the case back into a standard, correctly diagnosed instance of escalation.
