my dissertation is on bayesian confirmation theory and i've been stuck on the problem of old evidence for like six months now, and honestly what's getting to me isn't the problem itself but the shape of the debate around it. so Glymour raises the issue in the early 80s with the Mercury perihelion case: Einstein knew about the anomalous advance long before GR entailed it, so for him P(e) = 1 and P(e|h) = 1 and conditionalizing gives you no boost, but intuitively GR's explanation of perihelion is like THE textbook case of dramatic confirmation. fine. but then look at the response literature: you've got the counterfactual people saying to consider what the agent would have believed if she didn't know e, the Garber-style move where the agent is uncertain about what her theory entails so she can learn h ⊃ e, Howson & Franklin and their weird patch where you tinker with the probability function, Eells trying to "sever" the dependencies. each proposal handles perihelion and breaks on some other case, and every one of these moves is decades old at this point. i've read the back and forths and i genuinely cannot tell you if anyone has changed their mind since roughly the 90s. it's a stalemate among very good formal epistemologists working with the same math.

so what i want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. my working hypothesis, which i half believe: the whole debate is a proxy war about idealization. if your bayesian agent is an idealized reasoner who knows all logical and mathematical consequences, then old evidence just isn't a problem, it's a non-event, and Glymour's case is really a disguised complaint about applying the idealized model to actual scientists. if your bayesian agent is supposed to be even a rough model of real cognition, then Garber-style moves are the only game in town but they turn confirmation theory into a theory of learning deductive facts, which feels like it changed the subject. on this reading the two camps aren't disagreeing about confirmation at all, they're disagreeing about what the probability function is a model OF, and they've been talking past each other for forty years while using the same notation. is that right? or is there a genuine formal disagreement hiding in there, maybe about whether probabilities of already-known propositions are even well defined? and either way, tell me honestly what would count as progress here -- a better formalism, a clean separation into two different questions, or is this just what happens when a normative ideal gets asked to do a descriptive job? i ask because my advisor thinks i can get a chapter out of this and i'm starting to suspect the honest answer is that the chapter is about why the question was confused in the first place.
