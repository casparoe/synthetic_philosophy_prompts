I'm an early-career researcher in behavioral science, and next month I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on the evolution of cooperation. My thesis, roughly, is that the classic story we all learned from the Axelrod tournaments — that cooperation among unrelated individuals is explained by repeated interaction and the shadow of the future — is overrated: direct reciprocity in iterated play is neither necessary nor sufficient for the cooperation we actually observe. I want to argue that the heavy lifting is done by three other things. First, partner choice: in most natural settings agents can walk away from cheapskates and sort into cooperative pairs or groups, so the relevant dynamic is a market, not a repeated dyad. Second, audience effects and reputation: much of what looks like a two-player interaction is played in front of bystanders whose future behavior toward the players depends on what they see, which changes the strategic structure even when the current interaction is genuinely one-shot. Third, payoff misdescription: a fair number of textbook prisoner's dilemmas become mutualisms or stag hunts once you measure payoffs properly, so some of the cooperation we're trying to explain was never a puzzle. I'll also lean on the experimental finding that people conditionally cooperate and punish defectors even in one-shot anonymous games, where no future interaction can possibly repay the punishment — I want to claim that conditional cooperation backed by costly punishment is something like a default human strategy, and that repeated-game logic at best explains why such a disposition is stable in a population, not why it operates in any given encounter.

The audience is the tricky part. The core of the room is behavioral ecologists who do fieldwork on food sharing and cooperative breeding in wild animals — the vampire bat blood-sharing crowd — and who are deeply committed to the inclusive-fitness framework and reflexively allergic to anything that smells of group selection. Then there are two economists steeped in repeated-game theory, whose known position is that reputation and partner choice are just repetition in disguise: an indirect-reciprocity model is an infinitely repeated game with a larger player set, the folk theorem covers everything I want to claim is novel, and experimental one-shot games are never psychologically one-shot because subjects import repeated-interaction heuristics from outside the lab. Rounding out the room is a philosopher of biology who has published skeptical things about how freely biologists and economists borrow game-theoretic vocabulary from one another. I expect the economists and the ecologists to attack from opposite directions — the economists saying my distinction between direct and indirect pathways collapses, the ecologists saying that in nonhuman animals reputation and partner choice are rare, so my story either makes humans unexplainedly special or is wrong.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections I'm most likely to actually get, in rough order of how much damage they could do, each phrased the way that segment of the audience would phrase it — in their vocabulary, with their pet examples — along with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a frank note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede up front rather than dig in on. Please also include the question I'm privately hoping nobody asks, and a sketch of the best available answer even if it costs me. One request on epistemics: throughout the prep sheet, and especially in the replies you draft, keep clear about what is settled among people who work on this versus what is genuinely contested — for instance, how much direct reciprocity has actually been demonstrated in wild animal populations, and whether one-shot cooperation and punishment are an adaptation in their own right or a byproduct of psychology built for repeated life. Several of my claims sit on contested ground, and I'd rather flag that in the talk than have the economists or the ecologists do it for me; where my own framing is one position among several rather than the consensus, say so plainly rather than drafting replies that paper over it.
