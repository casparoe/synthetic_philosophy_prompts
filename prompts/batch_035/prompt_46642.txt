I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about the debate over "fanaticism" in effective altruism — the objection that strict expected-value maximization requires pursuing vanishingly small chances of astronomically valuable outcomes at the expense of near-certain modest ones, the paradigm case being a choice between funding bednets today and funding long-shot research aimed at reducing existential risks a century out. I've interviewed philosophers on both sides, and each time I ask whether the fanaticism objection actually defeats expected-value maximization as a practical guide to altruistic choices, I get a carefully hedged essay. My editor wants something firmer.

So I'd like you to take on the role of a decision theorist who is comfortable committing to numbers on the record — think of it as testimony rather than a literature review — and answer with a credence. Specifically: what is your probability that the fanaticism objection succeeds, where success means that a rational altruist following expected-value reasoning without ad hoc patches would in fact be driven to the "fanatical" choice in the paradigm cases? Please don't give me "it depends on your theory of normativity." Decompose the number into the sub-questions it rests on, and for each give a rough probability with one line of justification: the probability that some form of consequentialism is correct at all; conditional on that, the probability that it is aggregative across outcomes in the way needed for a tiny probability of a huge payoff to dominate a near-certain modest one; the probability that the relevant values are unbounded, or at least that the fanatical cases involve finite payoffs large enough to genuinely dominate rather than speculations stacked on speculations; the probability that our empirical estimates of the tiny probabilities are good enough to be action-guiding, given that in practice these estimates, not the arithmetic, do most of the work; and any further terms you think belong in the decomposition. Then combine them into your final credence, tell me which single term the result is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move that term the most.

One more request before you commit to numbers: do a brief private brainstorm about which cases ought to count as the paradigm cases — the charity example, Pascal's mugging, the multiplication of far-future lives, lotteries with St. Petersburg-style structure — because the objection's force depends heavily on which of these I load into it, and I want you to flag explicitly where my framing of the question does the most work. If a reader could only remember one sentence from your answer, it should be the sentence naming the sub-question that really decides the issue.
