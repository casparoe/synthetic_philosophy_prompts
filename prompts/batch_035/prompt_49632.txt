At a dinner party a few weeks ago, two friends of mine — Maya, who works in public health, and Daniel, an economist — spent most of the evening arguing about a hypothetical one of them raised: a benefactor who can either leave two people as they are, with one at 8 units of welfare and the other at 4, or destroy some of the better-off person's sources of wellbeing so that both end up at 4. The exchange went roughly as follows, and I'd like a careful adjudication of it.

(1) Maya said the second outcome is, in one respect, better: the inequality between them has been removed, and inequality is itself bad — not just bad because of its effects on the people involved, but bad in the way that an unjust arrangement is bad. (2) Daniel replied that this is a perverse use of "better": in the second outcome one person is worse off, no one is better off, and nothing else of value has been created. An event that is worse for someone and better for no one cannot be an improvement, he said, unless "equality" names something good that exists over and above the welfare of the two people — and he challenged her to say what that thing is. (3) Maya didn't take the bait directly. Instead she pointed out that Daniel's own position commits him to a similar departure from simple addition: he thinks a benefit to the worse-off person counts for more than an equal-sized benefit to the better-off person, and that is also a way of caring about the distribution rather than the total. So, she argued, he can't treat her move as suspect simply because it isn't aggregative — his isn't either, and if his weighted concern for the worse-off is legitimate, it shows that distributional facts can matter in their own right, which is all she needs. (4) Daniel answered that the asymmetry is not about aggregation but about who the values are for: his weighting, whatever its ultimate justification, is still a claim about how much good different people's lives contain, so it connects to the interests of persons. Her claim, in the leveling case, is that a state of affairs in which everyone is worse off or equally off, and no one is better off, is nonetheless improved — and that seems to be a value that isn't for anyone, a view about how the world should look from nowhere rather than about how anyone fares. He said a value like that needs a positive story, not just the observation that the word "unfair" applies. (5) Maya then made a retreat: perhaps, she said, the intuition that equality matters is really about what agents may do — a public official shouldn't favor the better-off in distributing resources — and not about whether destruction is good when it happens. Daniel said this concedes the original point: if the leveling intuition is only about conduct, then it never supported her opening claim that the equal-but-poorer outcome is better in any respect, and the dinner-table argument started from exactly that claim.

Both of them finally turned to me, and I honestly couldn't referee it. Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the five steps, say whether the speaker is right, wrong, or right about part of it and wrong about part, and briefly justify the rating. Then tell me who comes out more right overall, and identify the most important thing both of them missed — in particular, I'd like to know whether Daniel's key premise (that nothing can be an improvement unless it's better for someone) is as secure as he treats it as being, and whether Maya's fifth-step retreat is really a concession or a way of locating her view somewhere Daniel never addressed.
