I'm a second-year PhD student in computer science, and a friend of mine from a philosophy reading group has an argument against the formalisation of Occam's razor that I keep defending, and I have stopped being able to see where it goes wrong. His reasoning runs as follows. (1) Solomonoff induction tells us to weight hypotheses by the length of their shortest description relative to some fixed universal machine. (2) The invariance theorem is supposed to make the choice of machine harmless: any two universal machines assign complexities differing by at most an additive constant. (3) But now fix any hypothesis H you please — geocentrism, vitalism, whatever — and I can build a universal machine U* that has H wired in as a single built-in instruction. (4) Relative to U*, H is just about the simplest hypothesis there is, so the simplicity-based prior concentrates on H, and nothing about this is retroactive, since U* was fixed in advance of any data. (5) No universal machine is objectively privileged over any other; the choice among them is a mere convention. (6) So for every hypothesis there is a perfectly legitimate convention under which Occam's razor crowns that hypothesis the simplest, and Solomonoff induction endorses whatever it was entered with. He concludes that the invariance theorem is a sleight of hand: it assures us that machine choice matters only up to a constant while letting the constant swallow everything of practical interest.

I have a feeling the trouble is somewhere around (3) and (4) — the machine that crowns H seems to have H smuggled into it somewhere — but every time I try to make this precise it comes out sounding like special pleading. After all, U* is a perfectly ordinary universal machine with a finite description, and the invariance theorem applies to it like any other, so on what non-arbitrary grounds could I forbid it? Please tell me exactly which step I should reject, why that step looked fine to both of us, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, I want to know whether my friend is right on his fallback point: even if he gives up the argument, the additive constant in the invariance theorem depends on the pair of machines and can be enormous, so for any realistic volume of data the 'harmlessness' of the machine choice may be purely theoretical. Please write your answer in British English.
