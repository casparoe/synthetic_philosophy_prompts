I teach a seminar on consciousness, and a physicist colleague walked me into an argument at the departmental lunch that I've been unable to get out of my head (or out of my inbox, since he keeps following up). The conclusion is obviously too much, but every step looks individually defensible to me, and I can't locate the fault cleanly. Here's the argument as he gave it:

(1) Panpsychism is true: every fundamental particle -- every electron, quark, photon -- has some phenomenal experience, however faint.
(2) An entity has moral status iff it has valenced phenomenal experience, i.e. experience that can be better or worse for the subject.
(3) Any phenomenal experience, however faint, has some valence or other -- pleasant, unpleasant, or somewhere in between. There's no such thing as experience with no valence at all, any more than there's a surface with no temperature.
(4) So every particle has some nonzero positive or negative moral status, however vanishingly small.
(5) The moral weight of any collection of subjects is just the sum of their individual weights -- no caps, no diminishing returns, no discounting for smallness.
(6) There are roughly 10^80 fundamental particles in the observable universe, and fewer than 10^30 animals.
(7) So even if a single particle matters a billion billion times less than a mouse, the aggregate moral weight of particle experience swamps the aggregate weight of all animal experience combined.
(8) So if panpsychism is true, virtually all of our moral attention ought to go toward particle-level matters -- minimizing entropy production, avoiding exothermic reactions, keeping things cold, that sort of thing.
(9) That's insane.

For what it's worth, I'd very much like to keep both (1) and (9) -- I've defended panpsychism in print and I have no intention of saying that my coffee mug's molecules matter more than my cat. My instinct is that something fishy happens somewhere between (3) and (7), maybe in how "however faint" gets traded against "nonzero," or in what it means to sum across subjects rather than within one. But I can't turn that instinct into a principled objection, and every time I try, my colleague accuses me of just refusing to do arithmetic because I don't like the answer. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? And if the answer is "reject (5)," I want to know what else I lose along with unrestricted aggregation -- I use sums like that elsewhere and I'd rather not find out I've been inconsistent about it for twenty years.
