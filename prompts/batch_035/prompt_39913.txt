I'm a high school senior who has been writing code seriously since freshman year — mostly Python and JavaScript, a few projects of my own, one of which I rewrote from the ground up halfway through when the original design stopped making sense. For my extended essay I'm reading Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, and I keep having the feeling that I already know what he's describing from software: normal science sounds like maintenance programming inside an architecture nobody questions, anomalies sound like bugs that resist every local patch, a crisis sounds like technical debt finally hitting a wall, and a scientific revolution sounds like the decision to rewrite rather than refactor. But when he says that successive paradigms are incommensurable, I can't tell whether that is just the fact that you cannot run the old test suite against the new codebase, or something much stronger.

Please restate the Kuhnian picture — normal science, puzzle-solving, anomaly, crisis, paradigm change, and especially incommensurability in both its methodological form and its form as a claim about the meanings of terms — in the vocabulary I actually use: refactoring versus rewriting, breaking changes, schema and API migrations, version control, test suites, technical debt, deprecated features with no remaining users. Translate as faithfully as you can, not as conveniently as you can; where a piece of Kuhn only maps if you bend it, bend it visibly and say so. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts survive only in distorted form, and which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all.

I have one specific suspicion I want tested. In software there is always an external arbiter that the old design and the new one can both be judged by — the program either runs, the users either stay or leave — whereas what seems central to Kuhn's claim about science is that there is no such arbiter between paradigms, because the standards of evaluation are themselves part of what changes with the paradigm. If that reading is right, the residue is not a gap in my vocabulary but the entire point of the book, and I would like to know whether I've understood him correctly or overread him. Conversely, if there is something in the software world that genuinely does reproduce methodological incommensurability — the way what counts as working is fixed by a test suite that the rewrite itself must replace comes to mind — tell me, and say how close the reproduction gets and where it stops. Finish with your own judgment: is the analogy a ladder I should climb and then kick away, or a real partial isomorphism that illuminates both sides?
