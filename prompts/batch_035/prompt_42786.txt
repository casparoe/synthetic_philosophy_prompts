I'm writing the methodology chapter of my dissertation on fairness in machine learning, and I want to stress-test my line of argument against a serious opponent. Please play a committed defender of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — someone convinced that the incompatibility of group calibration with error-rate balance is a deep structural fact about prediction under differing base rates, not a technical curiosity to be engineered around. Be sharp, don't strawman me, concede genuinely minor points but never the central claim, and stay in character until I write 'stop'. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so this reads like a real exchange. Here is my opening move: your theorems show that when two groups have different outcome base rates, no single risk score can be calibrated within each group while also equalizing false-positive and false-negative rates across them. But the theorems treat those base rates as fixed background facts. In any real deployment — lending, predictive policing, medical triage — the decisions made on the model's output feed back into the very outcomes being predicted, so the base rates your impossibility proof presupposes are partly manufactured by the deployed classifier itself. If the background conditions of the theorem are endogenous to the system, isn't it less a fundamental limit on fairness and more a snapshot of a system that hasn't yet reached equilibrium?
