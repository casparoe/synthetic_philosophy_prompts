I teach a 75-minute seminar on decision-making to 18 second-year undergraduates. They've had one week of basic game theory — dominance, best responses in the abstract — but no psychology of choice. Design an in-class activity built around the dollar auction that does something more than simply running it: I have in mind a sequence of three or four staged variants in which we alter one feature at a time — for instance, removing the rule that the second-highest bidder also pays, removing the rivalry (one bidder "against the house" with a preset schedule), moving the bidding decision to a committee, or inserting a mandatory pause between bid rounds — so that by the end the students themselves can say which ingredient was doing the work when bids blew past the value of the prize. Constraints: no real money (use points or stakes the participants actually care about), the participants should not hear the name of the game or any psychological explanation until the debrief, and the whole sequence must fit the time available with materials I can carry in one bag.

For the debrief, map concrete moments from each variant onto the concepts at issue — sunk costs, entrapment, escalation of commitment, competitive arousal — and tell me which variant-to-concept pairings are clean and which are muddied by the classroom setting. Two further requests. First, be explicit about which parts of the psychological explanation of escalation are settled and which are genuinely contested among researchers who study it; I don't want the debrief to present one causal story (say, pure sunk-cost reasoning) as if it were the consensus when the literature is not of one mind. Second, be honest about what a classroom auction cannot reproduce from real-world escalation — for example, continued ambiguity about whether the project might still succeed, or reputational stakes that unfold over months — so I know which parts of the phenomenon I have to supply myself in the debrief rather than expect the activity to generate. And please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
