I'm giving the toast at my best friend's wedding this summer, and I'd like your help drafting it. She and her fiancé met as volunteers at a farm animal rescue, and their shared commitment to animal ethics — specifically their opposition to speciesism, the view that moral status should be assigned on the basis of species membership itself rather than on anything about the individual being — is genuinely central to who they are and to why they're together. The complication is the room. The groom's family are third-generation dairy farmers: proud of their work, warm-hearted people who have travelled a long way, and at least some of them are quietly hurt that their son's wedding will be a vegan one. My own relation to all this is recent and a bit raw: until six months ago I had never thought seriously about any of it, but I then spent weeks helping my friend tube-feed a newborn calf that a farm had discarded, and I found I couldn't un-see what she sees. I am, in short, a convert of a few months' standing, and I know from awkward dinners how converts can sound.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in animal ethics who also has real experience writing for occasions like this, and think carefully before drafting. I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorm about what a toast under these constraints can and cannot do. It cannot preach at the groom's family, cannot smuggle in a version of the argument from marginal cases, and cannot treat the farmers as the enemy — but it also cannot be so studiously neutral that the couple's deepest shared conviction becomes a polite silence, since that would be its own kind of erasure. The philosophical idea should do real structural work: the point is to make care for animals the thread of the toast without ever drawing the very line between the two halves of the room that the toast exists to soften.

Then write the toast itself: about four minutes when read aloud, addressed to a mixed room of farmers, relatives, and a scattering of philosophers, in language everyone can feel rather than argued into, ending on the couple rather than the cause. After the toast, add two or three sentences noting where you deliberately held back philosophically and why, so I can judge the trade-offs you made.
