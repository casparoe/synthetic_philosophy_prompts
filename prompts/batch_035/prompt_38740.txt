My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about how historians explain things like the outbreak of the First World War or the 2008 financial crisis, and the conversation kept collapsing onto a single phrase of hers: hindsight is 20/20. She deployed it in at least three different ways over the course of the evening, and I have come to suspect that only some of them are defensible, while others may actually undermine each other. First she used it to excuse the historical actors: we cannot blame the statesmen of 1914 for not seeing what only became visible afterward. Then she used it against the historians: any explanation of the war's outbreak is suspect precisely because the explainer already knows the ending, so whatever story they tell is guaranteed to fit. And late in the night she used it against me when I floated a counterfactual — that a different British policy in 1912 might have avoided the war — as if the slogan ruled out such speculation as meaningless rather than merely difficult.

I would like a careful disambiguation of what this proverb could actually be claiming. I can see at least the following families of readings, and I suspect there are more: a psychological one about hindsight bias proper, the tendency to retroactively inflate how foreseeable an outcome was; an epistemic one about the asymmetry between predicting and explaining, where explanation after the fact may require less knowledge than prediction would have; a methodological one about narrative, the worry that humans are so good at constructing coherent stories linking known endpoints that narrative fit is nearly zero evidence of causal truth; and a normative one about what we may fairly blame people for failing to foresee. For each reading, please tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or vague, and — crucially — whether it supports the use my friend put it to in each of her three deployments. I am particularly interested in whether the excuse-the-actors reading and the discredit-the-historians reading can both be true without tension, since the first seems to presuppose that the outcome was genuinely hard to see in advance while the second seems to presuppose that seeing-in-advance is the wrong standard for explanation altogether. And if there is a reading under which invoking the proverb against counterfactuals is legitimate — perhaps something about how we overestimate how well we could reconstruct the branching of events — I want to know what that reading commits her to. Please lay the readings out as an ordered list with verdicts, since I intend to bring it to our next argument, and she has accused me of treating a piece of folk wisdom as if it were a single thesis.
