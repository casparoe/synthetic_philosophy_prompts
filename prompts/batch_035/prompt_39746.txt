I am a teaching assistant grading midterm exams for an upper-level undergraduate course in applied ethics, and I need help grading one answer. The exam question, worth 25 points, read as follows:

Question 4 (25 points). In her 1971 paper A Defense of Abortion, Judith Jarvis Thomson concedes, for the sake of argument, that the fetus is a person with a full right to life, and yet argues that abortion can still be morally permissible. Explain the violinist case and the distinction on which her argument turns, and say why the case is supposed to show that the standard anti-abortion argument fails. Then explain Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument from his 1989 paper Why Abortion Is Immoral, including why he believes the abortion debate has reached a stalemate and how his account of the wrongness of killing is meant to break it. Finally, assess whether Thomson's argument survives: does the future-like-ours account, if sound, give the fetus any claim on the pregnant woman's body that Thomson overlooked?

The student's answer, transcribed below, was written in fifty minutes under exam conditions:

Thomson's strategy is to grant the anti-abortion side everything they usually insist on. She allows that the fetus is a person, with a full right to life, and then argues that abortion is still permissible in many cases. Her main tool is the violinist case: you wake up to find that the Society of Music Lovers has connected you, without your consent, to a famous violinist with a fatal kidney ailment, and you alone can keep him alive by remaining connected for nine months. Thomson says you may unplug yourself, and the case shows that the standard anti-abortion argument is invalid. That argument assumes that because the fetus has a right to life, it is wrong to kill it; the violinist also has a right to life, and yet it is permissible to unplug. The key is that the right to life is not a right to everything you need in order to go on living. It is one thing to have a right not to be killed unjustly, and another to have a right to the use of someone else's body. Thomson makes this vivid with her example of the dying woman whose life would be saved if Henry Fonda's cool hand were placed on her fevered brow: she has a right to life, but not a right that Fonda be flown in from the West Coast. Since unplugging merely lets the violinist die rather than killing him, the right to life is not engaged at all, and the woman who aborts likewise merely declines to provide life support. Because every pregnancy involves the fetus's use of the woman's body, Thomson's conclusion extends straightforwardly to all pregnancies, including those resulting from voluntary intercourse, with no further complications.

Marquis thinks the debate has stalled because both sides have been arguing about whether the fetus has a right to life, and neither side's arguments are convincing on that question. His way forward is to argue that the fetus is a potential person, and that a potential person already possesses the rights of an actual person, including a right to the valuable future it will eventually have. Killing is wrong, on his view, because it deprives the victim of a future like ours; therefore, since the fetus has such a future ahead of it, abortion is wrong in the same way that killing an adult is wrong. Since Marquis has established that the fetus has this right to its future, and since gestation is necessary for the fetus to enjoy that future, it follows that the fetus's right to life entails a claim on the pregnant woman's body, which is precisely what Thomson denied. So Thomson's argument does not survive: once we accept the future-like-ours account, the woman's bodily rights are outweighed in every case.
