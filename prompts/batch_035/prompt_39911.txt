So I write about science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I'm deep in reporting a long feature on whether we might someday (or already?) owe some kind of moral consideration to AI systems. Every conversation eventually slams into the hard problem of consciousness -- you know the drill, does the system actually *feel* anything or is it just processing. But then last month I talked to a researcher who brushed all that off and said the hard problem is basically a mistake people make about their own minds: on this view, associated with people like Daniel Dennett and Keith Frankish, phenomenal consciousness as we intuitively conceive it -- raw feels, the private redness of red -- is a kind of user illusion, like the icons on a desktop. Useful fiction generated by the brain to summarize what it's doing, not a real thing that could be present or absent in a system. I gather this gets called illusionism.

If that's actually right, it seems like it should reshuffle the whole AI welfare debate in ways I find hard to articulate, and honestly nobody I've interviewed has laid it out cleanly. Like, if the "what it's like" is a misrepresentation the brain tells itself, then asking whether an AI has real phenomenal pain might be a confused question -- but then what exactly IS the thing we should care about, morally? The state that generates the misrepresentation? The misrepresentation itself? And if introspective access is that unreliable in humans, doesn't that undercut using self-report ("I'm fine, I don't want to be shut down") as evidence in AI too, in both directions?

So here's what I want from you: rank the open questions in this neighborhood by how much they actually matter for how societies should handle AI welfare -- not for solving the philosophy once and for all, but for the practical decisions regulators or companies might face in the next decade or two. Some candidates off the top of my head (add or drop as you see fit): whether illusionism can even ground moral status at all, or whether it weirdly implies nothing has moral status the way we thought; whether there's a defensible surrogate for "consciousness" -- functional, behavioral, architectural -- that policy could be keyed to while philosophers fight; how much weight to give an AI's own reports about its states; and whether the hard problem itself deserves all the attention it gets in this debate or is basically overrated for practical purposes. Defend the ordering -- tell me your criteria (importance, tractability, how much downstream stuff depends on it, whatever else you'd weigh), where the tradeoffs bite, and what developments in the next few years would genuinely move things up or down your list. I want a defensible editorial agenda for the piece, not a survey of positions, and definetly not a reading list -- I have enough of those already, trust me.
