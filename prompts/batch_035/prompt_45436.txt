I spent my career in marine geophysics, and I was a graduate student during the years when the discipline turned, over barely half a decade, from treating continental drift as something between an error and an embarrassment to treating plate tectonics as the organizing framework of the whole earth sciences. In retirement I have finally read the philosophy of science properly, Kuhn included, and I have arrived at a position I would like stress-tested rather than admired. The position is this: the mobilist conversion of the early and mid-1960s was a Kuhnian paradigm shift in the strong sense, not the loose rhetorical sense the phrase has since acquired. Pre-revolutionary geology was not simply a discipline short of data; it was a fixist paradigm in Kuhn's full sense, with its own exemplars of good practice and its own tacit standards of what counted as evidence, standards under which drift was not so much refuted as unhearable — which is why Wegener had been dismissed for forty years, largely on the demand for a driving mechanism, while the theory that eventually prevailed was embraced without any comparably settled account of what drives the plates. And the conversion itself, though I watched senior colleagues convert on the strength of a single symmetric magnetic anomaly profile across a spreading ridge, had more the character of a change in which questions seemed worth asking than of a Bayesian weighing of evidence that had been on the table all along.

What would count against this? I want the map of the position's exposure, not a defense of it. Which features of the historical record would have to look different for the strong-Kuhnian reading to be wrong — for instance, what if it turned out that the pre-1960 community's resistance to mobilism was, given what was actually available to them, the rational verdict of standards shared with the winning side, so that the episode is better told as evidence accumulating until it simply overwhelmed a weaker theory? What if the postwar flood of marine geophysical data — the ridges, the trenches, the thin sediment cover of the sea floor — makes the change look like the gradual filling-in of a framework that was never really displaced? Which of these would committed defenders of the strong reading concede as counting against them, and which would they absorb by reinterpreting the episode? I am particularly troubled by the thought that the position may have been insulated by retreat: Kuhn himself spent decades qualifying the strong claims of his original book, and if every gradualist or accumulational feature of the actual history can be declared compatible with a localized, taxonomic notion of incommensurability, I cannot see what the thesis excludes. So rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which are live given what is actually known about the period rather than merely logically possible, and give me an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have stated it, risks anything at all — and if it does not, whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply the price of doing history of science at this grain. What I would personally find most persuasive is a demonstration that geologists before the conversion and geologists after it were, in a precise sense, applying the same standards to the same questions, and that the older men simply lost. Tell me whether the record sustains that, and, if it does not, whether anything short of it would genuinely embarrass the position.
