I'm preparing the framework chapter of my thesis on diachronic rationality, and I've decided that before I take sides on whether money pump arguments work, I should first be clear about what a theory of rational choice over time is supposed to deliver in the first place. The literature I've read keeps sliding between two different things: showing that an agent with cyclical or dynamically inconsistent preferences can in principle be drained of all its money, and showing that such an agent violates some substantive requirement of rationality. Those come apart more often than people admit, and I suspect the standard debates about whether the "exploiter" must be cleverer than the agent, or whether the agent must be able to foresee the whole sequence of trades, are really disputes about which desiderata matter. So instead of another round of "here's a pump, here's a blocking mechanism," I want the spec first.

Write me the list of requirements a theory of rationality over time must meet, with the case or consideration behind each. A few things I want you to take a position on, since I haven't seen them separated cleanly anywhere. First, it seems to me that money pumps divide into "round trips," where the agent is returned to its starting option minus a fee, and what I want to call "shuffling pumps," where the agent never comes back to where it started but pays for a path to an option it could have had for free at the outset; I can't decide whether only the first kind shows irrationality or whether the weaker condition suffices, so tell me which the theory must rule out and why. Second, some pumps require the exploiter to know the agent's preferences better than the agent knows its own future ones, while others run blind, on a fixed menu announced in advance; it matters to me whether the theory's requirements are allowed to depend on that contrast. Third, the theory has to say something about agents who bind themselves in advance — the sort of resolute choice McClennen defends — and whether permitting such commitments is a legitimate way of meeting the requirements or just re-labeling the problem. Beyond these, cover what else genuinely needs to be on the list: whether the theory must keep synchronic and diachronic standards of rationality apart, how it should treat changing tastes as opposed to inconsistent ones, whether it may demand that agents be able to predict their own future preferences, and whether empirically documented phenomena like status quo bias and the endowment effect are things the theory must accommodate or errors it is licensed to explain away.

For each requirement, say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp, and — this is the part I most want to see — whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, such that the list itself forces a choice about which rationality to give up. Close with a brief indication of which single requirement does the most work in excluding the main contenders (transitivity-only approaches, backward induction with sophisticated choice, resolute-choice theories, and the view that diachronic consistency is a requirement not of rationality but of prudence), but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard — I'll do the detailed evaluation in the chapter itself.
