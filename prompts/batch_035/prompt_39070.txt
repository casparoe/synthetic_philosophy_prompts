I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and one line I've seen repeated for years — usually deployed as a knockdown argument — is that if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie can construct a package of bets, each of which you would accept, that guarantees you a net loss; and that satisfying the axioms is what protects you from this. I've come to suspect both halves are looser than the folklore suggests: the first conflates being *vulnerable* to a book with being *exploited* by one, and the second holds, at most, of coherence at a moment. I want a pair of thought experiments for a blog post making this concrete, and I'd like you to build them to specification.

Set both cases in the same friendly wagering circle: a group chat where people post odds and take small bets on real-world contests — awards, elections, that sort of thing. Stakes in the tens to hundreds of dollars, small relative to everyone's budgets, so utility is effectively linear and non-linearity of value is not doing any work. The bookie, where one appears, is an ordinary sharp member of the group: never superhuman, never certain of any contest outcome before it is announced, working only from what the agents post and from public information. One paragraph per scenario.

Case A: an agent whose posted prices violate the probability axioms — give an explicit incoherent price list over an exhaustive three-way partition (three finalists, one of whom must win; her implied probabilities should sum to more than 1) — who nonetheless cannot be Dutch-booked: no sequence of transactions available to the bookie guarantees him a profit in every possible world. The protection must be structural, not epistemic: she is neither suspicious nor lucky, and she accepts every bet she considers favorable at the moment it is offered. What protects her must be some standing feature of how her wagering is organized — for instance something about how many wagers she allows to be open at once, or about when wagers settle, or some other structural feature you devise — and it should be a feature a normal person could have for ordinary reasons (simple bookkeeping, capped attention), not an obviously contrived firewall. Assume the contests are unrelated and that no assignment of outcomes is ruled out by anything the bookie knows at booking time.

Case B: an agent whose prices satisfy the probability axioms at every single moment, yet who is exploited all the same. The natural home for this is across time. Have her post, today, a coherent prior over a two-stage question — a signal that will be observed publicly (an exit poll, a lab result, a leaked shortlist) and a hypothesis it bears on — together with a posted updating plan that deviates from conditionalization in both branches: overconfident in the signal in one branch, dismissive of it in the other, with whatever realistic psychology motivates it. The bookie, reading her posts, then constructs a sequence of transactions, each placed at a price she endorses at the moment it is placed, that guarantees her a net loss in every possible world.

After each paragraph, show the mechanics. For A: exhibit the book that would exist without the protective feature, then explain why, with the feature in place, no strategy can guarantee the bookie a profit — the all-adverse-outcomes line of argument is acceptable if you state it carefully. For B: give the four states, list the transactions in order, and give a table of her net in each state — every entry negative.

Then, for each case, tell me three things. First, which feature of the case is doing the work. Second, what happens when you remove it — in A, dropping the protective feature should collapse her into the standard synchronic book, and you should check that it does; in B, replacing her update plan with conditionalization should make the book impossible, and you should check that it does. Third, how a defender of the Dutch book argument would reply. The obvious reply in A is that the theorem never promised actual exploitation, only vulnerability — say whether that reply saves the folk reading or only the theorem. The obvious reply in B is that this merely shows the argument's framework extends diachronically — say why a critic might regard that as a concession. Throughout, don't stipulate results: the books, and their absence, must be derived from the prices and the setup, not announced; and don't give either agent a stipulated quirk that begs the question (in particular, do not describe the agent in A as merely "never exploited").

After the walkthrough, append a JSON object in a code block with exactly this schema:

- "case_a": an object with fields "scenario" (one sentence), "posted_prices" (the incoherent list), "protection" (the structural feature), "load_bearing_feature" (one sentence on why it blocks all guaranteed-profit strategies);
- "case_b": an object with fields "scenario" (one sentence), "posted_prices_today" (the coherent prior), "posted_update_plan" (the deviation in each branch), "the_book" (a compact table of her net by state, as an array of objects with "state" and "net" fields);
- "collapse_tests": an object with fields "case_a_without_protection" and "case_b_with_conditionalization", each one sentence;
- "defender_replies": an object with fields "case_a" and "case_b", each two or three sentences;
- "verdict": a string saying which case does more damage to the folk reading, and why.
