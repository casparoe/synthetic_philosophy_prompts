I'm a junior analyst at a foreign policy think tank, and my job is mostly background memos that get a paragraph of a senior person's attention, max. The senior staff reach for historical analogies constantly -- "this is Munich all over again," "we've all seen how Vietnam ends," "rising power vs ruling power, that's the Thucydides trap" -- and half my work is figuring out whether the analogy is doing real inferential work or just decorating a conclusion the writer already had. Write me a procedure I can actually run on an analogy before it goes in a memo: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "cut the analogy and make the argument directly," and where it has to hand the decision back to me because it's a judgment call dressed up as a checklist. Ground it in the actual reasons these analogies mislead -- that out of many historical cases that pattern-match, the writer picks the one whose lesson they already like; that a contested interpretation of the past gets frozen into an apparent fact; that similarity of surface situation gets swapped for similarity of causal structure -- not just a generic "analogies are risky, be careful." Then run it on three cases: a trade dispute with a rising power where people keep saying the pre-1914 Anglo-German naval rivalry is the template; a sanctions debate where someone claims a Cuba-style embargo "never works, look how that turned out"; and one where I personally like the anology and the procedure shouldn't care that I like it -- say I want to call a leader's foreign policy blunder a Suez. Include at least one place where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and be honest about what the whole thing can't settle. And no reading lists -- if I want the litereature I'll find it myself, I need something I can run in twenty minutes before the memo goes out.
