I'm putting together a short position paper for a graduate workshop on democratic inclusion, and before I circulate it to the group I want a proper consistency check, because I have a suspicion I'm committed to more than I can actually defend as a package. Here is where I've landed. (1) I endorse the all-subjected principle: anyone who is subject to the binding, coercively enforced laws of a collective decision-making body is entitled to a say in that body's decisions, which is why I support voting rights for long-term resident non-citizens and have little patience for the view that political membership is essentially cultural or ethnic. (2) I'm a cosmopolitan about moral status: I think borders are morally arbitrary, that equal concern for persons doesn't stop at a boundary, and that in the long run democratic institutions ought to be organized on something closer to a global scale rather than a state scale. (3) At the same time, I believe democratic authority is more than a decision procedure — it presupposes a bounded people with some baseline of shared history, mutual trust, and a common civic language, since a majority vote among mutual strangers is just external imposition by headcount, and this is also why states can legitimately refuse to be dissolved or merged by a plebiscite of outsiders. (4) I'm confident that future generations, and distant people affected by a state's climate or trade policy, are not wronged by lacking a vote in that state's elections — their claims are real, but they should be met through representation, duties of justice, and judicial review, not enfranchisement. (5) I reject the all-affected principle as hopelessly indeterminate: being affected by a decision can't specify a demos, because nearly everyone is affected, in some attenuated way, by nearly everything.

So the question I'd like you to work through is: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, which only look like it, and where does the set become untenable? I'm less interested in a tour of the literature than in an audit of my own position — for each real tension, say how severe it is, what would have to give, and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the package. A few things I specifically want your judgment on: does the "bounded people" claim in (3) quietly do the cultural-membership work I officially disavow in (1)? Does (4) actually follow from (1) and (5), or am I helping myself to a subjected/affected distinction that (2) won't let me draw cleanly at the global scale? And is there a single revision — to one of the five claims — that would clean up most of the mess, or is the problem structural enough that any fix just relocates it? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally, and don't assume you know which commitment I'd be most willing to give up, because I honestly don't know.
