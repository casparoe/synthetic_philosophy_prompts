I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophical training. For the past year I've been following an online reading group that works through philosophy of science, and the current unit — dispositions, and the fink and mask cases invented to trouble the conditional analysis of them — is where I've ended up out on a limb. My position, put plainly: a disposition is just an intrinsic property that supports a certain counterfactual. The fragile glass has an internal structure such that, if struck while that structure is held fixed and nothing interferes, it would break. The cases the group treats as deep discoveries strike me as contract violations. A fink — some process that would remove the disposition at the very moment the trigger arrives, like the stock sorcerer who would render the glass unbreakable a split second before the blow lands — is a watchdog that rewrites the state before the request propagates. A mask — the glass packed in foam, struck, not breaking, yet still fragile, something preventing the manifestation without removing the disposition — is a firewall dropping the outbound packet. Every engineer knows the difference between a service's contract (given a healthy network and no concurrent fault, respond within the agreed latency) and what actually happens when the network is down or the watchdog restarts the service mid-request; nobody concludes from network failures that responsiveness was never an intrinsic feature of the service, or that we need an ontology of irreducible powers behind it. We just write the preconditions into the contract. That, I claim, is the entire lesson of finks and masks: the naive one-line conditional analysis was too simple, and the fix is the kind of fixed-background clause Lewis proposed, not a new metaphysics in which dispositions are extrinsic, or partner-dependent with their triggers and manifestations, or unanalyzable.

The group disagrees, loudly, and I have a session coming up in which I have to defend the line. So what I want from you is a committed assessment of what would actually count against my view — not a survey of positions and not an invitation for me to choose. Give me exactly five defeaters, ranked from most to least serious, each stated in one or two sentences, and nothing outside the numbered list: no introduction, no conclusion. For each item, take a definite position in the sentence itself — genuinely counts against the view, merely embarrasses it, or turns out on inspection to support it — with the reason in a clause. The final item should be your overall verdict on whether the view, once its fixed-background clause is spelled out, has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation would be a defect for a view of this kind or simply what any analysis has to look like once it names its preconditions. What would move me most, for what it's worth, is a case where the background clause cannot be specified without appealing to the very disposition being analyzed — so tell me honestly whether that circularity worry is one of the five or whether you think it dissolves. Don't hedge into observations about how it all depends on how things get spelled out; declare your own sympathies and rank anyway. I would much rather walk into the session with a definite ranking I can argue against than a balanced overview that leaves the picking to me.
