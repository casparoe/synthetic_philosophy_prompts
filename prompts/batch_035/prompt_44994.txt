I'm teaching an intro probability/philosophy crossover class, 18 students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity around Bertrand's chord paradox where the students discover the problem themselves instead of being told. The idea: they generate "random chords" of a circle with three different physical procedures -- say dropping straws on a circle drawn on the floor, picking two random points on the circumference, picking a random radius and a random point along it -- each group tallying the fraction of chords longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. The punchline should land when the groups compare numbers and realize they got 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4 from what everyone insisted was the same experiment, "pick a random chord."

Rules, materials, how to randomize with cheap stuff (spinners, dice, coins -- no laptops), roughly how many chords per group before the empirical fractions start separating, and a debrief that maps each procedure onto a choice of parametrization, so they see that "at random" was never fully specified and the principle of indifference is doing silent work. Then two extra things I want in the debrief: (1) a way to get them to see that a real physical process fixes an answer, so the paradox is partly about language, not just about probability; and (2) a follow-up where groups try to invent a fourth procedure giving yet another fraction, and we discuss whether there's any principled way to say which of the four answers (if any) is THE probability of a random chord being long.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- e.g. straw-dropping turns out not to be uniform in any of the three senses, students cluster their "random" circumference points, or the fractions don't separate with small samples -- and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: which part of the indifference-principle debate just has to be lectured at the end.
