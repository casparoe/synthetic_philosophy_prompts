My game theory seminar has spent a year with two members in permanent deadlock over the centipede game, and I would like you to write the joint statement they should now be able to sign, on the model of an adversarial collaboration.

The two parties are as follows. The first, whom I will call the orthodox theorist, holds that in the standard alternating-move centipede game with strictly increasing payoffs and common knowledge of rationality and of the payoffs, the uniquely rational play is to take at the first node, that the backward induction argument establishing this is sound as a piece of reasoning, and that any observed or intuited cooperation must trace back to the failure of one of the premises rather than of the reasoning. The second, whom I will call the cooperation-friendly theorist, holds that rational players can sometimes justifiably pass, drawing on at least one of the following grounds: that common knowledge of rationality cannot coherently be assumed to persist at a node whose reachability the induction argument itself denies, so that the argument eats its own premises; that an opponent who has already passed has revealed something that licenses forward reasoning rather than backward reasoning about her subsequent play; or that under realistic uncertainty about payoffs, competence, or types, passing can be optimal from the start.

I would like the document you draft to contain, in this order:

(1) A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These should include claims about the formal structure of the game (alternating moves, common knowledge of the payoff tree, take ends the game with payoffs favoring the taker), claims about the validity of each individual step of the induction argument taken in isolation (given its premises at that node), and claims about the robust stylized facts of experimental play: that human subjects typically pass a few nodes before taking, that taking comes earlier when stakes are raised, and that play drifts toward the first node with experience.

(2) A disagreements table stating precisely where the parties still part ways, each row phrased so that the party holding the view would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own position. The disagreements should be genuine rather than verbal where possible; but where the parties discover that a disagreement is really about the word "rational" or about which premises are being held fixed, the document should say so explicitly and say which rows this affects.

(3) A cruxes section. I suspect the following are among them, and I want them addressed, alongside any others you judge important: (a) whether the off-path instability of common knowledge of rationality is a technical defect of the analysis, repairable by weakening to common belief or by limiting the depth of mutual knowledge, or whether it refutes the backward induction conclusion outright; (b) whether the standard incomplete-information repair — introducing a small probability that the opponent is an altruist who always passes, so that passing becomes a best response while waiting to find out — explains all sensible passing, or merely relocates the puzzle, since a further question then arises about whether the players' beliefs about such types are themselves formed in a way the induction analysis can vindicate; (c) whether the structural analogy with the surprise examination paradox is a genuine kinship or an accident of surface form, and whether it cuts in favor of either party.

For each crux, include a case, experiment, or argument that both parties would accept as bearing on it, together with each party's prediction about it, recorded as if in advance. For the empirical cruxes, use variations the seminar could actually run or examine: raising stakes, lengthening the centipede, letting subjects play repeatedly with feedback, announcing that opponents have been pre-screened for computational competence, and so forth. For each party, state in its own words what outcome it would count as evidence against its view.

Close the document with a short agreed paragraph on what, if anything, both parties concede would genuinely move the debate, and note candidly if either party believes no possible observation or argument could move it.
