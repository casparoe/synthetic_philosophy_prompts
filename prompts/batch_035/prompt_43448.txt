My friend is really into lottocracy and last week he walked me through an argument I keep failing to find a hole in. (1) The core of democracy is that political power is distributed equally among citizens. (2) Elections can't do that, since who wins depends on money, charisma, connections, and the willingness to make a career of politics, so real influence ends up concentrated in a self-selected class. (3) A lottery does distribute power equally: everyone has the same chance of being drawn, so ex ante each citizen holds exactly the same share. (4) So a legislature chosen by lot is more democratic than one chosen by election. He says that's the whole case for sortition in four steps.

Here's my problem. I tried to accept it and immediately noticed it proves too much. Imagine we scrap the whole legislature and instead, every four years, draw one citizen by lot, and that person rules alone by decree until the next draw. Everyone still had an equal chance — the distribution of chances is exactly as equal as in his scheme, its just that the prize is bigger. If equal chances is what makes a power distribution democratic, then random dictatorship is democratic too, which is obviously insane. When I pushed him on this he got shifty and said the difference is that his scheme divides power among 500 people instead of 1. But that can't be the whole story, because a lottery that picks 5 people would also divide it, and it still seems obviously wrong — so now his argument depends on some magic cutoff number that nobody has given.

So which step do I actually reject? I'd rather keep (2) — I think he's genuinely right about elections — and I suspect the real problem is that "equal chance" means something different in (3) than it does when I extend it to the dictator case, but I can't pin down exactly how. Walk me through where the argument breaks and what it costs to break it there. And if the difference really is about diluting power across a large body, tell me whether there's a principled cutoff or whether his view just has to eat the 5-person legislature.
