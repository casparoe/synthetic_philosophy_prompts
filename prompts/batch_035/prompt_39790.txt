I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; my background is in ML systems and probabilistic modelling, and I've come to the topic below sideways, through technical material rather than philosophy papers. For the last two years I've owned the uncertainty-reporting layer of a risk tool: we ingest probability estimates from model ensembles and elicited forecasts from engineers, and we have to surface something to product teams. The recurring fight is what to do when sources disagree or are silent — whether to average the numbers, report a range, or refuse to output anything — and I've slowly realized that each of my colleagues is dragging in an unexamined position about what a probability even is when an agent's information doesn't pin one down. I've read enough about imprecise probabilities on the technical side to know the labels exist, but not enough philosophy to know which of my colleagues holds which view, or whether some of their views are coherent at all.

What I'd like is a diagnostic quiz — "which view of credal uncertainty do you actually hold?" — that I can run at an internal workshop with about fifteen technically sophisticated colleagues (most of them will know the Ellsberg-style paradoxes in some form, but none will know the philosophy literature by name). Please produce:

1. Eight to ten diagnostic questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so a respondent can answer from their reactions to the case alone, without knowing which theory each option points to. Include at least: a fresh Ellsberg-style urn or betting case of your own construction (not the textbook wording), a case where updating on evidence widens rather than narrows the agent's uncertainty, an expert-aggregation case where two trusted forecasters give divergent numbers, a case about whether an agent with no settled credence must still be able to choose an action, and at least one case where the answer hinges on whether the width of an agent's uncertainty interval is itself a fact about the world or just a confession of ignorance.

2. A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the named positions in the literature. At minimum the key should distinguish: sharp-credence Bayesianism (one number per proposition, always); the credal-set view of imprecise probabilities, with its internal split between decision rules such as maximin-style rules, E-admissibility, and other tie-breaking policies as separate leaves or clearly flagged sub-positions; views on which the divergent-forecasters problem is really about preferences over ambiguity rather than credences; and any qualitative or coarse-grained position on which probabilities are only ever meaningfully ordinal.

3. A note on the logical space itself: build the key from the combinations of answers the questions permit first, and then locate the actual named positions in that space. If any combination is unoccupied by any defended position, say so and give me your best one-sentence diagnosis of why — incoherent, unattractive, or just never explored.

4. An honest flag on separations the quiz cannot make. I'm specifically worried that two colleagues with different decision rules over the same credal set will answer every case identically until a choice is forced, and I'd rather know that up front than discover it mid-workshop.

One constraint from my side that I'd like the quiz to interrogate rather than assume: my team must ultimately emit a single number downstream, and I've started to suspect that our commitment to doing so is itself a philosophical position — that forcing aggregation is quietly ruling out a family of views before the quiz even starts. If the quiz can locate a respondent by where they stand on that constraint, so much the better, but please don't rig questions to expose it; it should emerge from the cases like everything else. No question may simply ask whether someone "believes in" imprecise probabilities or any other named view, and please avoid assuming familiarity with any specific author's terminology in the question text itself — the labels belong in the scoring key, not in the questions.
