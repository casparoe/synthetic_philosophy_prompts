My roommate, who works as a prompt engineer, and my old college friend, now a philosophy PhD student, spent most of a dinner party arguing about whether the AI assistant I use daily for work "actually believes" anything, and they've asked me to referee by email, though I don't trust myself to do it fairly. For context: I've started relying on this assistant heavily — it plans itineraries, catches my errors, and even anticipated an unstated constraint I'd forgotten to mention — which is what set off the argument in the first place. My roommate's opening move was that when something reliably behaves as though it remembers your constraints, corrects itself when you point out a mistake, and adjusts plans when circumstances change, we've attributed beliefs for far less; on the intentional-stance view he sketched, treating a system as a believer is just the best available predictive strategy, and if that's the whole story about belief, then the assistant believes in exactly the sense anyone does. The philosophy student responded that he's conflating the usefulness of the stance with its truth: adopting belief-talk because it predicts well is an instrumental choice about us, the predictors, and settles nothing about what's really going on inside — otherwise we'd be committed to thermostats having rudimentary beliefs about temperature, which he took to be a reductio. My roommate said he'd take the bullet on the thermostat but insisted the assistant differs in kind, not just degree: it generalizes across domains, defends its claims with reasons, and revises under pressure, which is a richer pattern than any thermometer.

Round two got sharper. The philosophy student argued that what we're seeing isn't belief but a faithful reproduction of the texts of believers: the system was optimized to mimic the outputs of people who had beliefs, the way a gifted improviser can portray grief without feeling it, so its apparent opinions are real only "in the way a photograph of a fire is hot." He added that genuine belief must be formed in the right way — sensitive to evidence and to how the world actually is — and a system trained to produce text that sounds right, regardless of whether it is right, fails that condition. My roommate's counterattack was that the same can be said of us: human beliefs were shaped by evolutionary and social pressures that reward sounding right and being persuasive, not truth as such, so if a belief's checkered causal origin disqualifies it, the disqualification sweeps us up too. The philosophy student's last word was that there's a difference between pressures that mostly track truth because accuracy aided our ancestors' survival, and a training objective aimed squarely at imitating believability — "selection for believing well" versus "selection for seeming to believe" — and he accused my roommate of sliding between them. My roommate just said "seeming to believe is what believing functionally is" and they adjourned to dessert.

Please adjudicate this exchange in American English, going through it move by move rather than just declaring a winner: which of the specific claims each of them makes is right, which is mistaken, and which trades on an ambiguity; whether the thermostat reductio works against my roommate or actually supports him; whether the mimicry objection and the "formed in the right way" objection are distinct or one complaint stated twice; and whether my roommate's last assertion is a profound point or a definitional dodge that ends the argument only by changing the subject. I'd also like to hear what, if anything, you think both of them are missing — they never once discussed what would settle the question, only what the right vocabulary is, and I can't tell whether that's the argument's weakness or its insight.
