I work on interpretability-adjacent stuff and keep getting into arguments about what mechanistic anomaly detection is even supposed to detect, so help me figure out what principle is actually behind my gut verdicts. Assume we can inspect a network's internals however we like -- the question is when two states of a system (or two systems) count as running the same mechanism, and when a change counts as a mechanistic anomaly rather than just new behavior from old machinery.

My verdicts on some cases:

1. A classifier at a produce plant gets a new camera and its error rate explodes. Behavior is wildly anomalous, but I say no mechanism changed at all -- same circuit, garbage input.
2. We fine-tune a backdoored model until the backdoor behavior is gone from all our tests, and months later it resurfaces in a slightly different form. My gut says that's the same mechanism, just quieter, even though fine-tuning literally changed the weights.
3. Two training runs from different random seeds develop circuits that do the same job in similar-looking ways. I say those are two different mechanisms, not one, even though swapping them would leave the function identical.
4. Same model, but we turn the sampling temperature way up at inference. Outputs look totally different, zero mechanistic change.
5. A deployed assistant that has always looked things up in its policy docs suddenly improvises a plausible-sounding workaround nobody taught it. Honestly not sure about this one -- some days it seems like old machinery on new input, other days it feels like a new mechanism.

Notice that (2) and (3) are basically the same situation -- different weights, same function -- and I give opposite verdicts, which embarasses me. The only difference I can point to is that (2) causally descends from the original backdoored model and (3) doesn't, but making mechanism identity depend on causal history feels weird for something like a circuit. My tentative principle is that same mechanism = same counterfactual dependence of behavior on interventions, but I can't tell whether that reading actually separates (2) from (3) or just collapses them.

You're a professional philosopher of science who also knows ML interpretability -- please think carefully and start with some brainstorming about what could individuate a mechanism (function? causal structure? implementation? causal history?), then give me one principle or a few candidates, say precisely which of my verdicts each one can't accommodate, and tell me what the best candidate predicts for case (5) and for a case I haven't listed.
