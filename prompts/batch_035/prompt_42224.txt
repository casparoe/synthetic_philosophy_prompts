My friend has firm and, as far as I can tell, consistent opinions about whether misleading someone is morally as bad as lying to them, and I want to predict her verdict on a new case before I raise it with her. Her track record so far: she says the real-estate agent who, asked directly about flooding, replies that the sellers never mentioned any water problems — knowing full well the basement floods — is every bit as culpable as a liar; likewise the landlord who answers a question about whether the old footbridge is safe by noting that it passed its last inspection, knowing the inspection predates the visible cracking. By contrast, she says the politician who answers "Have you taken money from that lobbyist?" with "I have never taken a bribe" — technically true, since the donation was legal and made precisely to buy influence — is misleading but less culpable than a liar; and the child who deflects "Who broke the vase?" with "The cat was in the living room all morning" is naughty, but not as bad as a child who lies outright. The new case: a financial advisor, asked by a nervous client whether her savings are safe in this fund, replies that the fund has returned capital to every investor to date — knowing the fund is barely a year old and loaded with illiquid junk. What will she say about the advisor, which rule is she implicitly running, what rival rules fit her track record just as well, and what further case would best tell those rules apart?
