I'm on the interpretive planning panel for a mid-size regional museum, and we're building an exhibit about the Irish famine of the 1840s — it's partly local history for us since so many of the famine immigrants settled in our area, and the centerpiece is a wall panel that will basically define the whole exhibit. We're stuck on the framing, and it's getting a bit heated. One faction (mostly the older trustees) wants the heading to present it as a natural disaster — the potato blight was a plant disease, the weather was bad, framing it that way keeps us "neutral" and out of politics. The other faction says that's not neutral at all, it's quietly exculpatory: they point out that food was still being exported out of the country while people were dying, that relief was grudging, means-tested, and shaped by an ideological faith that the market would sort itself out, and they want the panel to say outright that the deaths were a policy failure, not a harvest failure. A couple of them have even used the phrase "political crime," which made the curators wince.

We have a meeting Thursday and I'm supposed to circulate a recommendation memo beforehand. What I'd like from you is a draft of that memo: a clear recommendation for how the panel should be framed (including a proposed heading and a sentence or two of actual panel text), plus the reasoning behind it. The part I keep circling and can't get right is this: when a crop fails but there's still food in the shops, and people starve anyway because they can't afford it — what exactly is it that caused the deaths? The blight, or the fact that having money was the price of admission to eating? It feels like the whole framing fight reduces to that question, and I'd like the memo to actually engage it rather than just gesture at "balance." It should also honestly deal with the strongest point on the other side — the curators' worry that "crime" language implies someone intended the deaths, which is precisely the thing historians argue about, and that it will read as advocacy and drag the museum into a fight we didn't ask for. If you think there's a wording that neither excuses nor accuses, and can say why it's more than a dodge, that would help — and if the commitee is likely to deadlock anyway, include a fallback option with the interactive question we're considering for visitors ("what turns a crop failure into a famine?") and how the exhibit could put the question to them instead of answering it for them. Sorry for the length, this has been eating at me all week.
