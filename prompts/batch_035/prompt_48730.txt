I teach an upper-level seminar on Bayesian epistemology, fifteen students, one 75-minute session, and by this point they've seen ordinary conditionalization and worked through a few likelihood-ratio examples. I want a game that makes them feel why uncertain evidence is a genuinely different kind of thing, not just weak evidence. Design an in-class activity around Jeffrey conditionalization: start with rounds where the evidence is clean and straight conditioning works, then a round where the signal is noisy and the obvious move — treat what you saw as certain and condition on it — visibly wrecks their posteriors. Payoffs in points, no real money, and materials should be stuff I can actually get my hands on (cards, coins, envelopes, maybe a spreadsheet if it earns it's place). Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal machinery: where the likelihoods P(E|H) get held fixed, where the new probabilities over the evidence-states come from, and how Jeffrey's rule is different from just conditioning on "I saw E".

Here's the part I care most about, because I think it's where the real content lives. In the noisy round, the reliability of the signal is announced — say I tell them the deck matches urn A seventy percent of the time — so a sharp student can condition on the disjunctive proposition "red card drawn from the 70% deck" and recover exactly the Jeffrey answer. That escape hatch is fine for the noisy round, but I want a third phase where it closes: a setup where the probability of the evidence shifts but there's no obvious proposition to condition on. Maybe a witness whose reliability the participants can only estimate themselves, or a signal they simply become less sure they saw. Then the debrief should ask the group whether the game has actually produced Jeffrey updating at that point, or whether the shift is still explicable by conditioning on something hidden in the participants' own credences — and it should be honest that this is a live issue people still argue about, not something with a settled answer I can hand them.
