I teach an applied ethics elective for second-year undergraduates — about sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute session — and next week's topic is the moral difference, if any, between lying and merely misleading. Rather than lecturing on the distinction between asserting something false and getting someone to believe something false, I want the students to feel the strain of it in practice and then interrogate whether the distinction they have spent an hour exploiting does any real moral work.

Design a negotiation game with the following backbone: half the class plays sellers' agents who must unload a flawed product (a used car with a hidden defect works well, though you may choose something better) under a strict truth rule — every assertion they make must be literally true, checked against a fact sheet only the referees see — while the buyers try to learn what is wrong through free-form questions. Emphasis, topic shifts, conspicuous silences, and technically true answers are all legal moves; flat-out false assertions are fouls carrying escalating point penalties. Rotate the roles so that everyone misleads, everyone is misled, and everyone referees. After the main round, include a prosecution phase in which each exchange is read back literally, the buyer states for the record what they came to believe, and the class votes on whether they were lied to — I want the gap between the literal record and the induced belief to be the hinge of the whole session, and I want the debrief to pick up from there.

A few things I want you to decide rather than leave open. First, commit to a debrief that takes a definite position: either the debrief argues that the moral difference between lying and misleading is real and substantial, or it argues that the difference is morally thin, a concession to self-image rather than to ethics — pick one, build the debrief's discussion questions around it, and tell me why you chose it over the alternative. Second, I have a hunch that the game's most important lesson is that misleading only works because buyers assume their interlocutors are cooperating in the usual way — the misleader is free-riding on a norm they are covertly violating — and I want the debrief to make the sellers notice that they were parasitic on trust rather than merely clever. Tell me whether that hunch fits the mechanics you design, or whether you would rework the game so that it does. Third, somewhere in the debrief I want the students' technically true moves mapped onto the historical repertoire of the same trick — the inquiring murderer whom Kant refused to let us lie to, and the casuistic practice of mental reservation by which the scrupulous reconciled deception with conscience — but without turning the debrief into a history lecture: a few minutes at most, tied to specific moves the students actually made in the room.
