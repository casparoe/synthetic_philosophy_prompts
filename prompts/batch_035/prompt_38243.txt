I am a recently retired inorganic chemist who has, somewhat to my own surprise, become absorbed in philosophy since leaving the laboratory—mostly ethics and philosophy of history, which I read about the way I once read spectroscopy tables, hunting for underlying regularities. I now volunteer on my small city's Commemoration Review Board, an eleven-member body that advises the council on statues, monuments, plaques, street names, and the naming of public buildings. My scientific instinct is that our past decisions must implicitly implement some classification rule, the way a taxonomic key does, and I would like your help reverse-engineering it.

Here is our track record over the past six years. First, we recommended full removal of the courthouse-square statue of General A. H. Redmond, a nineteenth-century militia commander who led a massacre of an Indigenous village, though we asked that the pedestal be retained with an interpretive panel about the massacre itself; this followed two years of sustained public protest. Second, we recommended renaming Verraneau Street, which honored a newspaper editor whose entire public reputation rested on decades of campaigning for racial segregation; a residents' organization had petitioned for the renaming. Third, we declined to remove the campus statue of Elias Kettering, the founder of our university, who enslaved people for much of his adult life, but recommended an added contextual plaque; the faculty senate had requested removal. Fourth, we took no action at all on Hollister Street, named for an industrialist whose foundry crushed a strike in which three workers died, though he also endowed the public library and the children's ward of the hospital; only a lone local historian had raised the matter. Fifth, we voted to move the bronze bust of Dr. Cordelia Marsh, a pioneering surgeon, from the public library's entrance to a glass case in the local history museum with explanatory text, after newly digitized records showed she had performed involuntary sterilizations on institutionalized patients as a young clinician; the disclosure came from a graduate student and was covered in one newspaper article before our hearings began.

Our new case: Bannerman Park contains a statue of Dr. Owen Fairweather, the physician who established the town's children's hospital and is credited with containing the 1908 fever epidemic; he is honored specifically and solely for those achievements. A retired archivist has just shown us unpublished ledgers indicating that Fairweather, to test a treatment theory, deliberately infected a dozen orphaned wards of the county home with a mild form of the disease, two of whom died. There has been no protest and no press coverage beyond a brief item, and the board must decide whether to recommend removal, relocation to the museum, a contextual plaque, or no action. Please give me your analysis as a JSON object with the following fields: "recommended_decision" (a string stating one of the four options and any conditions); "closest_precedent" (which of the five earlier cases is the strongest analog, and why); "candidate_principles" (an array of objects, each with a "name", a "statement" of the rule in one sentence, and "prediction_here" for the Fairweather case); "preferred_principle" (which rule you would extract from our record and the reasoning, especially how you weigh severity of wrongdoing against whether the honor rests on the wrongdoing against the presence or absence of public pressure); "principle_being_adopted" (an honest statement of what principle my recommendation would commit the board to going forward, including any uncomfortable implications); and "discriminating_case" (a hypothetical commemoration decision, described in two or three sentences, on which the leading candidate rules would give different verdicts, so we can learn which rule we are actually running).
