I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I've read enough to follow a real argument when I overhear one. Two of my colleagues — Dana and Marcus, both also engineers — have been going back and forth for a week about Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, and at lunch on Friday it went something like this. I've numbered the moves so you can follow the sequence.

(1) Dana said the theory is refuted by its own consequences. Nozick's conditions — roughly, that you believe P, and in the closest world where P is false you wouldn't believe it, and in the closest world where P is true you would — end up implying that knowledge is not closed under known entailment. You know you have hands; you know that having hands entails that you're not a handless brain in a vat; yet by the tracking conditions you don't know you're not a brain in a vat, since if you were one, you'd still believe you weren't. Dana said any analysis with that consequence is broken, full stop: it's a reductio of the analysis, not an insight about knowledge.

(2) Marcus answered that closure is just one intuition among many, and Dana was treating it as if it were a theorem. The tracking theory earns its keep by explaining things rival theories can't: why Gettier's luck cases aren't knowledge, why the grandmother who is told her grandson is fine even when he's ill (so she'd believe it either way) doesn't know he's fine, why nobody knows their lottery ticket will lose even when it's overwhelmingly probable that it will. Every theory of knowledge has to bite some bullet; the tracking theory is just transparent about which one it bites.

(3) Dana pressed on the specific shape of the bite. The problem isn't merely weirdness; it's instability. On the tracking theory you can know P, competently deduce Q from P, retain your knowledge of P, and still fail to know Q. Dana said that licenses someone to say: I know the build passed, I know the build passing entails the integration tests weren't all silently skipped, but I don't know the tests weren't skipped — and Marcus's theory is supposed to tell me that's a coherent epistemic state rather than a contradiction. Dana claimed that's not a bullet being bitten, it's the gun exploding.

(4) Marcus then brought in an analogy from our actual work. A good regression test, he said, is exactly what the sensitivity condition describes: a test that would fail in the world where the bug exists and passes in the world where it doesn't. And everyone accepts that such a test gives you knowledge of the code's behavior. But the test tells you nothing about whether the CI runner itself is compromised, or whether you're dreaming that the test passed. We don't say the test therefore gives you no knowledge at all; we say it gives you knowledge of the code and nothing about the environment it ran in. That, Marcus said, is just the non-closure of knowledge made visible in engineering practice, and it's fine.

(5) Dana's last word was that the analogy proves too much. If the test suite runs on infrastructure you have no reason whatsoever to distrust, and the tests all pass, then Dana claimed you do know the tests weren't silently skipped — the skeptical scenario is idle, and a theory that withholds knowledge in idle-skepticism cases has misdiagnosed what knowing is. Marcus's theory, Dana said, confuses what a method tracks with what a knower knows, and ordinary knowledge attributions side with the knower.

They then went back to their desks, and I've been unable to work out who's right since. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether the person is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and — since I suspect they're both missing something — identify what, if anything, both of them overlook about how closure and tracking are supposed to interact.
