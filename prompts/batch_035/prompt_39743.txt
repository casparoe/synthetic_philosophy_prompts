Since Amotz Zahavi proposed the handicap principle in his 1975 paper on mate selection, evolutionary biology has hosted a running argument about whether honest signals must be costly, and after half a century it has, as far as I can tell, stopped moving: the participants are still trading the same three moves, and each camp reads the same formal results as vindication. I don't want a survey of the positions — I want a diagnosis of the impasse, so let me lay out the stall as I see it and offer a hypothesis, which you should feel free to dismantle.

The original claim was that signals are reliable precisely because they are expensive: a stag's roaring display or a peacock's tail is believed only because only a high-quality individual can afford to waste that much. Early game-theoretic critics, in the tradition of Maynard Smith, objected that cost alone cannot stabilize honesty — if a low-quality individual gained by paying the same cost, cheating would invade — and that a system in which everyone burns resources on display for no functional return is exactly what natural selection should prune. Alan Grafen's strategic models of the early 1990s are the pivot of the whole dispute. Zahavi's camp read them as vindication: here, formally, cost deterring low-quality mimics. The skeptics read the same models as refutation: in Grafen's setup, honesty is maintained by the equilibrium relationship between signal level and quality, and the cost is just the ordinary cost of doing the thing the signal accompanies — a cost any effective action carries — not a self-imposed burden, so nothing answering to the word "handicap" is required for reliability. Meanwhile a third literature points to reliable signals that appear cheap or even mutually beneficial — alarm calls within kin groups, "index" signals like the low croak that only a large body can physically produce — where honesty rests on structural necessity, relatedness, or repeated interaction rather than expense. The most serious synthesis attempt, in Searcy and Nowicki's 2005 monograph on the evolution of animal communication, proposed that signals need only be costly enough to be inefficient instruments of deception rather than handicaps at all; strikingly, both camps cite that work approvingly, which I take to be diagnostic in itself.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement is at least partly verbal, turning on at least three distinct things that get called "cost": the handicap cost paid over and above any functional benefit; the unavoidable cost that any effective act carries; and the fitness reduction attributable to the signal as such. And that it is partly two questions under one heading — what keeps a signal honest at equilibrium, versus why signals take the physical form they do — where cost may answer the second while being unnecessary for the first, so that each camp keeps winning its own question while believing it has won the argument.

Is that right? If the dispute is verbal, say which distinction would dissolve it, and whether that distinction carves nature at a joint or is an artifact of model-building style — whether "handicap," "index," and "efficiency cost" mark genuinely different mechanisms or points on a continuum that our taxonomy has arbitrarily chopped. If there is a substantive residue, identify it precisely: what empirical observation or formal result would settle it, and has anything close to it already been produced and absorbed? Finally, give me an honest answer about the future of the debate: given your diagnosis, is there anything — a class of field experiments, a theorem, a better taxonomy, a reframed question — that would count as progress, or is this a case where the stall is telling us the question, as posed, is not well posed?
