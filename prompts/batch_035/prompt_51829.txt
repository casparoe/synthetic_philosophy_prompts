I've been thinking about money pump arguments in decision theory, and a particular kind of response to them keeps nagging at me. The standard story is that if someone's preferences are circular — they'd pay something small to swap A for B, pay again to swap B for C, and then pay a third time to swap C back for A — a patient trader can strip them of arbitrary amounts of money without ever giving them anything they didn't, at each moment, want. The usual conclusion is that such preferences are irrational, and the obvious fix is to tidy them up so they're no longer circular. But a friend of mine proposed what looks like a completely different fix: he says he would simply refuse the last trade. Not because he won't want it — he's confident that when the trader comes around the third time he'll genuinely prefer paying a little to get A back — but because he decided, at the very start, that his earlier choice binds him. His preferences never change; he just lets a past decision outrank a present one. And it seems to work, since the pump needs all three trades to go through and he'll only ever accept two.

Since he put it that way, I've noticed him doing the same thing elsewhere. Before we go to an expensive restaurant he tells me what he's decided to order, and once there he sticks to it even while admitting the tasting menu tempts him more; his line is that the choice was made when his head was clear, and nothing that happens at the table gets to overrule it. What's odd is that there's no external device in any of this — he doesn't hand his wallet to the waiter or sign anything with the trader. It's purely that he treats his own earlier decision as having a kind of authority over his later one, even while his later preference is fully intact and points the other way.

So, does this stance have a name in the rational choice or decision theory literature? I want to be careful, because there are several nearby things it is not, and a bare label won't help unless the boundaries are drawn. It isn't simply changing your preferences over time — his preferences are stable, and their stability is exactly what makes the pump possible in the first place. It also isn't the classic binding of oneself by external means, the arrangement where you physically cannot defect; he can defect at any moment, and sometimes it visibly costs him effort not to. And it isn't obvious stubbornness either, because the whole point is that it protects him from a loss that sensible-seeming, one-step-at-a-time choosing would walk him straight into. If there's an established concept for letting your past self overrule your present self without force and without changed preferences, I'd like to know what it's called and how it's defined — and in particular whether the money pump literature treats it as a genuine escape route for circular preferences, or whether there's some reason it doesn't actually work (say, if the trader can re-order the offers or space them out over months). Relatedly, I'd like to know where its edges are: at what point does this become indistinguishable from just having a standing preference for sticking to your own plans, and is that a difference that matters for the argument?
