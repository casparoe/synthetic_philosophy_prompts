I'm a philosophy graduate student working on confirmation theory, and I've become preoccupied with the fact that the raven paradox seems to have produced a permanently stalled debate rather than a settled one. On one side sit those who regard it as dissolved: once we distinguish between a piece of evidence entailing part of a hypothesis and a piece of evidence raising the hypothesis's probability, the puzzle evaporates, and the intuition that white shoes are irrelevant to the hypothesis that all ravens are black is explained away as a psychological effect of the enormous disparity between the number of ravens and the number of non-ravens in the world. On the other side sit those who think this dissolution changes the subject: the interesting question was never whether observing a white shoe marginally raises the probability that all ravens are black, but whether such an observation is evidentially relevant to it at all in the way that observing a black raven is — and there are classic counterexamples in the literature showing that, given suitable background knowledge, observations of non-ravens can even lower the probability that all ravens are black, which suggests the Bayesian story is at best incomplete. My supervisor tells me the paradox is a solved problem and that I shouldn't waste a chapter on it; a visiting speaker last term said, to the contrary, that the standard dissolution is a case of answering an easier question than the one posed. A year into my reading, both camps are still in their trenches, and the exchange has become entirely predictable: each side restates its favoured notion of confirmation, notes that the other side is using the word differently, and stops.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science with serious expertise in confirmation theory, and I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Please begin by brainstorming, outside the main answer, a short list of candidate diagnoses, and then commit to the one you find best supported. My working hypothesis, offered purely as a hypothesis, is that the residual dispute is verbal in a specific and non-obvious way: the two camps are operating with different background conceptions of what a theory of confirmation is for — one that takes it to systematise judgements about how to update on data, and one that takes it to explicate a pre-theoretic notion of evidential relevance that no updating rule fully captures. But I can see two rival diagnoses that need taking seriously: that there is a hidden substantive question about whether confirmation ought to be relativised to background knowledge at all, and that the impasse reflects not an ill-posed question but a genuine hard problem about how projectibility enters into evidential support. Please assess the debate by reference to what each side actually claims, tell me which diagnosis you endorse and why, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question asked — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected. Please write the entire answer in British English, and be willing to conclude that my supervisor is right and I should move on, if that's where the diagnosis leads.
