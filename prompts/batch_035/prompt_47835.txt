Take the case of Han van Meegeren's forged Vermeers — in particular the Supper at Emmaus, which a leading Dutch connoisseur pronounced genuine in the late 1930s and which was exhibited to public acclaim as revealing an unexpected religious side of Vermeer's oeuvre — and analyze it through the philosophy of originals and forgeries. Two positions to bring to bear: the view that a forgery's unmasking cannot change the work's intrinsic visual properties, and so cannot change its aesthetic value, only our non-aesthetic, art-historical judgments about it; and the rival view that a painting's aesthetic properties are partly relational — that a picture is a communicative act by a particular artist, so a forgery literally presents different content from the original it pretends to be. Map the case onto both carefully: who was deceived, about what exactly, and whether the painting's admirers were admiring a Vermeer, a van Meegeren, or neither. Then say what the episode adds that armchair examples of perfect duplicates cannot: these forgeries were not visually identical to any real Vermeer, were praised partly because they seemed to enlarge our sense of what Vermeer was capable of, and were exposed only when the forger confessed to save himself from a worse charge. Does this asymmetry — that the works' reception depended on a false attribution that was bound to be revised — support the intrinsic-property view or undermine it? Where is each position silent about a case in which the fake is not a copy of anything?
