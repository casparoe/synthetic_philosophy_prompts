I sit on the moderation panel of a large online community, and last month we received two complaints about the same word in the same week: one concerned a member of the group the word targets, who had used it of herself in a post; the other concerned an ally who used it, in apparent solidarity, in a thread he did not start. The panel let the first stand and removed the second. When I was asked to write up the reasoning, I discovered that I have instincts about these words but no theory, and that the honest order of work is the specification before the rules. So that is what I am asking you for: the desiderata — what any adequate account of how slurs work has to deliver.

Let the list be motivated by cases rather than abstract properties. At minimum it should cover:

1. Inertness at the level of truth conditions. In most sentences one can substitute the slur's neutral counterpart without changing the sentence's truth value, yet something has plainly been added. A theory must say what was added and where it lives: in the word's content, in a presupposition, in something like conventional implicature, or in the pragmatics of the utterance.

2. Projection. Whatever is added survives under negation, in questions, and in the antecedents of conditionals, which is one reason it cannot be an ordinary conversational implicature. The theory must accommodate this without collapsing the added content into an at-issue description.

3. Derogation versus offense. A slur can demean a group with no member of it present to be offended, and an ordinary insult can offend its hearer without demeaning any group. Our policy cannot treat someone's being upset as the criterion, so the theory must mark this distinction for me.

4. The same word in different mouths. In-group uses, reclamation, quotation, teaching, the reporting of harassment: the word appears constant while its force varies with speaker, audience, and setting. Either one mechanism generates that variation, or the theory should say honestly that it cannot.

5. Use and mention. A theory should not predict that a sentence about the word derogates anyone, and my policy must permit metalinguistic uses. Yet it must also handle the bad-faith quoting of a lyric, which suggests the use–mention line is not purely grammatical.

6. Drift. Words become slurs and cease to be slurs, and this varies across communities. The theory should say what changes when a word becomes a slur: something in the word, or something in the practice around it.

For each requirement, give the case that makes it a requirement. Then mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion about the uniformity demanded by the projection data and the variation demanded by reclamation, and I would like your view on whether that tension is real. Tell me also which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. I do not need a scorecard of named accounts, and no reading list: the reasoning is what I want. Finally, the point I actually have to answer for the panel, and I would like it stated plainly: given all of the above, can a policy be written about the word, or only about the utterance and the user?
