i'm in a reading group that went through Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea last month, and one image stuck with everybody: explaining complex things is either building them up from below with cranes — gradual, mindless processes that accumulate design over time, natural selection being the paradigm case — or hoisting them from above with skyhooks, some intelligence reaching down and doing the designing work in one go. the way my group uses it now, anything that can't currently be given a crane-style story is a skyhook, which basically amounts to an appeal to magic. one friend has started applying this everywhere: he says free will is a skyhook that neuroscience will eventually dismantle, moral realism needs a skyhook because evolution can explain why we hold moral beliefs but not (in his telling) what makes any of them true, and consciousness research should give up on the subjective feel stuff and just look for the cranes or admit there are none.

someone else pushed back that this is exactly the greedy reductionism Dennett warned about — that my friend is using crane-talk to skip the actual explanatory work, declaring problems solved just by classifying them as crane-shaped, without ever building the crane. and honestly i can see both sides, so i want an audit of the crane/skyhook analogy itself as it's being used in our arguments: what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks, what the analogy quietly assumes about what counts as an explanation, and whether my friend's conclusions survive once all that is on the table.

format-wise: give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro and no conclusion. (1) the correspondence in prose — what plays the role of the crane, the skyhook, the ground it builds from, and the thing being lifted; (2) the main place the analogy breaks when applied to free will, moral realism, or consciousness; (3) what it smuggles in about explanation being all-or-nothing; (4) your verdict on whether my friends conclusions survive; (5) whether greedy reductionism is actually the right name for his mistake or whether its a different one dressed in the same clothes. keep it in the weeds — no textbook intro to Dennett, we've all read the book. no reading lists either, just the reasoning.
