When a public figure is caught in disqualifying conduct — a recording of abusive behavior surfaces, an old post resurfaces, an allegation goes headline — a familiar sequence unfolds: the platform suspends the account or quietly drops the person from its editorial promotion, the employer fires them or places them on indefinite leave, venues cancel appearances, collaborators issue disavowals, and other institutions are then expected to follow (bookings withdrawn, invitations revoked), with pressure applied to any that don't. Meanwhile the condemning institutions often keep monetizing what they condemn: the streaming service removes the artist from its curated playlists but continues to sell the catalogue, the network cancels the show but leaves it on-demand, the publisher drops the author but keeps the backlist in print. And there is no defined term: the same people who insist this is merely social consequence also resist restoration after years have passed and the person has, on any account, changed.

What theory of sanction does this practice presuppose, if any? Defenders of the practice say it is not punishment at all — just freedom of association, market feedback, expressive disassociation. Can a purely associational or expressive account actually explain the whole pattern, or only parts of it? In particular, explain whether that account covers (a) the expectation that third parties disassociate too, (b) the differential treatment of conduct that was never charged or was acquitted, (c) the absence of any proportionality constraint, and (d) the resistance to restoration. If the associational story fails for some of these, reconstruct what the practice does presuppose — some hybrid of condemnation, exclusion, and prevention, perhaps, or something like a reputational tariff with no concept of a served sentence. Note which features criminal punishment insists on and this practice lacks: a specified offense, an adjudication, a proportionality ceiling, an endpoint. Is that absence itself a presupposition (that this is not the sort of sanction that needs them), or mere drift? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival accounts would license it, and tell me what the practice would have to look like if we took the "consequences, not punishment" line seriously — including whether, on that view, refusal to ever restore the person becomes incoherent.
