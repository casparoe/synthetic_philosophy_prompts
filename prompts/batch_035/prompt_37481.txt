I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though over the past year I've been reading around the topic of personal identity — thought experiments like teletransporters and brain transplants, and the Buddhist idea that there is no self. One thing I've noticed in online discussions is that people's reactions to concrete cases don't match the labels they'd claim for themselves: someone who says they're "basically their mind" will refuse to step into a machine that scans and destroys their body, and someone who talks about the self as an illusion will still plan for "their" old age. So, as a side project, I'm building an interactive web quiz tentatively titled "Which View of the Self Do You Actually Hold?", and I'd like your help designing it.

The quiz's purpose is diagnostic: it should sort users by what they say about cases, not by which school they name they like. No question may simply ask the user to self-report a theory (e.g., "do you believe you are your psychological patterns?"); every question must present a scenario and ask what the user would do or what they'd say has happened.

Please produce the following:

1. Eight to ten questions. Each presents a concrete scenario with two or three answer options. The scenarios must include at least these: a teletransporter that records, destroys, and reconstructs you elsewhere; gradual replacement of neurons with functionally identical synthetic components; fission, where two later people are each psychologically continuous with the one earlier person; total permanent amnesia in an otherwise intact body; and a rebirth-type case in which memories are lost but dispositions, habits, and moral tendencies carry over — this one is important for testing whether users track continuity through something other than memory. At least one question should be about prudential concern rather than survival (e.g., whether to dread a future pain), since I suspect users' answers come apart there, and at least one should be about retrospective concern (e.g., feeling guilt for a past act) for the same reason.

2. Format each question as a JSON object with a unique id, the scenario text, and an options array; each option should carry a short label and a scoring vector assigning weights to the positions below, which I'll sum in JavaScript on the results page. Keep the question text free of unexplained philosophical jargon; technical terms may appear only on the results page, each defined in one sentence.

3. A scoring key whose leaves include at least: psychological-continuity views with and without a no-branching (only one continuer counts) clause; the closest-continuer view; bodily or organism views; narrative identity views; and the no-self or deflationary option, where the question "will I survive?" is treated as confused because there is no further fact of the matter — I want this leaf to cover both its Buddhist and its contemporary deflationary forms.

4. An honest note on separability: flag any pair of positions your questions cannot cleanly distinguish (for instance, if no case you can write separates narrative views from psychological continuity minus memory, say so rather than papering over it), and for each flagged pair suggest, in one sentence, the kind of case that would separate them if it exists.

5. A note on any region of the resulting logical space that no leaf occupies, with a brief diagnosis of why: is the combination incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

6. A tie-handling rule: when two positions receive equal top scores, the results page should present both plus a single follow-up question that separates them, phrased so the user can answer it without knowing the labels. Write one sample follow-up of this kind.

Finally, tell me which question you would cut first if I trim the quiz to six questions, and why that one carries the least diagnostic weight.
