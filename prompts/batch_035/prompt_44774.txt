I serve on the ethics board of a regional health service, and we are deadlocked over a proposal that is a fresh coat of paint on what Derek Parfit called the nonidentity problem, in a form I have not seen handled cleanly anywhere. Our fertility program has a cohort of cryopreserved embryos and a budget that covers either (a) transfer cycles beginning this year, or (b) transfers beginning in three years, after a planned laboratory upgrade that would substantially reduce the risk of a manageable but lifelong developmental impairment in the resulting children. Children born under option (a) would, on the best available modeling, have lives that are clearly worth living, but with meaningfully lower expected welfare than children born under option (b). And here is the part that matters: the three-year delay would change which embryos are transferred when, and to whom, so that the two policies would result in numerically different children. There is no option that both waits and produces the same children.

Settle on this proposition, P: choosing option (a) wrongs the children who are born as a result of that choice. Not that it is impersonally worse, not that it produces less total wellbeing — that it wrongs those particular children, in the way we ordinarily say an action wrongs a person when it treats her as she is entitled not to be treated.

Give me your credence in P, and give me the decomposition. I want at least these terms, each with a number and one line of justification, plus any others you think the arithmetic actually needs:

1. That the identity-affecting premise holds in this case — that the children born under (a) and under (b) would genuinely be numerically different people rather than the same people with different properties. Take seriously what individuates a person here: genetic origin, gestational origin, the causal history of the gametes, or something else, and note that cryopreservation makes the timing question stranger than the ordinary conception-delay case.
2. That a life can be worth living overall for the person living it while that same person is wronged by being brought into existence under those conditions — that worth living and wronged in creation are compatible.
3. That person-affecting morality is correct — that wronging a person requires making her worse off than she otherwise would have been — and that the comparison with never existing at all is not a coherent baseline that could ground the wrong.
4. Conditional on 3 being false, that an impersonal or group-directed principle can still make sense of the claim that these particular children are wronged, rather than merely that the choice is worse in some agent-neutral sense.
5. That the harmless-wronging strategies — a right to a decent start in life, or a contractualist claim that the resulting children could reasonably reject the choice — can do the work without smuggling the comparative baseline back in.

Combine them and give me the final number, and tell me the rule you used to combine them; the terms interact, and independence is itself an assumption worth flagging rather than silently making. Then tell me which single sub-credence the final number is most sensitive to, and which argument — not evidence, since none is coming — would move that sub-credence the most.

For calibration: two members of my panel are at roughly 0.7 for P and two at roughly 0.2. My own suspicion is that the 0.7 side is not weighing how strange it is to say to an actual person, living a life she affirms, that she has a grievance about having been created at all. Tell me which pair you think disagrees about which sub-question — my guess is that the real disagreement does not live in the ethics of welfare-maximization at all, but I would like to know whether you think it lives somewhere I have not listed.
