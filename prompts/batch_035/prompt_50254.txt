I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and in three weeks I'm giving my first proper departmental seminar — not a job talk, but the audience will include people whose position I'm directly attacking, so it feels like one. My thesis: the two standard routes to a state's right to exclude would-be immigrants — the freedom-of-association analogy (the state is somehow like a club or a marriage and can pick its members) and the collective-self-determination route (a people needs control over membership in order to govern itself) — both fail, and interestingly for the same reason: they locate the right in the wrong place. If there's a right to exclude, it's derivative of the right to jurisdiction over territory, and jurisdictional rights are conditional on the state being legitimate towards foreigners, not just towards its own citizens. Which means exclusion is never sovereign: a legitimate state may exclude to keep its institutions functioning, but not to preserve its culture, its welfare standard, or its wage level. So I end up somewhere inbetween the open-borders people and the nationalists, and both sides will hate it, which is fine, but I'd like to survive the Q&A.

The audience: a department of about twenty. The ones I'm most worried about: my supervisor's supervisor, who is basically a self-determination theorist in the David Miller mould and thinks the association analogy is a sideshow anyway, so my first move ("here are the two routes, both fail") may just bounce off him; a younger colleague who works on brain drain and the migration-and-development side of things and thinks any admission-rights framework is too state-centric — she'll ask whether jurisdiction-talk can even handle cases where the "state" in question is de facto governed by someone else or barely exists; a Kantian cosmopolitan who will want to know why legitimacy-towards-foreigners doesn't just collapse into some global egalitarian principle and give everyone a claim to enter; and a law-and-economics person who will want to know what my view implies about actual admission systems (points systems, refugee quotas, guest-worker schemes) and will be visibly annoyed if the answer is "philosophy has nothing to say here". Also our department administrator attends every talk and always asks something about the moral situation of the individual border guard just following the exclusion policy — she's asked a version of it at every seminar for years and I refuse to be caught out by it again.

Write my prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from these specific people, in roughly the order I expect to get them, phrased the way each of them would phrase it (you know the types), each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks — my honest worry is I don't have a good answer to whether my "protect the institutions" proviso lets a state exclude during a pandemic or comparable emergency, and someone will spot that the proviso might expand exactly when nationalists want it to. And this part really matters to me: for each reply, flag clearly where I'm leaning on something that's genuinely contested among political philosophers versus where I'm standing on something like settled ground — I do not want to get caught presenting a contested premise as common sense in front of people who've published on exactly that.
