I'm writing a paper for a decision theory seminar where the running theme this term is ergodicity and the Kelly criterion, and instead of another essay about stock markets I got permission to do a film, so I picked Rounders (the 1998 poker one with Matt Damon). My pitch is that the movie is basically a two-hour dramatization of the fight between arithmetic and geometric rationality, i.e. maximizing expected wealth versus maximizing the time-average growth rate of your bankroll so you never flirt with ruin. You've got Joey Knish, the eternal grinder who never risks going broke and lives comfortably off a modest edge; Worm, who reloads his entire bankroll every few months and treats survival as optional; and Mike in the middle, who in the opening scene sits down with Teddy KGB with everything on the line and busts, then spends the rest of the movie half-rebuilding a bankroll that his own temperament keeps wanting to shove all-in.

So read the film through Kelly for me: who is actually rational in this movie, on the log-wealth/time-average picture rather than the expected-value picture? In particular I want to know whether Mike's final heads-up rematch against Teddy KGB -- where he sits with basically his last money, needs to double through or he's finished, and does it partly by cracking Teddy's oreo tell -- is a vindication of Kelly thinking or a straight-up violation of it, since he's putting his last chips in on what is by his own admission close to a coin flip for his whole life. And does the film itself take a side? It clearly romanticizes Mike's degenerate genius and is kind of condescending about Knish's grinding, which is the opposite of the aesthetic a Kelly partisan would predict, so tell me honestly where the movie resists the reading rather than illustrating it instead of just forcing everything to fit.

One more thing I'd love your take on: is Worm best understood as straightforwardly irrational, or as someone optimizing something else entirely -- arithmetic expected value with no ruin term, or a utility function with a thrill-of-destruction term in it? And is there anything the film gets about the actual economics of poker wrong that I should flag in the paper before my professor (who is a bit of an Ole Peters stan) catches it? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, and please no preamble or restating my question.
