Write an advice column, about seven hundred words, for an interwar Viennese newspaper, in which a devoted second-generation psychoanalyst answers three letters from religious readers. His replies must be recognizably competent psychoanalysis rather than caricature: he should locate the wish behind each correspondent's belief (usually a father of some kind), he should at one point patiently correct a reader who has called religion a delusion by explaining the technical difference — a delusion contradicts reality, whereas an illusion is not an error but a belief held because it fulfills a wish — and he should at another point retell, with complete seriousness, the story of the sons who killed and ate the primal father, as an account of what the correspondent's church is really doing on Sunday. The comedy should come from the analyst's inability to keep his own discipline on the non-symptom side of the diagnosis: his prescribed cure has ritual sessions, confessional speech, a father figure who is conveniently never the analysand's actual father, and an article of faith that reason and education will one day dissolve the religious need — and the second letter should press him on precisely this symmetry, so that his reply visibly strains to meet it. The third letter should be from someone reporting a boundless, oceanic feeling of oneness with the universe, the very feeling Freud admitted a correspondent had described to him and that he could not locate in himself, and the analyst's answer is where his confidence quietly cracks — he should find something in himself he can only gesture at. Keep the piece deadpan and fair: a reader who knows the Freud corpus should wince in recognition rather than object to distortion, and a reader who doesn't should still follow it as an advice column whose columnist is slowly losing the argument with his mail. Do not name Freud, any of his works, or any living person. Write it as continuous prose only — no headings, no lists, no bold or tables — just the letters and replies running one into the next, the way the column would appear on the page.
