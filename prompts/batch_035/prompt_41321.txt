So I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book set in 1816 — the "year without a summer" after Tambora went off. Failed harvests, weird red-tinted snow, Mary Shelley stuck inside writing Frankenstein, all of that. Last month I was on a panel about historical fiction at a festival, and a historian there kept saying things like "it wasn't the generals, it was the germs and the volcanoes that made the modern world," and everyone nodded along like it was terribly deep. Now my editor has latched onto it: she wants me to basically demote my human characters to weather instruments (her phrase, not mine) and let "the eruption itself be the protagonist." Meanwhile one of my beta readers says the current draft reads like the characters are puppets getting shoved around by the sky, which stung, because that was sort of the point of the book — but now I honestly can't tell weather that's a bug or the whole thesis.

The thing is, I'm not sure anyone on that panel knew what the historian's line was actually claiming, my editor least of all. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "germs and volcanoes, not generals, make history" could be standing for? Like, ranging from the totally trivial (Tambora caused the 1816 harvest failures in New England — sure, fine, obviously) up through stronger ones (environmental factors do more explanatory work than human decisions do), to the radical end where human choices are basically decoration, and maybe even a reading where it's only true in some loose literary sense about who gets to be the "agent" in a narrative at all. For each reading I want to know whether it's plausible, whether it's just a platitude dressed up, and — this is the part I actually need — whether it supports my editor's demand that the humans in my novel shouldn't get to make choices that matter to the outcome. Give me a menu I can bring to our next call, not a lecture. And if one of the readings somehow saves my book, I will take it.
