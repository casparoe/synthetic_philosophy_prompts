At a wedding on Saturday I got drawn into a two-hour argument with my oldest friend, Miriam, about what it takes for someone to be me, and I've been quietly working through it ever since. She turned out to hold a cluster of views on origins that were firmer and more worked-out than I'd expected — apparently she argued her then-supervisor about it during her master's, and someone at the wedding kept invoking Kripke's footnotes, which she waved off as "dressing." Her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them: a person conceived from an entirely different sperm and egg, however similar to me in temperament and appearance, would simply not be me — that case was, in her words, easy. But a child gestated by a surrogate abroad, raised under a different name, with a different personality, would still have been me, provided it grew from the very embryo that in fact became me (she was unmoved when I said it would be a different sort of life). She also insisted that if that embryo had split in two, as embryos occasionally do, and both halves had been implanted and born, neither twin would be me — not "one of them is me and I can't say which," but flatly neither; on that scenario, she said, I just never arrive. She was equally firm that an atom-for-atom duplicate of me, memories and all, assembled tomorrow by some lab, would not be me, and, when I pressed, that if my embryo had been frozen for fifty years and implanted in 2075, the resulting person would be me without qualification.

The reason the argument has stayed with me is that Miriam's mother recently discovered, through one of those consumer DNA tests, that she is a chimera — she absorbed a fraternal twin very early in gestation and carries two genomes in one body. So naturally the argument ended on a case neither of us could settle: suppose that, a few days after my conception, my embryo had fused with a second embryo conceived from a different sperm and egg, and the single person that resulted — a chimera, some of whose cells descend from my embryo and some from the other — were sitting here now, writing this. Would Miriam say that person is me? We never got to her answer, because the speeches started and then she had a train to catch.

I'd like you to predict her verdict. More than the answer itself, I want the reasoning worked out: what rule (or family of rules) best fits her actual track record — is it that I must have originated from the very sperm and egg that produced me, or from the very zygote, or something about a continuous, non-branching developmental line from fertilisation to now — and which of these fits all five of her earlier verdicts equally well, so that her past answers don't yet decide the chimera case? Then say what each surviving rule predicts about the chimera, which prediction you find most defensible and why, and — this is the part I actually need — which further case would best force her to choose between the rules: I'm thinking of things like a clone grown from one of my skin cells, or an embryo split where one twin dies at the four-cell stage, or a fusion where my embryo contributes only a tenth of the eventual cells, but I'm happy to be told my intuitions about which of these are diagnostic are themselves muddled. Please treat her verdicts as fixed points to be made sense of, not as positions to be argued out of; I'm trying to work out what my friend believes, not what's true.
