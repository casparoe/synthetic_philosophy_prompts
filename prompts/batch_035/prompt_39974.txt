I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a second-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I want a one-page handout that sorts my students out of a specific muddle. They've just worked through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the one that runs: you're morally responsible for what you do only if you're responsible for how you are in the relevant respects; you're responsible for how you are only if you made yourself that way; but any attempt to make yourself presupposes an earlier self you didn't make, so nothing can be self-made in the way required, and responsibility never gets off the ground. Half the class comes away thinking this is just another argument against determinism. It isn't, and the interesting lesson is that the argument's premises divide people along lines that cut across the usual free-will/determinism battle. So: please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of free will, think through the logical space carefully before writing anything down, and produce a decision tree.

The tree should start from a student who accepts only this: she sometimes rightly feels gratitude toward a friend who went out of her way for her, and she isn't sure whether that reaction commits her to anything metaphysical. Every internal node must be a question she can answer from a concrete case or her own reactions, never from knowing the literature — no "are you a semicompatibilist?" nodes. The tree must at minimum force answers on these questions: whether responsibility for a particular act requires responsibility for the character and motives it flowed from (test it against a kleptomaniac, a person whose compassion is pure temperamental luck, and someone who acts from values absorbed in a cult she joined as an adult); whether responsibility for how you are can be supplied by a luck-informed but reasons-responsive history, or only by self-creation; whether a bounded regress is good enough — the person who deliberately reshaped her character at twenty, out of materials she never chose — or whether only ultimate self-creation would do; and what follows from a Frankfurt-style case where someone could not have done otherwise but acts from her own settled values. The leaves must include at least: the impossibilist who follows the argument all the way; the hard incompatibilist who accepts the conclusion about desert-based responsibility but wants to keep something in the neighborhood; the semicompatibilist who lets responsibility for action come apart from alternative possibilities; the source incompatibilist who agrees with the argument that responsibility requires a kind of self-creation the world doesn't supply, but thinks it can be supplied anyway; and a revisionist who keeps the practices while conceding the metaphysics. One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment.

After the tree, two diagnostic sections. First, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate — any pair where every question answerable without the labels gets answered the same way on both branches; I suspect the hard incompatibilist and the revisionist will merge on my node set, but see whether some case can split them (whether to feel gratitude toward someone who hasn't acted yet, or how we should regard the long dead, might do work here). Second, note any combination of node answers that comes out unoccupied, and say whether you think nobody holds that view because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it hasn't been explored. If you find yourself adding or deleting a node to make the tree come out cleaner, do so, but tell me exactly what you changed and why — the tree is the handout, but the diagnosis of where it fails is what I'll actually teach.
