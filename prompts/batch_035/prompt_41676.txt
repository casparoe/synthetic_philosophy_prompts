At our recent reading group on Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay on the meaning of life, two members got into an extended dispute about what the theory actually commits us to, and I would like a careful adjudication of it.

For context, the view under discussion was Schlick's so-called play theory of life's meaning. On his account, the meaning of life is found not in work but in play, where play is activity that is valued as an end in itself, while work is activity valued merely as a means to some further end. Schlick connects the meaningfulness of an activity to the relation between its means and its ends: meaning is most intense when the means-end gap nearly vanishes, so that what one does and what one does it for coincide. He also associates true play with the enthusiasm of youth, the stage of life most given to activities pursued for their own sake.

The dispute ran as follows.

(1) Marta opened by claiming the theory has an unpalatable implication that amounts to a reductio. If meaning lives in play, and play is characterized by this self-contained absorption, then the paradigmatically meaningful lives turn out to be those of children at play or leisured idlers, while lives of difficult striving — the scientist working through the night, the parent raising children through exhaustion — count as meaningless or nearly so, since they are saturated with effort and deferred ends. A theory that demotes such lives while promoting the sandbox, she said, has gotten something badly wrong.

(2) Lukas replied that this misreads the theory's central contrast. Schlick's distinction is not between the easy and the hard, or between the leisurely and the strenuous, but between activities valued as ends and activities endured merely as means. The exhausted scientist who is gripped by her questions is playing in Schlick's sense, however gruelling the work, because she wants the activity itself; the parent may be playing too. Effort is compatible with meaning; being a mere instrument of some further end is what hollows an activity out.

(3) Marta pressed a dilemma. If any activity at all can count as play whenever the agent happens to value it as an end, the theory loses all explanatory power — it just relabels whatever a person loves as meaningful and predicts nothing, since it can no longer classify any life as less meaningful than another on independent grounds. But if Schlick wants the theory to do real classificatory work, he must tie play to something substantive like spontaneity, lightness, or freedom from burden — and then her original objection returns, since strenuous, burden-laden lives get demoted after all. Either the theory is empty or it is false.

(4) Lukas responded that Marta is demanding the wrong kind of thing from a philosophical diagnosis. The theory is not meant to forecast which lives will feel meaningful; it is meant to explain a familiar structural failure — the life of endless deferral, in which every means is endured only for the sake of a further means, and the end never arrives. That explanation survives her dilemma: it does not need an independent criterion for classifying lives, because it works from the agent's own orientation toward the activity. And Schlick's remarks about youth should be read the same way — as an empirical observation about the stage of life at which absorption in ends tends to be most common, not as a definition equating meaning with childlike ease.

(5) Marta closed with a challenge about what is left of the famous thesis. If Schlick is only offering a redescription — call an activity play when it is loved for its own sake, and meaning lives in play — then the celebrated claim that meaning is found only in play is either trivially true by definition or, if intended as something more substantive, unsupported. Lukas's charity had rescued the theory from refutation, he had to admit, only at the price of making it nearly vacuous. Lukas replied that a diagnosis of why deferral lives feel hollow is not nothing, and that a theory can be worth holding even if its central claim is closer to a constitutive stipulation than an empirical generalization. Neither would concede, and the group dissolved without a verdict.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is right, mistaken, or partly right, and why. Then address the two questions that hang over the whole dispute: whether Lukas's reading of the play/work contrast genuinely escapes Marta's dilemma, and whether the thesis that meaning is found only in play survives as anything more than a definition. Finally, state who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them missed.
