I am a psychometrician by training and trade: my working vocabulary is item response theory, Rasch models, test equating and linking, measurement invariance and differential item functioning, and the representation theorems of conjoint measurement, which I can follow though I do not prove them for a living. Restate the philosophical debate over interpersonal utility comparisons in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report.

Concretely, I would like you to attempt something like the following mapping, improving on it where my analogies are loose or misleading: treat the ordinalist denial of interpersonal comparability as the claim that no empirical structure is available whose axioms, once tested, would license an interval-scale representation with a common unit across persons — the trouble being that utility has no concatenation operation and no external calibration standard; treat each person's observed choices or preference reports as item responses and the person's utility function as a person-specific scale; treat the extended-preference or impartial-observer approach as a proposed equating design, in which hypothetical exchanges of places function as common items administered to a single judge, allowing the separate person-scales to be linked; and treat the objection that this judge's own preferences contaminate the linking as a form of differential item functioning — the anchor items behave differently depending on who is being positioned on them. Where that mapping breaks, say so rather than propping it up.

Then the residue report, which is what I most want. Separate the debate into three piles: what translates cleanly into measurement-theoretic terms, what translates only with distortion or by smuggling in assumptions the translation should not carry, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For the third pile, I specifically want your view on the normative residue — the question of whether social choice ought to depend on comparisons that perhaps cannot be made — and on whether it is genuinely inexpressible in measurement terms or just badly posed. Finally, step back and tell me which side of the failed translation is at fault: is my vocabulary missing something the philosophers need, or does the residual dispute look like an artifact of treating utility as a quantity that merely awaits a measurement theory, when the underlying objects — preferences revealed in hypothetical choices, or reported feelings with no performance behind them — are not the kind of thing my field ever has to measure? I want an honest answer, not reassurance that the two literatures are secretly about the same problem.
