I've been in the collapse discourse forever -- was around for the ACX book review contest entry on Tainter's Collapse of Complex Societies, read Cline's 1177 BC when it made the blog rounds, did the whole Easter Island spiral where I went from Diamond's ecocide story to the Hunt & Lipo revision (the rats ate the palms! it was slave raids and disease that actually finished off the people, not their own statue obsession) and then back partway when I saw pushback on the revision. And somewhere in there I realized I genuinely don't know what the word "collapse" is supposed to pick out anymore. The Maya "collapse" is the one that really gets me: the southern lowlands cities get abandoned, monumental construction stops, long-count dates stop being carved, and yet there are millions of Maya today speaking Maya languages, and the northern cities like Chichen Itza kept going strong through the whole thing. Collapse of a state system, sure, but nobody says France "collapsed" when Rome's administration left Gaul, they call that a transformation. Or the Bronze Age: palatial bureaucracy dies, Linear B is forgotten, trade networks sever -- but the villages, the people, the languages mostly carry on and eventually you get new stuff (alphabets, iron). My archaeology-buff friend says the whole concept is a holdover from a 19th century habit of equating "civilization" with courts and monuments, so that when kings stop commissioning steles, historians panic, while the peasants involved mostly experienced a reorganization. My x-risk-adjacent friends, meanwhile, use "civilizations are fragile" as a load-bearing premise, like the whole Bronze Age episode proves societies sit near a tipping point.

So: write me an essay on whether "collapse" is a real, unified phenomenon -- something societies do or suffer -- or whether it's a projection, a value judgment by outside observers (and by elites, and by moderns with a boner for monumental architecture) onto what are really just transformations, simplifications, or relocations. Weigh the Tainter-style view where collapse is an economic process with a real mechanism (diminishing returns on complexity, and the budget math finally failing) against the "it's a category error / it's in the eye of the beholder" view, using the Bronze Age, the Maya, and Easter Island as your test cases. Please actually engage with the awkward fact that these three cases look pretty different from each other -- one maybe a systems accident (sea peoples or not, drought or not, general systems collapse or not), one regional and partial, one small and maybe never even happened the way the word implies. And don't end on some "it depends on your definition" mush -- I get that definitions are choices, but I want you to pick one, defend it as the one doing real work, and tell me straight whether, on that definition, anything has ever actually collapsed. If the honest answer is that collapse-talk smuggles in a moralized story about "failure" that dissolves under scrutiny, say that. Bonus points if you can say whether any of this matters for the people who lean on historical collapse as evidence that modern global civilization is fragile, or whether that inference was always sloppy for other reasons. (I ask because I used to make that inference myself, casually, in comment sections, and I'm starting to suspect I never earned it.)
