My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the argument from divine hiddenness, and the whole dispute kept collapsing onto one analogy, which we ended up using against each other: God's relationship to us is like a parent's relationship to a child. She pressed it for the skeptic: a loving parent would never let a child who is capable of relationship doubt, even briefly and through no fault of her own, that the parent exists and loves her; so if God is loving, nonresistant nonbelief should not occur, and its occurrence is evidence against a loving God. I pushed back that the analogy cuts the other way: good parents step back as children mature, leave room for doubt and autonomy rather than hovering, and sometimes withhold themselves so the child can grow — so hiddenness may be exactly what love requires. Neither of us could say where the analogy actually stops working. Please audit it for me. Lay out a correspondence table: what plays the role of the child, the parent, the parent's finitude, the parent's physical unavoidability, siblings, the developmental trajectory, the possibility of deception or weakness on the parent's side. Then identify where each of our two uses breaks down — which disanalogies matter for the skeptical conclusion, which for the pedagogy reply, and which cut no weight either way. Say explicitly which features of ordinary parenthood each side quietly imports that the God–creature relation arguably lacks, and whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Since we got nowhere by trading intuitions, I'd also like you to say which of your judgments the whole audit most depends on, and what consideration would most plausibly move you to the opposite verdict.
