I maintain some open-source projects, and I've watched (and helped carry out) the standard enforcement ritual often enough that I've started to wonder what its design actually commits us to: someone behaves badly, a callout thread spreads, they're banned from the community, and the ban then follows them around — other projects pre-emptively remove them, conference invitations dry up, conduct from ten years ago gets treated exactly like conduct from last week, and colleagues who keep working with the banned person come under pressure too. There's no proportional-sentencing guidance, no appeal, no defined end state, and no mechanism by which the sanction is ever over; yet the same communities talk a lot about growth, rehabilitation, and second chances. As an engineer I keep noticing this has the structure of a system nobody designed, so I want the reverse-engineering done properly: what theory of punishment and moral desert does the practice presuppose? Is the sanction retribution for a specific act, deterrence, expression of community values, or something else like brand self-protection? And which parts of the practice — permanence, no appeal, unlimited retroactivity, guilt by association — does that implicit theory strictly require, versus which parts are underdetermined, compatible with several different theories of desert? I'd like you to consider seriously the possibility that the practice is internally inconsistent rather than an expression of any coherent theory, and to say what a version of it would have to change if we took the rehabilitation talk seriously. One request on how to present it: clearly separate what is genuinely settled in the philosophy of punishment from what is contested between camps — for instance, whether informal social sanction even counts as punishment, or whether desert attaches to persons rather than acts — and don't pass off one camp's view as the consensus.
