I run a team of content reviewers at a mid-sized social platform, and a growing share of our queue is reports filed against conspiracy content. Our current internal guidance is a list of banned topics, which fails constantly: it catches old, well-documented claims as "conspiracies" and misses novel ones, and it gives us nothing to say on appeal except that the topic is on the list. I want to replace it with something principled — a decision procedure grounded in what philosophers of science and epistemologists have actually worked out about when conspiracy claims are warranted and when they are not — but usable by reviewers who have two minutes per item, no subject-matter expertise, and whose removal decisions can be appealed and end up in my lap.

The philosophical ideas I'd want the procedure to operationalize, roughly: the thought that a conspiracy claim is epistemically suspect not because conspiracies never happen (they obviously do — companies bury trial data, governments run covert programs) but because of structural features like the mismatch between the size of the posited conspiracy and what it explains, the implausibility of large secrets holding over time, the tendency of the claim to absorb counterevidence by positing further conspirators, and the shift from specific falsifiable allegations to a general posture of distrust. But I am not the expert; use whatever is actually load-bearing in the literature.

Please write the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a remove, label, or leave-alone, and where it must stop and hand off to a human because the question is one of values or context rather than epistemic structure. It should distinguish, explicitly, between the content of a claim (which can be warranted) and the reasoning pattern around it (which can be conspiracist even when the claim happens to be true), since our policy treats those differently. It should also be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about the characteristic ways it will misfire.

Then run it on three cases from our actual queue, lightly disguised:

First, a post alleging that a pharmaceutical company suppressed adverse-event data on a specific drug, citing the company's prior regulatory fines, with some figures that look plausibly sourced and some that look invented. This is the hard one: the pattern matches both the worst conspiracist material we see and the documented history of this industry, and I suspect the procedure will have to output something other than remove, which I'll need to defend to my management.

Second, a video claiming that a recent building collapse in a nearby country was orchestrated rather than accidental, produced within hours of the event, which argues from the fact that officials initially said little and asks why they would withhold information if they had nothing to hide.

Third, a post asserting that a well-known historical assassination involved a second shooter, argued carefully, citing decades-old witness statements, and conceding points that count against it.

For each, show the procedure's path step by step, and flag where two reviewers following it in good faith could still diverge. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — the kinds of reports it should not be run on at all, and what I should say in an appeal when the honest answer is that our rules are about a platform's values and not about truth.
