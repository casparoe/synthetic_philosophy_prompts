I've never studied economics or philosophy formally — my working life was spent in corporate retail logistics, which is the only credential I'd claim for what follows — but for the past couple of years I've been reading my way, on my own, through the old socialist calculation debate. I think I've got the standard moves straight: Mises arguing that without genuine market prices for capital goods, a planner cannot do the arithmetic of what to produce and how; Hayek widening the point from calculation to knowledge, insisting that the relevant knowledge never exists in one place but only as, to use his words, "dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess"; Lange and the market socialists replying that a central board can get by with trial and error, adjusting administered prices by watching inventories rise and fall until the market's answer is simulated. I understand this is the textbook version and that historians would quarrel with details. That's fine. What I want is help with a case that has been nagging at me precisely because the textbook doesn't seem to cover it.

The case is Sears under Eddie Lampert. The salient facts, as I understand them from the business press: Lampert was a hedge fund manager who took control after the 2005 Kmart merger and became chief executive in 2013. He restructured the company into roughly thirty separate business units — separate brands and functions, each judged strictly on its own reported profits — which were made to buy and sell services from one another through competitive bidding, competing for capital at internal meetings rather than receiving it from a coordinated plan. Divisions billed one another for using shared resources, and executives were rewarded for their unit's individual numbers regardless of what those numbers did to the company as a whole. The company declined through this whole period and filed for bankruptcy in October 2018. It is generally agreed that the structure failed to slow, let alone reverse, the decline.

Here is what I want, and I want it done carefully rather than as a rhetorical gotcha against either side.

First, the mapping. Analyze the Lampert experiment as an instance of the calculation debate, and say explicitly which theory best describes what he was trying to build. It looks to me like Lange's market socialism turned inside out: a headquarters that owns all the capital, watching internal "prices" emerge from bidding among units, allocating funds by watching unit profit reports the way Lange's board was supposed to watch inventories. If that mapping is right, then Sears is one of the few real tests we have of the Langean feedback mechanism — but tested inside a capitalist economy, which raises the second question.

Second, where the mapping breaks. Mises's argument was that socialism lacks genuine prices for the means of production, formed by real exchanges among real owners. Sears, sitting inside the United States economy, had external market prices available as benchmarks for nearly everything it bought and sold; its problem was not the absence of prices in the surrounding sea. So if the internal market failed anyway, the failure can't be put down to what Mises said was missing for the socialists. Does that mean the episode vindicates a broader Hayekian point — that any headquarters, socialist or corporate, cannot aggregate dispersed practical knowledge — or does it show the knowledge argument was never the operative one here, and that what killed Sears was plain incentive design: managers graded on divisional profit had every reason to wage war on their neighbors and no reason to cooperate across the very boundaries the internal prices were supposed to coordinate? And a sharper version of the same question: when one unit sells to another, the internal "price" is administered or negotiated, not discovered by competition among outsiders — is that a miniature of the Lange trial-and-error process, and if so, why did the feedback loop fail where Lange said it should work?

Third, run it the other way. What does this episode do to the theory? If a firm — an island of economic planning that Hayek himself acknowledged, and that capitalist economies spawn voluntarily — can be made to fail by having a market bolted onto it, that seems to complicate the simple story that market prices coordinate knowledge and central direction suppresses it. Maybe the moral is that what Sears lacked was neither prices nor a plan, but something Hayek said less about: shared purposes, or trust, or the ability to make cooperative investments whose benefits cross unit lines. Or maybe the moral is that the knowledge problem and the incentive problem are one problem, and the debate never really separated them.

Please be disciplined about where each theory is genuinely silent rather than forcing it to speak — I'd rather hear that the debate simply doesn't address the corporate case than get a stretched application — and be candid if you think I've misdescribed the facts or misread the debate, since I'm working well outside my depth and know it.
