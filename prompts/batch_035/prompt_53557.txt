I run a small podcast about everyday ethics — the ethics of ordinary stuff, like queueing and splitting the bill, not trolleys and transplant cases. Next week's guest is a philosopher who's just written a book defending the view that everyday conventions — queueing, tipping, not jaywalking — are genuine moral obligations and not just etiquette. Her basic line is the one you can trace back to David Lewis's work on convention and Edna Ullmann-Margalit's work on the emergence of norms: a convention solves a coordination problem, and once it's settled, each person's conformity is what makes everyone else's conformity worthwhile, so the person who jumps the queue is free-riding on everyone else's patience. She thinks that free-riding is a moral fault rather than a social gaffe, though she allows that these duties are defeasible — they can be overridden when something weightier is at stake.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond just getting her to restate the view? At minimum I want: one question pressing on whether the arbitrariness of conventions undermines the obligation (the queue is completely arbitrary in one sense — in some places you take a number, in others it's first-come-first-served, and presumably she thinks you're obliged either way — so what exactly is the obligation tracking?); one on visitors — does a tourist arriving somewhere with different conventions, say a country where tipping is insulting or where nobody queues the way the British do, have to work out the local convention before they can be blamed for violating it, and does genuine uncertainty excuse where laziness wouldn't; one on jaywalking, where the convention is backed by actual law in some places and pure custom in others — does the legal backing change the moral weight of the violation, or only the prudential cost of getting a fine; and one on the etiquette objection, namely that if queueing is now a moral matter, morality is doing too much work — everything from murder to not waving thank-you on the motorway ends up in the same category, and doesn't that cost us the sense that morality is specially serious? For each main question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — e.g. if she says the confused visitor is excused, the follow-up should press on whether there's then a duty to find out, and how demanding that makes ordinary travel.

Two last things: please write the questions in British English (so queue-jumping rather than cutting in line, and I'd rather not have a question about the "restroom" etc.), and keep the phrasing conversational — this is a podcast, not a seminar, so no "kindly distinguish, professor, between X and Y" formulations. And I'd like the final question to be the single one you'd least want to answer if you were her — the one that, if she handles it badly, the whole book looks shakier coming out the other side. She's a lovely guest and I want to be fair to her, but my listeners can smell a softball from a mile off.
