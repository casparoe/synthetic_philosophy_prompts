As a journalist covering science and ideas, I spend much of my working life reporting on expert disagreement, and over the years I have settled on a set of commitments about when such disagreement is rational. Before I lean on them in print, I would like to know whether they hang together. They are: (1) Aumann's agreement theorem is sound — agents who share a prior and commonly know each other's posteriors cannot disagree; (2) the common-prior assumption is, in practice, an implausible idealisation, since a person's prior is unavoidably shaped by their accumulated experience, so two honest and intelligent observers can rationally start from different priors and never converge; (3) when two informed, sincere experts remain far apart after engaging with each other's work, that is usually evidence that at least one of them is reasoning badly somewhere along the chain; (4) because the theorem's conditions are essentially never satisfied in real conversations, a panellist who closes a debate by agreeing to disagree with a respected colleague has done nothing epistemically improper; and (5) in reporting a live controversy, my job is to convey the distribution of expert opinion as it stands, not to search for a resolution the experts themselves have failed to reach. Please audit this set rather than lecture on the theorem: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity rather than treating all as equally grave, and for each genuine tension say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me elsewhere. Do not tell me which commitment I ought to hold; I want the map of pressures, not a verdict. Please keep your response to at most 400 words.
