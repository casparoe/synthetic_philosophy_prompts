In debates about illusionism, I keep encountering the analogy between consciousness and a computer's user interface: the desktop with its files, folders, and icons. The claim is that just as the desktop shows you icons that look like paper documents sitting in folders, when in reality there is nothing in the machine resembling documents or folders, introspection shows you phenomenal properties that seem intrinsic and private, when in reality nothing in the brain resembles them either. And just as the interface is a useful simplification for the user rather than a deceptive bug, the illusion of phenomenality is a functional feature that we are not wrong to rely on in daily life. The analogy is then used to do serious work: to argue that since the icons are not real objects in the machine, qualia are not real properties in the mind, and that this "user illusion" framing defuses the objection that illusionism is self-defeating or incredible. Please audit the analogy. Give me the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the icon, the desktop, the user, the underlying files and electronics, and the interface designer — and then say where the mapping breaks. Some disanalogies I suspect matter more than others: a computer interface exists for a user who is outside the machine and who genuinely experiences the screen, whereas the brain's "interface" would have to be for the brain itself, which risks smuggling the experiencing subject back in; and the desktop, unlike phenomenality on the illusionist view, does not misrepresent — real files and folders do exist as structures underneath, so the interface simplifies rather than confabulates. Which disanalogies are fatal to the intended use and which are incidental? Does the eliminativist conclusion survive once they are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? And if the interface picture fails, is there a better analogy available to the illusionist — and what would it have to get right that this one does not?
