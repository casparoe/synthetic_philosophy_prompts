Something I've been chewing on: on prediction markets, the prices of contracts on mutually exclusive outcomes basically have to add up to 1 (minus fees), because if they don't, someone comes along and dutches the market — bets on every outcome in proportions that guarantee profit — and prices snap back. Bookmakers do the same thing with odds, tightening their book whenever the exposure leaves them arbitrgeable. So there's this real institution whose whole functioning depends on incoherence not persisting. My question is what view of rationality that practice presupposes. The obvious reading is the Dutch book argument: credences that violate the probability axioms leave you open to a sure-loss package of bets, so prices and beliefs had better be coherent. But is that the right reading? A bookmaker snapping odds back into line is just protecting its balance sheet — it doesn't need to treat its odds as degrees of belief at all. And the classical Dutch book argument leans on extra assumptions everyone mentions: that an agent would accept a bet at any odds matching their credence, and that money is linear in utility (why would a poor person take an even-money bet on a life-changing sum?). Are those assumptions somehow presupposed by the market practice too, or does the practice strictly require only that someone with deep pockets is around to dutch the error? It also puzzles me that casinos profit off gamblers who violate coherence all day, and we treat that as fine business rather than as proof those gamblers have irrational beliefs — so does the practice support the normative conclusion about rational credence, or only a descriptive claim about who ends up with the money? Reconstruct what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with, and please seperate what's basically settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested (I gather the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalizing is a real fight, but I'm not sure how much of one). Don't just restate the textbook.
