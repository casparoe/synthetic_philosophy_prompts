I've landed on the view that welfare conditionality is basically right: if the community supports you, you owe it a willingness to contribute back if you can, so attaching work requirements, job-search obligations, and sanctions to benefits is legitimate — not because it "works" in some policy-evaluation sense, but on the reciprocity principle itself. What would count against it?

I don't want the standard "the poor are blameless" speech. I want the stuff that should actually move me. Concrete cases where the view seems to give the wrong verdict — like the guy who paid in for thirty years, becomes unemployed, and refuses the first job offer that comes along: does his ledger reset, or did he bank a claim? Or the flip side, a healthy 22-year-old who's never contributed a dime. Empirical findings the view is oddly silent about — say it turns out sanctions demonstrably reduce employment: for a defense that's supposed to be principled rather than consequentialist, is that fatal, merely awkward, or not even a defeater at all? And whether the position has quietly been insulated by fallback moves — the one I've noticed is sliding from "the unwilling forfeit their claim" to "conditions are just an epistemic screen separating can't from won't," which seems to change what the view is even about, since a screen can be justified even when forfeiture isn't.

Also curious whether the reciprocity principle, taken seriously, has to take a side on a more in-the-weeds question: does it push you toward contribution-tracking insurance systems (what you get scales with what you paid in) or flat-rate universal support, since those give very different answers about what the "undeserving" are actually denied.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender of the view would themseleves concede count against it (versus what only critics say), and be honest at the end if the view turns out to risk nothing at all — and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind.
