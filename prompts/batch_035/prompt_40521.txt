Quick backstory before the ask: I'm on the governance working group for a ~400-member housing co-op, and we're finally rewriting the bylaws chapter on how we elect the board (5 seats, usually 8-12 candidates, sometimes with organized slates). Right now we use sequential runoffs which everyone hates, and three proposals have come in. One faction wants instant-runoff ranked ballots. A second wants approval voting, and their memo claims approval voting is "immune to the Gibbard--Satterthwaite problem" because voters approve rather than rank. The third proposal is from our treasurer, who got excited reading about random dictatorship: one ballot drawn at random decides the whole election. He argues it's the only rule where no one can ever gain by lying about their preferences, and that it's fair in expectation, so any complaint is just risk aversion. A fourth member (retired math teacher) keeps saying none of this matters because with 400 voters no single person's manipulation is pivotal anyway.

I'm supposed to circulate a written recommendation before Sunday's meeting. Please draft it. What I need:

1. A clear recommendation of one rule (could be one of the three, a hybrid, or something else like Borda -- your call), with actual reasoning the working group can respond to, not just a vibe.

2. An honest treatment of the Gibbard--Satterthwaite theorem and what it does and doesn't show here. The approval faction and the treasurer are each using it rhetorically in opposite directions, and I suspect both are partly wrong -- e.g., what the theorem assumes about the number of alternatives, whether it applies to rules that ask for more than rankings, and whether the treasurer's beloved random-dictatorship result actually survives when voters care about the whole committee slate rather than a single winner. Also whether the math teacher's "no one is pivotal" point dissolves the problem or just relocates it (slates, coalitions, whips coordinating how people rank).

3. A fair statement of the strongest argument for whichever option you reject, so it doesn't look like we strawmanned anyone.

4. Something on implementation: what would have to change in the bylaws text for your recommendation to work, and any conditions under which we should revisit it.

Keep it to roughly a page and a half, memo format, addressed to the governance working group. And after the memo, could you add a small JSON summary with these fields: "recommended_rule" (string), "second_choice" (string), "gs_lesson" (one or two sentences on what Gibbard--Satterthwaite actually tells us here), "strongest_counter" (the best argument for the runner-up, one sentence), "bylaw_changes" (array of strings), "revisit_if" (array of strings -- conditions that should trigger us to rethink), and "confidence" (integer 0-100). Thanks!!
