For a school essay I've adopted the situationist critique of virtue ethics: experiments like Milgram's obedience studies and the bystander research suggest that trivial features of circumstances — whether someone is in a hurry, whether another helper is present — predict how people behave far better than any stable trait does, and that there may be no robust cross-situational character traits like courage or compassion for virtue ethics to be about. My teacher says every position needs to be tested, so tell me what would count against situationism itself. Which empirical findings, arguments, or ordinary observations would genuinely embarrass the view, and which would its defenders shrug off? I have a few candidates I'd like weighed: evidence that people's behaviour is fairly consistent across decades of life; the claim that the classic experiments have since been reinterpreted so the subjects weren't blindly obeying or apathetic but reasoning their way through the situation; and the idea that virtue could be reconceived as a statistical tendency across many situations rather than a guarantee in each one. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed situationist would concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss, and be honest about whether the position can be qualified into something unfalsifiable — and whether that would matter for an essay defending it.
