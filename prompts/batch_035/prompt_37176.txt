I've been a reader of the rationalist blogosphere for well over a decade — LessWrong in its old incarnation, Slate Star Codex and its successors, the whole ecosystem — and while we usually fight about Bayesian statistics, decision theory and AI, the one long-running dispute I follow outside that world is the argument over the Anthropocene's start date. I've watched it since the Anthropocene Working Group was formed to look into formalising the term, through the years spent sifting candidate boundaries (Ruddiman's early-agriculture thesis that farmers were altering atmospheric chemistry millennia before industry, Lewis and Maslin's case for 1610, when a dip in atmospheric CO2 plausibly tracks forest regrowth after the collapse of Indigenous American populations, and the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide layer that the group ultimately fixed on, pinned by convention to 1950), through the selection of Crawford Lake as the reference section, and then the rejection: in early 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted the proposal down, the appeal went nowhere, and the whole thing stalled completely. Years on, the same people are making the same points in the same venues, and nobody has budged an inch.

What strikes me is that the stall doesn't look empirical. Both sides agree the mid-century layer of radionuclides, plastics, fly ash and concrete exists, is global and near-synchronous, and is unprecedented in kind. The objections that killed the proposal were structural and jurisdictional: that a few decades of sediment cannot bear the rank of an epoch when the Holocene it would terminate has run eleven thousand years; that a knife-edge boundary is the wrong shape for a transformation spread over centuries; that the group effectively chose its date first and then went shopping for a section that displayed it (an accusation they deny, but one that stuck); and, from another flank, that the Anthropocene is an Earth-system or cultural concept that simply doesn't belong on the geologic timescale at all. Meanwhile humanities scholars and climate communicators reacted to the rejection as though a scientific tribunal had ruled humans innocent of geological agency — which no stratigrapher claims.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, because I'd want it stress-tested — is that this is a disagreement about what the geologic timescale is for: a record of what a future geologist could resolve in rock, or a ledger of planetary change with whatever markers we care to use; and, hiding inside that, a fight over who decides, since the International Commission on Stratigraphy holds the gatekeeping authority over a word the public uses for something much larger. So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'epoch', two communities answering different questions (one chronostratigraphic, one historical or Earth-systemic) under a single heading, or a hidden empirical question — for instance about the long-term preservability of the mid-century signals — doing the real work? Diagnose the structure of the dispute from what each side actually maintains, tell me whether the impasse shows the question is ill-posed or merely hard and political, and then say what, given your diagnosis, would genuinely count as progress: a new formal distinction, a change of question (perhaps severing the Earth-system concept from the epoch altogether), or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please answer in British English.
