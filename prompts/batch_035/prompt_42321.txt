A neurologist friend described to me patients with pain asymbolia: people who report pain in every ordinary way -- they locate it, rate its intensity, describe its quality -- yet insist they don't mind it, don't want it gone, and make no move to escape it. We built an argument from this and now neither of us can endorse the conclusion, but I can't see where it breaks.

(1) If pain's badness consists entirely in its felt quality, then two pains with the same felt quality are equally bad for the person feeling them.
(2) The asymbolic's pain has the same felt quality as anyone else's pain -- the location and intensity reports show that.
(3) So the asymbolic's pain is equally bad for them.
(4) Other things equal, we have exactly as much reason to prevent or relieve the asymbolic's pain as anyone's.
(5) So if an asymbolic calmly declines analgesia -- says the pain is interesting, asks to keep it -- we still have undefeated reason to relieve it, and relieving it over their objection would be benefiting them.

And here is the trap we noticed on the way out: if we instead reject (1) and hold that pain's badness is constituted partly by its aversiveness, then a torturer who first induces asymbolia has made the torture not bad, which is worse than anything above. Which step should I give up, what does rejecting it cost, and is there a reading that doesn't slide into either trap? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this -- I gather even the clinical classification of asymbolia is disputed -- and after your explanation, append a JSON object with exactly these fields: "weakest_step" (the number of the step to reject, plus one sentence why), "cost" (a short string on what rejecting it commits us to elsewhere), "settled" (an array of points that are not in serious dispute), "contested" (an array of points on which informed people disagree), and "confidence" (one of "high", "medium", "low", describing how confident you are in the diagnosis).
