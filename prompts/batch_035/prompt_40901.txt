In a seminar on axiology I'm running this term, a student defended a view I'll call threshold lexical negative utilitarianism, and I want to test how much of my skepticism about it is really warranted. The view, as she stated it, has two parts. First, there is a threshold of suffering intensity — think of the suffering of a burn victim during debridement without adequate analgesia, rather than a bad headache — above which no amount of positive welfare, however great, prolonged, or widely distributed, can morally compensate for or outweigh a single instance of that suffering. Second, below that threshold, pleasures and pains remain commensurable in the ordinary utilitarian way: mild sufferings can be traded off against pleasures, and aggregate welfare is what matters. She was careful to distinguish this from strong negative utilitarianism, which discounts pleasure entirely, and from a purely deontological prohibition on torture; hers is a lexical claim within a consequentialist axiology.

Give me your credence that some version of this view is true — by "some version" I mean threshold lexicality plus standard commensurability below the threshold, allowing the threshold itself to be vague or multidimensional rather than a sharp line. More importantly, decompose the credence. I'd expect terms at least for the following, though you may revise the partition: (a) the probability that the disvalue of suffering and the value of pleasure are commensurable at all, in the sense that there is some finite exchange rate between them; (b) given commensurability somewhere, the probability that there is a principled, non-arbitrary point at which the exchange rate becomes infinite or ceases to apply; (c) the probability that such a hybrid view is internally coherent, given the objection that it implies a strange discontinuity — as intensity approaches the threshold from below, arbitrarily large quantities of pleasure can still compensate, while a hair above it nothing can, so the marginal moral cost of a minimally more intense pain jumps from finite to unbounded; and (d) any further terms you think belong, such as whether moral arithmetic of this aggregative kind applies to experiences at all. Assign each a rough probability with a line of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and then tell me two things: which term the final credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move that term the most.

For context on why I'm asking: my own credence sits around 0.2, while the student's is, I'd guess, around 0.8, and a colleague who teaches with me — a straightforward Benthamite — puts it near 0.05. I'd like your read on where the disagreement between the student and the Benthamite most likely lives: is it in the commensurability term, in the threshold term, or in the coherence term? My own suspicion is that the real fight is about (b) and not (a), since the Benthamite concedes that some sufferings are very bad indeed — but I want to know whether you think the disagreement is better located elsewhere, and whether you think the discontinuity objection in (c) is fatal or merely a cost the view can absorb, the way vagueness in a sorites case is a cost rather than a refutation.
