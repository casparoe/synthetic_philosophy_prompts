I'm the vice-president for societies at a mid-sized British university students' union, and the constitution makes me the first decision-maker on whether a proposed society event goes ahead — guest speakers, debates, film screenings, charity stalls, the lot. There are maybe four or five of these a week during term, I'm supposed to turn each around within forty-eight hours, my decisions can be appealed to a trustee panel that meets fortnightly, and I have to publish short written reasons for every refusal, which the student newspaper then takes an interest in. Everyone invokes the paradox of tolerance at me, from both directions: the societies whose speaker I've declined say it's censorship dressed up as philosophy, and the ones whose opponent I've permitted say I'm naively treating tolerance as a suicide pact. I've read enough to know Popper's actual point was narrower than either side assumes — he claimed that unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance, which is an argument about the limits of the policy, not a list of the people it licenses me to bar — and I've also come across the 'tolerance as a peace treaty' framing that circulates online, on which the intolerant forfeit protection by breaking the terms rather than by holding bad views. But neither gives me anything I can actually run on a Tuesday afternoon with a speaker's abstract and a complaint from two societies in my inbox.

What I want is a decision procedure, in the sense of an ordered set of questions with pass/fail conditions, that operationalises the toleration literature without doing violence to it. I gather there are real distinctions to preserve here: between the content of a view and the likely conduct of an event; between a view being offensive and its being directed at getting a protected group's members excluded or driven out; between the epistemic case for hearing bad arguments and the political case; and between what a public university owes speakers and what a private association like a students' union owes them, which I understand is not the same thing. I also want the procedure to take seriously the consequentialist worry that refusing a speaker can hand them the publicity and the martyrdom they came for, and the slippery-slope worry that once 'intolerant' is doing the work, the category quietly expands to cover whoever the loudest society dislikes this term. And it has to be honest about where it runs out: I suspect there are cases where the philosophy genuinely underdetermines the answer and I just have to own a judgement call, and I'd rather the procedure marked those points explicitly than pretended to be mechanical.

Please write the procedure as I'd actually use it: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a 'yes, permit', a 'permit with conditions' (specify what conditions are legitimate — security presence, a chaired Q&A, a counter-speaker — and what conditions are just disguised refusals), and a bare 'no', plus the escalation route for the genuinely undecidable ones. Then run it on three matters I've actually faced or expect to face: (1) a libertarian society inviting a pundit who argues for drastic cuts to immigration using crime statistics that our policy officer calls misleading, with no suggestion of violence in any of his material; (2) a socialist society whose speaker's abstract ends by endorsing 'no platform for fascists, by whatever means necessary', which the speaker's supporters insist is just a slogan; (3) a religious society hosting an internal debate, open to all attendees, in which one invited participant will argue that homosexual conduct is sinful. I'd like the worked applications to include at least one case where the procedure makes me permit something I personally find odious and one where it makes me refuse someone whose politics I broadly share, and I'd like you to state plainly which steps of the procedure are judgement calls in a costume, and what the procedure can never do for me.
