I am preparing a seminar session on the dispute over the so-called Cartesian theater, and I would like a decision tree for my students. The tree should take a student from a starting commitment that the brain processes perceptual content in parallel, through widely distributed pathways, with no neuroanatomical evidence of a single convergence point, and end at a named position on where, if anywhere, consciousness is localized.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first choose the branch questions, then locate the actual positions in the space those questions generate, and briefly note any combination of answers that nobody occupies, saying whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The branch nodes should be phrased as questions a student could answer without knowing which position they lead to. At minimum the tree should split on the following: (a) whether the contents of a given moment of experience must all be present together somewhere — in the mind or in the brain — for the experience to be unified; (b) for those who deny any such place, whether there is, in cases of apparent backwards-referral such as the color phi phenomenon and the cutaneous rabbit illusion, a determinate fact about what the subject experienced that outruns anything the subject could sincerely report; and (c) for those who answer yes to (a), whether the theater is made of physical stuff.

The leaves should include at least: the dualist's theater; its materialist cousin, a brain-local point at which everything relevant to a moment of experience is simultaneously available (name it what you like, but describe it precisely); the multiple-drafts view on which content becomes conscious by winning influence in the cortex rather than by arriving somewhere; and, if your logical space generates it, a leaf for those who accept fully distributed processing yet insist on further facts beyond all possible reports while denying any theater. If you include that last leaf, say what it is committed to and whether your tree can really distinguish it from its neighbors.

One further requirement, and I suspect it is the hard one: somewhere in the write-up, address the pair of positions the tree cannot separate. I have in mind the pair that disagree over whether, in the color phi case, the subject's experience was first one way and then misremembered, or was from the beginning as the subject reports — a dispute no possible observation of the subject could adjudicate, since both sides agree on every report the subject would give. If your tree cannot tell these apart, flag it honestly rather than inventing a question that pretends to; if you think some question does separate them, defend it against the objection that its answer could never be checked.

Keep the whole thing to something a student can work through in ten minutes: no more than four internal nodes and five leaves, each node one question, each leaf one sentence of description plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
