In my psycholinguistics reading group, the tinted-glasses picture of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis is doing a lot of work. The running analogy goes: your native language is a pair of glasses you were fitted with as a child and can never take off; the grammatical categories of the language are the tint; speakers of different languages therefore look out on the same world through different hues; and the perceiver behind the lenses — the eyes, the mind itself — is unchanged by which pair it happened to be issued. My colleagues lean on this to license a package of strong conclusions: that translation between grammatically distant languages is structurally lossy rather than merely laborious, that a bilingual is someone who switches between worldviews rather than someone who inhabits a single one, and that certain thoughts are strictly unavailable — not just harder to formulate — to speakers of certain languages.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a rehash of the relativism debate. Specifically:

1. The correspondence table. What plays the role of the glasses, the tint, the eyes behind them, the optometrist who fits them, and the act of taking them off or having them prescription-swapped (second-language acquisition)?

2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the intended use and those that don't. Some candidates to consider: a tint colors everything uniformly, while the effects my colleagues care about are supposed to be category-specific (grammatical gender, evidential marking, obligatory tense); a tint shifts hue but doesn't change which distinctions are available, whereas the unavailable-thoughts conclusion needs something much stronger than hue; glasses are in principle removable, and the analogy quietly concedes too much by implying there is an unmediated, untinted view that some hypothetical observer enjoys; and lenses are worn by a perceiver who exists prior to them, which may beg the question against the view being defended.

3. Whether the three conclusions survive, treated separately rather than as a package. It's open to my colleagues that translation-lossiness falls while unavailable-thoughts falls too, or that bilingual worldview-switching could stand while the rest goes. I want to know which disanalogies are load-bearing for which conclusion.

4. Whether a better analogy is available for whatever the psychological evidence actually supports — for instance something that captures obligatory grammatical categories steering attention and ease rather than filtering content, along the lines of being handed a particular ruler rather than tinted glass: it doesn't change what you can see, it changes what's easy to measure — or whether the glasses picture is so compromised it should be abandoned rather than repaired.
