I'm a third of the way into a novel set in the late summer and autumn of 1929, told from the point of view of a young salesman at a tire company in Akron who has been buying shares on margin with his brother-in-law and planning to marry on the strength of it. My first readers have all raised the same objection: the characters seem implausibly blind. They know, as I know, that October is coming, and they keep asking why nobody in the book senses it.

I'm resisting two fixes, and I want to be sure I'm right to resist them. The first is the obvious one: introduce a Cassandra — a skeptical uncle, a sour bank teller — who warns of a crash early on, so the reader's foreknowledge is licensed within the story. But my research suggests that people who did sell in the summer of 1929 mostly did so for narrow, local, wrong reasons, and my fear is that the Cassandra figure is a lie of a specific kind: he makes the catastrophe look like something the age was entitled to know, when what actually happened is that people were worried about the usual things and kept being wrong for years before they were suddenly right. The second fix is subtler: my instinct as a craftsman keeps pulling me to plant signals — a dropped remark at dinner, a foreclosed farm glimpsed from a train — because the conventions of the form seem to demand that a large event be prepared for. But I've started to wonder whether that demand is the reader's knowledge running backwards through the book, imposing a pattern on months that did not feel like a pattern from inside them.

So my questions, and I'd appreciate advice that takes the craft constraints seriously rather than treating them as an excuse. Is there a principled way to decide what a character in 1929 may plausibly register and what he may not, given that I know the thing he doesn't and cannot fully un-know it? Is the reader's complaint even fair — is a character's unawareness of what we know really a flaw, or is it the whole point of setting a novel there? And is there an honest middle path between the Cassandra, who makes the past look prescient, and total obliviousness, which reads as authorial contrivance in the opposite direction — some way of dramatizing how the months before a catastrophe are full of people correctly worrying about the wrong disaster? I'd also like to know whether you think the form itself is against me: whether a novel, unlike a life, is under a structural obligation to make what happens look like it had to happen, and if so, whether I should be fighting that obligation or finding a way to acknowledge it — for instance by letting something in the book arrive completely unprepared-for and unexplained, exactly as it did in life.
