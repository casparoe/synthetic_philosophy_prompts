In my phil of bio reading group my supervisor keeps pushing the line that species aren't classes at all, they're individuals — a species is basically one big organism-ish thing, a single chunk of genealogy that starts at a speciation event, ends at extinction, and gets named (like you name a child) rather than defined (like you define a class by membership conditions). Apparently this is roughly Ghiselin and Hull's old line from the seventies. She's using the analogy to push three conclusions at once, and I can't work out which of them the analogy is actually carrying:

1. asking for the essence of a species — what all and only its members have in common — is a category mistake, like asking what all my cells have in common that makes them mine rather than part of some bigger body
2. species selection is real: species can compete, be selected, and even reproduce (by budding off daughter species), so the unit of selection can be the species itself, not just the organism
3. a species going extinct is more like a death than like the last copy of a type going out of print — so losing a species is a loss in the way a death is a loss, not just "fewer instances of a kind around now"

Can you give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the organism, the cells/organs, birth, reproduction, death, the boundary of the body — then say where the mapping breaks? The things that bother me: the parts of an actual organism are integrated, my liver does something for the whole of me and recieves something back, whereas the members of a species in 1900 and the members in 2024 never causally interact at all, and the ones alive at the same time mostly just compete with each other. Also real organisms have fairly sharp boundaries and species famously don't (ring species, asexual lineages, constant hybridisation). But I can't tell which of these breaks matters for which of her conclusions — maybe the lack of integration sinks species selection but leaves the death point intact?

So basically: audit the analogy, say which of the three conclusions it can carry, which it was smuggling in, and whether any survive once the disanalogies are on the table. And if it's a bad analogy, is there a better one doing the rounds — species as lineages, species as populations, something else?
