In my history of economic thought course we just covered Hume's 1752 essays on money and trade, and the lecturer leaned on two of his analogies to argue for a strongly anti-mercantilist, quasi-neutralist reading of Hume. The first is the oil analogy from "Of Money," which opens: "Money is not, properly speaking, one of the subjects of commerce; but only the instrument which men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another. It is none of the wheels of trade: It is the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy." My lecturer used this to attribute to Hume the claim that the quantity of money is of no consequence for real activity — that doubling the money stock just doubles prices, so monetary expansion can never make a nation richer. The second is from the essay on the balance of trade, where Hume argues that specie flows between nations the way water in connected vessels finds its own level: a country that tries to hoard money by trade restrictions just gets dearer goods, and the money drains back out anyway. A friend of mine, who picked this up from a popular economics blog, now cites the water picture as a knock-down argument that trade deficits and balances are self-correcting and that any intervention to hold money in is futile by definition.

I want an audit of both analogies rather than a defense of Hume. First, lay out the correspondence table for each: what plays the role of the wheels, the friction, the machine the oil is chosen for, the connected vessels, the fluid; and note what each mapping quietly imports. For the oil picture, some obvious questions are whether oil has the feature that doubling its quantity only changes a gauge reading somewhere else in the machine, and whether the fact that oil is deliberately selected to have as little effect as possible on the engine is doing work the monetary case can't support. It also seems worth flagging that Hume himself, later in the same essay and in the one on interest, allows a transition period: newly arriving money stimulates industry before prices rise, and manufactures migrate from rich countries to poor ones where labour is cheap — which sounds like non-neutrality, at least in the interval. So a second question is whether the oil analogy, as my lecturer used it, licenses a stronger neutrality conclusion than Hume actually held, and where exactly the disanalogy does or doesn't matter for that use. For the water picture, ask whether the automaticity is smuggled in: water needs no merchant, no expectations, no debts, and no capital controls, so is the self-correction a physical necessity in Hume's argument or an empirical claim that the analogy merely makes feel inevitable?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusions survive once the analogies are discounted — whether Hume's substantive argument about prices, competitiveness, and the migration of manufactures can carry the anti-mercantilist conclusion on its own, with the analogies as ornament, or whether they were doing load-bearing work; and if either analogy is beyond repair, say what a better one would be and what it would get right that these miss. Please be explicit about which disanalogies are fatal for the use the analogy was put to and which are merely untidy, since that distinction is the part my lecturer never drew.
