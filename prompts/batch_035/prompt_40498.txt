I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for the past year I've been reporting on the disputes over monuments to slave traders, colonial administrators, and the like. What interests me is not the shouting but the settlement most cities have converged on. The statue stays, but it gets re-labeled: a new plaque recontextualizing the man, sometimes a QR code to an online essay, occasionally a new sculpture installed nearby to stand in visual argument with the old one. Heritage bodies have formalized this as a doctrine of retaining and explaining. And here is what puzzles me: the same cities that do this have been far more willing to rename streets and remove the honoree's name from schools. A street's name can simply be changed; a statue, apparently, can only be annotated.

What I'd like you to work out is what theory of commemoration the plaque-and-keep policy presupposes. It seems to assume that a monument functions something like an assertion — that it says something about the person, and that the city can amend the assertion with a footnote, rather as a journal corrects an article by appending an editorial note rather than destroying the printing press. But both the fiercest defenders and the fiercest opponents of these statues seem to treat them differently: as acts rather than statements — as honors, things done to and for the person depicted. On that view a plaque is not a correction but a strange appendage, like standing next to someone at a ceremony while muttering that you don't really respect him.

So, my questions. What view of commemorative meaning does "retain and explain" actually need — is it the propositional, amendable-in-place picture, or could something else license the same policy? Is the pairing of plaque-friendly statues and rename-happy streets coherent, or does it reveal that the city is tacitly running two theories at once: one on which monuments are historical evidence and art objects whose testimony we can supplement, and one on which names are merely labels that ought to track our current values? Could a thoroughgoing honor-based view of commemoration — one on which a monument is essentially an award, and awards can't be re-described, only kept, rescinded, or reassigned to a new purpose — endorse any of this, or would it force a different policy entirely? And if we concluded that monuments really are irreducibly honorific rather than assertoric, what concretely would have to change in what cities are now doing? I'd find it especially useful if you distinguished what the plaque policy strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit, since I suspect several quite different theories of commemoration could live with it, and I'd like to know whether that's a strength of the practice or a sign that nobody has thought it through.
