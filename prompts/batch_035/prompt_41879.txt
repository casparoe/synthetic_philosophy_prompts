Last weekend at a friend's birthday dinner, two of the guests — Dana, who's writing her thesis on population ethics, and Marcus, a proud total utilitarian — spent basically the whole main course arguing about Parfit's repugnant conclusion while everyone else just wanted cake. I took a population ethics elective once so I followed most of it, and I'll try to reconstruct the exchange because I'd really like a proper verdict.

(1) Marcus opened by stating the repugnant conclusion roughly as follows: for any population of very happy people living excellent lives, there's some much, much larger population whose members each have lives that are only barely worth living, and the huge population is better overall once you add up all the welfare. He said this falls straight out of total utilitarianism — just keep adding people whose lives are a hair above zero — and since total utilitarianism is right, we should bite the bullet and accept that the enormous barely-positive world really is better. Dana said any view with that implication is broken and should be rejected, not defended, and that Marcus was treating a reductio as a feature.

(2) Dana offered average utilitarianism as the fix: what matters is how well off people are on average, not the total. Marcus said that's far worse: a world containing exactly one person whose life is slightly better than anyone's life in our world would rank above a world of ten billion people all living fantastic lives, since the lone person's average is higher. Dana conceeded that's bad but said at least her view doesn't have the repugnant implication, so she's choosing her poison.

(3) Dana then tried a critical-level view instead: adding a person only makes the world better if their welfare clears some positive threshold, say a life that's comfortably good rather than merely worth living. Marcus said this generates the opposite monstrosity: adding a person whose life is genuinely good for them but just under the threshold would make the world *worse*, even though it hurts nobody and adds a life the person is glad to have. Dana didn't really have an answer to that one and moved on.

(4) Her final move was person-affecting: she said the reason the repugnant conclusion feels repugnant is that when you compare the world of ten billion flourishing people with the huge barely-positive world, nobody in the flourishing world is made worse off by the second world existing instead — so worse for whom, exactly? Claims of betterness need someone the badness is for. Marcus replied that by that same logic it isn't better for anyone when you add a billion blissful lives to a miserable world either, and that Dana's view can't even certify as an improvement a world where everyone is somewhat happier AND there are more people, which seems like something any decent theory should be able to say. Dana ended with a shrug that maybe population ethics is just an inconsistent mess and no view gets everything right, and Marcus said that was throwing in the towel.

Can you go through the four moves and tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And who comes out more right on balance, if either? Also curious whether there's an angle neither of them brought up that would've helped.
