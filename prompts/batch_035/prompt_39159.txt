I'm a high school senior who has spent the past two years reading philosophy of mind and epistemology on my own, mostly after my physics homework. For a selective summer humanities program, applicants are asked to submit a short statement of their philosophical commitments on one topic, along with an explanation of how the views support one another. Before I send mine in, I want an honest audit of it. Here is what I currently believe about perception and its relationship to belief:

(1) Perception is a basic source of justification. If it visually seems to you that there is a chair in the room, you are entitled to believe there is a chair without first establishing that your eyes are generally reliable; the seemings themselves carry the epistemic weight.

(2) Cognitive penetration is real and fairly widespread. Once you are told that a tangled black-and-white image is a dalmatian in the snow, you cannot go back to seeing it as noise; trained chicken sexers and radiologists see things in the very same stimulus that novices simply do not see.

(3) Still, I am fairly convinced that beliefs affect perception only by steering attention: they determine where you look, how long you look, and what you are prepared to find, but they never alter the character of an experience itself — how things look is fixed by the stimulus and the visual system, and the rest is downstream judgment.

(4) When an expert's trained expectations make a feature visible — the radiologist seeing the tumor, the chicken sexer seeing the chick's sex — the resulting experience gives the expert entirely fresh justification for a belief they did not previously hold, even though the belief-shaping came from inside their own head.

(5) The very same mechanism is also the root of serious epistemic bias. A physician who expects certain patients to be drug-seeking will literally perceive those patients' behavior differently, and I want to say this is a defect in the perception itself, not merely a prejudice in the judgment layered on top of it.

(6) Whether penetration happens at all is an empirical question, settled by experiments rather than armchair reflection. I have read that the classic studies in which children judged desirable coins as looking larger than they were have not held up well under later scrutiny, which seems to me exactly why careful experiments matter more than anyone's intuitions about looks.

Please tell me whether these six claims hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? For each genuine tension, what is the cheapest fix and what does it cost to adopt it? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating everything as equally problematic. If a single revision would clean up most of the mess, tell me which one, and also tell me what that revision would force me to give up elsewhere. No references, please — I will add citations myself before submitting.
