Lately it's epistemology, and I keep hitting the regress problem: every justification apparently needs another justification, and there are three standard responses — foundationalism (some beliefs are self-justifying and stop the chain), coherentism (beliefs justify each other in a web), and infinitism (the chain just never ends, and that's ok).

Here's what I want from you. Translate each of the three positions into the vocabulary I actually used at the bench, as faithfully as you can. To me, foundationalism sounds like metrological traceability: every measurement traces back through a chain of calibratons, and since the 2019 SI redefinition the chain terminates in constants like c, h, and k that are fixed by definition rather than measured — a definitional stopping point, which looks a lot like a basic belief. Coherentism sounds like method cross-validation: when you determine the same quantity by gas chromatography, gravimetry, and titration, and the answers agree, you trust the result even though no single method certifies itself. Infinitism, honestly, sounds like the infinite regress of calibration we always tried to avoid in practice — every instrument checked against another instrument forever.

So do the translation one theory at a time, and then give me the residue report. Wich parts of each position map cleanly onto calibration and traceability, which parts have to be distorted to fit, and which have no expression at all in my vocabulary? I'm especially curious about the foundationalism case: the SI constants are fixed by definition, but a committee of humans fixed them based on decades of prior measurements, so I can't tell whether that's a genuine terminus or coherentism wearing a lab coat. Then tell me where the residue comes from — is it that my metrology vocabulary is missing something the epistemologists actually care about, or is their dispute less substantive than it looks from the bench?
