Had a massive row with my friend Ravi last night about thought experiments, and he reckons he walked away with a proof that the whole genre is a confidence trick. It went like this:

(1) A thought experiment persuades, when it persuades, by producing in you a pull toward some verdict about the stipulated scenario — an intuition, in the philosopher's sense.
(2) Those pulls are generated by heuristics, and heuristics are calibrated on actual experience: everything we've actually seen, done, and been told.
(3) The scenarios in philosophical thought experiments are, pretty much by definition, far outside anything in that calibration set — perfect predictors, planets where the watery stuff isn't H2O, worlds where you're plugged into an experience machine, trolley tracks that split and rejoin.
(4) A heuristic running on inputs unlike its calibration data has no claim to being right rather than wrong; it's outputs there are unconnected to how things could actually be.
(5) So the intuitions a thought experiment reports carry no evidential weight — they tell you about the reader, not about the scenario.
(6) There is no thought experiment in philosophy that doesn't rely on far scenarios of this kind; that's what makes them thought experiments rather than opinion surveys.
(7) Therefore all philosophical thought experiments are rhetoric: at best theatre, at worst propaganda with the argument hidden in the staging.

And the conclusion seems to definately prove too much. Einstein imagining riding alongside a beam of light; a mathematician convincing herself there's no such object by picturing the would-be counterexample; but also just ordinary hypothetical reasoning, like me asking what would happen if I quit my job and moved to Lisbon — none of those are in my actual experience either, and Ravi's argument seems to sweep them all out. Worse, his own step (4) looks like a generalisation from cases where extrapolation went wrong, and I'd want to say that's actually a decent intuition. But I can't find where the argument slips. He insists it's valid and that the moral is simply that philosophy has to change its methods.

For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (2) — the picture of intuition as something calibrated on experience and running off-distribution in far cases feels right to me. That bit was actually mine, we'd been talking about how ML models behave on inputs unlike their training data and I thought the analogy was doing real work. But I suspect holding onto it costs something. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? And be honest with me if the answer turns out to be that the argument is valid and what has to go is (5), or the idea that these scenarios count as far in the relevant sense.
