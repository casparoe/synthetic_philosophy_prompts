I am a retired analytical chemist — thirty-odd years in trace analysis and measurement science, most of them spent arguing with calibration curves, limits of detection, and the propagation of uncertainties. In retirement I have been reading philosophy, and I have stumbled into the sorites paradox and the debate about vagueness between the epistemicists and the supervaluationists. I believe I follow the positions themselves, but I keep involuntarily translating them into the vocabulary I used at the bench, and I can no longer tell whether that translation is illuminating or quietly corrupting the philosophy.

So here is my request. Restate the epistemicism-vs.-supervaluationism debate in the vocabulary of my working life, as faithfully as you can: the true value of the measurand, precision versus accuracy, calibration and traceability, expanded uncertainty intervals, detection and quantification limits, specification limits and guard-banding, interlaboratory reproducibility, significant figures, and — importantly — the distinction metrologists draw between a measurand that is poorly *defined* and a measurand that is well defined but poorly *measured*. Do not merely decorate the philosophy with laboratory imagery; give me a systematic translation, claim by claim.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims of each side translate cleanly into that vocabulary? Which translate only with distortion — where you had to bend either the philosophy or the metrology to make the fit? And which claims cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in three pressure points. First, supervaluationism's truth-value gaps for borderline sentences: is that anything more than my old expanded uncertainty interval, where I could not certify a sample as in or out of specification and said so? Second, the epistemicist's sharp but unknowable cutoff — one grain making the difference between heap and non-heap: is that like a true value that exists but no instrument, however ideal, could resolve, or does the analogy cheat, because in the laboratory the sharpness always lives in the specification while the fuzziness lives in the measurement, whereas the philosophers seem to locate the fuzziness in the predicate itself? Third, the supervaluationist's precisifications: do they correspond to different calibration protocols, or different definitions of the measurand, and does it matter which they correspond to?

Close with your verdict on the residue itself: does it reveal something my measurement vocabulary simply cannot say, or does it suggest that parts of the philosophers' disagreement are less substantive than they look — that what they are arguing about, at bottom, is the difference between an ill-specified measurand and an unresolved measurement, a distinction we were forced to get clear about decades ago for purely practical reasons?

Throughout, please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and explicitly single out the single point in your translation you are least sure of.
