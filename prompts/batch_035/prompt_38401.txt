I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days — LessWrong, Slate Star Codex, the EA Forum — and over the years I've noticed something I'd like your help thinking through properly. The effective altruism world runs on metrics: DALYs averted, GiveWell's moral weights for deaths averted versus consumption gains, self-reported life satisfaction, measured household consumption. Each of these quietly takes a side in the old "equality of what?" debate that Amartya Sen opened in his 1979 Tanner Lecture — welfare, resources, or capabilities — and in the follow-on exchange among Dworkin, Cohen, and Arneson over the proper currency of egalitarian justice. A QALY weights life-years by health-related quality of life, which is a welfare metric confined to health; measured consumption is a resource proxy; a "years of good life" measure would be capabilities-flavored. Yet the philosophers almost never ask what happens to their favored currency when it must be cardinal, interpersonally comparable, and computable from messy survey data, and the EA measurement people almost never ask which currency their machinery implicitly commits them to. My thesis is that the genuinely interesting open questions live at that joint, and I'm planning a long-form piece — a blog sequence, possibly expanding into something bigger — making the case. I have roughly a year of evenings to spend on it.

What I want from you is not a survey of the debate but a defended agenda. Which unresolved questions in this area actually matter most for that project, and in what order should I tackle them? For each item you place, I'd like the reasoning that drove the placement: whether it's upstream of the others (for instance, whether interpersonal comparisons of welfare are coherent at all seems to gate nearly every operationalization question downstream), whether it's tractable for a non-academic with a statistics background rather than for a professional philosopher, and what evidence or argument over the next couple of years would move it up or down the list. I'd also like you to be candid about which famous parts of this debate are overrated for my purposes. My working suspicion is that the luck-egalitarian fight over whether chosen disadvantages should count — absorbing as it is — changes very little once you're trying to score a deworming program against a cash transfer; tell me whether that's right, and if not, where the responsibility-sensitivity question actually bites in a metric. Finally, be explicit about anything I haven't mentioned that belongs above some of what I have: adaptive preferences and what they do to survey-based welfare measures, the currency question for people who cannot report preferences (young children, severe cognitive disability), and whether any capabilities measure can be made cardinal without quietly collapsing back into resources or welfare.

One constraint on your answer: do not respond with a bibliography or reading list. Give me the ranking argued from the ideas themselves, as you understand them.
