A friend and I argued late into last night about epistocracy. He is drawn to the idea of weighting political power by competence — competence tests for the franchise, extra votes for the demonstrably informed — and what finally made him waver was not the standard who-decides-who-decides retort, which he shrugged off as proving too much, but my suggestion that any test operationalizes a contestable conception of the good citizen, so that the political fight over the test merely re-runs, in worse form, the very disagreement it was supposed to arbitrate. Next month I am presenting a fuller version of that argument to my university's PPE society: a mixed audience that will include a couple of libertarian-leaning economics students sympathetic to my friend's position, several political theory students committed to deliberative democracy, and a mathematician who will take Condorcet-style jury theorem arguments very seriously and will notice any hand-waving. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections each group will actually raise, phrased in their own vocabulary and ordered by how much damage they could do to my case, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is soft; what I should concede up front rather than defend badly; and the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks.
