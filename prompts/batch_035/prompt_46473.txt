I'm TA-ing an undergraduate aesthetics course, and this week we're on the paradox of tragedy: why do audiences actively seek out and take pleasure in works that depict genuinely distressing things? In section, a student brought in an explanation she'd encountered in a popular psychology article, which goes roughly like this: enjoying sad art is like enjoying hot chili peppers. When you eat a chili, your pain receptors fire much as they would for a real burn, but some monitoring process knows there's no tissue damage; with repeated exposure, the combination of a strong bodily signal and a verdict of safety gets relabeled as pleasure. The article claimed that one and the same machinery handles every case of safe negative affect — spicy food, sad music, tearjerker films, roller coasters, horror — and gave the mechanism a catchy label. Her conclusion, which the student endorsed enthusiastically, was that the paradox of tragedy is a pseudo-problem: there is no distinct mechanism for tragic pleasure, so the philosophers who have puzzled over it since the eighteenth century have been analyzing a mislabeling.

I'm suspicious, but I can't quite pin down why, and I'd like to walk the class through it properly rather than just assert my unease. It seems to me that my response to the death of a fictional heroine is not merely a strong negative signal with a safety verdict attached — it seems to be *about* her and her fate, directed at a situation, in a way that the burn from a chili pepper is not about anything. But the student could push back that a capsaicin burn is in some sense about the mouth, and I don't have a clean reply to that; I also worry I'm romanticizing my own moviegoing.

So I'd like an audit of the analogy. Lay out what maps onto what — what plays the role of the capsaicin, of the pain receptor, of the knowledge that one is safe, of the acquired taste that comes with repeated exposure — and then say where the mapping breaks. Crucially, tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion she wants to draw (that there is no special problem about tragedy) as opposed to which are just noise. If the analogy can't carry her conclusion, is there a patched version that could — say, benign-masochism machinery plus something else — and what would the something else have to be? A correspondence table would help for the students, and a verdict at the end on whether the "pseudo-problem" claim survives or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time.
