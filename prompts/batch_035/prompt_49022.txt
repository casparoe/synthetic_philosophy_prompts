After rewatching Stanley Kubrick's A Clockwork Orange, I've been trying to work out how it bears on a puzzle I came across while reading about philosophy online: Harry Frankfurt's famous thought experiment in which a person really has only one path available to them, because someone would covertly intervene to block any alternative, and yet our intuition is that they are still responsible for what they chose. Alex after the Ludovico treatment looks like a strange mirror image of that setup: he is left unable to choose evil at all, and the film, partly through the prison chaplain's doubts about the treatment, suggests that this conditioned goodness is somehow worthless. Please read the film through the debate about whether moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. Does the film support Frankfurt's claim that responsibility can survive the absence of alternatives, or does it dramatize something his case leaves out, namely that Alex's good behavior no longer comes from who he is? I'd also like you to be honest about where the reading strains: does the treatment actually remove alternatives in Frankfurt's sense, given that what seems to be removed is a desire and a capacity to act on it rather than an available option, and how much does that difference matter? Finally, what should we make of the ending, in which Alex is restored to his old self and the film presents it as a recovery — is the film claiming that the freedom to do evil is partly constitutive of being a moral agent, and can the Frankfurt-style debate accommodate that thought, or does it cut against it?
