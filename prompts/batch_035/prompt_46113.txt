A few of us in a political economy reading group have spent the better part of a year on the socialist calculation debate and its modern revival, and the discussion has crystallized in a way that makes me suspect we are not disagreeing about what we think we are disagreeing about. The classic exchange is familiar enough: Mises's 1920 argument that without genuine market prices for capital goods there is no common denominator for rational economic calculation, Lange's reply that planners could converge on equilibrium prices by a trial-and-error procedure, and Hayek's later relocation of the problem from calculation to knowledge — the claim that the relevant information is dispersed, tacit, and often not available to anyone as articulable data until the market process elicits it. What has reignited our arguments is the more recent computational turn: since the early 1990s, most prominently in the work of Paul Cockshott and Allin Cottrell, and now amplified by people pointing at supermarket logistics, modern linear programming, and the data exhaust of platform capitalism, some in my group maintain that the calculation problem has simply been solved — that hardware and algorithms have caught up with the problem Mises posed. The others say this is a category error: Hayek's problem was never computational complexity but epistemic, a matter of what an economy's data-generating process is, so no amount of computing power is even relevant. After months, not one person has budged.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading. One side is answering whether a static allocation problem of realistic size can be solved to an acceptable approximation in acceptable time; the other is answering whether an economy is that kind of problem at all — whether it is better modeled as a discovery process that generates the relevant information than as an optimization that consumes it. If that is right, then the computational results are strictly irrelevant to the Austrian complaint, and, symmetrically, no algorithm the Austrians could ever be shown would count for them, because their real objection is about process and surprise, not output. But I am not certain this is the right diagnosis. There seem to be genuine empirical questions lurking underneath: how much of the economically relevant knowledge really is tacit in a way that market participation alone can extract; whether observed consumer behavior and inventory data from retail systems could substitute for price signals at the margin; whether a planning system with fast iteration and feedback could mimic the discovery function Hayek attributed to entrepreneurs. And there is also, we all privately suspect, a submerged evaluative disagreement about freedom, consumer sovereignty, and who bears the risk of error, doing quiet work in making each side's standards of proof so different.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who won. Using what each side actually claims, tell me which parts of this disagreement are conceptual, which are empirical, and which are evaluative; whether the debate as revived is substantive or partly verbal (e.g., trading on an ambiguity in what "solving the calculation problem" means); and whether my two-questions hypothesis holds up or papers over a real clash that could, in principle, be settled. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper distinction between the calculation and knowledge problems, a concrete specification of what a planning system would have to demonstrate to meet the Hayekian objection, an empirical program about the tacitness of knowledge — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected because the dispute is at bottom about what an economy is for.
