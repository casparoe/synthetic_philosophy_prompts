I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background at all — CS degree, day job in ML infrastructure, where I serve models rather than study them. This year I got pulled into a model-debugging effort and started reading interpretability research on my commute: papers about features living in superposition, sparse autoencoders recovering "dictionary" directions in a network's residual stream, linear probes that read a model's guess about the next token straight out of its activations. I take this stuff at face value: it seems to me these papers are finding out what is actually in there. A coworker who took one philosophy elective in college and has never let me forget it thinks that's naive, and over Slack he laid out an argument that I haven't been able to beat. It concludes that nothing I think I'm finding is really in the network at all, and although a week has gone by, every step still looks fine to me.

(1) When an interpretability paper says a certain direction in the activations is a detector for, say, code containing an off-by-one error, that claim is meant to be true or false about the network itself, not just a convenient label humans attach to it — call this taking features seriously. (2) For such claims to be true or false, there must be a fact of the matter about which parts of the network carry which features. (3) But in real trained networks, features live in superposition: each unit participates in many features, each feature is smeared across many units, and the decomposition you recover depends on the tool — a linear probe, a sparse autoencoder, and a clustering method will each carve the same network up differently. (4) And this isn't just measurement noise: the network's input-output behavior is unchanged under any invertible re-expression of its internal states — rotate the residual stream however you like and you have, functionally, the identical network — so the carving can't be a fact about the network, only about which spectacles we put on before looking. (5) So there is no fact of the matter about which components carry which features. (6) So networks contain no features in the sense of (1), and interpretability research produces useful fictions rather than discoveries.

What convinces me something must be wrong somewhere is that the argument never uses any fact specific to neural networks — I checked, and you can run it word for word about any causal system, including brains, in which case it shows that nothing has ever represented anything, which seems like a lot to derive from linear algebra. My own experience also pushes the other way: last quarter I found a single direction whose ablation reliably knocked out one specific capability and nothing else, and that felt like a fact I discovered about the model rather than one I imposed on it. But I can't convert the feeling into a rebuttal — every time I try, my coworker points out that the direction I intervened on only exists relative to a basis, and that I chose the basis. So: where exactly does the argument go wrong, which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? For what it's worth, I'd like to keep the thought that my ablation experiment uncovered something rather than conferred structure on it, but if keeping that means paying somewhere else, tell me where the payment falls.
