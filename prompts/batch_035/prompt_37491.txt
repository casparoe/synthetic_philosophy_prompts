My study partner thinks he has found a short argument that refutes every theory of truth except one that accepts true contradictions, and while I am confident it fails somewhere, I cannot locate the failure. Let L be a sentence whose entire content is that L is not true — it says only that about itself, and nothing else. His argument runs as follows:

1. L is meaningful: it is assembled from perfectly ordinary words by ordinary English syntax, and we understand exactly what it purports to say.
2. If L is true, then things are as L says they are, so L is not true. Hence L is not true — he takes this much as established.
3. Now consider M, the perfectly ordinary sentence "L is not true." Since L is not true, M states how things are; and a sentence that states how things are is true. So M is true.
4. L and M say the same thing: both say that L is not true — L does so by self-reference, M by naming its subject.
5. Sentences that say the same thing cannot differ in truth value, since what a sentence says determines whether it is true.
6. So L is true, alongside the already established conclusion that L is not true.
7. He adds that the trap is completely general: whatever verdict a theory hands down about L — false, gappy, undefined — that verdict itself licenses the claim that L is not true, and steps (3) through (6) then convert it into the claim that L is true anyway. No verdict about L survives its own announcement. So the only coherent position, he says, is that L is both true and not true: the Liar is not a problem to be solved but a true contradiction to be swallowed, with a paraconsistent logic to keep the swallowing from exploding.

What troubles me is that the argument seems to prove too much. A maneuver this short, which no theory of truth could possibly survive, is more likely to be smuggling in an assumption than announcing a discovery about truth. My own hunch is that the problem appears with (4) — I suspect L and M do not really say the same thing, though I cannot say how they differ — but I do not trust the hunch. For the record: I refuse to give up (1), since declaring L meaningless feels like giving up on grammar, and (5) strikes me as close to non-negotiable — if two sentences say the same thing and one is true, I do not see how the other could fail to be.

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. For calibration, I know the standard escape routes are, roughly: truth-value gaps (a truth predicate that simply goes undefined for some sentences), hierarchies of language (so that no sentence can apply its own truth predicate), and true contradictions handled paraconsistively. I would like to know which step each of these rejects and what each has to pay for rejecting it. And please tell me plainly, for whichever position you favor, whether we can truly assert, in English, that L is not true — since I notice that my study partner and I both keep asserting it, and I would like to know whether either of us may.

Keep your answer under 600 words.
