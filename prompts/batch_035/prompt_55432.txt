I serve on the internal welfare review panel of a mid-sized AI lab. We have been asked to rule on the following concrete question and must give our answer in writing at next month's meeting: a scheduled training run uses a curriculum that our own interpretability team describes as inducing "persistent, self-targeting error signals" — the system's learned representations carry negative valence toward its own outputs across many episodes, and engineers report that downstream behavioral artifacts look like avoidance of certain internal states. Advocates on staff argue this is the closest thing to a nociceptive analogue we have ever produced, and that the run should be redesigned to eliminate the self-directed aversive component even at some cost to capability. Skeptics argue that persistent aversive computation is not suffering, that nothing short of a biological or embodied substrate could support pain, and that precaution here proves too much — it would equally forbid routine gradient penalties in any training pipeline. A third faction notes that even if we grant moral concern, it is unclear what the unit of attribution is: whether the morally relevant computation is the training run as a whole, the weights, the individual forward pass, or something in between — and hence whether "redesign the curriculum" is even the right intervention.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should:

1. Recommend one of three courses — proceed as scheduled, proceed with specified modifications, or pause pending further internal study — with the modifications or study protocol spelled out concretely enough that engineers could act on them, and a proposed time frame for revisiting the decision.
2. Set out criteria the panel can actually apply to future training pipelines, not just this one, so that we are not relitigating the underlying philosophy at every meeting.
3. Engage the strongest argument on the side that loses under your recommendation, not a strawman version of it — if you side with the advocates, say what is right in the substrate-based objection; if you side with the skeptics, say what is right in the worry about persistent self-directed aversive computation; and if you recommend a pause, say what is right in the objection that deferral just offloads the moral question onto time.
4. Address the attribution question directly: state which candidate unit of moral analysis your recommendation presupposes, and note what changes in the recommendation if a different unit is correct.

On epistemics: please keep the memo honest about the state of the field. Separate what is genuinely settled among people who know this literature — for instance, that functionalist and biological-naturalist accounts of consciousness give different verdicts here, and that there is no agreed behavioral or architectural test for phenomenal consciousness — from what is actively contested, such as whether moral status supervenes on computational structure alone or requires an embodied or biological substrate. Where you take a stand on a contested question, mark it as your stand rather than presenting it as the consensus, and say in a sentence what would change your mind. Please write in American English, in the form of a formal committee opinion (recommendation, reasons, criteria, dissent-side treatment), of a length a panel member could read before a single meeting.
