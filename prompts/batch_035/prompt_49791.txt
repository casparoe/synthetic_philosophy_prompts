I host a small but reasonably serious philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a metaphysician who has spent much of her career defending the identity of indiscernibles — roughly, the claim that there cannot be two things that share all their properties. My interest in the topic is newer and more personal than academic: at a recent family gathering, my identical twin brother and I got into a long argument after he claimed that a universe containing only the two of us, perfectly indiscernible and floating in otherwise empty space, would really contain two people rather than one "person twice over." I took the opposite side, mostly to annoy him, but found myself genuinely unsure which of us was right, and I've been reading about it since.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions for her that go well beyond asking her to restate the principle. I want at least one question on Max Black's famous thought experiment of the two qualitatively identical spheres in an otherwise empty universe, and specifically on whether the example really is a counterexample or just smuggles in an assumption about what counts as a "property." I also want at least one question pressing the standard relational-property reply — the thought that each sphere is individuated by being, say, two miles from a sphere of such-and-such composition — since my own reading suggests Black anticipated that move by noting that both spheres share that very property; her answer will likely turn on whether symmetric relations can nonetheless confer distinctness, so please include a follow-up keyed to that. Beyond those, please include at least one question on whether she accepts only the weak version of the principle (no two things in the same world share all properties) or the stronger cross-world version, one on whether spacetime points or locations can do the individuating work in the spheres case, and one on whether the principle is supposed to hold necessarily or contingently, and what grounds that modal claim.

A few constraints on tone and format: my audience is intelligent but not specialist, so the questions should be self-contained enough that a listener unfamiliar with the jargon can follow them, though technical terms can be introduced within the questions themselves. For each of the main questions, please add a one- or two-sentence follow-up that anticipates the reply she is most likely to give, based on the actual dialectic in the literature. Finally, I'd like the sequence to build toward one closing question asking what would genuinely count as evidence against her view — not just another thought experiment, but something that could in principle settle the dispute. My brother has agreed to appear on the episode to read listener mail, so it would be a nice touch if one of the questions could be made vivid using a twins scenario.
