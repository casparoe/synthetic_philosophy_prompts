Before I teach the platonism–nominalism debate again, I want to stop doing what the field itself does: arguing over which theory is right before agreeing on what a theory here is supposed to accomplish. Platonists tend to press a semantic requirement — ordinary mathematical statements are literally true or false, and a theory earns credit for saying so without paraphrasing them into something else — while nominalists press an epistemological one: belief about a thing requires some possible causal or at least explanatory interaction with it, and abstract objects are, by stipulation, outside every causal order. Each side presents its requirement as simply what any adequate philosophy of mathematics must deliver, and I have come to suspect that much of the debate is a fight over which of these is a genuine desideratum and which is a camp prejudice dressed up as one. So write me the specification first.

Construct the list of desiderata an adequate account of the ontology and epistemology of mathematics must satisfy — adequacy being relative to two clients at once: the working mathematician, who needs her practice vindicated rather than corrected, and the metaphysician, who has scruples about what exists. For each desideratum, give the case that motivates it. The following should be treated, though I do not insist the list is exhaustive:

1. Face-value semantics. Ordinary mathematical claims — that there are infinitely many primes, that every vector space has a basis — must come out truth-apt, and the account of their truth or assertibility must be compositional enough to make embedded inference intelligible. Consider the bridge whose safety is said to depend on a theorem of stress analysis: if the theorem isn't truth-apt, what is the status of the conditional saying the bridge is safe if the theorem holds? A theory that treats mathematics as a game of symbols owes an explanation of why we can reason with mathematical content under negation, conditionalization, and embedding at all.

2. Identification. Benacerraf's 1965 problem: two children raised by set theorists learn arithmetic, one on von Neumann ordinals, the other on Zermelo numerals; they disagree about whether 3 is a member of 17, yet agree on every arithmetical fact. A theory must either say what the numbers are (and settle which reduction), or explain why no identification is needed — structuralism being the classic escape, which brings its own obligations, e.g., what is a structure, and what makes two instantiations instantiations of the same one?

3. Epistemic access. Benacerraf's 1973 dilemma: a uniform, Tarskian semantics for mathematical and empirical discourse pushes toward platonism, while a plausible epistemology of belief acquisition — on which knowing about a thing normally involves contact with it — pushes away. But is the epistemological horn a genuine desideratum, or an over-extension of a picture of knowledge built for perception? Take a position.

4. Applicability. Mathematics is not merely useful but seemingly indispensable to our best physical theories, and eerily effective at it. A theory must explain why mathematics applies to the world rather than merely record that it does — without explaining it away so thoroughly that it cannot explain why physicists keep reaching for heavier mathematics. Note that Field's Science Without Numbers program had to nominalize Newtonian gravitation to make its case; quantum field theory has not been nominalized. Is completing that project a requirement on a theory, or a research program a theory may decline to join?

5. Open questions. Set-theoretic statements independent of the accepted axioms — the continuum hypothesis being the canonical case. Must an adequate philosophy preserve the mathematician's sense that there is a fact of the matter about such statements, or may it tell her that her question is incomplete without further structure, whether new axioms or a multiverse of set theories? Whose demand is this — the working mathematician's or the philosopher's?

6. Consistency with practice. Proofs as the way mathematicians settle things; the pattern by which mathematical disagreement actually gets resolved; the fact that mathematicians speak and behave as though describing objects. A theory on which practitioners are systematically mistaken about the surface of their own practice owes a debunking explanation; a theory that merely reads metaphysics off that surface begs the question.

Then do three things with the list, which are the part I most want. First, mark each entry as non-negotiable or negotiable, and for each negotiable one, say whose interest it serves — diagnose which desiderata are genuine constraints on any theory and which are prejudices of one camp smuggled onto the spec as universals. Second, identify any subsets you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable. If, for instance, face-value semantics and epistemic access cannot be met together, then the list itself is a result: adequacy for both clients at once may be impossible, and the choice becomes which client to shortchange. Be explicit about whether any unsatisfiability you allege is proven, argued, or merely felt. Third, say which single desideratum excludes the most actual candidates, and why.

Close with a brief scorecard — no more than a short paragraph each — of mathematical platonism, Field-style nominalism, and Hellman's modal structuralism against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard is a check on the list, not the deliverable.
