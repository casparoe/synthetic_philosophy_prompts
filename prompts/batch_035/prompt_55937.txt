I'm a phd student in bioethics, working on decision-making capacity in dementia, and there's a move I keep hitting in the clinical ethics literature that I want to stress-test: advance directives are Ulysses contracts. You know the story -- Ulysses knows that when he hears the sirens he'll want to steer toward the rocks, so while he's still in his right mind he has himself tied to the mast, wax in the rowers' ears, and gives standing orders that no matter what he screams later, they keep sailing. People use this to argue that a directive is binding even when the patient, now demented and seemingly content, says she wants the antibiotics, wants to live: her current plea is the sirens' song, and the whole point of precommitment is that it wins against the later self's wishes. The rowers don't untie you when you beg.

But I have a nagging feeling the analogy is doing more work that it should. Ulysses before and during is the same guy with the same values -- he wants to get home alive, the song just temporarily hijacks his judgment. The demented patient seems more like a genuinely different person than the same person under a spell: her saying "I want to live" isn't a hijack, its arguably her real preference, formed in the only life she now has. If that's right, the analogy smuggles in exactly the thing that's in dispute.

Can you audit it properly? I want the correspondence table first (who's Ulysses, what's the mast, what's the song, who are the rowers, what is "getting home" supposed to be), then the places the mapping breaks -- especially the same-values point, and also that Ulysses never gets to sit down and talk with the rowers, whereas the later self can have an actual conversation with the clinicians about what she wants. Sort the disanalogies into ones that matter for the bindingness conclusion and ones that don't. Then tell me: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive on its own, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if there's a better analogy lying around (a will? a promise to a friend who's since changed? a founder's binding instructions to the company she started?), sketch it and say what it gets right that the mast story gets wrong.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning -- no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
