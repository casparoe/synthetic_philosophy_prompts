I convene a year-long seminar that has, for the past twelve months, hosted a standing argument between two members: a theoretical physicist who holds that philosophy has on balance been an obstacle to science, and a philosopher of science who holds that this judgment rests on a caricature. They have finally agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to commission the joint statement they would both sign. Draft that statement for me.

The physicist's position is roughly the one Steven Weinberg pressed in a well-known popular book from the early 1990s on the search for a final theory: that philosophy has done physics more harm than good, that physicists have repeatedly had to shake off philosophical fashions — positivist suspicion of unobservables, operationalist scruples hardening into dogma — and that when physics has moved forward, the credit belongs to mathematics and experiment rather than to any philosophy department. He is fond of the ornithology quip usually attributed to Feynman, and he endorses the opening declaration of Hawking and Mlodinow's 2010 book The Grand Design that philosophy has fallen behind and that scientists have carried off the deep questions for themselves. But he is also careful: he insists the statement record only claims he could defend in his own voice, not appeals to celebrity authority, and that his side's most quotable champions not be allowed to substitute for arguments.

The philosopher's position is that the skeptical story cherry-picks. Her view: it fixates on logical positivism at its most doctrinaire and ignores the philosophers who read physics closely — the decades of work clarifying the measurement problem, the exegesis of Bell-type results, the slow rehabilitation of interpreting quantum mechanics rather than merely shutting up and calculating. She also presses the historical side: the empiricist currents that shaped Einstein's generation cut in both directions, and the very questions the skeptics declare annexed by physics — why there is something rather than nothing, what makes a theory an explanation rather than a curve-fit — are philosophical questions whether or not the people answering them hold philosophy appointments.

Produce the document their collaboration should issue, with the following parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: about the historical record (the fortunes of atomic and molecular hypotheses in the decades before Einstein's work on Brownian motion, the role of instrumentalist attitudes in the early interpretation of quantum mechanics, and the border traffic between philosophy departments and quantum foundations since the 1990s), phrased so that both parties could put their names to each sentence without squirming.

2. A disagreements table: each remaining dispute stated twice — once in wording the physicist would accept as a fair statement of his own view, once in wording the philosopher would accept of hers. Somewhere in this section, the parties' implicit disagreement over the word useful — generating new theories, blocking bad ones, supplying interpretive vocabulary and standards, or providing intellectual caution — must be brought to the surface and sorted into disputes that are substantive and disputes that turn out, on inspection, to be merely verbal. Say which is which and why.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each, describe a concrete test, historical counterfactual, or line of argument that both parties agree would bear on it — for instance, a hypothetical history in which molecular reality is accepted a generation earlier without the empiricist scruples, or an accounting of what the post-1990 revival of quantum foundations owes to developments internal to physics versus pressure from philosophy — and record each party's prediction about the outcome in advance, before any judgment is made.

4. A closing section in which the collaboration is candid about its own fragility: which agreed claims are at risk of being vacuous, which formulations a real partisan would probably refuse to sign, and what development — in science, or in the practice of philosophy itself — would most plausibly change the collective mind, i.e., which single consideration, were it to emerge, would move one or both parties to switch sides.

The register should be that of a document intended for joint publication, not a debate transcript, and neither side should win: the value lies in the exactness of what remains after both have signed. Finally, append a short drafting note, clearly set off from the statement itself, in which you say where your own drafting is most likely to have failed: which formulations you suspect a committed partisan would balk at, where an agreed claim is secretly slanted toward one camp, and what would lead you to reclassify a disagreement between the verbal and substantive columns.
