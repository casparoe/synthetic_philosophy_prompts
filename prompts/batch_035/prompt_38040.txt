A friend and I spent the better part of a Saturday arguing about sentences that talk about their own truth, and I came away with a question I can't get out of my head, though I don't know what to call it. We started, as people do, with the sentence that says of itself that it is not true. She took the line that such a sentence is simply neither true nor false — a defective sentence, outside the range of meaningful verdicts. I noticed, and she grudgingly conceded, that I could then construct a new sentence that says of itself that it is neither true nor false, and her verdict applied to that new sentence seemed to make trouble again: if the new sentence is true, then it is neither true nor false; if it is neither true nor false, then it is false that it is neither. We went around like this for hours. Every time she proposed a diagnosis — the sentence is meaningless, it fails to express a proposition, it's not really a statement — I could build a sentence that applies that very diagnosis to itself, and the pattern seemed to come back, slightly reshaped but recognizably the same shape.

Here is what I'm after, and a bare label won't satisfy me. Is there an established name in the literature for this recurring pattern — the phenomenon whereby a proposed treatment of the problematic sentences, once you can formulate the treatment inside the language itself, regenerates the very problem it was meant to remove? If so, I'd like to know how the standard discussions carve it: is it one uniform phenomenon or a family of loosely related ones; how is it supposed to bear on the original choice of treatment (does it defeat every treatment, or only some); and what the currently live escape attempts even say about it — including the ones that restrict what the language can say about its own truth, which my friend vaguely recalls some logician proving something about in the 1930s, though neither of us could reconstruct the claim. I'd also like the edges of the concept: for instance, self-reference alone is clearly not the culprit, since a sentence that says of itself that it is true seems harmless, and truth is not the only predicate that misbehaves this way — a sentence saying of itself that it is unknown or unprovable appears to do something similar. If the pattern has no established name of its own, tell me so plainly, and then coin a term for it: give the definition, say what falls inside and outside it, and distinguish it from nearby concepts it might be confused with.

Throughout, please be candid about your confidence. Where you're reporting what the literature actually contains, mark how sure you are of the attribution or the standard reading; where you're carving the phenomenon yourself, say so. And single out the single point in your answer you're least sure of — my guess is it will be whether the regeneration trick I described is genuinely the same phenomenon across all the different treatments, or whether it only feels that way from the outside, and I'd like your honest verdict on that.
