I'm on the board of a mid-sized international humanitarian organization, and I've been asked to draft the decision memo for our worst governance headache in years. Our country director in a conflict zone paid roughly the equivalent of $40,000 in "logistics fees" to a militia that controlled the only bridge into a famine-affected region, after the militia had already turned back two convoys and people were dying in the camps the convoys were supplying. She didn't clear it with HQ and didn't tell us; we found out from a whistleblower six weeks later, after the trucks had gone through and local staff estimated the supplies reached tens of thousands of people who would otherwise have waited weeks more. She's been with us eleven years, is widely trusted by the local teams, and says plainly that she'd do it again. The complication: our anti-corruption policy is zero tolerance, no exceptions, and it's not just internal — a major institutional donor conditions its funding on it, and her home country's anti-bribery law reaches conduct abroad, so referring this to the authorities there is a live option the board has to consider, not a hypothetical.

The board is split in a way that maps almost embarrassingly well onto the dirty hands literature I've been rereading — one faction thinks she did the right thing and any punishment is perverse moralism (she got aid to starving people, what else matters), another thinks that if we don't fire her the zero tolerance policy is dead letter and every future bribe gets a humanitarian justification attached, and a third wants some middle path — censure plus removal from that posting but not dismissal — which everyone privately suspects is just the coward's way of not deciding. Two prior cases hang over this: a logistics officer we fired outright for pocketing fuel money, and a field team that paid a small "registration fee" to a local commander that HQ quietly wrote off as an unavoidable cost of doing business there. I keep coming back to that contrast — if the difference between her case and the fuel officer's is just that she wasn't lining her own pockets, haven't we effectively replaced zero tolerance with "don't steal for yourself"? And there's a deeper thing nagging me: dirty hands reasoning, the whole idea that you sometimes have to do wrong in order to do right, always seems to be about an individual agent bearing guilt. What does it even mean for a board of twelve people to authorize or refuse to authorize that? Does our ratifying her decision launder it, spread the stain, or just reveal that the policy was always going to bend?

I lean toward keeping her but I honestly don't trust my own lean here, which is why I'm outsourcing the spine. Write the memo the board will circulate: one concrete bottom-line recommendation (dismissal, referral to authorities, censure with conditions, full exoneration, whatever you actually think is right), with the reasoning laid out so the losing factions could respond to it rather than just feel overruled. I want you to engage the strongest version of the side you reject, not the strawman version, and to say explicitly what your recommendation costs us — where the policy or the precedent has to bend and what we're accepting when it does. Don't hand me a balanced "options with pros and cons" document; I can get that from the secretariat. Take a position and defend it all the way down to the case comparisons, including the fuel officer one, since that's the one that keeps bugging me.
