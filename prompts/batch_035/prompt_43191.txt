I am a high school junior in an elective on philosophy of mind, and our class is producing a recorded interview series in which we question a defender of an unusual position. My guest, whom I will call the defender, holds the view set out in Hofstadter's dialogue "Prelude... Ant Fugue" from Gödel, Escher, Bach: that an ant colony can literally be intelligent, and even hold conversations, while no individual ant is intelligent at all. On this view the ants are like neurons, and the mind, if there is one, belongs to the colony as a whole. In the dialogue the character of the Anteater chats amiably with one such colony, Aunt Hillary, even though, as I recall, he also eats ants; if I have misremembered details like that, the questions should be adjusted or the misreading flagged rather than silently built upon.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions for this defender, in the order I should ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. The questions must press on the actual content of the position, not invite him to restate it. Specifically, I need at least one question on each of the following, and you may distribute the rest as you see fit: the disanalogy between ants and neurons, since ants wander between functional teams, die, and get replaced, while the colony supposedly remains the same thinker; whether the view is a testable scientific claim about what colonies do or a metaphysical claim about where minds are located, and what observation could bear on it; whether the defender claims the colony has experiences or only that it solves problems, and what work the word "understanding" is doing when predicated of a colony; whether, if organization rather than material is what matters, the same argument makes a market, a city, or a corporation a candidate mind, and where he would draw the line and why; and whether the defender's own view, extended to humans, implies that we are just colonies of neurons, and what that does to the moral status we assume for ourselves and possibly for Aunt Hillary.

A few constraints on what I want from you. Write the questions so they anticipate the standard replies — that the level of description with the intelligence is simply the one at which interesting patterns recur, that neurons are also replaceable, that the claim is about organization and not about experiences — and so that the follow-up makes those replies harder to sustain rather than easier. Avoid questions about his biography or how he came to the view.

Finally, after the questions, please add a short note telling me two things: which of these questions rests most heavily on my possibly imperfect reading of the Hofstadter dialogue, and which question you think the defender can answer best and which will give him the most trouble, along with what consideration would most likely change your own judgment about that. I do not want confidence theater; where you are unsure whether a point I have attributed to the dialogue is really there, say so, and tell me what in your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
