Every month my reading group does a session where one of us defends an argument we half-believe and everyone else tries to break it. This one is mine and I've stopped being able to see where it fails. (1) A person is blameworthy only for what was under their control. (2) Once the drunk driver decides to drive, whether he kills a kid depends only on whether a ball rolls into the street — not on him. (3) Two people who differ only in factors outside their control cannot differ in blameworthiness. (4) So the driver who kills and the driver who gets home untouched are exactly equally blameworthy, and a court that sentences them differently is punishing luck, not wickedness. (5) So sentencing should track the attempt, not the outcome — punish the botched crime exactly like the completed one (I'm told at least one European legal system already does roughly this, so (5) isn't a fantasy). So far I think I believe all of this. But push the same lever one more time and it seems to prove way too much: whether you're the kind of person who decides to drive drunk at all — temperament, upbringing, the whole shape of you — wasn't under your control either. So (1) plus that fact appears to wipe out blameworthiness entirely, which I don't believe, and my colleague says that's a clean reductio of the whole argument. My hunch is that "control" means something different in (2) than it does in the constitutive-luck step, but I can't make the two readings precise, and if there's no equivocation then some premise has to go and I'd rather it not be (1). So: where does it slip, if it slips? And if it doesn't slip, which step do I give up and what does giving it up cost me? There's also a side problem I keep stubbing my toe on: the same reasoning seems to imply we owe the failed rescuer exactly the gratitude we owe the successful one, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a cost of the view or a feature of it.
