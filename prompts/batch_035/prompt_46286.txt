I'm a PhD student in bioethics working on a dissertation chapter about the concept of disease, and I want your help diagnosing a debate rather than adjudicating it. In our philosophy of medicine reading group we've spent two terms on the naturalism versus normativism dispute: the naturalist line, associated with Boorse's biostatistical theory, that health is normal functioning of parts and disease is statistically subnormal part-function relative to the species' design, versus the normativist line, associated with people like Nordenfelt, that disease judgments are ineliminably evaluative — that a condition only counts as a disease against a background of goals or harms that we care about. We've also read the hybrid attempts, like Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, which tries to require both a biological dysfunction and harm judged by social values. The frustrating thing is that everyone in the room agrees on appendicitis, measles, and a broken femur; everyone also agrees on conditions where the fight actually happens — moderate depression, grief, short stature, age-related cognitive decline, congenital deafness when the Deaf community itself resists the label. And on exactly those cases, nobody has moved an inch in decades. The literature keeps regenerating the same three or four moves on each side like a fixed game.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to treat as a hypothesis and not let me get away with, is that a good chunk of the dispute is verbal: the naturalists are answering something like a question about the explanatory category that biomedical science organizes itself around, while the normativists are answering a question about which category should gate access to the sick role, treatment, insurance, and excuse from obligations. On that reading the two "theories of disease" aren't competitors but partial precisifications of an ill-posed question, and the hybrid views look less like a synthesis and more like an admission that we've been packing two concepts into one word. But I can see problems with my own diagnosis: the naturalists don't seem to be merely stipulating a technical term, since they claim to be analyzing the ordinary concept and to get the contested cases "right" by ordinary standards; and the normativists don't seem merely to be doing social ethics, since they make claims about what doctors are actually tracking when they diagnose. There may also be a hidden empirical or conceptual question underneath — about whether "species-typical functioning" is itself value-laden through evolutionary teleology, or about whether the contested cases share anything real at all.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of medicine who has watched this debate for a long time and is a bit impatient with it. Think carefully, and before you commit to a verdict, do a quick brainstorm of the possible diagnoses — verbal dispute, different questions under one heading, substantive disagreement about a real category, disagreement about which cases count as data versus as noise, or bedrock starting intuitions (about naturalness, about suffering, about medical authority) that neither side can argue for without circularity. Then give me your actual diagnosis with reasons, drawing on what the respective sides actually say, and be honest about whether it supports my verbal-dispute hypothesis. Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that would dissolve the question, an empirical finding that would settle some load-bearing sub-question, a change in how we carve up the cases, or an argument that we should expect no progress and why. If your view is that my hypothesis is wrong, say so plainly — I'd rather find out now than at my committee meeting.
