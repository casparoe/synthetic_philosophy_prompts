Over the past few months I've been keeping notes on my gut reactions to cases of taking someone's word on a moral question, and I'd like help extracting the principle behind them, if there is one. Here are my verdicts, with a couple of sentences of context each.

1. My doctor tells me that a fetus at eight weeks has nothing like the neural machinery for pain. I take her word and let that shape my view of early abortion. Fine — that's factual testimony with a moral upshot, no different from asking a geologist about a fault line before building on the slope.

2. A colleague I trust, who has spent a decade on animal ethics, tells me that eating factory-farmed pork is wrong. I quit on her word, without reading the arguments. Not fine — or not quite fine. I'd be a kind of moral parasite: right behavior, borrowed conviction. It bothers me that I couldn't defend the view if pressed.

3. My grandmother, who has known me and my brother our whole lives, tells me I was wrong not to invite him to the wedding. I accept it and apologize. Completely fine — and I can't articulate why without noticing how different it feels from case 2, though both involve a trusted person delivering a moral verdict about my own conduct.

4. Jury duty. I was distracted through stretches of the trial. In deliberation, the other eleven are unanimous and visibly moved by testimony I only half-heard. Going along to avoid a hung jury: not fine, and the more unanimous the room, the less fine it somehow feels.

5. My oldest friend tells me a mutual friend betrayed her. I don't ask the other side — I take her word and quietly cut the other person off. Fine. It's what loyalty demands; demanding proof would be its own betrayal.

6. After a single lecture by an admired visiting professor, I adopt his position on the ethics of inherited wealth as my own and argue it at dinner. Not fine — though in retrospect I can't tell whether what embarrasses me is the deference or the zeal it came with.

7. I poll my group chat about whether ghosting someone was wrong and go with the majority. Not fine, and I knew it even while I was typing.

My tentative principle is something like: taking someone's word is fine where the testimony transmits a competence I lack and couldn't feasibly acquire, and not fine where forming the judgment myself is part of the point of the judgment. But I can see this doesn't cover case 3, where I was perfectly capable of judging for myself, and case 5 worries me because it looks structurally like case 4 — a verdict accepted without checking, from a source I'm partial to — yet my verdicts go opposite ways. So please do the following: test my tentative principle against the seven cases, repair it or replace it, state the result precisely enough that it delivers determinate verdicts on new cases, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best available principle cannot save, with a recommendation about which should be overturned. Then apply the principle to a case I haven't listed: suppose a friend asks me whether it would be wrong to cut her aging parents out of her life, and I give her a confident answer, which she then acts on. Is she doing what I did with the pork, or what I did with my grandmother — and what does your principle say should mark the difference, if anything does?
