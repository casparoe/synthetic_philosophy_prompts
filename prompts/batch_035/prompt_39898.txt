I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book where one chapter is basically a riff on the old bald present king of France bit — a country that has no king anymore but where people keep talking about him like he's real. Anyway, my friend D, who did a philosophy degree and never lets anyone forget it, read the chapter and gave me this whole lecture about Russell afterward. The way he tells it: Russell proved that whenever you say the X, you're secretly making three claims at once — that an X exists, that there's only one X, and that it's whatever you say it is. And since my fictional country has no king, every sentence my narrator says about the king is flat-out false, which apparently means my whole novel is just "a sequence of false assertions," his words. He also threw in that some later guy named Strawson totally demolished Russell on this so philosophers all agree the theory is dead now, and that Russell's real agenda was proving ordinary language is confused and everyone should write like logicians.

Something about this felt off to me — like, if the sentences are just false, why do readers understand them perfectly and even get sad when the king dies in chapter nine? But I honestly can't tell which parts of D's story are real philosophy and which are him showing off for the MFA crowd. Can you go through it piece by piece? Which bits would a Russellian actually endorse, where did he overreach or garble things, and does the your-novel-is-a-bunch-of-falsehoods conclusion actually follow from whatever the correct part of the theory is? Also, a practical question for the book itself: if the theory were right as D tells it, would it say anything genuinely interesting about how fiction works, or is it basically orthogonal to what a novelist is doing? I'm half tempted to put D's rant in the novel as a character, so it'd be good to know whether its the kind of thing that would make an actual philosopher wince or just nod along.
