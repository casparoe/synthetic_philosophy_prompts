I hold a fairly strong underdetermination thesis, and I want to know what would actually bring it down. Let me state it precisely, because the strength matters. First, it is global: for any theory and any body of evidence, there exists a rival incompatible with the theory but empirically equivalent to it relative to that evidence. Second, it is permanent: the equivalence is not an artifact of our current evidence that further observations could break; the rival can be constructed to match the theory on all possible observations, not just the actual ones. Third, and this is the part I care most about, it is evidential: I claim that nothing non-question-begging can rationally break the tie between such rivals. The usual escape routes — simplicity, unification, explanatory power — seem to me either aesthetic preferences dressed up as epistemic reasons or covert appeals to an inductive track record that the rival can share.

I've also worked out what I take to be the strongest formal support, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than treating it as a flourish. If two theories are empirically equivalent in my strong sense, they assign the same probabilities to all possible evidence, so the likelihood ratio between them is 1 for anything we could ever observe. Under Bayesian conditioning, the posterior odds equal the prior odds forever: shared evidence can never separate them. So if you insist that priors favoring one theory over the other constitute a genuine epistemic asymmetry, you seem to be admitting that some knowledge is prior to all possible experience — or you are admitting that "evidence" includes something beyond the predicted observations, which is exactly what I deny.

Tell me what would count against this, in the form of a ranked map of its exposure. I want: (1) formal defeaters — results or arguments showing that empirical equivalence does not entail evidential equivalence, for instance the observation that two theories compatible with the same evidence can still differ in how probable they render that evidence, so confirmation relations can separate rivals that observation cannot; (2) historical defeaters — actual cases where a tie between empirically equivalent rivals was broken in a way that seems principled rather than sociological in hindsight; (3) conceptual defeaters — problems with my notion of "possible evidence," or with the claim that matching on all possible observations forces matching on probability assignments; and (4) what defenders of this thesis — Duhemians, Quineans, and their successors — would themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only critics say. Where would a sympathetic defender say I have overstated: is the global version really defensible, or only the local and transient one?

Two further requests. First, assess honestly whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability — my fallback that any choice among rivals is "merely pragmatic" makes me suspect I have built a position immune to disconfirmation — and whether that immunity is a fatal flaw for a thesis of this kind or simply appropriate for a claim about the structure of evidential support. Second, say what would change your mind about your own ranking of the defeaters: which argument, if it turned out to fail, would most shift your verdict, and where in your reasoning do you think you are most likely to have gone wrong?
