I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a longform piece on what happens to moral arithmetic if the universe is infinite. Before I go further, I'd be grateful for your comments on the draft below — it's about 450 words, and the argument is only half-formed, so I'm more interested in conceptual corrections than line edits. The piece is intended for educated general readers, not philosophers.

The draft:

"Last month I reported on the tightened measurements of spatial curvature, and the quietly vertiginous corollary buried in the press materials: if the universe is exactly flat, then it is probably infinite, and if it is infinite, then somewhere beyond our horizon there are infinitely many galaxies, and on some of them, presumably, infinitely many lives. Cosmologists treat this as a technical footnote. Moral philosophy has not caught up.

Here is the problem in one sentence: our ethics is additive, and addition doesn't work on infinity. The moral theories most of us quietly rely on — the ones that say a world with more flourishing and less suffering is better — work by summing. Faced with a finite collection of lives, you add up the welfare. But there is no total of an infinite collection that contains both good and bad; the sum is not some inconceivably large number, it is undefined, as undefined as the sum of all the integers. So a universe of infinitely many flourishing lives and a universe of infinitely many tormented ones receive the same report card: N/A.

The standard illustration involves an infinite hotel. Take it as moral rather than mathematical. Every room is occupied by someone in torment. You renovate the room at the end of the corridor: its occupant moves next door, doubling up, and a blissfully happy person moves in. The hotel now contains one more happy person than before and no fewer tormented ones — yet cardinality, the only arithmetic infinity permits, reports no change. We cannot say the hotel is better. We also refuse to say it isn't.

And there is a stranger consequence. If the universe is infinite and roughly uniform, then somewhere, at some vast distance, every arrangement of matter occurs — which would seem to mean that a copy of every person you might save, or destroy, already exists somewhere. Saving a life becomes a local reformatting of an infinite document.

Perhaps the right response is to treat infinity not as a fact we will ever have to act on but as a boundary condition — a limit our theories approach without ever standing on it. We do our moral arithmetic in the finite suburbs, and let the infinite city look after itself."

Specific questions:

1. Have I stated the aggregation problem correctly? In particular, is the line "the sum is undefined, as undefined as the sum of all the integers" defensible? My worry is that readers will take "undefined" for "very large," and I want the sentence to do real work.

2. Does the hotel example earn its keep, or does it import mathematical machinery (shuffling guests between rooms) that distracts from the moral point? I adapted it freely, so if the adaptation breaks something, I'd rather know now.

3. The paragraph claiming that in an infinite uniform universe "every arrangement of matter occurs" — I have a feeling this is not entailed by homogeneity alone and needs some stronger assumption about randomness or typicality that I can't articulate. Is it true, and if not, is there a defensible version of it, or should the paragraph go?

4. Is the closing move — treating infinity as a boundary condition and confining ourselves to finite decision contexts — a genuine philosophical position or a rhetorical shrug? I can't tell whether I've found a resolution or merely changed the subject, and I don't want to publish a dodge dressed as an answer.

5. Finally: is any of this interesting to someone who already knows the philosophy, or have I reinvented the most basic version of a well-worn problem? If the latter, tell me where the live debates are, since I'd rather point readers at the open questions than at the settled ones.
