ok so I teach a course on decision making, more policy/business flavored than philosophy, and I've been telling students the sunk cost stuff for years while privately noticing my own actual views are kind of a mess. I want a consistency audit. here's roughly where I stand:

(1) The orthodox line: only forward-looking consequences matter, the ticket price is gone whether you attend or not, rational choice is prospective only. I beleive this, I mark down students for getting it wrong, and I roll my eyes at managers escalating commitment to dying projects.

(2) But I also think "finish what you start" is tracking something real. I bailed on a couple of things early in life and it did something to me that I can't cash out in future-terms — I became a person who bails, and that seems bad in itself, not bad because of reputation or any payoff down the line. I'd defend someone who keeps playing an instrument they'll never make a dime from partly *because* twelve years are already in it, and I don't want that reduced to "well, it builds character for later."

(3) I think promises bind even when keeping them is pointless by any forward-looking measure. If I promise a dying friend I'll visit her grave every year, and after she's gone noone alive could ever know or care whether I do, I still think I have to. The promise is a fact about the past that reaches forward and grabs me.

(4) Yet I consider reasoning like "we can't pull out of the war now, our soldiers died for this" to be among the worst reasoning there is, and I've said so publicly. Similarly I'm pretty suspicious of someone staying in a marriage mainly because twenty years are invested in it, though I'll admit I feel way less confident about the marriage case than the war case.

(5) One thing I want to flag: I do think there's a legitimate Bayesian angle — the fact that past me dumped huge resources into a project is evidence it was worth dumping resources into, so the sunk cost carries information. But I'm pretty sure that's NOT what I'm relying on in (2). I'd hold (2) even in cases where past me was provably an idiot when he made the investment.

So my question: is (1) actually compatible with the rest, or did I stop beleiving (1) a long time ago without noticing? (2) and (3) look like the same shape as the war and marriage cases in (4), and I have no story for why the first pair feels like integrity to me and the second pair feels like fallacy. Also tell me if (5) rescues anything or if it's just a side door back into (1). Go through the pairs, tell me which tensions are real and which dissolve on inspection, rank them by severity, and for the real ones say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me to accept it. Don't just say "philosophers disagree" — I want the actual audit.
