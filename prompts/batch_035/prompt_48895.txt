I do a small podcast on political philosophy — pretty niche audience, mostly grad students and insomniac lawyers. Next month I've got a guest who's spent her career defending the view that present-day states genuinely owe apologies for historical wrongs, not as PR or as a nice gesture, but as an actual duty: the state that exists today is somehow the same bearer of obligation as the state that did the wrong, even though every person involved is long dead. She's written a lot about apologies for slavery, colonial stuff, internment, forced sterilization programs, that kind of thing.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions, and please make them actually press on the content of her view rather than letting her do her standard talk. Specifically I want at least one question each on:

1. The persistence thing. What exactly survives from the wronging state to the apologizing state such that the latter owes the apology? She'll probably say something about the state as a continuing agent that inherited assets and debts like it inherits territory and treaties — so give me a follow-up keyed to that reply, maybe pressing on whether a state that's had a revolution, a new constitution, or mass immigration is still the "same" debtor in any sense that does the work she needs.

2. The non-identity problem, applied to the apologizing side rather than the usual victim side. Most citizens of the apologizing state exist *because* of the historical wrong (settlement, labor extraction, whatever changed who married whom). She'll likely reply that the duty attaches to the corporate agent, not to individuals — so the follow-up should test whether a duty that nobody alive inherits can still bind anyone.

3. Whose voice the "we" is speaking in when a state apologizes. Here's a wrinkle I want her to deal with that I haven't seen discussed much: in the hard cases a meaningful fraction of current citizens doesn't think the thing was wrong at all, or thinks it was regrettable but justified. Does an official apology spoken over their heads misdescribe the collective's actual attitude — is it an apology or a performance of one by the government with the citizens as set dressing? And if she bites the bullet and says the apology is valid regardless of what citizens believe, ask her whether that hollows out the sincerity condition we'd demand from an individual apologizer.

4. Cheap talk. Every government, university, and cereal brand does acknowledgment-of-country style gestures now, and my suspicion — push on this — is that apologies for universally-condemned wrongs have become the moral equivalent of a participation trophy: low cost, high polish. The sharp version: is apologizing for something no one defends anymore even hard? Follow up on whether the duty scales with how contested the wrong still is, which would mean states owe the *strongest* apologies precisely where they're least able to give them.

5. The promissory note problem, which is my own hobbyhorse so tell me if it's unfair: an apology works like a promise — it raises expectations of repair, restitution, at least changed behavior. If that's right, a state gets maximum moral credit per unit of cost by apologizing for wrongs where material follow-through is structurally least likely (the victims are dead, the group is dispersed, the statute of limitations has any plausible reading you like). Is there a rational-course-of-conduct worry here, and if so what blocks it — does her view have resources for saying an apology that strategically outruns its follow-through is worse than no apology?

6. Wrongs to groups that no longer exist as groups. Extinct peoples, dissolved communities, stateless populations absorbed into other states. Who's the apology for and who can accept it — and does an unaccepted or unacceptable apology discharge anything?

Also throw in one or two more wherever you see a soft spot in her position.

One big ask about how you frame the questions: separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on collective responsibility. Like, I gather there's a real live debate about whether collectives can bear duties in their own right versus only via their members, and another one about whether historical injustice creates obligations in descendants at all — and I don't want a question that smuggles in one side of that as if it were the consensus and then have her (rightly) eat me alive on the premise. So when you draft each question, add a one-line note flagging whether it presupposes anything that's actually up for grabs in the literature, and if it does, phrase the question so she can go after the presupposition itself if she wants. If some of my premises above are themselves tendentious — the promissory-note thing especially — say so bluntly, I can take it.
