Question 4 (20 points). A Bayesian decision maker faces a one-shot choice from a finite set of acts A. The state θ is drawn from a finite set Θ according to her prior p, and her utility from act a in state θ is u(a, θ); she is an expected-utility maximizer. Before choosing an act, she may observe the outcome of a free experiment E: a random message m drawn from a finite set M, with likelihoods p(m | θ) known to her. On seeing m she updates by Bayes's rule and then chooses an act.

(a) [6 points] Define the value of the experiment, V(E), and state Good's theorem concerning it.

(b) [8 points] Prove the theorem.

(c) [6 points] Answer briefly: (i) If running the experiment costs c > 0, when should she run it? (ii) Now suppose the setting is strategic: before any choices are made, she decides whether to run E; a second player observes whether she ran it (but not, if she did, its outcome); that player then chooses a response; she chooses her act last; and payoffs depend on both players' choices and the state. Does Good's theorem extend to this setting? Justify briefly.

The student's answer, as written:

(a) Let a₀ be the prior-optimal act, i.e., the act maximizing E[u(a, θ)] under the prior p. If she runs E and sees the message m, her posterior is p(· | m), and she chooses a(m) to maximize expected utility under that posterior. The value of the experiment is V(E) = E_m[ max_a E[u(a, θ) | m] ] − max_a E[u(a, θ)], where the outer expectation averages over messages, using the prior and the likelihoods. Good's theorem: V(E) ≥ 0; a free experiment can never have negative value for an expected-utility maximizer.

(b) Fix any message m. The act a(m) she chooses on seeing m maximizes expected utility under the posterior, so it does at least as well as sticking with the prior-optimal act a₀ would have, evaluated under that same posterior: E[u(a(m), θ) | m] ≥ E[u(a₀, θ) | m]. Averaging over m and applying the law of iterated expectations to the right-hand side yields V(E) ≥ 0. The intuition is that the informed agent can always fall back on a₀, so extra information can only help. Note that the result is stronger than the averaging suggests: conditional on any particular message m, her expected utility, evaluated with her updated beliefs, is still at least as high as the expected utility she had before running the experiment. So she can never regret having looked — a message may be surprising, but it can never be bad news for her. Also, the inequality is strict whenever the experiment is informative, that is, whenever the distribution of messages depends on the state; a completely uninformative experiment, whose messages are independent of θ, is the only case with V(E) = 0.

(c)(i) She should run the experiment iff V(E) ≥ c. The gross value is unchanged by the fee, and since Good's theorem gives V(E) ≥ 0, she is never worse off for having run it, apart from paying c.

(ii) Yes, the theorem extends. She can always disregard the message and do whatever she would have done without it, so having the experiment available is a free option; and since the opponent conditions only on her observable action, not on what she knows, Good's argument goes through verbatim. In a game, as in a decision problem, more information can never hurt a rational agent.
