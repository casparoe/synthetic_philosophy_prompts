Grading take-home midterms for my intro course on well-being and the good life, and I'd love a second pair of eyes on one answer before I commit to a number. It's a 30-person class, mostly sophomores, no philosophy background assumed, so the bar is "accurate on the core argument, shows some judgment about what the argument does and doesn't establish." The question was:

"Nozick's experience machine is meant to be an argument against hedonism about well-being. Explain the argument, say carefully what it does and does not show, and then present and evaluate what you take to be the strongest hedonist reply to it."

And here is the student's answer, typed out more or less as submitted:

"Nozick asks us to imagine a machine that could give you any experience you wanted while you floated in a tank with electrodes attached to your brain. You could have the experience of writing a great novel, or of deep friendships, or anything else you choose, and you'd have no way of knowing you were in the machine, since it would give you the experience of having chosen to plug in. The argument against hedonism is this: if hedonism were true, plugging in would be the best thing you could do for yourself, since the machine guarantees maximally pleasurable experiences. But almost nobody would plug in for good, and we think someone who did would be making a terrible mistake. So experiences are not the only thing that matters, and hedonism is false.

The argument actually refutes more than hedonism, though. It refutes any theory that locates well-being in mental states, since the machine gives you the best possible mental states and it still seems like you'd be losing something. Desire theories go down with it too, because having your desires satisfied merely in experience clearly isn't enough. What the machine really shows is that we care about being in actual contact with reality, about doing things rather than merely having the experience of doing them, and about being a certain kind of person. That's why pleasure turns out to be at best a small component of the good life.

The strongest hedonist reply is that refusing the machine doesn't show plugging in would be worse for you. Maybe you stay out because you have duties to family and friends that outweigh your own well-being -- those are moral reasons, not evidence about what's good for you -- so the machine conflates reasons with prudential reasons. But this reply fails, because we don't just feel we ought to stay out; we feel we'd be losing something for ourselves, and the hedonist has to explain that away. A second reply is that over time the experiences would feel hollow, since deep down you'd know something was off. But Nozick could just stipulate that away, and anyway that concedes hedonism can't handle machine-grade pleasures. So the argument is basically sound: hedonism is false, and the good life requires actual contact with the world."
