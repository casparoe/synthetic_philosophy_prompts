I'm preparing to read the literature on letterform identity — the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' across the wild variety of shapes that carry that name — and I want to work out my demands before I start, rather than after. My background is half typographic design and half cognitive science, and what I want is a theory that is adequate for both sides of the phenomenon: the recognition side (how a reader assigns the same identity to a blackletter 'a', a geometric sans 'a', and a four-year-old's scrawl, when those shapes share almost no geometry) and the generative side (what lets a designer draw a glyph for a letter that doesn't exist yet, in a style they've only seen a few samples of, such that readers immediately parse both the letter and the style). It's the second side that made me take this seriously: the fact that we can say "that's a Helvetica sort of letter, whatever letter it is" shows that letter identity and style are two independent dimensions of similarity that a theory has to keep apart, and I suspect most theories quietly collapse them.

So that I'm not just gesturing, here are the cases and considerations I expect any adequate theory to answer to, though I don't want to prejudge which are genuine requirements and which are reflexes of one research camp: the double-storey and single-storey 'a' (and the single- and double-decker 'g') bear the same name while differing drastically, whereas a sans-serif lowercase 'l', a capital 'I', and a numeral '1' can be nearly or exactly the same shape and bear three different identities; there are graded near-misses, where an 'a' with its crossbar drifting down gradually stops being an 'a' and becomes an 'o' with a tail, and the theory has to say where and why, without falling into the usual trouble that graded resemblance-to-exemplar views have with instances unlike any previously seen one; there are the well-known demonstrations where an ambiguous vertical stroke is read as an 'H' in one surrounding word and an 'A' in another, so recognition is partly top-down, and yet we can still read isolated letters off license plates and monograms, so it can't be wholly word-driven; there is the fact that a child learns the category from a handful of exposures and then generalizes to fonts never seen; and there is the fact, emphasized in Douglas Hofstadter's old work on this problem, that we instantly judge a brand-new typeface to be a coherent alphabet — twenty-six strangers that all seem to belong to one family — which suggests the category is something like a position in a system of contrasts rather than a shape.

Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of letterform identity has to deliver — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect (identity versus style, category membership versus typicality, letter versus the grapheme-sound it carries), which empirical facts about reading and letter learning it cannot contradict — with the case or consideration behind each. Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are prejudices of a particular camp (I'd include under that heading things like "must be statable as explicit rules" and its mirror, "must be learnable by gradient descent" — tell me if you disagree). Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see, since if some subset is unsatisfiable the list itself is the finding. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular contenders, and close with a scorecard of at most one sentence each for template matching, feature or stroke decomposition, and prototype or exemplar views.
