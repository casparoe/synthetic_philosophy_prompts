I studied math and work as a software engineer, so philosophy is a hobbyist interest for me and I have no training in the history of ethics. Over the holidays I read a popular history of the Bloomsbury Group — the book is well reviewed and generally careful — and one paragraph in it summarizes G. E. Moore's ethics in a way that mostly hangs together for me but that I can't evaluate on my own. Here is what the book says, condensed but close to its wording:

Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) demolished every previous attempt to define the good: "good," he argued, is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property, so the utilitarians' equation of good with pleasure was a definitional blunder. But having exposed the blunder, Moore quietly reinstalled pleasure at the top of his own system, because his recipe for the good life was essentially refined hedonism — higher pleasures, as Mill would have said, plus a Bloomsbury shopping list. "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may roughly be described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects." In other words, what matters is the feeling: the buzz of conversation with clever friends and the glow of contemplating a Cézanne — and the more intense the feeling, the better. And since each such pleasant state of mind is good on its own, the value of an evening among friends is simply the sum of the values of the individual feelings it contains. It is not hard to see why Keynes and Strachey took the book as a charter for putting art and friendship above Victorian convention.

Two things about this nag me, though I may be wrong. First, I had the impression that Moore's refusal to define "good" in terms of pleasure was supposed to be an attack on hedonism, not a prelude to reinstating it — so the phrase "quietly reinstalled pleasure" sounds like it might be the author's cleverness rather than Moore's position. Second, the claim that value adds up feeling-by-feeling sounds like exactly the kind of thing a philosopher would complicate, and I have no way to check whether Moore actually held it.

Could you audit this for me? Please go claim by claim and tell me which parts a Moore scholar would accept, which parts are simplifications a fair popularizer might make, and which parts Moore explicitly denied. Pay particular attention to how the author reads the sentence he quotes: does "the pleasures of human intercourse" mean what he takes it to mean, and does his gloss about intensity of feeling actually follow from the quotation? I'm also curious whether the final point — that the Bloomsbury set found in Moore a charter for their way of life — survives whatever corrections you'd make. I don't need a bibliography or citations; I just want an analysis I can trust, written so that someone at my level can follow it.
