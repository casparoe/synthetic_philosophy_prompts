At my book cub we were arguing about a novelist whose personal life turned out to be pretty awful (abusive to his family, say), and when I said that had changed how I read him, someone waived me off with "separate the art from the artist." Thing is, I realized I don't actually know what that slogan is claiming. Is it saying the artist's biography is factually irrelevant to the work's aesthetic value? Or that it's relevant but we should ignore it anyway? Or something about the work being autonomous once it exists — like it belongs to readers now and the author's biography is just noise? Or is it not a claim about the work at all, but advice about not letting your enjoyment be spoiled, which would be more of a coping tip than a philosophical thesis? And is there a reading where it only applies to the artist's biography and not to immorality in the work itself — like a racist character in the novel seems like a different beast than a racist author? Lay out the different things "separate the art from the artist" could actually mean, from the trivial to the substantive, and say for each whether it's plausible, defensible, or basically false, and — this is the part I actually need — which reading my book club guy was invoking when he used it to shut me down. Because some readings would support him and some wouldn't: if it's just "the quality of the prose doesn't depend on his biography," that doesn't even touch my point, which was that knowing the biography changed what the books are about for me. Give me a list I can bring back next month.
