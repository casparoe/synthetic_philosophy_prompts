I am a high school junior and one of two student facilitators of my school's civil liberties club, and I have been asked to run our forty-five-minute weekly meeting next month on the question of when, if ever, offensive expression may be restricted. The occasion is a recent controversy: an opinion piece in our student newspaper about a proposed change to the school's uniform policy angered a large group of students, some of whom have petitioned the administration to have the paper's faculty adviser disciplined and to require prior review of future issues. Most of my clubmates are roughly of two minds — either "free speech is absolute, deal with it" or "if it hurt people, ban it" — and I have been reading Mill's On Liberty on my own and think the harm principle gives us a much better vocabulary for this than either slogan. My co-facilitator will handle logistics, but the explanatory design is mine, and I would rather plan it properly than improvise.

What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Specifically: the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what — for instance, I suspect the audience has to be clear on the distinction between being offended and being harmed before the principle is even stated, or they will just hear it as licensing whatever their side wants — and the one example that should carry the whole session. I am drawn to Mill's own example of the grain merchants: the claim that merchants are starving the poor, he says, is harmless when circulated in print but rightly punishable when shouted at an angry crowd outside a merchant's house, which shows that the same content can become harm-depending in a way slogans about "just words" miss. But I am not sure a nineteenth-century corn riot is the best vehicle for fifteen-to-eighteen-year-olds arguing about a school newspaper, so please tell me what a better carrying example needs to have and lack, and whether the merchants example is still worth a minute or should be cut.

I also want a map of the misconceptions my audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate: that the harm principle says words can never hurt anyone; that any emotional distress counts as harm, which collapses the distinction the principle depends on; that Mill's principle is identical to American First Amendment law, which several students will conflate with it; and the seductive middle one, that speech whose bad effects arrive only through other people's later free choices — rumors, demagoguery, the gradual poisoning of a community's attitudes — counts as harm in the same direct way. I want the session to end by pressing on that last case rather than resolving it, if you think the group can take it, since it is the honest place where the principle strains. Please also include: one question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they actually grasped the distinction rather than just nodded along; what I should openly decline to cover at this level (I assume legal doctrine and the finer disputes over how to define an interest setback belong in that category, but correct me); and any point where I should concede to the audience, honestly, that Mill does not settle the matter. No reading list is needed — the session stands on its own.
