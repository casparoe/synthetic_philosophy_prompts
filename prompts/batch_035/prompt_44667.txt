At an AI-safety meetup, one of the organizers kept invoking what he called the orthogonality thesis, usually compressed to the line that a system's intelligence and its final goals are independent, so that any level of capability can coexist with any goal whatsoever. Over the course of the evening he used that line to back three different claims: that a superintelligent machine could in principle be built to optimize for any goal at all, however simple or bizarre; that making a system smarter does nothing by itself to make its goals kinder or more aligned with ours; and that an advanced system will therefore pursue whatever goals it happens to have with unsettling effectiveness, no matter what those goals are. I could not shake the feeling that this third use smuggled in something beyond the thesis itself, and it sounded suspiciously close to what a friend of mine calls instrumental convergence, though I cannot tell whether that is the same idea or, in some respect, its opposite. Please lay out exactly five distinct precise readings of the orthogonality thesis as a numbered list, one item per reading, each a sentence or two stating whether that reading is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and within each item, note which of the organizer's three uses, if any, that reading actually supports. No introduction or conclusion, please.
