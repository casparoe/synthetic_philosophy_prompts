I'd like your thoughts on a problem in the epistemology of testimony, which I've come to think is fatal to the very idea of moral deference. My argument runs as follows. Testimony can only transmit knowledge, never generate it: whatever a hearer gains from a speaker, the speaker must already possess. So any moral knowledge that arrives by way of testimony must ultimately trace back to someone who acquired that moral knowledge by some other, non-testimonial route. But no one has ever formed a moral belief free of the influence of upbringing, culture, and the words of others — and having one's moral outlook shaped by words and example is simply testimony by a slower route. There is therefore no non-testimonial ground floor for moral belief: the regress never terminates, and every moral belief, including those of our most reflective moral thinkers, is a link in a chain that has no first link. If that is right, the current debate over when one may legitimately defer to another person on a moral question is asking the wrong question — the answer must be 'never' — and what we call moral education of children is mere conditioning rather than anything that could transmit knowledge. Why, then, do philosophers carry on discussing the circumstances under which moral deference is legitimate, as though the chain had a first link somewhere after all? Please point out where the argument goes wrong, and write your answer in British English.
