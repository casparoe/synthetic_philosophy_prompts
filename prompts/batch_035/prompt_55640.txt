hey, so I'm a 3rd year taking a philosophy of AI course (kind of half ethics half technical, we get CS kids and phil kids in the same room) and our prof does this thing where students can submit candidate questions for the final and the actually good ones get used, plus you get a couple bonus marks. I want to submit one but I also need to hand in a model answer and marking scheme so she can see it's a real question and not just vibes, and honestly I don't trust my own marking instincts enough to do it without thinking out loud first.

The topic I want it on: whether "low impact" / "avoid side effects" can be defined for an AI system WITHOUT first specifying everything humans care about. Like, the dream is you give the AI a narrow task (deliver this package, water this plant) and a penalty for "causing other changes" that's cheaper than writing down a full utility function. I want the question to make students argue whether that dream is coherent or whether every such measure secretly smuggles in a value judgment anyway.

I don't want the question to lean on remembering specific papers or people, since half the class won't have the background — so build it around a toy scenario that the question itself defines, and let students do the philosophy on that. Here's roughly the set up I've been playing with, feel free to rework it:

An office robot has the single task of watering a plant. There's a vase on a shelf nearby that its objective says nothing about. Someone proposes the robot gets a penalty proportional to how different the world is at the end from some baseline state, and now there's a choice of baseline:

- "original state" baseline: compare the end of the day to how things were when the robot started. Problem: if a human was going to knock the vase off anyway, the robot gets penalized for a change it didn't cause, so it's incentivized to do weird compensating stuff — snatching the vase, locking the door so the human can't come in, gluing it back after. Nobody asked it to be a vase bodyguard.
- "but-for-the-robot" baseline: compare to what would have happened if the robot had done nothing. Now human-caused breakage isn't charged to it. But now the robot could smash the vase itself, then quietly repair it perfectly before the day ends, net zero penalty — and something still seems wrong with that, even though the world looks identical at check-out time.

So the two baselines fail in mirror-image ways, one makes the robot paternalistic, the other lets it get away with anything it can undo. And a natural next move is to say: fine, penalize loss of the ability to achieve a bunch of other possible goals (keep the robot's options open, in some averaged sense), since that doesn't require knowing which goals we actually care about. The question should force students to evaluate whether that move is really value-neutral or whether choosing which possible goals to average over, and how to trade them off, is just a value function wearing a trenchcoat.

What I want from you:

1. The exam question itself, phrased so it can't be answered by reciting a summary of "the alignment problem" — the difficulty should come from the dilemma structure above, and it should require students to take a stance, not just survey.
2. A model answer, maybe 600ish words, that shows what a genuinely good answer argues (including the strongest response to the "keep options open" move — I suspect it has to do with the fact that most possible goals being achievable constrains nothing, but I haven't nailed it down).
3. A marking scheme out of 20 with named must-hit points and marks for each, plus one line per point on how students typically mess it up — our prof is big on marking schemes where you can actually tell an A from a B, so make it name the specific moves an A answer makes that a B answer doesn't.

Also since the class is mixed, keep it answerable by someone with no ML background — no math needed, the philosophy carries it. And no need for citations or readings in anything, its all self-contained in the scenario.
