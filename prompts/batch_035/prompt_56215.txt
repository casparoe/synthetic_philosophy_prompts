I'm a philosophy professor preparing a graduate unit on personal identity, and I want to build a session around a tension I find my students underappreciate. On one side, there is Daniel Dennett's picture of the self as a "center of narrative gravity": the self is not a thing inside the mind but an abstract posit, an intentional-system-level construct held together by the stories we and others spin about a person, roughly as physicists posit a center of gravity to organize their predictions about a physical body. On the other side, there is Galen Strawson's attack on what he calls narrativity: his claim that a great deal of human self-experience is not narrative in structure at all, that some people are "Episodic" rather than "Diachronic," experiencing little or no felt continuity with their past and future selves, and that it is a moral and psychological mistake — not merely a descriptive one — to insist that a well-lived life must be organized into a story. My students tend to treat these as simply incompatible, but I suspect the relationship is subtler: Dennett's construct is supposed to be an explanatory posit, and it is at least conceivable that a posit can do its predictive work even for people whose inner lives contain no narrative thread.

For the session, I'd like you to compare the two positions carefully. Where, precisely, do they conflict — is it over the metaphysics of the self, over the phenomenology, over ethics, or over some combination? Where could they be reconciled: could an Episemic temperament, as Strawson describes it, still be the kind of creature around whom a center of narrative gravity gets posited by others, or does the absence of inner narrative threaten Dennett's story? And which of the two, in your judgment, comes closer to offering a complete account of selfhood — the unity of a person over time, the ownership of experience, and the sense in which a self can persist or dissolve? Please justify your answer rather than merely surveying the debate, and if you think the comparison turns on an ambiguity in what "narrative" is supposed to mean — say, storytelling as an activity versus narrativity as a structural feature of experience — flag that and explain its significance for my seminar.
