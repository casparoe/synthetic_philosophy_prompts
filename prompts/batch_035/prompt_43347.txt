i cover science and ideas for a magazine, and right now im reporting a piece on the postwar grudge informer cases — the people who denounced their neighbors and spouses to the nazi courts, and the west german judges in the late 40s trying to decide whether you could punish someone for doing what the law of their own state at the time invited them to do. while reporting it, a law professor friend (a big natural law guy, loves radbruch) walked me into an argument and now i cant find the exit:

(1) positivism says whether a rule counts as valid law depends on its sources — who enacted it, how — not on its merits. (2) the nazi-era statutes and decrees underpinning the denunciations were enacted through the officially accepted procedures of the german system, so on positivism they were valid law. (3) if those statutes were valid law, then the informers acted lawfully — they broke no law of the state they lived under. (4) if they acted lawfully, then convicting them after the war was punishment of lawful conduct, which is retroactive punishment. (5) banning retroactive punishment is pretty much the core of the rule of law — its the thing we prosecuted the war criminals FOR violating, isnt it. (6) so positivism, once you take the rule of law seriously, implies the grudge informer trials were themselves unjust. (7) but the trials were just, or at least overwhelmingly we feel they were. (8) so positivism is false.

he thinks this is a clean kill and keeps quoting that line about intolerable injustice not counting as law at me. my instinct is that "lawful" is quietly doing two different jobs somewhere around (3) and (4), but i cant nail it and he just smiles when i say that. which step should i reject, and what does rejecting it cost? fwi id rather keep (5) than anything else in the list — feels like the one thing everyone on both sides of this whole debate agrees on — so if you make me give it up, tell me exactly what im buying with it.
