For a 300-level course on ancient philosophy, I gave the following exam question:

"State the premises that generate the Achilles and the tortoise paradox, and assess the standard response that the paradox rests on conflating an infinite series with an infinite sum. Distinguish the mathematical point from any remaining metaphysical question, and say which of Zeno's other paradoxes, if any, the convergence response addresses."

Please grade the following student answer. Give it a letter grade, and then justify the grade with a point-by-point assessment: say what the answer gets right, identify each error or conflation, and distinguish genuine mistakes from passages that are merely loosely worded. Note anything a hasty grader would likely let slide. I am not looking for your own take on Zeno, only an evaluation of this answer against what a well-informed response would say.

The student wrote:

"Zeno's argument in the Achilles is that before Achilles can draw level with the tortoise he must reach the tortoise's starting point, by which time the tortoise has advanced, and so on without end. Since Achilles has infinitely many catch-up tasks to complete, he can never complete them all, and so never passes the tortoise. The premises are (1) that the gap between the runners can be divided without end, and (2) that an infinite collection of tasks cannot be completed.

The standard response, which goes back to Aristotle, is that the paradox confuses an infinite series with an infinite sum. The distances Achilles must cover — say 100 m, 10 m, 1 m, and so on — form a converging geometric series, and so do the corresponding intervals of time. Both sums are finite, so Achilles passes the tortoise at a definite time and place, and the paradox is dissolved. Aristotle saw this clearly: he argued that Zeno's demand for infinitely many divisions of the distance applies equally to the time, so the two infinities cancel out.

What the convergence response shows, however, is only where and when Achilles passes the tortoise, not that he gets there. The limit of the series is never reached at any finite stage of the sequence: each stage leaves a remainder, and the limit point is approached but never attained by any member of the series. So Zeno is right that the passing never occurs at any stage; the mathematics gives us a description of the endpoint, not a demonstration that the sequence of tasks is completed. To answer Zeno fully we would need the notion of a supertask — infinitely many tasks completed in a finite time — which Aristotle's distinction between potential and actual infinity was designed to block.

Finally, if space is not infinitely divisible, the first premise is simply false and the paradox never arises. Modern physics supports this: below the Planck length spacetime becomes discrete, so there are only finitely many catch-up steps. In that sense Zeno was on the right side in the end: motion is only possible in a discrete space."
