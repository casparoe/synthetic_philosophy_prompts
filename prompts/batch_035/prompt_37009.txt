I have no background in philosophy or economics, but I've spent a lot of time lately puzzling over something I later found out is sometimes called the paradox of voting: in a large election, the chance that my single vote changes the outcome is vanishingly small, so if I vote only to affect the result, the effort seems irrational — yet most of us feel that not voting is somehow wrong, or at least that voting is worthwhile.

While thinking about this on my own, I came up with a distinction I'd like you to engage with, though I don't know whether anyone else has put it this way. It seems to me there are two different things I might mean when I say my vote "matters". One is outcome-changing: my vote matters if, had I stayed home, the result would have gone differently. That is almost certainly false for me. But there is another sense — call it contributory — like being one of a thousand people who each toss a shovelful of snow to clear a blocked road, or one voice in a stadium roar. No single shovelful or single voice was strictly necessary, and yet each of us did contribute something to a real result. My question is whether voting can be rational in this second sense without being rational in the first, and if so, whether that's enough to justify the trip to the polling station — or whether the snow-shovelling analogy breaks down in some way I haven't noticed.

Please write an essay that weighs the main considerations on both sides: the straightforward "your vote almost never decides anything" reasoning, and the sorts of counterarguments people raise, such as appeals to civic duty, to the bad consequences if everyone reasoned this way, or to the idea that voting expresses something rather than achieves something. Feel free to bring in my outcome-changing versus contributory distinction and press on it, since I'm honestly not sure it holds up.

Please do reach a conclusion and defend it — don't end by saying it's a matter of personal values. At the same time, I'd ask you to be careful to separate what is settled among people who study this issue (for instance, the probability arithmetic itself) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, what the correct account of rational action is, or whether there really is a duty to vote), and not to present a contested position as if it were the consensus. Please write in British English.
