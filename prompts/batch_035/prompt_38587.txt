My sister is going through IVF, and at a family dinner last weekend two friends of hers — a pediatrician named Marcus and a hospice chaplain named Dana — spent an hour arguing about a decision her doctor had put to her. Of several viable embryos, two stood out: one carries a markedly lower genetic risk of early-onset diabetes, and the clinic suggested, without pressure, that this could weigh in the choice of which embryo to transfer. No one at the table disputed that transferring the lower-risk embryo was permissible; the fight was over what, if anything, makes it the right choice, and how far the reasoning extends. I stayed out of it, but the exchange stayed with me, and I'd like an outside verdict on it. The argument went through five numbered moves, and I've tried to record them as they actually happened: (1) Marcus said that if the parents pass over the lower-risk embryo while it is available, that is not neutrality but a decision — deliberately bringing about a child with a foreseeable, avoidable burden is continuous with withholding a medicine from a sick child, and we would rightly condemn the parent who did that. (2) Dana answered that the comparison fails at the crucial point: medicine helps a patient who already exists and already has interests, whereas embryo selection decides which future person exists at all. The unchosen embryo is not a sick child denied treatment; it is no one, never anyone. And the chosen child is not helped by the choice either, since the alternative open to her was never "a harder life" but "no life at all" — so the choice can't be explained as benefiting her, and Marcus's whole moral picture collapses once that's clear. (3) Marcus conceded that he couldn't tell a story in which the selected child is a beneficiary, but said this only shows the reason isn't a benefit owed to anyone: it is simply better that the child who comes to exist be one with fewer health problems, in the same impersonal way it's better that the seed you plant be the one likelier to flourish, and since the choice wrongs no one, what could count against it? (4) Dana said she could count two things against it. First, the standard of "better" doesn't stay confined to disease: once selection is on, the same logic favors taller, smarter, better-looking children, and the norm quietly becomes the optimal child — which is eugenics with better table manners, the difference being only that this time it's individual parents holding the spec sheet rather than the state. Second, and more important to her, a child you chose partly for satisfying a spec is partly your project rather than a person you receive, and unconditional acceptance — the thing that makes parenthood a relation between persons rather than production — cannot survive that shift. (5) Marcus replied that every parent already shapes children after birth — schooling, music lessons, athletic training — and no one calls that eugenics; the only difference in this case is timing, and if timing alone turns ordinary parenting into a eugenic project, then the objection proves too much.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with your reasons. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether there is something both of them missed — in particular, whether the "it's just earlier parenting" point in (5) and the "designed child" worry in (4) can really both be about the same difference. Please answer in American English.
