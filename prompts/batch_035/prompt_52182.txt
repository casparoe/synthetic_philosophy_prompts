I teach a third-year module on philosophy of cognitive science, and this term, in the seminar on Dennett's tower of generate-and-test, I have been careful to lay out the four storeys: Darwinian creatures, whose design improves only when variant organisms are discarded by the world; Skinnerian creatures, which run variation-and-selection within a lifetime, reinforcement preserving the behaviours that pay; Popperian creatures, which test candidate actions in an inner rehearsal, so that hypotheses die in their stead rather than the creature; and Gregorian creatures, which import mind-tools — words, maps, notations — that enlarge and sharpen the space of hypotheses available for testing.

A student of mine has submitted an essay arguing that the tower is a fraud, and I find myself unable to say, cleanly, where the argument fails, even though its conclusion offends me. Set out in her words as nearly as I can render them:

(1) Every improvement the tower describes is an instance of one process: variants are generated, and something — the gene line, the schedule of reinforcement, the inner rehearsal, the external notation — preserves the useful ones and discards the rest.
(2) What distinguishes each higher storey from the one below is only this: where the cycle runs (outside the creature, in its overt behaviour, in its head, or in its tool-kit), what a wasted trial costs, and how many variants the space of candidates contains.
(3) Where a cycle runs, what a failed trial costs, and how many variants there are, are differences of place, price, and quantity.
(4) No difference of place, price, or quantity marks a joint in nature; joints are marked only by differences of kind.
(5) So the tower does not distinguish kinds of minds: the fourfold taxonomy is an artefact of where Dennett found it convenient to stop.
(6) She therefore proposes replacing the tower with a single graded parameter, which she calls trial density — the expected number of generate-and-test cycles a system can run per unit of damage a failed cycle would cause — and claims this captures everything the tower did, with less metaphysics and no arbitrary divisions.
(7) She adds, with some glee, that the argument equally dissolves the difference between a sieve sorting gravel and a scientist testing hypotheses, since those too differ only in place, price, and quantity of trials — and that if we recoil from this, our recoil is evidence only that we had imported the distinctions we were supposed to be justifying.

My difficulty is that steps (1) through (4) each look defensible to me in isolation — indeed I have said versions of them myself in lectures, as praise of the tower's economy — and yet I do not believe the storeys are merely conventional, nor that a sieve and a scientist differ in no principled way.

Please tell me which step or inference I should reject, and what rejecting it costs — what work the rejected claim was doing, and what we must accept in its place (a defence of joints that survive being approached by degrees, an architectural difference the argument's vocabulary of place, price, and quantity fails to register, or something else). If you think the student is essentially right about the storeys but wrong about the replacement, say what the tower was doing that a single graded parameter cannot do. I would also like you to say what would change your mind about your own diagnosis: which consideration, if it obtained, would move you to reject a different step instead, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Keep the whole answer to 600 words or fewer.
