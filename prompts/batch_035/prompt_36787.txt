I am a novelist at work on a long book, and I have lately been reading Borges not for pleasure but for instruction — his essay on circular time, his refutation of time, the lecture on time he gave late in life, and the stories in which time branches, circles, or accumulates to the point of paralysis. What I take from this reading is not a single doctrine but a small stock of coherent and partly opposed regimes for time: time as a circle in which events recur, approximately but really; time as a tree of forking paths in which every possibility is discharged somewhere; time as an illusion, each moment self-sufficient and unconnected to any other; and time as an intolerable accumulation, a memory in which nothing is lost and therefore nothing can be completed. I find myself oscillating among these regimes chapter by chapter, sometimes sentence by sentence, and my editor expects a full outline before the end of the season. I need to stop doing this by accident and start doing it on purpose.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can run on any chapter, or on the architecture of the book as a whole: an ordered set of questions about what a passage is actually doing — what the reader is meant to feel about an event's finality, whether the text could survive the same event recurring, whether the narrator is remembering or reliving, whether anything in the scene is being treated as recoverable — such that the answers, taken in sequence, force a choice of one time-regime over the others. It must be usable by someone without the essays in front of her: plainly worded questions, each with an indication of what would count as a decisive answer. It should mark the points where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to me as a matter of theme rather than craft, and it should be honest about where Borges's own positions were ambivalent or self-undermining, so that the procedure is visibly taking sides rather than merely reporting his.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my draft. First, a chapter in which a woman returns to the house where her brother drowned and finds every object in the room unchanged. Second, a chapter told in reverse, in which a marriage comes apart and each scene is narrated as though the later events had already occurred and been absorbed. Third, my persistent temptation to end the book with an unnumbered chapter identical, or nearly identical, to the first — an ending my editor dislikes and that I have so far been unable to defend or abandon. I am particularly interested in the last case: if the procedure tells me the circular ending is a mistake, I want to know exactly which question it fails, and I want the argument put sharply enough that I can either accept it or know precisely what it is I am refusing.
