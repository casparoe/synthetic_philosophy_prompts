So I keep a notebook where I record my gut reactions to variants of the Mary case (the color scientist raised in the black-and-white room), and looking at them all together I'm genuinely unsure they add up to anything coherent. Here are my verdicts, and what I want from you is a principle -- or two, if that's what it takes -- that captures as many of them as possible, an honest accounting of which verdicts have to go if the principle is going to be worth anything, and what the principle says about a couple of cases I haven't decided on yet.

My verdicts:

1. Classic Mary, who knows every physical fact about color vision and then sees a ripe tomato for the first time: she learns something. Confident about that. What I'm NOT confident about is whether she learns a new fact or just picks up abilities.

2. Implant Mary: same setup, except while still in the room she has a device that directly stimulates her visual cortex and gives her genuine red experiences, though no red light ever enters the room. When she later walks out and sees the actual tomato: I say she doesn't learn anything new about red this time. Already knowing what it's like is what mattered.

3. Pre-exit Mary can derive, from inside the room, every sentence she will ever utter after leaving, including "now I finally know what red looks like." I say yes, she can derive all of it, and no, that doesn't mean she won't still learn something on exit. I'm aware #1 and #3 are uncomfortable together.

4. My colorblind friend, who knows all the physics I do and then some: no amount of propositional knowledge will ever get him to know what red looks like.

5. The anti-ability case: someone with normal color vision who, because of some weird memory/imagination deficit, can't recognize red things from memory, can't imagine red, can't retain red experiences at all -- but while actually looking, red looks completely normal to them. I say they DO know what red looks like. This one makes me distrust the ability hypothesis, which is awkward because #2 seemed to lean on it.

6. The forgotten-red case: someone saw red plenty as a small child, then suffered a total wipe of visual episodic memory. They've had the experience but can't access anything about it. My verdict: they don't currently know what red looks like, though they once did.

7. The mango case: I've never tasted mango, but I can imagine it sort of vividly based on papaya-plus-peach descriptions. Verdict: I don't know what mango tastes like, and no amount of imagining gets me there.

So my tentative principle is something like: you know what an experience is like iff you've had it (or had a token of it via implant or whatever) and can still access it in some way. Test that against all seven verdicts, fix it where it needs fixing, and then tell me the harder thing: does holding #1 and #3 together force me to drop the idea that Mary learns a new fact and settle for the old-fact-under-a-new-mode story -- and if so, what is the "learning" that's left over? I want to know if there's a version of that story that doesn't just quietly collapse into "she didn't learn anything after all," because I'm not willing to bite that bullet for the classic case even though I bit it for the implant case, and I'd like to know if that's a defensible line or just me being inconsistent.

Also, two cases I haven't decided: someone having a red experience right now that they're not consciously aware of (some kind of subliminal presentation) -- do they know what red looks like? And someone who hallucinates red, taking themselves to be seeing red when there's nothing there -- do they know what red looks like, on my principle or on yours?

Please write your answer in American English, and be blunt with me if the verdicts just can't all be saved -- I'd rather overturn one than carry around a principle with three epicycles bolted onto it.
