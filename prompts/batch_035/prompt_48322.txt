My politics reading group has spent basically a whole term going in circles on performance legitimacy, and I want a diagnosis of the circle rather than another lap around it. The setup, if you don't know it: some political theorists and a lot of China/Singapore watchers claim that a regime can be legitimate in virtue of delivering prosperity and order rather than through consent — that's why the CCP is stable, why Singapore's ruling party keeps winning real (if unfair) elections, and why asking whether authoritarian rule is "justified" misses the point. The other camp — more normatively inclined theorists — says this is a confusion: legitimacy is a normative status that has to do with how power is acquired and held to account, and at best what the first camp has found is popularity or stability, which dictators can buy with growth the way a man can buy affection with gifts. We've had this argument about six times now and I genuinely cannot tell anymore whether we're disagreeing about facts, about the meaning of a word, or about two entirely different questions being run under one heading.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the "performance legitimacy" people are really making a descriptive claim about where obedience and acceptance come from (and pointing at survey data, policy responsiveness, the sheer durability of these regimes), while the normative camp is answering the question "would these subjects be wrong to resist?", and neither side notices that the other is answering a different question — so each side's strongest point slides off the other like teflon. But I can think of at least two complications and I want you to take them seriously rather than just confirming me. One: there seems to be a genuinely empirical disagreement hiding inside it, namely whether performance-based acceptance is robust to a serious downturn — if a regime's legitimacy evaporates the moment growth stalls (the way some argue happened to the Soviet bloc), that's evidence it was never legitimacy at all, just a transaction; and we have arguably been running that experiment on China for a decade now with inconclusive results. Two: some people in the group insist the whole framing is politically loaded — that calling order and prosperity "performance" already concedes something to the technocrats, and that the real dispute is about what counts as a success in the first place (growth for whom, order enforced on whom, since neither Singapore's migrant workers nor China's rural petitioners experience those goods the way the aggregate numbers suggest).

So, what kind of disagreement is this really? Verbal, factual, about the standards that should govern a political concept, or about bedrock intuitions (say, about whether consent is the only route to justified authority) that nobody in the room can argue for without begging the question? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a sharper distinction (e.g. separating stability, popularity, and justification into three different things and giving the word 'legitimacy' to only one of them), an actual event (a serious economic shock in a performance-legitimate regime), or nothing — is this one of those cases where the impasse is telling us the question is ill-posed? Please answer in British English. Also feel free to tell me if my three-camps description of the debate misses a position that's actually doing real work in the literature — but I'm after the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of who's written what, so keep names and citations out of it.
