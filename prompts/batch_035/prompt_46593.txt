My dad is retiring after thirty-six years as an administrative law judge — for basically his entire career he ran adversarial hearings where utility companies and consumer advocates fought about rate cases, stuff full of engineering and accounting claims he had to rule on without being able to verify any of it himself. Meanwhile I ended up working in AI safety, on the approach people call AI safety via debate: the idea that when models get smarter than the humans who have to check them, you have two AIs argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge who is weaker than both debaters, and you hope that finding the flaw in a bad answer is easier than producing a good one. When I finally managed to explain my job to him a couple years back, he basically shrugged and said oh, cross-examination, I've been doing that since 1989.

So now I'm giving the toast at his retirement dinner. The room is roughly a third lawyers and fellow ALJs who will catch any legal nonsense, a handful of my AI researcher coworkers who will notice if I butcher the debate stuff, and the rest is alot of family who just want it warm and funny. Write me the toast — around five minutes — built around the parallel between his career and my research, and I mean actually built around it, so the idea does real structural work instead of being a throwaway line about how we both settle arguments for a living.

Two constraints: it has to land as affection and not as a lecture — he hates being turned into a moral, and he's suspicious of flattery to the point where he'll discount anything that sounds like a compliment with an agenda. And whatever you do, don't have the toast imply he ever claimed his hearings found "the truth." His whole thing, which he has repeated at every family argument since I was a kid, is that an adversarial process only ever gives you whatever survives cross-examination, and that this is not the same thing as truth, and it's dangerous to forget the difference. I'd like the toast to end on that note rather than resolving it — let him have the last word even though it's his own retirement party.
