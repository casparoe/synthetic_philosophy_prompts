My friend and I spent the better part of last evening arguing about the St. Petersburg paradox, and I came away unsure how much of what he said I can trust. He had recently read a popular book on probability and gave me its take, roughly as follows: "A casino offers you a game where a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads. If that first heads comes on the nth flip, you are paid 2 to the nth power dollars. The expected value of the game is infinite, because the payouts double with each flip while the probabilities of reaching the big payouts shrink only slowly, so the contributions pile up without limit. This means that at any finite ticket price your expected profit is positive, so a fully rational agent should be willing to pay anything the casino could actually ask. The fact that no ordinary person would pay even fifty dollars therefore demonstrates that people are systematically irrational about risk, which is just what behavioral economics keeps showing. In any case, Bernoulli settled the whole thing back in 1738: the subjective value of money is logarithmic, so the true, utility-adjusted expectation is finite, and the paradox reduces to a psychological fact about how humans value money. It has no further lesson for decision theory."

He concluded from all this that expected value is a broken concept and that for one-off decisions people should trust their instincts instead. I'm inclined to think that refusing to pay much for the game isn't irrational, but every time I pressed him he had an answer, and I lack the background to say where his summary goes wrong, if it does. Would you go through his account claim by claim and tell me which parts a decision theorist would accept, which they would wince at, and which they would flatly reject? In particular, I'd like to know whether his mathematical explanation of why the expected value diverges is stated correctly, whether the inference from infinite expected value to "any price is a bargain" is as straightforward as he made it sound, and whether the Bernoulli resolution really closes the matter or leaves something important open. Finally, does his sweeping conclusion — that expected value is useless for one-off choices and gut feeling should rule — actually follow from the parts of his summary that survive scrutiny?
