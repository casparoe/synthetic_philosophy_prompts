hi, novelist here, stuck on an epigraph and hoping you can write me a poem. some backstory first: in the book im drafting, my narrator inherits her grandmother's farm and early on she pulls out this line of old fence posts running through the creek bottom at the edge of the hayfield. it's crooked, half of it is down, the guys at the co-op shrug when she asks why it's there, and she needs the pasture, so out it comes. the reason comes out about two hundred pages later, and im telling you now so you can work with it: her grandmother ran cattle on this place for thirty years, and that fence is what kept the cows out of the creek bottom long enough for willows to grow up along the bank — the willows only exist because of the fence, and the bank only exists because of the willows. once the fence is gone the new tenant grazes the bottom, the cattle strip the willow shoots, and three spring floods later the creek cuts a new channel and eats a couple acres of the field. so the fence's job was to maintain an absence, which is exactly why nobody could explain it.

the ask: a sonnet for the epigraph of the chapter where she pulls the posts. i want the octave to be the reformer's impatience — her voice, the fence as an ugly irrational relic of a dead woman's habits, the pull of "nobody can tell me why, so there is no why." the turn has to land at line 9 (so loosely petrarchan or just do whatever rhyme scheme as long as the volta is the ninth line), and the turn is the reason arriving — except it can't arrive as information, it has to arrive as image: water, roots, the bank holding or not holding. closing lines should carry the actual principle, the one about how you shouldn't clear away a fence until you know why it was put up — but as something the reader assembles rather than a maxim she's handed, and nothing in the poem should name the guy who said it. a reader who knows the idea should recognize it by structure alone; a reader who doesn't should just get a good sonnet about a farm. one more constraint: the reason itself should stay beneath the surface of the poem — a farmer should be able to reconstruct it, my narrator's book club should not.

and after the poem, could you give me a small json object for my notes file, with these fields: "octave" (one line on what the first eight lines are doing), "volta" (what breaks at line 9 and what image triggers it), "fence_function" (one plain sentence on what the fence was actually for, so my editor can check i didnt botch the riparian logic), "ending" (how the closing lines smuggle in the principle without preaching it), and "tell" (the single detail a specialist would check to see whether the poem actually understands the idea or is just gesturing at it). keep the json lean, no markdown fences, just the object.
