I'm reading a popular book on philosophical skepticism aimed at general readers, and a chapter near the end presents Robert Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge as the most promising modern reply to the brain-in-a-vat argument. Condensed slightly, the chapter's summary runs as follows:

"Gettier showed back in 1963 that justified true belief isn't sufficient for knowledge, and Nozick's answer, in his 1981 book Philosophical Explanations, was that knowledge is true belief that tracks the fact. In effect this is a refined reliabilism: what matters is that your method of belief-formation stands in a reliable counterfactual connection to the truth. Tracking itself is a pair of conditionals — if p were false, you would not believe p, and if p were true, you would believe p. This cleanly explains Gettier-style cases. Henry, driving through fake barn country, truly believes he is looking at a barn; but had the structure been a mere facade, he would have believed the same thing, so his belief does not track and is not knowledge. The same machinery answers the skeptic. You know you have hands, since that belief tracks; and equally you know you are not a handless brain in a vat, since your ordinary methods of belief-formation are responsive to the real world and not to vat-illusions. The skeptic's argument is thus disarmed at both ends. The one bullet the theory must bite is closure. Nozick conceded that knowledge is not closed under known entailment — one can know p, know that p entails q, and still fail to know q — and he took this failure to be perfectly general, an unavoidable cost of the counterfactual analysis. Since closure is as central to epistemology as anything gets, most philosophers regard this consequence as a fatal objection, and the tracking theory is best remembered as a brilliant failure."

Something in this bothers me, though I can't locate the problem. If the theory really secures knowledge that one is not a brain in a vat, I don't see why Nozick would ever have needed to give up closure — closure only produces trouble if there is some consequence of what you know that you cannot know. The author presents the two claims side by side as if they fit together, and he presumably knows the primary literature far better than I do.

Could you audit the passage for me? Please sort its claims into those a defender of Nozick would accept as stated, those that are harmless simplifications, and those Nozick himself would flatly deny — with particular attention to whether the reliabilism gloss is faithful, whether the chapter has the brain-in-a-vat case right, and whether a perfectly general failure of closure is what Nozick actually held. I'd also like to know whether the chapter's closing verdict, that the theory survives only by paying a price no epistemologist should accept, still follows once the misdescriptions are corrected.
