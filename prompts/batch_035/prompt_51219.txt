Got into a huge argument with my friend last night about self-deception, it went like two hours and now the whole thing is rattling around in my head. He thinks he's proved that no one ever really deceives themselves. The worst part is each step looked fine to me on its own, but the argument also seems to prove stuff I'm pretty sure is false, and I can't find where it slips.

Here's his argument:

(1) To deceive someone, you must intend to make them believe something you know is false.
(2) To intend an outcome, you must believe, when you act, that your action will bring it about.
(3) So a self-deceiver must, at the moment of deceiving, believe the truth (that's what makes it deception rather than accident) and simultaneously believe the falsehood (that's what the deception is supposed to achieve).
(4) Nobody can believe p and not-p at the same time.
(5) So intentional self-deception is impossible.

Then he says fine, self-deception still happens, it's just non-intentional -- motivated reasoning running cold, no intent anywhere in the machinery. But if there's no intent, he says, it's just an honest error with a dramatic name, the way a calculator that glitches isn't "deceiving" you. So his conclusion: "self-deception" literally never applies to anyone, ever. People make motivated mistakes and dress them up.

What bugs me is that if the argument works, it also seems to prove you can't intentionally fall asleep, or deliberately calm yourself down, or decide to stop dwelling on something -- since in all of those you intend to bring about a mental state in yourself, and by his step (2) you'd have to already be in that state for the intention to exist. That's obviously wrong; people psych themselves up before races all the time. When I pushed him on this he said no, the sleep case is different, because in self-deception the state you're aiming at is one you know to be false, so the true belief never goes away, it just gets quarantined somewhere. And I couldn't tell whether that saves him or quietly gives up the game -- if the belief is still there, "quarantined," isn't he admitting people can hold a belief in a way that doesn't conflict with anything, which is close to what step (4) was supposed to rule out? Or maybe not, since a quarantined belief isn't quite believing at the time. I honestly can't see it.

So: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to know whether the fix is some kind of two-levels-of-mind story, or unconscious beliefs, or just conceding that "deception" is a metaphor here, and which of those is least ugly. He also kept saying that even if the impossibility argument fails, self-deceivers are never responsible for it anyway, since you can't wrong yourself with your own consent -- is that a separate question or does it fall out of the first one? He name-dropped Davidson and Sartre alot near the end and I don't know if I should care.

One thing I'd like back: tell me whether his final claim -- the concept never applies to anyone -- actually follows even if the impossibility argument fails. It seems like he could be wrong about (5) and right about the "it's all just motivated mistakes" part, and I want to know whether I'm conceding anything by granting the falling-asleep point.
