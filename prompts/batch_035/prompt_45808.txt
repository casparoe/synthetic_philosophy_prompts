Two friends of mine got into it at dinner after we watched some documentary about evolutionary psychology (of all things). They went several rounds and eventually asked me to referee, and honestly I couldn't, so I'm passing it to you. Let me reconstruct the moves in order.

(1) Maya said the doc reminded her of an argument against moral realism she half-remembers: our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection to help our ancestors reproduce, not to track moral truth. If realism is true, the moral facts don't guide selection, so evolution would've pointed us at the truth only by dumb luck. Since roughly the same moral beliefs would've evolved whether or not any of them were true, they're probably mostly false, and that's supposed to be bad news for the realist.

(2) Jules said the argument eats itself: selection also shaped our logic, our perception, our trust in 'best explanations' — all the machinery Maya is using to run the argument. If selection only cares about fitness, then by her own standard her belief in the debunking argument is just another fitness artifact, so the argument saws off the branch its sitting on.

(3) Maya said there's an asymmetry: with perceptual beliefs, the explanation of why we believe them runs through the facts — creatures who thought the tiger was far away when it was close got eaten. With moral beliefs the explanation never needs to mention moral truth at all: kin selection would've made us feel special obligations to kin whether or not those obligations actually exist. So the real test is 'would the same fitness story have produced the same belief even if the belief were false' — perceptual beliefs pass that test, moral beliefs fail it.

(4) Jules said the test proves way too much. Math: we'd believe 2+2=4 even if numbers turned out to be some kind of fiction, since believing it was useful either way, so the fitness story doesn't route through mathematical truth either — but Maya obviously doesn't want to debunk math. And the realist has an easy reply anyway: just say the moral facts, whatever they are, are facts about suffering and cooperation and flourishing — things selection really did push us toward tracking — in which case the dependence Maya demands is sitting right there.

(5) Maya called that a rescue by coincidence: you can always save the test by building the moral facts to order around whatever evolution happened to hand out, and then the fit between 'what selection gave us' and 'the truth' is exactly what needs explaining. On math she said the cases differ, because the fitness of believing math routes through the world actually being regular — a brain that models consistent regularities survives because reality is consistent — whereas with morality the fitness story never needs the truth to come in at all.

(6) Jules' last move was that even granting all that, the debunking only directly covers intuitions selection actually shaped — protect kin, reciprocate, punish freeloaders. Maya's belief that factory farming is wrong isn't a directly selected belief; it's built by reflection out of selected raw materials, and reflection can launder a bad etiology the way a careful calculation can fix a biased estimate. Maya said that just relocates the taint: the reflective method itself — which intuitions it treats as data, which it dismisses as noise — is evolved and selected too, so the debunking transmits along the chain.

That's where they left it, arguing about whether 'transmits along the chain' was even a coherent idea.
