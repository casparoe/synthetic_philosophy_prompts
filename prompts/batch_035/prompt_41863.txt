I'm a PhD student in political science working on collective memory, and next term I'm running a graduate seminar unit on how societies metabolize military defeat. The reading list covers the usual suspects: the Lost Cause of the Confederacy, the Masada narrative and its archaeological politics, the Kosovo myth in Serbian national memory, and the Dunkirk evacuation as an exercise in reframing. My students will have done the readings, so I don't want a straight lecture opener; I want something satirical that presupposes the material and lets them feel clever for catching the references, while still being a genuine piece of satire rather than a list of jokes about the South losing a war.

Please write the brochure copy (roughly 650-800 words) for a management consultancy that specializes in what it calls "strategic defeat rebranding" — the conversion of lost causes into national myths. The firm should offer tiered service packages (something like a basic "Grief-to-Grandeur" package and a premium "Eternal Flame" tier with pilgrimage-site management and an annual commemoration calendar), plus à la carte add-ons: sanitization of the client's original war aims, reframing of a rout as a moral or spiritual victory, a playbook for dealing with historians (who should be referred to only as "revisionists"), and brand partnerships for the gift shop. The brochure should gesture at anonymized past engagements — a naval evacuation successfully repositioned around small boats and civilian courage, a desert fortress siege whose less flattering details were "archived for future scholarship," a Balkan battle whose defeat seeded a centuries-long grievance franchise, and a rebellion whose cause was "streamlined for modern audiences" — without naming them outright. Include a testimonial sidebar with invented clients, and end with a fine-print disclaimer that quietly undermines the entire pitch.

The craft constraints matter as much as the content. The humor has to come from the real mechanics of lost-cause mythmaking — selective omission, the transmutation of tactical defeat into moral victory, the capture of the myth by later political projects, the tension between commemoration and historiography, the commercialization of grievance — so that someone who knows the memory-studies literature recognizes that the author understands exactly what is being satirized, and finds it fair rather than sneering. Keep it deadpan: the brochure must never state the objections it is dramatizing, and it must remain legible as irony throughout. No named living persons, and no real organizations as clients; invented consultants with plausible business-speak names are fine. Feel free to name the firm yourself — I'd enjoy something with a classical defeat hidden in it.
