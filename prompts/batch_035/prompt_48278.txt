I want a probability estimate, not a survey, on a strong version of the base–superstructure thesis. The proposition: for any large-scale, stable society, the character of its economic base — who controls the major productive forces, and the social relations of production that go with that — explains the main features of its legal, political, religious, and ideological life, in a sense of "explains" strong enough that if the base had been relevantly different, those features could not have remained broadly as they were. This is not the hedged claim that material factors matter a great deal; it is the roughly functional claim Marx's 1859 preface gestures at, with the elements of the superstructure persisting because they stabilize the existing base.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following terms, assigning each a number with one or two lines of justification. First, the probability that the base/superstructure distinction can be drawn non-arbitrarily at all — given that in a modern capitalist economy, property law, corporate law, and central banking look constitutive of the economic base rather than mere reflections of it, and one could equally describe them as superstructure doing base-work. Second, the probability that functional explanation is legitimate here — that it is acceptable to explain an institution by its stabilizing effects without anything like a selection mechanism as strong as natural selection, given that one cannot rerun history to observe failed superstructures being weeded out. Third, the probability that the obvious hard cases are not decisive counterexamples: the same mathematics and much of the same natural science proving out under feudalism, capitalism, and state socialism; durable religious forms persisting across radically different bases; legal codes migrating between economies with their basic architecture intact. Fourth, whatever term you think belongs for the worry that the thesis has been reformulated so often — Engels' late concession that the superstructure reacts back on the base, with economics decisive only in the last instance — that it has become unfalsifiable, and that its surviving plausibility rests on survivor bias in what historians bother to record.

Give the combination, then tell me two things: which term your final number is most sensitive to, and whether a much weaker thesis — the base constrains the superstructure without determining it — captures most of what is attractive here, in which case what exactly is left over that the strong thesis still claims. If you think the disagreement between a committed historical materialist at 0.8 and a skeptic at 0.2 is really a disagreement about one of these terms, say which, and what historical or conceptual evidence would move it most.
