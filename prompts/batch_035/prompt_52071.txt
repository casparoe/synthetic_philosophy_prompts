I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I'm working on a feature about what happens when a newsroom adopts engagement metrics to manage its own journalists — time-on-page, shares, subscriber conversions per story. Rather than another pair of dueling op-eds, my editor wants the two researchers I've been interviewing to produce the kind of joint statement an adversarial collaboration issues. One holds that the arrangement is doomed by Goodhart's law: any measure of journalistic quality, once tied to evaluation and pay, will be gamed and will corrode the thing measured, so the only live question is how fast. The other holds that this is bad institutional design, not destiny: combining several imperfect proxies, keeping senior editors' judgment in the loop, and revising the metric suite on a fixed schedule keeps the failure tolerable — as it mostly has been in peer review and in central-bank target-setting. Draft that joint statement. It should contain: the claims both can sign about newsrooms, metrics, and Goodhart's law, including where regressional, extremal, and causal Goodhart each bite in this setting; the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether optimization pressure itself corrupts a proxy or whether corruption requires competition on it, and another is whether human judgment, used as the meta-metric that audits the dashboard, can itself be Goodharted; and, for each crux, a concrete development in the newsroom that both would agree counts as evidence, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Be decisive in the drafting: draw the line between agreement and disagreement sharply, commit to which disagreements are substantive and which are verbal, and say so plainly if you think one crux, once settled, would collapse the other side's position. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and keep the whole document under 700 words.
