Okay so, background: I'm a software engineer (frontend, so I spend my whole day looking at type whether I like it or not), and for the last few months I've been wierdly obsessed with letterforms. It started when I was debugging a font rendering bug and realized I'd been staring at the lowercase 'a' for like twenty minutes. There are two totally different shapes that both count as 'a' — the double-story one with the little hat and tail, and the simple one that's basically a circle with a stick next to it — and a kid just learning to write produces an 'a' that's neither, and honestly a ransom-note 'a' clipped out of a newspaper might not share a single stroke with the 'a' in the font I'm typing this in. Yet I have never once, in my whole literate life, failed to recognize one on sight. So what exactly am I recognizing?

I have no philosophy background, but I've been chewing on two ideas that seem to bear on it. One is that there's no visual feature shared by all 'a's — no line, angle, or proportion survives across all of them — so maybe an 'a' isn't a shape at all but a role in a system: something is an 'a' partly by not being an 'o' or an 'e', the way each letter gets its identity from standing in the whole crowd of letters at once. The other is that the one constant across all the variation isn't in the ink, it's in the reader — the act of recognition itself, which fires the same way every time no matter what the thing looks like. I genuinely can't tell which of these is right, or whether it's some mix, and part of why I want a poem rather than an answer is that I don't think this is settled.

Here's the ask: write a poem, maybe 16–24 lines, in tercets or quatrains, where each stanza presents a different 'a'. Some can be the ones above (the geometric single-story one from fonts like Futura, a child's wobbly backwards one, the ransom-note 'a', an 'a' traced in the air with a finger, or the @ sign, which I read somewhere descends from an 'a' though I never fact-checked it), but at least a couple should be your own invention — an 'a' formed by people at a party, or in a water tower, whatever you like. After every stanza, repeat the exact same one-line refrain, completely unchanged. That refrain is doing the philosophical work: it's the invariant, the thing that stays fixed while everything else varies — which is exactly the question, whether the 'a' lives in the shapes or in the refrain. The refrain shouldn't name the letter outright; it should be a line about seeing or knowing that could apply to all of them equally. Then let the final stanza complicate things — maybe an 'a' that keeps flirting with being an 'o' until the word around it saves it, so that context gets a vote too. Don't resolve where the spirit of the letter actually lives; leave that open, since that's honestly where I am. And it still has to work as a plain poem for someone who's never thought about any of this — my wife's going to read it, and she thinks the whole obsession is nonsense.
