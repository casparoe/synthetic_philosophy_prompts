I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and no debate recurred there as reliably as the torture-versus-dust-specks matchup: whether some sufficiently vast number of barely-noticeable eye irritations can outweigh a single instance of torture. Watching that argument flare up again and again taught me that my intuitions about pleasure and pain don't sit comfortably on a single scale — but it never told me which formal position in the neighborhood I actually hold. I write a small blog whose readership enjoys exactly this kind of self-sorting, and I'd like a quiz to publish.

Please design a "Which axiology of pleasure and pain do you actually hold?" quiz: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. A few constraints:

1. Sort by cases, not labels. No question may ask directly whether the respondent thinks pleasure and pain are commensurable, whether they accept a lexical threshold, or whether they are a negative utilitarian. Every question must be a trade-off or comparison that someone can answer from intuition alone.

2. Four positions must appear as leaves: (a) classical hedonic utilitarianism, on which any quantity of pleasure can outweigh any quantity of pain given enough of it; (b) weak asymmetry, on which pain counts more than pleasure but nothing is lexically prior to anything else; (c) lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, on which suffering above some intensity threshold cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, though sub-threshold suffering still can; and (d) strong negative utilitarianism, on which no pleasure outweighs any suffering at all. If the same questions let you cleanly separate further positions — for instance, a view on which the threshold holds within a single life but dissolves when the goods and bads are spread across many lives — include those as well.

3. At least one question must probe whether threshold reasoning survives aggregation: not merely whether a pinprick can be outweighed by enjoyments, but whether any number of pinpricks can outweigh one severe pain — the point on which the dust-specks debate actually turned. Feel free to escalate the numbers as far up the tower of Knuth up-arrows as you like, but each option should remain something a reader can sincerely choose.

4. At least one question should involve the creation of new lives, since some respondents will discover that their intuitions there are orthogonal to everything else the quiz measures, and the scoring key should acknowledge this rather than pretend it doesn't happen.

5. In the scoring key, flag the combinations of answers the quiz cannot cleanly sort — say, answers indicating a lexical threshold in single-life cases but full commensurability across lives — and for each unoccupied region, say whether you think nobody holds that view because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it hasn't been explored.

Please write the answer in British English throughout.
