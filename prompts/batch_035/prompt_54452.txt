Quick bit of background: I teach a survey course on philosophy of mind and language, and every year I do a session on Wittgenstein's private language argument. This term I got ambitious and sketched an extension of it to large language models — my idea being that the argument, read carefully, actually splits the "can machines mean?" question in two. Before I inflict this on students I'd like a second pair of eyes. Please comment on the sketch below: does the argument actually work, am I being fair to Wittgenstein, and which parts would a sharp critic tear apart? Be blunt, I can take it.

Here's the sketch as it stands:

Wittgenstein (Philosophical Investigations, roughly from §243 onwards) argues that there could be no language whose words refer to what only the speaker herself can, in principle, know about — the famous case being the diarist who writes a sign in her diary whenever she has a particular private sensation. On the standard reading, the point isn't that such a language would be unknowable to others, but that a purely private ostensive definition can't establish any norm of correct use. With no distinction between seeming to remember that this is the same sensation and actually remembering it, there's no standard of getting it right or wrong, so nothing has been defined. The beetle-in-the-box thought experiment is meant to clinch this: if everyone has a box with something in it that everyone calls a beetle, and no one can ever look into anyone else's box, then the word beetle gets its role in the language game entirely from the public pattern of use — the boxes might as well be empty, or contain different things.

Now my extension. Large language models look like a strange inversion of the beetle case. Each model has a box — its internal activations, whatever is going on in there — that we can't inspect in ordinary communicative practice. But unlike Wittgenstein's diarist, the model never learned its words by private ostension in the first place. It learned entirely from the public side, from the pattern of use across billions of utterances. So the private language argument, which is usually taken to establish that meaning must be public, arguably says nothing threatening about whether an LLM's words mean anything: the argument's target, private ostension, is simply absent from its training. Public meaning comes cheap for these systems, or so I claim.

The interesting part is the flip side. Suppose a model developed some internal quasi-symbol it uses to track, say, uncertainty about its own next output — a self-monitoring signal, not directly expressed in what it says. That would be much closer to the diarist's sign: a mark whose application is fixed only by what goes on inside, with no community to check against and no distinction between correctly applying it and merely seeming to. So the private language argument might bite there instead — not on the model's ordinary words, but on whatever private bookkeeping it invents for itself. Conclusion: the question splits. Public meaning comes cheap for LLMs; private meaning, if that's even coherent, is where Wittgenstein would raise trouble.

Specific things I'd like your comments on:

1. Is my reading of the diarist case — as being about norms of correctness rather than about verification or epistemic access — defensible, or am I presenting one contested interpretation as the standard one?
2. Is the move from "no private ostension in training" to "public meaning comes cheap" too quick? A colleague muttered that it conflates learning from use with participating in a practice, and I suspect she's onto something but I can't quite see wether it sinks the whole move or just qualifies it.
3. The self-monitoring signal example — is it genuinely analogous to the diarist's sign? The diarist is a subject with sensations; the model's internal signal isn't obviously sensation-like, and I worry I'm sneaking in the very interiority the argument is supposed to problematise.
4. Generally: does the extension feel like a natural application of Wittgenstein or a gimmick I'm forcing because LLMs are fashionable?

Don't worry about giving me references or a reading list — I mostly want your judgement on the argument itself.
