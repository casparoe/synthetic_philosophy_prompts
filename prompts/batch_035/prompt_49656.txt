I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I'm working on a piece about why smart, informed people still end up on opposite sides of questions with facts attached -- inflation forecasts, lab leak, how bad a given winter wave will be, that kind of thing. At a panel I moderated last month, an economist shut down another panelist (a forecaster, actually) by saying that "rational agents can't agree to disagree," dropping Aumann's name and his 1976 paper as if that settled it -- the idea being that if two people have genuinely talked it out and still disagree, one of them must be irrational or lying about their confidence. Since then I've heard versions of this line from a superforecaster type on Twitter, in a Brookings podcast about polarization, and from a friend who trades options, each time doing slightly different work. I'm now not sure it means the same thing in any two of these mouths.

Here's what I'd love: treat "rational people cannot agree to disagree" as the slogan it's become and lay out the distinct precise claims it could actually be expressing. Like -- is it a claim about priors (that everyone starts from the same one, whatever that's even supposed to mean for a real human), about information (that once opinions are publicly on the table they become common knowledge and the beliefs have to merge), about what counts as "disagreement" at all (does it mean different probabilities for the same proposition, or different willingness to bet, or just saying different things out loud)? For each reading, tell me whether it's basically a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, and -- most important for my piece -- whether that reading actually supports the dismissive move the economist made, i.e., "if we still disagree after talking, someone here is being stupid or dishonest." I suspect the honest answer is that the theorem is beautiful and the slogan does way less work than people think, but don't let me off easy if that's wrong.

Format it as a numbered list of readings I can actually use while reporting, with a short verdict on each -- I'm on deadline-ish, so crisp beats exhaustive, but I do want the one or two readings that would let me tell readers "here's the version of this claim that survives contact with real humans, and here's the version you should roll your eyes at." If there's a reading under which the slogan turns out to be basically a point about betting markets vs. polling averages or something like that, flag it, because I keep circling that and can't tell if it's a real distinction or my confusion.
