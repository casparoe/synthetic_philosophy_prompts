I work in procurement at a mid-sized company, so every year I have to sit through the compliance training, and I've become weirdly obsessed with the lines those gift-and-bribery rules draw. The pattern I keep seeing, across companies and countries, goes roughly like this: gifts below some modest cap are fine; anything in cash or near-cash (gift cards, vouchers) is banned outright no matter how small; the same gift becomes way more suspiscious if it's given right before a tender decision than if it's given at Christmas; gifts have to be disclosed, not just kept under the cap; and there's a whole special-case culture around weddings, funerals and conference dinners that keeps expanding. My friend who works in development aid tells me that in a bunch of the countries her org operates in, giving presents to officials is just what you do, and yet the Western-style anti-corruption framework still treats a lot of that as corruption. So here's my actual question: what theory of what corruption *is* do these rules jointly presuppose? Is it that corruption is an exchange that distorts the official's role (so a gift is okay when it's too small or too ritualized to plausibly buy anything), or is it about hijacking reciprocity norms, or is the cash line based on some distinction between a gift and a payment? And honestly I suspect the rules don't presuppose any single coherent theory — maybe they're just risk-management kludges — in which case I'd like to know which parts of the practice are genuinely theory-loaded and which parts would look the same under several completely different views of corruption. It would also help if you could say what would have to change in the rules if you swapped the implicit view for one where corruption just means any undisclosed payment for services rendered (a more honest market view, basically) — would the cash ban even survive that?

To make it easy to compare, please give me a table with these columns: "Feature of the rules" (one row each for: the cash ban, the dollar caps, timing sensitivity, the disclosure requirement, and the wedding/funeral carve-outs), "What it seems to presuppose" (the implicit view of gifts/corruption that best explains that feature), "Rival view it appears to rule out", and "Underdetermined?" (yes or no, plus one sentence on whether a different theory of corruption could still license that same feature). After the table, a short paragraph with your overall verdict on whether the practice rests on one theory or several, and finish with one sentence naming the single change to the rules that would most increase its philosophical coherence.
