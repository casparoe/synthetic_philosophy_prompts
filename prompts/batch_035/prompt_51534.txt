Two of my grad students got into it after my seminar last week and I'd love an outside read on it. We'd been discussing Dennett's line about Darwin and Turing — the idea that Darwin's "strange inversion of reasoning" and Turing's comparable move amount to one discovery: competence without comprehension. The short version of the background: Turing stops asking whether machines can really think and asks whether a machine could do everything a thinker does while having no grasp of meaning, and Darwin shows you can get exquisitely designed things with nobody designing them. Okay, here's the argument.

Maya said the inversion is deflationary all the way down. If a system can match our performance across the board while being blind to meaning, then comprehension was never a real ingredient in our performance either — it's like pulling back the curtain in Oz and finding a guy with levers. She said our command of grammar is like this: we all produce flawless sentences and none of us can state the rules we're following.

Devon pushed back: the inversion shows you can *predict* the competence without positing comprehension, not that comprehension is absent where it's present. His analogy was a weather simulation that forecasts storms without containing a single storm. He also said Maya's move proves too much — run the same argument on digestion, explain it chemically, and you'd conclude there was never any real digesting, just molecules moving around.

Maya's comeback: digestion was never supposed to be more than molecules, so explaining it costs nothing; but comprehension was supposed to make a difference. If the system does the same work with it or without it, it's epiphenomenal, and an idle wheel isn't part of the machine in any way that matters.

Devon's last word: same performance *at the level we happened to test*. The Turing test only sees behavior, and the whole live question is whether behavior fixes the inner facts at all — so Maya is smuggling in behaviorism to reach her deflationary conclusion, which is circular.

I've read enough Dennett (and Dennett critics) to know both of these positions have serious defenders, and honestly they each walked away convinced they'd won. Can you adjudicate move by move — say who's right at each step, and also tell me what, if anything, they're both missing?
