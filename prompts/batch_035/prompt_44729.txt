I teach political philosophy, and every other year I run a small seminar on how historical analogies get used (and abused) in public reasoning -- basically a course about people saying "this is just like 1938" as if that settles things. Well, my department is currently in one of its periodic flame wars over whether to sign an open letter about a live crisis, and the whole argument has collapsed into dueling history lessons, so I want to prepare for tomorrow's faculty meeting properly. The situation: a regional power has massed troops on the border of a smaller neighbor and is demanding "security guarantees" -- meaning the small state must never join the defensive alliance it's been flirting with. The hawks in my department keep saying that any concession here is another Munich: Chamberlain bought an extra year of peace at the price of making the eventual war unwinnable, and the lesson is that you stop an aggressor early, cheaply, and while he's still weak. The doves keep saying it's another Vietnam: a peripheral quarrel on someone else's border, badly understood by outsiders, where incremental entry bleeds you for a decade and the domino premise that justified everything turned out to be false. And then there's a third colleague who keeps invoking the Thucydides trap -- rising power, ruling power, war more or less baked in -- to argue that we're distracting ourselves with the border while the real game is managing the great-power rivalry. A historian friend of mine also grumbles that the "trap" reading rests on a famous one-line summary of the war's cause that is really a paraphrase, and that the actual war Thucydides describes was set off by Corinth and Corcyra and a Theban raid on Plataea, so even the source case doesn't fit the tidy story being sold.

So here's what I want: an audit of these analogies *as arguments*, the way we'd audit any argument by analogy. Please give me the correspondence table for each -- what plays the role of Hitler, of the Sudetenland, of the piece of paper; what plays the role of the domino, of the quagmire, of the Gulf of Tonkin; what plays the rising power and the ruling power in the trap -- and then work through where each mapping breaks. Crucially, I want you to separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the decision at hand (whether to sign a letter urging, essentially, early firmness with military backing behind it) from the ones that are just pedantic. Which features does each analogy quietly import that the target situation may lack -- e.g., does the Munich analogy smuggle in that the aggressor is insatiable and can never be appeased at any price, versus a revanchist with bounded and negotiable aims? Does the Vietnam analogy smuggle in that the stakes are peripheral, when maybe here they're alliance credibility itself? Does the trap analogy import fatalism that does no argumentative work either way? And then the key question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion each side was recruiting its analogy for survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If you think some other analogy (deterrence scenarios, 1914, Suez, whatever) would honestly map better, say so and say why.

A few constraints since this is for my own prep and I'll be scribbling on it in the meeting: keep it at the level a smart non-historian philosopher can follow, don't pad it with every disanalogy you can think of -- rank them -- and dont pretend there's a clean scholarly consensus where there isnt. Structure it however you like, but end with a final line reading "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating which of the three analogies, if any, can actually bear the conclusion it was being used to support in the letter debate.
