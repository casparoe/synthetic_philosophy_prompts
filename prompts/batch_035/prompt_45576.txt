I'm a retired physical chemist -- 35 years of nonequilibrium thermodynamics, mostly polymer melts, so stat mech was my bread and butter -- who got into philosophy of physics after retiring, and my son-in-law, a philosophy grad student, walked me into an argument over Thanksgiving that I haven't been able to get out of since. Here it is, as best I can lay it out. (1) The microscopic laws -- Newton's equations, or unitary evolution of the Schrödinger equation if you like -- are invariant under time reversal: run any solution backwards and you get another perfectly legal solution. (2) The way we actually derive the Second Law from those laws is: take the macrostate of a system now, put a uniform probability measure over all the microstates compatible with it, evolve each one forward with the dynamics, and note that the overwhelming majority of them go to higher entropy. (3) Nothing in that recipe mentions "forward." So run it the other way: same macrostate now, same uniform measure over the compatible microstates, evolve each one *backward* -- and by exactly the same counting, the overwhelming majority of them came from *higher* entropy. (4) A uniform measure over the present macrostate doesn't come labeled with a direction, and refusing to use it toward the past while using it toward the future would smuggle in the very arrow we were supposed to be deriving. So if the method is any good at all, retrodiction says the past was hotter, messier, and higher-entropy than the present. (5) In that overwhelmingly probable past, the fossil record, the geological strata, my memory of yesterday, and the lab notebooks in which the experiments establishing statistical mechanics were recorded all got there by random fluctuation out of a higher-entropy mess. (6) So our best scientific theory implies that the evidence for our best scientific theory is almost certainly fake. Which I refuse to believe, but I can't spot the bad step.

My son-in-law says this is old news with a standard answer, but when I push him, the answer is always "well, you conditionalize on the past having had low entropy" -- which to my chemist's ear sounds like rejecting step (4) by fiat. You're allowed to use the uniform measure toward the future but must quietly weight the past toward low entropy, and it looks to me like you've just installed the arrow by hand and called it a boundary condition. So tell me: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the low-entropy past is a legitimate extra posit rather than a fudge, I want to know exactly what it buys -- does it restore the reliability of records in general, or only of the records you used to fix it in the first place? And is there something to the self-undermining character of (6) itself: can the fact that a theory eats its own evidence be a reason to reject it, or is that illicit somehow? For what it's worth, if I'm forced to choose, I'd rather give up (6) than (4), but I half suspect that's just stubborness, since (6) looks like plain modus ponens on the rest.

(apologies for the length, but I wanted to get the steps down before the family argues about something else at Christmas.)
