A pop-history book I picked up summarizes the idea of Whig history like this: "Whig history, a term coined by Herbert Butterfield in 1931, is simply the belief that history is the story of progress — that things have on the whole gotten better over time, and that the historian's job is to narrate that improvement. Butterfield attacked the Whig historians of his day, who saw English history as a triumphant march toward Protestant liberty and parliamentary government. His remedy was that historians should be value-neutral: they have to drop all judgments of better and worse, since judging the past by your own values only tells your readers about you. Good history therefore never says things got better, it only describes what changed, and treats every period as equally valid." I feel like something's slightly off here but can't put my finger on it — I've heard Butterfield was religious and didn't think historians had to be moral blanks. Can you go through the summary and tell me which parts a historian of historiography would accept, which are simplifications, and which are just wrong, and whether the book's conclusion about what "good history" requires survives the corrections?
