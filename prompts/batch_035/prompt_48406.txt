I've become fairly convinced of a position I absorbed from the formal epistemology of markets, and I want to stress-test it properly. The view, roughly: if a group of agents start from common prior beliefs and update rationally on their private information, they can never bet against each other purely on the basis of their opinions about how a security will perform. Any offer to trade reveals information to the counterparty, who then updates until the gains from trade evaporate. It follows, I take it, that all purely speculative volume is evidence of something gone wrong -- irrationality, or priors that were never common to begin with, or strategic behavior that amounts to preying on those flaws. And so the enormous trading volume in derivatives and currency markets, where positions seem overwhelmingly speculative rather than tied to delivering actual goods, strikes me as a standing indictment of the comfortable picture of markets as arenas of rational information aggregation. I'm not looking for a general defense of finance; I want to know what would actually count against this view.

Please take the position as stated and map its exposure. I'm aware of the standard escape hatches -- hedging, liquidity shocks, differences in initial endowments, differences in when people need to consume -- and I understand that trade driven by those motives is fully consistent with the theorem. So don't just list them. What I want sorted out is the residual claim: that trade which cannot be attributed to those motives reflects genuine disagreement, and hence a failure of rationality or of common priors. What empirical findings, formal results, or conceptual arguments would move a reasonable holder of that residual claim? For instance, I've heard there's a result suggesting that if prices fully revealed everyone's information, no one would have any incentive to gather information in the first place, so perfectly informative prices are impossible in equilibrium -- does that undermine the view, or merely qualify it? And there seems to be a related result that rational agents who simply tell each other their posterior beliefs must end up agreeing -- if that's right, why does persistent, voiced disagreement not already refute the picture in every market where analysts publish opposing forecasts?

I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability. Given any observed trade, can't a defender always redescribe the parties' motives as risk-sharing after the fact, so that no volume, however speculative it looks, could ever count as disconfirming? If so, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or just the ordinary modesty of an equilibrium concept? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish those that are live from those that are merely logically possible. And say where committed defenders would draw the line: which of these would they concede as counting against the view, and which would they treat as already absorbed by the standard caveats? For my part, I suspect I'd be most moved by evidence about what traders themselves think they're doing -- if surveys or experiments showed informed parties knowingly taking both sides of the same bet for reasons none of the escape hatches covers -- but I'd want to know whether the view's defenders would be moved by that too, or whether they'd simply file it under heterogeneous priors and carry on.
