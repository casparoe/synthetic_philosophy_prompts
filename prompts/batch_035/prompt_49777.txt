I'm currently taking an upper-level course on collective decision-making, and we've just finished a unit on Arrow's impossibility theorem. I feel like I've got a decent handle on the formal statement: when there are three or more alternatives, no social welfare function can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship all at once. Our instructor spent a lot of time on the axioms themselves, but comparatively little on what she called the "upshot" of the theorem for actual electoral reform, and that's where I've managed to confuse myself.

Here is my understanding, which I suspect contains an error somewhere. As I understand it, Arrow's theorem shows that every ranked voting system is vulnerable to strategic manipulation — that is, voters can sometimes get a better outcome by reporting preferences that differ from their sincere ones. This would explain a lot of what we see in practice: under plurality rule, voters abandon their favorite candidate to back a "lesser evil," and the same vulnerability should presumably carry over to any rank-order method. Given that, I don't understand why electoral reform advocates keep pushing alternatives like instant-runoff voting or the Borda count as genuine improvements over plurality. If Arrow already proved these systems are just as gameable, isn't promoting them misleading? Relatedly, my study group has been debating the natural fix: since the theorem only applies to systems that take ordinal rankings as input, shouldn't we simply drop the ordinal framework and let voters submit cardinal scores (e.g., 0–10 ratings or approval ballots), thereby escaping the theorem's conditions altogether? A friend of mine disagrees and insists the manipulation issue is a separate theorem I'm conflating with Arrow's, and that my proposed fix doesn't get us out of trouble even if we switch to cardinal ballots. I'm preparing a short essay on this and want to make sure my framing is sound before I commit to it. Where exactly, if anywhere, is my understanding going wrong, and what's the correct way to think about the relationship between Arrow's theorem, strategic manipulation, and the choice between ordinal and cardinal voting methods?
