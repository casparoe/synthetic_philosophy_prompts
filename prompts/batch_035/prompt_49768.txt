For years I've held the doctrine of doing and allowing: the view that there is an intrinsic, morally significant difference between harm that I bring about and harm that I fail to prevent, such that killing is worse than letting die all else equal -- not because of uncertainty about whether the bad outcome would really occur, not because allowing is harder to verify, not because of slippery slopes, but in itself. I came to this view through hospital ethics work, where it does constant load-bearing duty: we withdraw a ventilator knowing the patient will die, and treat that as categorically different from injecting a lethal dose, even where the family's request and the patient's prognosis are identical in both cases. I ground my version of the doctrine in something like a negative-duty constraint: it is harder to justify imposing a threat on someone than it is to decline to avert or redirect a threat that already has them in its path, so what matters is not the mere difference between motion and stillness but whether the victim's peril traces back through me or over me.

Tell me what would count against the view. I already know the standard streamlining objection -- the pair of bathtub cases where one man drowns his cousin for the inheritance and another, intending the same, merely lets him slip under and watches -- and I want to know how much weight it should really carry, since a defender can reply that it shows only that the distinction can be defeated by intention, not that it isn't there. What I want instead is the fuller map of exposure: cases where the doing/allowing status is held fixed but the intuition flips or disappears, as when a victim is positioned so that averting one threat inevitably presses them into another's path, or when the harm is the result of redirecting a pre-existing peril rather than initiating one -- do intuitions there track doing and allowing, or do they secretly track being a means rather than an obstacle, or contact versus non-contact, and would that make the doctrine idle? Should I concede, if every intuition that seemed to support the distinction turns out to be capturable by the doctrine of double effect instead, that the distinction does no independent work? Is there a metaphysical problem about what omissions even are -- whether a failure to water the plants is an event I cause or just an absence -- that could sink the view from below rather than through cases? And what results elsewhere, say about the permissibility of redirecting risks across a population or about how we may treat pre-existing threats, does my version stand or fall with?

I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, with an account of which ones a committed defender of the doctrine would themselves concede as counting against it and which they would dismiss as begging the question. And be honest about the insulation worry: my view already carries the ceteris paribus clause and the tracing condition, so I want to know whether it has been qualified into something that can only lose in the seminar room and never in the hospital, and -- if so -- whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or just what constraints are like.
