I build ranking systems — the kind that decide which of a few thousand items go into a feed. Ties are my daily bread: when the model scores two items identically, or close enough that the difference is noise, something downstream has to break the symmetry, and in practice that something is usually a random seed or a "stable" tiebreak like item ID that everyone knows is arbitrary. A colleague half-jokingly calls this the production guarantee that we'll never starve like the donkey.

That's a reference I only just got, by the way. Apparently there's an actual philosophical debate about Buridan's ass — the donkey exactly between two identical haystacks who supposedly starves because it has no reason whatsoever to go to one rather than the other. And from what I can tell the interesting part isn't the donkey, its the replies, because each reply seems to commit you to something different. Some people say perfect indifference never really happens, there's always some tiny asymmetry that tips it one way. Some say the ass should just flip a mental coin, and that arbitrary choice between equally good options is perfectly rational. Some say rational choice needs a sufficient reason, so if you're genuinely tied, being stuck is at least permitted. And there's apparently a further thing about whether you can rationally choose to *acquire* a preference — deliberately cultivating a tiebreaker — as opposed to just having one.

Restate this debate in my vocabulary — scoring functions, what argmax does on ties, deterministic vs stochastic policies, epsilon-greedy exploration, random seeds, exploration bonuses, and the compute cost of deliberating further — as faithfully as you can, one reply at a time. Then give me the residue report: which replies map cleanly onto things my systems already do (the coin-flip one sounds like it's just a stochastic policy, and "cultivate a tiebreaker" sounds like adding a tiny epsilon bonus or a jitter feature), which ones I'd have to fudge to fit, and whether anything in the philosophers' debate has no counterpart in how engineers handle ties at all.

Here's the specific thing I want your read on. From where I sit, the ass starves only if compute is free: if deliberating further to break a tie costs anything, then stopping and randomizing is just the optimal stopping rule, and the whole puzzle collapses into an engineering tradeoff between more scoring passes and a coin flip. Is that right? Or is the philosophers' worry about something my vocabulary can't express — something about whether the ass is *justified* in its choice rather than merely alive at the end of it — in which case my "puzzle dissolved" feeling is itself a philosophical position I'm smuggling in as translation? Don't be nice about it.
