Last night a friend and I argued for hours about conscription — she holds the straightforward liberal view that it is two years of forced labor and that a professional volunteer army is the only arrangement consistent with taking people's choices seriously — and while I kept doing well on individual cases, I came away unable to state what I actually believe. Here are the verdicts I found myself defending. Conscription as such does not offend me, roughly the way taxation does not offend me: it seems to me one of the duties that come with membership in a state. Finland's practice of conscripting only men while women serve, if at all, purely by choice does offend me — if the duty exists, it should fall on both sexes, which is why I approved when Sweden reintroduced conscription and applied it to women as well as men. The old Swiss arrangement of the militiaman keeping his service rifle at home strikes me, if anything, as admirable: the means of national defense belonging to the citizen rather than to the state seems to me the point of a citizen-army, not a defect. Yet the volunteer professional army troubles me in a way I could not articulate to her: I kept saying that it lets a society subcontract the risks of its defense onto those with the fewest other options, and that paying wages converts a shared duty into someone else's job — the old Civil War device of letting a draftee pay a fee or hire a substitute, laundered into respectability. On the other hand, I have no sympathy for conscripting anyone to fight beyond the country's own defense: a conscripted expeditionary force, even for a good cause, seems plainly wrong, and I find Swiss-style armed neutrality coherent and even honorable — though I cannot see why, if shared risk is my rationale, it should end at the border. The case that kept me up afterward: conscientious objectors in Finland get an alternative civilian service deliberately made longer than military service as a disincentive, which I think is legitimate even given the extra length; but the total objector who refuses even that and, as I understand it, is still sometimes sent to prison is someone I cannot bring myself to say the state may jail — even though we jail tax evaders, and I have just compared conscription to taxation. So my question is what principle, if any, captures these verdicts. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with nothing before or after the list: (1) the most precise statement of the principle that best fits my verdicts; (2) the verdict it fits worst, and whether that verdict or the principle should give way; (3) what the principle says about the jailed total objector; (4) what it says about two cases I did not raise — a neutral state recruiting foreign volunteers into a professional force, and compulsory civilian service such as emergency hospital staffing during a crisis; and (5) the single sentence I should have said to my friend when she called conscription forced labor.
