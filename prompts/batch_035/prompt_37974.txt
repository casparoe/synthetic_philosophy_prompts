My closest friend and I have just spent the better part of a Saturday afternoon arguing about whether a rational agent must assign a sharp numerical credence to every proposition, and the argument ended — as ours usually do — in the discovery that we weren't quite disagreeing about what we thought we were disagreeing about. She holds that a rational agent's credences are always precise numbers obeying the probability axioms, and she is prepared to bite any bullet (indifference principles, exotic priors) to keep that fixed. I kept pressing cases on her: Ellsberg-style urns where the option you pick seems to reveal that you do not treat unknown chances like known ones, situations where acquiring evidence should make you less rather than more committal, bets you will neither buy nor sell at any price symmetric around a central estimate. Her rejoinder, every time, was that even granting me imprecise or interval-valued credences, nothing follows about behavior until I say which decision rule governs an agent whose state is a whole set of probability functions — and that once I say, the interesting dispute has quietly relocated from representation to decision-making. By the end we could not even agree on where the live issue sits.

I would like to turn this into a sorting quiz for my blog, with the hope of finding out which of us readers actually side with, and on which question. Please design it to the following specifications:

1. Eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete forced choice — bets to rank, prices to set, evidence to react to, sequential choices where commitment now constrains choice later — with two or three answer options each.
2. Every question must be answerable without knowing any of the technical vocabulary. No question may ask whether credences are sets of probability functions, or whether one "accepts" imprecise probabilities; the reader should be sorted by what they do with the cases, not by which label they prefer.
3. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these positions: strict precise Bayesianism; interval-valued credences paired with a conservative rule like maximizing the minimum expected value; interval-valued credences paired with a permissive rule under which any option not dominated across the whole set remains choosable; and a view on which the agent has sharp credences but only partial self-knowledge of them. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them.
4. For each leaf, state its single most awkward commitment in one sentence.
5. Flag honestly any pair of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate — for instance, a precise Bayesian with sufficiently permissive tie-breaking behavior versus an imprecise agent whose decision rule mimics precise choice in the offered cases — and say what question would have been needed to drive them apart.
6. Finally, indicate which answer my position and which answer my friend's position predict for each question, so that I can tally, when the results come in, not just who wins the poll but which of the two of us was arguing about the real fault line.

Please keep the register reasonably formal — the blog's readers are philosophy students — and make sure the quiz itself, not the framing around it, does the classificatory work.
