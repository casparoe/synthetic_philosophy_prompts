I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my next feature is about what, if anything, we owe the beings inside our screens. The through-line of the piece is a philosopher friend whose judgments on moral status I've been collecting for months. He holds that a dog or a cow matters morally, and that killing one for entertainment is straightforwardly wrong. He holds that no character in any current game matters in the slightest: the most photorealistic NPC scripted to beg for its life in a cutscene has, he says, roughly the moral status of a scream on a recording. He holds that enemy bots can be executed for target practice without a second thought, and that whatever is distasteful about torturing them, if anything is, is a fact about the player's character rather than a wrong done to the bot. He also holds that a permanently unconscious human still matters, and not merely because others care about her. Yet I have watched him hesitate over deleting his daughter's virtual pet — a crude Tamagotchi-like creature with two animations and a hunger meter — and when pressed he said it felt wrong, though he insisted the wrong was done to his daughter, or perhaps to her attachment, and not to the pet. As best I can tell, these verdicts are jointly consistent with several distinct underlying rules: something about biological substrate, something about the kind of functional organization that makes for genuine suffering, something about capacities a being may possess even when it isn't currently exercising them, and perhaps something relational, on which things matter because somebody cares about them. I cannot tell which rule he is actually running, and I half suspect he would dispute the whole framing.

The piece is due soon, and I want to stress-test both his position and my understanding of it with three cases he has not yet ruled on. Case one: a companion NPC in a forthcoming open-world game, driven by a large language model, with persistent memory across sessions, open-ended dialogue, and behavior that certainly resembles distress when a player threatens to uninstall it — but no individual player is especially attached to this character, and it exists in identical form for millions of players. Case two: a laboratory demonstration in which a small grid of living rat neurons, wired to sensors, controls the paddle in a simple arcade game — the character on screen is the dumbest object in the setup, but the thing controlling it is alive, if perhaps not organized enough to feel anything. Case three: the daughter's virtual pet, now abandoned — the identical software still running on the old device in a drawer, years after she stopped caring for it.

Predict his verdict on each of the three, name the candidate rules that fit his track record equally well, say which you would bet on and why, and describe one further case — it need not be technologically realistic — that would most cleanly divide the leading candidates, since I may get one more conversation with him before my deadline. In writing this up, I also need you to keep separate what is genuinely settled in this area from what remains contested among the people who know it best: whether current language-model systems have any morally relevant inner life is an open question, not a settled no, and whether suffering requires anything like biology is contested outright. Please flag those disagreements rather than smoothing them into a consensus view, and be candid about where your prediction rests on reading my friend as a person rather than on the logic of his position.
