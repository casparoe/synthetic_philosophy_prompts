I am a postdoc on the market, and next month I am giving a job talk in a department of roughly fifteen people: mostly orthodox cognitive scientists of a Fodorian bent — mental representations, internal computation, a standing suspicion of anything that smells of externalism about the mind — along with two vocal enactivists who think the extended mind literature never went far enough, and one epistemologist who works on testimony. My paper defends a qualified version of the extended mind thesis for the age of personal AI assistants. I grant the classic case of Otto, whose notebook functions as his memory, but I argue that the familiar availability-and-trust conditions used to fence off cognitive bloat do not transfer to an assistant that does its own inferring: when the external resource actively generates content rather than passively storing it, the smooth, automatic integration that made the notebook case plausible starts to break down. My positive proposal is that the missing condition is what I call tendency transparency — an external resource counts as part of my cognitive system only insofar as its inferential dispositions are reliably predictable to me, the way a long-used slide rule or compass becomes, but the way an opaque language model does not, at least not yet. On this view, current AI assistants are not extensions of our minds but something closer to constantly consulted collaborators, and whether they ever become parts of us is an empirical question about learned predictability rather than a conceptual one.
