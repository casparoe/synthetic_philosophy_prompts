I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm putting together a feature on the question of what a highly capable AI system should do if we simply ask it to act on what we want — the old alignment proposal known as coherent extrapolated volition, the idea that the machine should satisfy not our current, confused, contradictory wishes but the wishes we would have if we knew more, thought faster and clearer, and had grown up into the people we aspire to be. As part of my reporting I'm running a 90-minute session at a small ideas festival, roughly 22 adult participants with mixed backgrounds and no philosophy training, and I want the session to be an activity rather than a talk: participants should run into the hard problems themselves before anyone names them.

Concretely, design me a structured game with roles, rules, materials I can prepare on paper, and a timeline that fits the slot. I'd like it to make three things happen rather than be said aloud. First, participants should feel the gap between stated and idealized preferences: some of them should have to extrapolate another person's volition from a fixed record of choices and answers, without being able to interrogate the person. Second, the extrapolated result should come back to its subject for a verdict of "yes, that's me, only better" or "no, that's a stranger with better arguments" — I want the room to discover how unstable that verdict is. Third, the game should end with an aggregation step where the group's extrapolated volitions have to be merged into one coherent instruction that actually gets acted on: I can put a small real stake behind it, say a modest donation budget the final instruction must allocate, so the incoherence has a cost people can see. Include a debrief that maps the specific moments of the activity onto the parts of the idea, a candid note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and an honest statement about which feature of the real proposal the activity cannot reproduce no matter how I stage it — I suspect the competence asymmetry is the big one, since the whole point of the original idea is that the extrapolator is smarter than everyone in the room, and mine won't be.

One more thing: keep the participants in the dark about the philosophical pedigree until the final segment, so the debrief lands as recognition rather than as confirmation of something they were told at the start. Begin your response with a single sentence stating the core mechanic of the activity, then give the full design — no preamble, and don't restate my setup back to me.
