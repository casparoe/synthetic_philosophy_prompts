I am a recently retired physicist — thirty-odd years in laser spectroscopy and quantum optics — and in retirement I have been working through the philosophy of mathematics and physics more seriously than my training ever required. I have reached Thomson's lamp and find that I cannot let it go. I would like you to write an essay that adjudicates it for me, and I want to be upfront that I come to it with a suspicion, born of a lifetime in a lab, that the puzzle is an artifact of description rather than a fact about possibility. Please test that suspicion rather than flatter it.

The puzzle, as I understand it: a lamp begins off at time zero. The switch is pressed at t = 0, again one minute later, again half a minute after that, and so on, each interval halving, so that by the two-minute mark infinitely many presses have occurred. The presses are then over, and the lamp persists. Is it on or off at two minutes? The presses alternate the state, and an alternating sequence has no limit, so neither answer seems forced; yet the lamp must be in some state. Three positions strike me as live: (1) the description is coherent but simply fails to fix the final state — the state at the limit point is genuinely underdetermined by the states before it, and this is no more paradoxical than the fact that a converging sequence of values need not attain its limit; (2) the setup is covertly incoherent, and "the lamp at two minutes" is an empty description of nothing; (3) the question has an answer once one specifies the dynamics, and the felt paradox comes from leaving the switching mechanism unmodeled.

Here is the variant I want to anchor the essay in, because it is the one I can actually calculate. Replace the lamp with a two-level atom and the switch presses with resonant driving pulses, each pulse rotating the state by half a turn, with pulse durations halving so that the entire sequence fits inside a finite interval. At the level of the coarse-grained story — "each press toggles" — the final state looks underdetermined for exactly Thomson's reason. But the Schrödinger equation is continuous in time, and if you model the driving honestly, the state at the end of the interval is the exponential of the integral of the Hamiltonian, and the relevant quantity (the accumulated rotation angle) converges. The underlying continuous dynamics yields a perfectly determinate final state, and the toggling description was a coarse-graining that throws away the phase information which actually settles the matter. I can also imagine the mechanical analogue going the other way — a cam or a ratchet where impact dynamics leave genuinely no fact of the matter about the final configuration.

So the essay should weigh the three positions, using my atomic variant as a test case, and address at least these questions. Does the atom show that Thomson's lamp was never really indeterminate, or does it show only that one physically implementable refinement of the lamp is determinate, leaving the abstract puzzle untouched? Is there a principled difference between the quantum case, where the continuous dynamics is presumably the fundamental description, and the mechanical case, where it is not obvious anything continuous underlies the toggles? Does the fact that no laboratory device could execute the sequence — pulse generators have rise times, atoms decay spontaneously, thermal noise eventually dominates — bear on the modal question of whether such a supertask is metaphysically possible, or is that just an engineering complaint dressed up as philosophy? And more generally: when a mathematical description of a process has no well-defined limit, but a proposed physical instantiation of that process does have a well-defined limit, which one fixes what we were asking about?

Do not end non-committally. I want a verdict — that the final state is underdetermined in the abstract but determined relative to any honest dynamics, or that the supertask is incoherent, or something better than both — and I want it justified, with an honest account of what the verdict costs you. You need not cite literature; argue from the cases and the mathematics.
