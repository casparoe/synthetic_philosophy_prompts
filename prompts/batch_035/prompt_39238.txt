I'm a novelist and my editor keeps hitting me with this line from Plutarch's preface to his Life of Alexander, that it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives, and that "a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities." She used it most recently to argue I should cut an entire chapter covering a war my protagonist spends offstage, and instead show her hoarding letters or being rude at a dinner party. I sort of buy it and sort of suspect it's a bit of a dodge, and I'd like to know exactly what I'm buying. Can you lay out the different precise claims that "not histories but lives" could be making -- from the trivial (of course biography isn't chronology) to the substantive and contestable (that the great events are nearly worthless as evidence of character?) -- and for each say whether it's plausible and whether it would actually support my editor's use of it, i.e. trimming the record of what happened in favor of the small stuff? Bonus if you can say anything about whether Plutarch himself kept the promise, since I've been told he spends plenty of the Lives on campaigns and sieges himself, which would be a little funny given the slogan. Keep it as a list I can bring to our next call, she responds well to lists.
