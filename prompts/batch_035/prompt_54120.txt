I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form feature about whether consciousness can ever be a proper subject of laboratory science. The piece has come to revolve around Daniel Dennett's method of heterophenomenology: the proposal that scientists treat a subject's introspective reports as texts, interpret them as sympathetically as a translator would a foreigner's utterances, and posit the resulting "heterophenomenological world" as a theoretical construct, much as a physicist posits unobservable entities on the way to an eventual theory. One researcher I've interviewed champions what she calls "quantified heterophenomenology" — feeding subjects' report data into computational models and treating every divergence between what people say and what their eyes, hands, and neurons do (confabulation, inattentional blindness, change blindness) as signal rather than noise to be subtracted. Another, sympathetic to the neurophenomenology tradition, pushes back hard: she argues that heterophenomenology is not the neutral shared method Dennett advertised but a Trojan horse, because treating consciousness reports as merely more third-person data already presupposes Dennett's own conclusion that there is nothing to consciousness over and above what such data can capture.

I'd like you to help me think through this dispute before I write the piece. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind who has worked on the methodology of consciousness science, and think the matter through carefully before committing anything to the page — sketch the terrain privately first, then discard the scaffolding. The crux I want you to confront is not whether Dennett's theory of consciousness is true, but whether his method can be genuinely neutral between rival theories of consciousness, or whether any method for gathering data about consciousness must already take sides on what consciousness is. Note that on Dennett's own characterization the subject is authoritative only about their heterophenomenological world, not about what is actually going on in their mind — consider whether this concession is what makes the method workable or what makes it circular from the start.

Your response should be a compact essay in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no text outside the numbered list. The five points should do the following, in order: state the method precisely enough to distinguish it from mere behavioral report-collection; articulate the neutrality claim that is at stake; develop the strongest version of the circularity or deck-stacking objection; develop the strongest available reply; and finish with a clearly defended verdict on whether heterophenomenology can serve as the shared method for a science of consciousness, or whether the search for a neutral method is a mistake philosophers of mind should stop chasing.
