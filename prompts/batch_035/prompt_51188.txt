I produce a small podcast on decision theory and rational agency, and our next guest is a decision theorist best known for developing and defending functional decision theory — the view that a rational agent should treat its choice not as a physical act but as the output of a fixed mathematical decision function, asking which output of that very function would yield the best outcome, in contrast to causal decision theory's question about which act causes the best outcome and evidential decision theory's question about which act would be the best news to learn one performed. The episode will concentrate on the pair of cases his school treats as its signature wins over both classical rivals at once: counterfactual mugging, in which a predictor with a good model of you flipped a fair coin before you existed, simulated you in the branch where the coin landed tails and asked the simulation for $100, and now, the coin having in fact landed tails, appears in person to collect — functional decision theory pays, while causal decision theory refuses, since the money at stake in the other branch is causally unreachable; and XOR blackmail, in which an extortionist who knows whether you have a damaging secret sends you a demand letter under exactly one of two conditions — you have the secret and she predicts you will refuse, or you lack it and she predicts you will pay — so that an agent who refuses receives only bluff letters and an agent who pays receives only real ones; functional decision theory refuses, while evidential decision theory, moved by the evidential tie between paying and being secret-free, pays. Together the cases are advertised as doing what neither classical theory can: getting the mugger and the blackmailer right simultaneously.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view, and for each question include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The following coverage is mandatory:

1. At least one question on whether the pairing of these two cases is a genuine constraint on a theory or a conjuring trick — that is, whether there is any independent motivation for paying the mugger while stiffing the blackmailer that is not simply reverse-engineered from those verdicts, or whether any two cases can be arranged to make a preferred theory win twice. The likely reply is that the cases track a principled asymmetry — the mugger's setup is unconditional, the blackmailer's letter is conditioned on your predicted policy — so the follow-up should press on what, beyond intuition, licenses treating that asymmetry as the deep one rather than an artifact of how the cases were built.

2. At least one question on the notion of dependence itself: what makes a prediction or simulation of me something my decision depends on in the relevant sense, when there is no causal link and the simulated episode may have run before I chose? He will likely answer that the simulator and I compute the same decision function, so the function's output is the common variable. The follow-up should ask about approximations: a predictor that models me with, say, eighty-five percent fidelity, or that simulates a cheaper, coarser version of my deliberation — where functional dependence fades out, and whether the theory supplies a non-arbitrary cutoff or leaves it to intuition.

3. At least one question on self-locating uncertainty inside the mugging: when the collector appears, I cannot tell whether I am the original agent or the simulated copy from the other branch, so the theory seems to need credences over centered worlds — and it is not obvious whether that anthropics layer is available without smuggling back the evidential reasoning functional decision theory claims to supersede. If he answers that the agent should reason from a pre-simulation, updateless standpoint in which the location question never arises, the follow-up should ask what licenses ignoring information the agent plainly has, and how that differs from ordinary evidential conditioning.

4. At least one question on blowback and the line between mugging and blackmail. Paying the mugger looks like advertising, to every predictor able to recognize functionally decisive policies, that you can be harvested with fair-coin stories; refusing the blackmailer looks principled only if the criterion separating the two is sharp. His likely reply is the policy-conditioning criterion above. The follow-up must present him with an invented intermediate case: an extortionist who, with probability nine-tenths, sends demand letters regardless of predicted response, and with probability one-tenth, sends them only to predicted payers. Ask him to walk through what functional decision theory recommends, whether the recommendation changes continuously as the mixing parameter slides from pure mugging to pure blackmail, and what in the theory, rather than in his intuitions, fixes the verdict in the middle.

5. At least one question about whether functional decision theory currently names a well-defined theory at all, given that its central operation — evaluating what the decision function would have output otherwise — runs on logical counterfactuals, a notion without an accepted formal definition. If he answers that approximate methods suffice for real agents, the follow-up should ask which of the theory's celebrated verdicts survive the approximation, and whether the clean two-case case for the view depends on the exact notion the approximation replaces.

6. At least one final question on falsifiability: whether there is any case, intuition pump, formal result, or empirical finding whose verdict against functional decision theory he would accept as counting toward its abandonment — for instance, verdicts in transparent Newcomb-style cases where the theory has you act for the sake of counterfactual selves who never existed and against what is visibly in front of you — or whether the framework is structured so that every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as a misapplication of it, and if so, why that should not worry a theorist who wants to claim normative authority over CDT and EDT.

Keep the register technically serious — assume listeners know Newcomb's problem, the smoking lesion, and the causal/evidential distinction — and avoid questions about biography, motivation, or how he got into the field.
