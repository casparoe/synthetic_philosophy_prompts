For the humor issue of our graduate philosophy department's magazine, which this term is themed around population ethics, I'd like a satirical agony-aunt column, around 650 words, with a column title, tagline, and three or four short letters from readers answered by an invented columnist with a strident but warm persona. The columnist should be a strict and unwavering adherent of the procreation asymmetry: she holds that bringing into existence a person whose life would be worth living is neither good nor bad, that bringing into existence a person whose life would be worth avoiding is straightforwardly bad, and that the non-existence of happy merely-possible people is no tragedy at all. Crucially, she must stay perfectly consistent: she should never slip, even for a punchline, into treating the creation of a happy person as a positive good, or the failure to create one as any kind of loss, and she should never be caught accepting an argument that would embarrass her view, such as moving to total or average utilitarianism when the letters get hard.

Seed the column with letters of roughly these shapes: a couple expecting a child asks whether they're doing the world, or their future daughter, a favor by creating her — the columnist must explain, kindly, that one cannot benefit a person by bringing her into being, since there is no pre-existing subject of the benefit, while fending off their worry that this makes parenting pointless; a reader deliberating between conceiving now, when a manageable genetic condition means the child's life would hover just above the neutral line, or waiting a year for a likely-happier child, gives the columnist a chance to be hilariously untroubled by a choice that would drive a total utilitarian to distraction; and an angry adult with, by his own account, a genuinely good life demands an apology from his parents, allowing her to explain that the wrong would only have existed had his life been miserable, and that his complaint is structurally available only to a sibling he never had. If you have room, a fourth letter from an anti-natalist correspondent accusing her of running a pronatalist advocacy column would be a nice close.

The humor must come from getting the population ethics right rather than from mocking the view: the jokes should live in the machinery — the non-identity problem, the impossibility of harming the never-conceived, the strange neutrality of happy lives at the moment of their creation, the slogan about making people happy rather than making happy people, the lurking temptation toward the sadistic conclusion whenever she compares creating mediocre-but-worthwhile lives against adding suffering to existing ones — so that a philosopher working on Arrhenius-style impossibility theorems would recognize each letter as a familiar puzzle wearing a bathrobe, and would concede the satire is fair. She should remain deadpan and never wink at the reader that her position is odd; the oddness should speak for itself. A reader with no background in population ethics should still find the column funny as a portrait of an advisor whose ethical scruples have completely swallowed her bedside manner. Please format it as a proper column: the title and tagline at the top, then letters and responses alternating, with the letters signed in the style of newspaper advice columns.
