The debate over cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions seems to me to have reached a standoff rather than a resolution, and I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is. On one side are the experimentalists: Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich reported that intuitions about Gettier cases and about knowledge attributions varied with participants' cultural backgrounds, and Machery, Mallon, Nichols, and Stich later reported that participants with East Asian cultural backgrounds gave descriptivist-sounding answers to Kripke's Gödel case at substantially higher rates than Western participants. If the intuitions that anchor philosophical arguments are demographically contingent, they argued, the armchair method of taking one's own case judgments as evidence is in trouble. On the other side are critics with two quite different complaints. Some, in the vein of Deutsch and Cullen, argue that the framing is off because philosophers do not rely on intuitions in the first place: their judgments about cases are responses to arguments, or are otherwise theory-mediated, so demographic variation in untutored responses is beside the point. Others, in the vein of Williamson, accept that philosophers judge cases but deny that those judgments are a special, fragile kind of evidence whose reliability could be threatened by the mere fact of demographic variation -- ordinary perceptual evidence varies in quality across people too, and we do not conclude that perception is unreliable as a method. Meanwhile, replication attempts have come out mixed, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed camps in twenty years. My hypothesis is that the two sides are not disagreeing about a shared empirical question at all, but are answering different questions under one heading: a psychological question about how philosophical judgments are actually produced, and a normative question about which judgments are entitled to evidential weight in philosophy -- and that each side treats its own question as the one the debate is really about, which is why neither can lose. Is that right? Or is there a genuine shared empirical claim here that the mixed replication record does bear on, in which case the standoff is just an ordinary evidential mess? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: better instruments and larger samples, an agreed account of what intuitions are and whether philosophy needs them, a reformulation of the challenge the experimentalists meant to issue, or a demonstration that the challenge was never well posed?
