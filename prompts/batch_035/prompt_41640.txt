As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have recently read a trade book on the boundary between science and non-science, and its treatment of Popper and Darwin is the one passage I cannot get out of my head — partly because I am planning a piece of my own on the demarcation problem. The book's account, compressed, runs as follows. Popper, the arch-falsificationist, spent decades placing Darwin's theory of natural selection on the wrong side of his famous demarcation line. Since the fittest are simply those who survive, and the survivors are by definition the fittest, the principle of the survival of the fittest is circular — a tautology — and tautologies cannot be refuted by any possible observation. Darwinism, Popper concluded in the 1970s, is therefore not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical creed, and it belongs in the same company as the theories he most despised, Marxism and psychoanalysis, which likewise explain everything and predict nothing. The book then observes that after biologists protested, Popper grudgingly conceded the point in a short letter to a science magazine in the late 1970s, without withdrawing the substance of the criticism. Its closing flourish: whatever biologists maintain, evolution by natural selection is shielded from refutation by its own defenders, which is why the creationists were right to cite Popper, the century's greatest philosopher of science, against it — it is a matter of secular faith rather than science.

I know enough to suspect that this is neither straightforwardly accurate nor a caricature I can simply dismiss. Would you take the book's account apart piece by piece? Tell me what a philosopher of science would accept as correct — Popper did say something in the 1970s, in his intellectual autobiography, about natural selection not being a testable theory, and there is a later letter in which he announced a change of mind. Where does the book blur things? I have specific suspicions. First, was Popper's target natural selection as a mechanism or the historical fact of evolution, and does the book run these together? Second, did he really group Darwinism with Marxism and psychoanalysis, given what I have heard about his unusually high regard for Darwinism as a fruitful research programme? Third, are tautology and unfalsifiability the same complaint on his scheme, and what did his demarcation criterion actually exclude — I have read that he regarded metaphysical ideas as meaningful and sometimes immensely fertile, which sits oddly with the book's talk of a creed. Fourth, was the recantation as grudging and as hollow as the book implies, or did it involve a substantive rethinking of how historical sciences test their claims? Finally, and most important for my own article: does the book's conclusion — that evolution by natural selection is a faith rather than a science, and that the creationists cited Popper rightly — follow from whichever portions of the summary survive your audit? If some portion does survive, I want to know that as well; I would rather not publish a correction that overcorrects.
