A running argument with my housemates about whether a person can be too good has me keeping score of my gut reactions to actual people, and I'd like to know whether the verdicts add up to a principle or whether some of them have to go.

The verdicts: a colleague who gives away 30% of her salary, lives simply, and is still fun at parties — admirable, and I'd be glad to be more like her. A man I read about who gave away nearly everything, ate as cheaply as possible, and let his hobbies lapse because every hour on them felt stolen from the poor — I admire him more than almost anyone, yet I hope I never become him, and I feel a bit ashamed of hoping that. A friend who is flawless in small-scale morality — never gossips, corrects people's false beliefs even at awkward moments, never lets a small unfairness pass — I find him subtly worse company for it, which also embarrasses me. A neighbor who is kind and generous to friends but gives strangers almost nothing and spends a lot on his boat — I don't think he's failing; his life seems fine. Yet someone who gives nothing and is indifferent to others I do think is failing, even though he's nice enough to be around.

I suspect the answer is something like: morality sets a floor rather than a target, and once you're above the floor, further moral effort buys less than the personal life it costs. But I can't get that to cover the flawless friend: he is clearly above any floor, and yet my reaction isn't pure admiration the way it is for the 30% colleague, and the principle as I've stated it gives me no reason to distinguish them. So: is there a principle that saves all of my verdicts, or is the flawless-friend one (or the neighbor one — I wobble) the one to overturn? What does the surviving principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on, a parent who meets every duty to her kids decently but barely — never bad, never glowing — while pouring her real energy into travel and music? And please state how confident you are in the principle you land on, and single out the point at which you are least sure of it.
