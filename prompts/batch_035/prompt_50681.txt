For thirty-five years I was a condensed matter physicist, and my working vocabulary was built from things like ground states, degeneracy, spontaneous symmetry breaking, order parameters, nucleation barriers, metastability, and universality. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of technology, and I find that I can almost translate the debate over technological determinism into that vocabulary — but only almost, and I would like your help in doing it properly.

Restate the principal positions in the debate over the question Langdon Winner posed in his 1980 essay — whether artifacts have politics — in the vocabulary of my discipline, as faithfully as you can. By the principal positions I mean: (i) the deterministic claim that technical systems select, or demand, particular political arrangements, of which Winner's famous and much-disputed low overpasses on the Long Island parkways are the textbook case; (ii) the social constructivist rejoinder that the politics attributed to an artifact is merely the sediment of interpretive flexibility among relevant social groups — which sounds to me like an assertion that the ground state is massively degenerate, and that what we call the artifact is one among many locally equivalent configurations whose selection was fixed by initial conditions rather than by anything intrinsic; and (iii) the mutual-shaping compromise, which sounds like a claim that the system is driven far from equilibrium, such that neither the equations of motion nor the bath can be written independently of the other.

Take these one at a time. For each, say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in physicalism and present it as a finding, and where my vocabulary imposes a distortion, name the distortion.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Specifically: first, does anything in my vocabulary capture the normative core of Winner's claim — that an artifact can embody the interests of an identifiable group, such that the wrongness resides in neither the designers' intentions alone nor the object's geometry alone, but in the fit between the two? A broken symmetry records that a selection was made; it is silent on whose preference the selection expressed and on whether that preference ought to have governed, and I cannot find an operator for any of that. Second, am I right to think that Winner's second sense — systems compatible with only some social arrangements, his nuclear-power example being the one everybody reaches for — corresponds to a claim about the solution space: which stable solutions exist for a given set of constraints, so that the politics there is a fact about what equilibria are available rather than about any particular history? Third, is the residue I am left with — intention, interest, normativity — a defect of my vocabulary, or evidence that the philosophical thesis was less clear than it looked?

Above all, I want to know whether my working intuition is correct: that Winner's thesis, stripped of its rhetoric, is just the observation that technologies are frozen accidents — history-dependent selections among locally equivalent possibilities whose consequences persist and constrain later states — and that everything political in it is an anthropocentric gloss on which initial conditions happened to be realized. If that gloss is doing no real work, say so; if it is doing work that I cannot see from my side of the translation, tell me that instead.
