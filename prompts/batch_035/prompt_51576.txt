so last weekend my friend Dev stayed late after dinner and got into it with my roommate Keiko over spacetime, of all things. Dev's a physics grad student, Keiko reads a ton of philosophy and loves needling him. I was just there for the cookies but I ended up taking notes on my phone because it got pretty good. Here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Keiko said absolute space is a non-starter because its unobservable in principle — you could never do any experiment to find out whether the whole universe got shifted ten feet to the left overnight, so "absolute location" doesn't even mean anything. She said positing facts that make no difference to anything you could observe is just bad metaphysics (she'd apparently been reading Leibniz, though she said all this like she'd thought of it herself).

(2) Dev brought up Newton's bucket: hang a bucket of water from a rope, twist the rope, let go. Early on the bucket is spinning while the water is still basically at rest, so there's lots of relative motion between them and the surface is flat; later, once the water is spinning along with the bucket and there's almost no relative motion between them, the surface climbs up the sides. If all motion were relative, Dev said, this makes no sense — the water climbs because it's really rotating, not because it's rotating relative to anything in the room.

(3) Keiko was ready for that one: fine, it's rotating relative to the distant stars. All the mass out there sets what counts as non-rotating, so you don't need absolute space, you just need the rest of the universe. She even said if you could somehow spin the stars instead of the bucket, the water should climb the sides the same way (I think that bit is from Mach? she definately said it was).

(4) Dev said that's a nice story but it doesn't actually come out of general relativity — GR has solutions that are basically empty and still have perfectly good inertial structure; what counts as non-rotating comes from the metric / gravitational field itself, not from how much matter happens to be lying around. He admitted the situation is messy and there are special cases where rotation acts sort of relative to the matter distribution, but said Mach's full program basically failed.

(5) Keiko said fine, but then look what you've done: you renamed absolute space "the metric field" and kept everything that was supposed to be objectionable. It's still an invisible something that decides what's rotating and what isn't. If unobservability was your complaint, your inertial structure is unobservable too, so Newton's owed an apology. Dev said no — a gyroscope literally points along inertial structure, you can measure it, and that's a genuine difference from Newton's version, where absolute location makes no difference to anything measurable at all.

Then they started fighting about whether "you can measure it" was doing all the work and I lost the thread and everyone went home mock-angry. Can you adjudicate this move by move — who's right at each step, and is there something both of them are missing? I especially want to know whether Keiko's move 5 is as good as it sounded, because Dev kind of didn't have an answer to it and it didn't seem fair that she got the last word.
