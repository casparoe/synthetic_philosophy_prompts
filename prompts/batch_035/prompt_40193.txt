I sit on the volunteer board of the Milhaven Historical Society, and we're putting together the centerpiece exhibit for the town's sesquicentennial next year. Milhaven's whole identity is wrapped up in a founding story that everyone here grows up hearing: Elias Crane, the mill owner who built the town from nothing, paid for the school and the church out of his own pocket, and — this is the phrase you'll hear at any town function — treated his workers like family. The Crane name is on the library; there's a bronze statue of him on the green; the pageant every fourth of July ends with an actor in a frock coat reading to schoolchildren.

This winter, a graduate student volunteering with the society went through the county archive and the surviving company ledgers, and what she found doesn't fit the story. During the banking panic of the 1890s, Crane bought out struggling homesteaders at a fraction of what their land was worth, with the mill's store extending credit that kept them bound to it. The ledgers list children as young as ten on the mill floor during those same years Crane was endowing the school. And the "family" he treated his workers like turns out to include a strike in his second decade, broken with the help of imported replacements, after which several families left town entirely. Nobody on the board disputes the documents. The older members argue instead that the exhibit should tell our story as the town has actually lived and passed it down — that a ledger is one version of events, that the people who kept Crane's memory did so for a reason, and that a sesquicentennial is a celebration, not a trial. The student's reply is that an exhibit which quietly drops what the ledgers say isn't preserving anything; it's just advertising with older typography.

The board is split and, as it happens, I have something close to the deciding vote on the final panel text. What I want from you is advice on what the exhibit should actually say: where the town's remembered version and the documentary record conflict, which one should give way, and whether there's a way to hold onto both that isn't just mush in the middle. Take seriously both the older members' point about what a community's own memory is for, and the student's point about what it means to present something to the public as history. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the panel text, in essence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my situation.
