So I write novels for a living, which means people assume I've thought hard about what art is, and mostly I've gotten away with bluffing. But a painter friend of mine hit me with a take at dinner last week that I cant stop chewing on, and I'd like to know how much of it survives contact with actual philosophy of art. Her version goes roughly like this: after Duchamp stuck a urinal in an exhibition and called it Fountain, the philosophers basically gave up on beauty and craft as the markers of art, and the institutional view won — art is just whatever the artworld decides to treat as art, the way money is money because we all agree to treat it that way. She says Duchamp proved that literally anything can become art if the right people nominate it, that Wittgenstein had already shown with his family resemblance idea that art can't be defined anyway, and that this is why a banana duct-taped to a wall can sell for millions. Conclusion: the definition of art is a pure social fact, and getting worked up about whether something is really art is like getting worked up about whether a particular piece of paper is really a twenty.

I'm half inclined to agree — I do think the urinal was art, and I've written scenes I'd defend as art that have no beauty in them whatsoever. But the way she strung the pieces together felt slippery, and I don't know enough to say where. Can you walk me through her summary claim by claim? I want to know which parts a careful philosopher of art would accept, which parts are conflations or overstatements, and whether her punchline about the banana and the twenty-dollar bill follows from the parts that survive. The thing that's bugging me most: the institutional view sounds like a definition of art — a social one, sure, but still an answer to the question — while the Wittgenstein bit sounds like the claim that no definition is even possible. Those seem like opposite positions to me, but she treated them as one smooth argument, and neither of us knew enough to spot where the seam was.
