I've had an argument stuck in my head for a week now and I want to actually figure out what the parties were even disagreeing about. I was listening to a podcast where a guest, clearly channeling Dennett, dropped the line that consciousness is a "user illusion" -- the idea being that what you experience is like a computer's desktop, a convenient interface full of icons (the trash can isn't really a trash can, the file isn't really a folder-shaped thing inside the machine) that hides the machinery rather than revealing it. Fine so far. But then the host used it to completely shut down a caller who asked why there's "something it is like" to see red at all -- the host basically said the question is malformed, it's like a character in the interface asking why the desktop icons are the "real" electronics, and there's no residual mystery once you see that. My roommate, who's the one who got me into this stuff, thinks that move is a sleight of hand, and his counterargument is the obvious one: an illusion is normally something that is experienced, so an illusion of consciousness seems like it needs consciousness to be an illusion *of* anything. Who or what is the "user" being fooled, if the self doing the experiencing is also supposed to be part of the interface?

So what I'd like is a careful unpacking of the slogan itself rather than a defense of either side. What are the different precise claims "consciousness is a user illusion" could actually be making, ordered from the safe/trivial to the radical? Like, at the weak end maybe it just means introspection is unreliable and we confabulate a lot, which almost everyone accepts; somewhere in the middle maybe it means our self-model deliberately misrepresents the brain's architecture because a simplified interface is more useful for behavior; and at the far end maybe it's the eliminativist-sounding claim that the features we think we introspect -- the private, ineffable "feel" of things -- don't exist at all, the way the desktop really contains no folders. For each reading I want to know whether it's actually plausible, and crucially whether *that* reading is strong enough to justify what the host did with it, i.e. declaring the "why does it feel like anything" question confused rather than merely hard. It seems to me like only the most radical reading gets him his conclusion, and that reading is the one my roommate's regress objection bites on -- but I'm not sure, since maybe "illusion" here just means "useful simplification" rather than "false appearance," and if nothing is strictly *falsely represented* maybe the regress never starts. Also worth covering: if the self is part of the interface too, does the slogan end up undermining itself, or is that fine because interfaces are allowed to represent themselves?

One more thing, and please take it seriously: the people who push this line talk as if it's just science plus common sense and the other side is confused, and the people who push back talk as if the user-illusion thing is an obvious dodge, and I genuinely can't tell how much of this is settled versus a live fight. So when you lay out the readings, keep separating what's basically agreed upon (confabulation research, the general interface analogy, whatever) from what is actually contested among philosophers and scientists who know this literature -- e.g., whether the "hard problem" framing survives, what exactly Dennett-style views deny, whether the term "illusion" is doing coherent work or is just a rhetorical upgrade of "simplified model." Don't present the host's move as the obvious consequence of the slogan if what it really depends on is the most contestable version of it. And if some of my framing above is itself confused (like maybe the regress objection has a standard reply that my roommate and I both missed), flag that too rather than papering over it.
