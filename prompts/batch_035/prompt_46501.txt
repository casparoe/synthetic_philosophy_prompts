Here is the student's answer, copied exactly:

"The urn has 30 red balls and 60 black or yellow in unknown proportion. When offered bets that pay out on a single color, most people bet on red rather than black, and when offered bets that pay out on the union of two colors, most people bet on black-or-yellow rather than red-or-yellow. If we assign subjective probabilities, the first preference requires p(red) > p(black). The second requires p(black) + p(yellow) > p(red) + p(yellow), hence p(black) > p(red). These requirements are inconsistent, so no single probability assignment reproduces both choices, and the preferences violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory. What this reveals is the difference between risk, where the probabilities are known, and ambiguity, where they are not. People prefer to bet on the color whose odds are objectively fixed, which is called ambiguity aversion. This was first formalized by Kahneman and Tversky in prospect theory, where the weighting function overweights small probabilities, and since the observed choices can be reproduced with decision weights, there is no real violation of rationality here — the subjects were expected-utility maximizers all along, just over non-additive probabilities, so the 'paradox' is mostly a matter of labeling."
