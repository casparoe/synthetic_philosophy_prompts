so I'm a science and ideas writer at a mid-size american outlet, and something about how my newsroom talks about readers has been bugging me for months. my editor talks about "the public" like it's a single creature with a mind: the public is curious about AI, the public has lost trust in scientists, the public deserves to know. we have an engagement team whose whole job is hosting what everyone calls "the conversation" — comment sections on some stories, reader polls, little widgets asking what you think, a dashboard counting shares. meanwhile on my beat we're obsessed with survey research, and every other week there's a story about how "americans'" trust in science is falling, like there's one attitude out there we can just read off a crosstab. so the institution i work in basically assumes there is a real thing, the public, that has attitudes, that can be measured, and that our job is to serve.

then this winter i finally read kierkegaard's 1840s rant about the press and the public — i want to say it's the essay people usually cite as the one about the present age, someone correct me if that's the wrong text — and it landed weirdly hard. as i understood him, the public is not a real thing at all: it's a kind of phantom that the press itself conjures up, an abstraction that levels everyone down into spectators. no actual person is the public, and the public is nobody — and the more press there is, the more this nobody gets treated as the authority on everything, which is very convenient, because no actual person ever has to be responsible for what the public supposedly thinks. he was writing about cheap 1840s copenhagen papers and i'm sitting here with an engagement dashboard, but it felt uncomfortably close.

so here's what i want worked out: what view of what a public *is* does the modern practice actually presuppose? spell out the candidate theories, because i don't think our newsroom has one — like, is the public an aggregate of individual minds that polls sample (basically a statistical fiction but a useful one)? or a genuine collective entity over and above the individuals, something like a group with its own beliefs? or something weirder and more performative, where the public doesn't exist until the publication convenes it, and the metrics and comment sections are partly creating the thing they claim to measure? because i notice our practice is kind of inconsistent about this: we shut off comments on the stories people actually care about (so we clearly don't fully believe in deliberation), but we still publish "a conversation with our readers" pieces; we treat polls as passive measurements of the public's mind, but we also run framing and headlines that we know will move it. so which of these views do the different moving parts presuppose, and do they fit together at all?

and then the kierkegaard angle: does his phantom-public critique actually apply to us, or does it only work against the idea of the public as an authority — such that an aggregate-view newsroom can shrug him off (no one thinks the average respondent is anyone) while a conversation-view newsroom is in real trouble? i'm guessing the practice is underdetermined in places, i.e. several theories of the public would license the exact same dashboard, and i'd like to know where. and finally, if a paper took kierkegaard seriously — if it believed convening a phantom public makes everyone a spectator and nobody responsible — what would it actually have to change? would it just be more letters-to-the-editor, or something structurally different?

sorry for length, this is going in a piece, and i can't write 2000 words on it till i know what the positions even are. pls lay out the theories clearly, tell me which parts of what we do presuppose which, and be honest about where the philosophical payoff runs out — i don't want to dress up a management problem as kierkegaard if it's not one.
