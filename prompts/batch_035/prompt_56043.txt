I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level philosophy of law course, and the term paper requires a two-page research proposal before any drafting begins; the paper itself will run about 6000 words over one semester. I'd like you to write that proposal for me, developing a half-formed idea I have about the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism.

My idea is this. The standard telling of the debate treats Devlin's core empirical premise — that a society's shared morality is a kind of fabric, and that tolerating deviations from it erodes that fabric, with the erosion eventually threatening society itself — as either obviously true or obviously false, depending on one's sympathies. I suspect the more interesting question is what sort of claim this really is. If it is a causal claim, it should be possible to reformulate it so that its failure conditions become visible: for instance, as a claim about thresholds, on which a society can absorb a continuous increase in tolerated deviance up to some point and then collapses discontinuously — or, alternatively, as a claim about continuous decay, on which every increment of tolerated deviance does proportional damage. Hart seems to have assumed the second reading when he objected that Devlin's argument, if it worked, would license suppressing any nonconformity whatsoever, including eccentricities nobody thinks dangerous; Devlin, for his part, never separated the two readings, and his critics have mostly followed suit. My working hypothesis is that once the readings are separated, the threshold version is the only one that could possibly support legal moralism, but that it cannot be made both non-trivial and testable, since familiar historical cases of drastic change in a society's enforced morality — the abandonment of enforced religious observance, the repeal of Prohibition, the decriminalization this very debate was fought over — look on inspection like cases of a society changing rather than disintegrating, and it is hard to say what evidence could ever distinguish the two descriptions.

The proposal should include: a precise statement of the research question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer; an explanation of why the standard positions leave it open — covering Devlin's legal moralism as he defended it before and after Hart's response, Hart's liberal reply, and in particular Hart's distinction between positive and critical morality together with his insistence that the mere fact a majority disapproves of conduct cannot show that tolerating it destroys anything of value; a method section saying concretely what I would do month by month, which should center on building a taxonomy of fabric-claims (constitutive claims, on which shared morality is partly what makes the population a society at all; instrumental claims, on which it merely serves social functions; and threshold claims, as above) and then testing each against the historical cases, using no archival or empirical work beyond what a philosophy student can do from the library; a statement of what a successful outcome would look like and, just as importantly, what would count as the project failing — including the failure mode I most fear, that the taxonomy collapses into instrumental claims which turn out to be straightforward consequentialism about offense and externalities, which would make my paper a disguised restatement of the harm principle debate rather than something new; and an honest assessment of whether all this is really achievable in a semester, with a named fallback if it is not.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add the references myself. Refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by footnote. Keep the tone formal and academic, as befits a document my instructor will grade.
