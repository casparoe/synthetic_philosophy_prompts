I've been reading about the Valladolid debate of 1550–1551 — the junta Charles I convened in Spain, at the Colegio de San Gregorio, to adjudicate whether the conquest of the Indies was just — and I want to work through it as a test case for Aristotle's theory of natural slavery. On one side was the humanist Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, who argued from his Latin dialogue Democrates Alter that some Indigenous peoples could not govern themselves, that they were by nature subject to subjugation, and that war to impose Spanish rule (and to stop human sacrifice and cannibalism) was justified. On the other was Bartolomé de las Casas, the Dominican friar and bishop of Chiapas, who had spent decades among the encomienda system and who argued — crucially, on partly Aristotelian ground — that the peoples of the Americas were fully capable of reason: they had cities, laws, commerce, calendars, languages. If they met the description of beings who deliberate, the Aristotelian category simply did not fit them. Famously, the junta issued no clean verdict, and both camps later claimed victory.

What I'd like is a case analysis that runs in both directions. First, theory to case: reconstruct Aristotle's account of the natural slave precisely enough to do the work — the criterion of perceiving but not having reason, the slave as a living tool and part of the master, the claim that such rule benefits both parties, and the distinction between natural slavery and the merely conventional slavery that arises when the strong enslave those who don't deserve it. Then tell me whether Sepúlveda's use of that machinery is a faithful application or a distortion. Two things especially: Aristotle seems to locate the master–slave relation within the household, and it isn't obvious that his framework supplies the extra premise Sepúlveda needed — a right of one commonwealth to wage war on another and rule its members. And Aristotle himself says that natural slaves who are ruled unjustly know it, which looks like it gives Las Casas an internal criterion the junta could actually check.

Then, case to theory: what does the episode show about Aristotle's theory that its own texts cannot? I'm struck that both sides claimed overlapping authorities, that the disagreement turned on an empirical-looking criterion (does this population participate in reason?) that in practice functioned as a rubber stamp for whoever was already holding the whip, and that a panel of doctors and theologians, given two years, could not make the theory decide the question in front of it. So I want your honest view on whether the New World was a genuinely new test case the theory never anticipated, or whether it simply exposed a circularity that was there all along in the criterion for natural slavehood — with "benefit" defined by the ruler's standard, the theory risks becoming unfalsifiable, and Las Casas may have won the debate only by converting Aristotle's descriptive claim into a checkable one. Where is the theory genuinely silent here, and where does forcing it to speak do violence to it?
