I've spent the last few years in computational neuroscience, and I've ended up committed to a fairly strong version of computationalism: that the brain doesn't merely resemble a computer as a metaphor or a useful modeling convenience, but literally implements computations — that there is a fact of the matter about which formal system the brain implements, over which representations, such that if we knew enough we could say the brain computes X rather than Y, the way we can say what a running spreadsheet computes. My partner, who works on dynamical systems in physics, thinks this is confused: she says the brain is a continuous, noisy, chaotic physical system whose "states" are our descriptions, not its own, and that computationalism survives only by helping ourselves to whatever grain of description makes the thesis come out true. We've been going in circles for months.

So I want the strongest honest map of my view's exposure. Tell me what would actually count against strong computationalism, not just the standard objections, but the specific things it stands or falls with. I'd particularly like you to address: (1) the implementation or triviality problem — Putnam's old result that almost any open physical system can be mapped onto almost any finite automaton if the mapping is unconstrained, and Searle's version of it with the wall that implements a word processor; I've always leaned on the reply that implementation requires the right counterfactual causal structure, but I want to know whether that reply really closes the gap or just relocates it, and whether noisy, chaotic neural dynamics can even support the counterfactual transitions the reply needs; (2) the objection from biology — analog graded potentials, spike timing, neuromodulation, the possibility that the relevant state variables are continuous and not cleanly decomposable into the kinds of discrete states formal computation requires; (3) the Gödel-style arguments that human mathematical insight outruns any Turing machine, which I suspect are the weakest of the lot but want an outside check on; and (4) the worry that computationalism has been insulated by redefinition — from symbolic computation to connectionist computation to whatever predictive-processing stories require — until it becomes a thesis that couldn't fail, and whether that's a defect in the view or just how research heuristics work. Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and distinguish what I, as a committed computationalist, would genuinely concede counts against the view from what I would be entitled to dismiss as missing the point.
