In the philosophy of language and formal epistemology, David Lewis's 1976 triviality results showed that a seemingly innocent assumption — that the probability of an indicative conditional if A, C equals the conditional probability P(C|A) — combined with the assumption that conditionals are ordinary propositions embedded in a classical probability space, collapses the probability distribution into triviality: for arbitrary A and C, one is forced to conclude that P(C|A) = 1, or P(¬C|A) = 1, or P(A) = 0. I hold a position that purports to escape this, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it.

The position, stated together with its qualifications: indicative conditionals are truth-conditional propositions; they bear probabilities of their own; and for a restricted class of conditionals — atomic ones, with no conditional embedded in the antecedent or consequent, evaluated against a single fixed context and background — the Ramsey equation P(if A, C) = P(C|A) holds exactly. What dissolves the triviality argument, on my view, is the restriction to a fixed context: Lewis's construction iterates the equation across transformed distributions — conditioning on the antecedent, its negation, the consequent — and I maintain the equation is simply not preserved under those transformations, because conditioning changes the context, and the transformed distribution assigns probabilities to different conditional propositions. The equation is local, not global.

Please give me an honest map of the exposure of this position. Specifically:

1. Which observations, arguments, or formal results would count against it, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward. I am especially interested in whether the position stands or falls with commitments in other areas — for instance: whether the compounding behavior of conditionals is part of the data that any account must respect (what ordinary speakers do with if (if A, C), D, or with negated and disjoined conditionals); whether ordinary speakers' willingness to assign probabilities to conditionals with probability-zero antecedents, as in lottery cases, is admissible counterevidence; whether betting-price and Dutch-book arguments for conditional probabilities transfer to probabilities of conditionals; and whether there is any principled, non-ad-hoc way to individuate contexts finely enough to block Lewis's iterations while coarsely enough that we may still say the same conditional was evaluated before and after an update.

2. Which of these defeaters a committed defender of the position would concede as genuinely counting against it, and which they would dismiss, and on what grounds.

3. An honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its restrictions — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind, or simply the correct response to a formal result that was never about ordinary language in the first place.

The deliverable I want is the map of exposure, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list; assume I am familiar with the standard escape routes (no-truth-value views of the Adams tradition, Stalnaker-style possible-worlds semantics, restrictor treatments of conditionals, and the move to primitive conditional probabilities).
