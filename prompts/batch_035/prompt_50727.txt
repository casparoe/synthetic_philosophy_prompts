So a few weeks back I got into a long argument on a global poverty thread that mutated into a fight about luck egalitarianism, and now it's all I can think about. The starting point seemed obvious: inequalities are fine when they trace to choices and unjust when they trace to luck, so a just society compensates people for bad luck and leaves them to eat the consequences of their choices. Sounds like common sense, carve reality at the joints, etc. Then my friends and I started sorting actual cases at dinner and discovered we don't agree with each other, or really with ourselves, once you push past the slogans. I want to do this properly — actual reflective equilibrium, not vibes — so I wrote down my gut verdicts and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

The list: A logger paralyzed by a rotten tree that no inspection could have caught — full compensation, obviously. A logger paralyzed because he blew off safety training he received and understood — nothing, that one's on him. A surfer who could've been a corporate lawyer but prefers waves and beer — no support payments, even though he ends up poor. A guy who bets his life savings at a casino knowing the odds — no bailout, that's what "option luck" is for. A woman born with a degenerative disease needing expensive treatment — full coverage. But here's where it gets messy: a woman who starts smoking at 14 in a town where everybody smokes, parents included, and gets the same disease at 50 — my gut wants to help her almost as much as the congenital case, even though "choice" is in the causal chain somewhere. And the pair I flatly cannot reconcile: a rich hedge fund guy who loses a leg heli-skiing off-piste gets nothing from the public pot (correct, his problem), but a poor guy who loses a leg taking a dangerous roofing job because it was either that or his family misses rent — I want to compensate him fully. Both chose the risk with eyes open. Both knew the odds. I keep trying to taboo the word "choice" and see what's left underneath, and what's left is doing a lot of quiet work on things like who can afford to be prudent.

So, is there a single principle that fits all this — something like Dworkin's choice/circumstance split, or brute-vs-option luck, or "responsible only if you had genuine opportunity to do otherwise" — and if not, which of my verdicts has to go? I'd rather overturn one or two of them than bolt on epicycles, but I want you to be honest about which ones any clean principle forces me to drop, rather than quietly smuggling the dropped verdict back in through a "genuine opportunity" clause that swallows everything. Also please tell me what the principle says about two cases I didn't list: the guy whose natural talent made him rich (the talent itself being pure genetic luck — do we tax that as luck or leave it as his?), and children, who never chose anything at all, up to and including their parents. My working theory is that the whole view collapses once you trace choices back far enough, but it keeps feeling locally right, and I can't tell if that's a fact about justice or just about how my intuitions were trained. Definately want a real verdict at the end, not "it's complicated."
