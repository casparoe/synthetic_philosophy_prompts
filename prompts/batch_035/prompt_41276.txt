I'm a PhD student in machine learning, working on monitoring deployed neural networks for behavior that wasn't present during training, and I've hit a recurring disagreement I don't have the vocabulary for. My group keeps asking whether, in some new situation, a model is "still using the same mechanism" it used in training. The phrase sounds well-defined, but when I press people on it, it gets cashed out in at least four different ways, and the choice between them changes the verdict:

1. Same internal route. The same intermediate computations are doing the work — the same kind of attention-head pattern, say, still gives rise to the output, even if the input distribution has drifted. On this reading, a model can switch to a completely different internal pathway while producing the same outputs, and that counts as a new mechanism.
2. Same input–output profile. If the model's behavior on a battery of probes is unchanged, the mechanism is "the same" for our purposes, whatever is going on inside. On this reading, internal rewiring with stable behavior is not a new mechanism, which is exactly the opposite verdict from (1) in the same case.
3. Same parameters. Fine-tuning nudges every weight slightly, so strictly speaking nothing is "the same" after any update, yet nobody wants to say every fine-tune creates a new mechanism. So this criterion is obviously too strict as stated — but it's not clear what relaxation of it we're implicitly using.
4. Stable relations under shift. Some internal relation (between features, or between features and output) held in training and still holds in the new setting, where "holds" means roughly that it would still hold if we perturbed things further. This one is the least crisp but seems closest to what we actually care about.

A concrete instance: we fine-tuned a classifier on additional data, and two colleagues disagreed about whether a previously monitored behavior was preserved. One said yes, because the model's outputs on our evaluation suite were unchanged; the other said no, because the cues the model relied on had visibly shifted from one input feature to another. Both were looking at the same facts; they were simply using different criteria for sameness.

So my question is this. Is there an established body of work that addresses this directly — what it takes for one system at two different times (or two systems) to count as implementing the same mechanism, at a grain coarse enough that it's not just "same parameters" and fine enough that it's not just "same behavior"? I'd expect the nearest concepts to come from wherever this question has been faced before, possibly outside machine learning, but what I need to know is, for each candidate: what it actually claims, and precisely where it diverges from my case — where it draws the line in the wrong place, either too coarsely (collapsing internal rewiring into "same") or too finely (splitting things we'd want to call the same). If nothing established captures the notion, please coin a term for it, give a precise definition, and include two or three boundary cases that show where the line falls.

Please structure the reply in four sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:

- "The question, restated" (~120 words): say back in your own words what I'm asking for, so we can check we're aligned before you answer.
- "Nearest existing concepts" (~350 words): the closest established ideas, each with a short statement of what it actually says — enough that I can tell them apart.
- "Why each falls short" (~250 words): for each candidate, the specific respect in which it mis-carves my case.
- "A working definition" (~200 words): either the concept that fits best, or a newly coined term with a definition and two or three boundary cases.

No need for references or reading lists; I'm after the carving, not the citations.
