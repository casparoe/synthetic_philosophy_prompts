I'm a PhD student in epistemology writing a dissertation chapter on the methodology of our field, and I've become convinced that the most interesting fact about the Gettier problem is not any particular solution to it but the shape of the sixty-year stall: an unbroken cycle of proposed fourth conditions, stock counterexamples, and retrenchment to weaker claims. The pattern, briefly: no-false-lemmas conditions fall to stopped-clock cases where every lemma is true; defeasibility conditions fall to barn-facade cases where the subject possesses no hint of a defeater, unless mere possibilities count as defeaters, in which case knowledge is threatened everywhere; sensitivity and safety handle the barns but struggle with lottery propositions, where one's belief that one's ticket will lose is safe yet, intuitively, not knowledge; virtue-theoretic conditions require that cognitive credit be undefiled by luck, but the stock cases mix credit and luck in proportions we cannot seem to make precise. The knowledge-first program draws the anti-analytic moral -- knowledge is primitive, stop trying to analyze it -- and yet the question of what Gettier cases are tracking reappears within that program as the question of why a knowledgeable subject differs from her nearby lucky twin. Nobody on any side budges, and the field treats this as normal.

I'd like a structural diagnosis of the impasse rather than another entry in the counterexample exchange. My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed: 'knowledge' is a cluster concept with at least two functions that travel together in ordinary environments -- recording a species of cognitive achievement, and licensing reliance in assertion, testimony, betting, and action. Gettier cases are exactly the thin set of scenarios where the functions come apart: in some, the subject has the achievement but not the modal security (the barns); in others, the modal security is arguably present but the achievement is polluted by a false premise (the original cases). Our verdicts oscillate depending on which function we take ourselves to be asked about, and the sixty-year search for a single set of necessary and sufficient conditions is a search for a joint that is not there.

Three questions. First, is this diagnosis right, or is the impasse better explained as methodological disagreement about whether intuitions about cases are data at all -- in which case progress would come from work on the reliability of intuition, not from better conditions -- or as a genuinely substantive dispute about the extension of one concept, in which case we should expect eventual convergence and the stall merely reflects difficulty? Second, if the cluster hypothesis is right, what would count as progress here: a stipulative bifurcation of the concept, an error theory explaining why one word was made to do two jobs, or an account of the environments -- testimony networks, lotteries, courts -- where the two jobs come apart and the concept fails in ways we can characterize independently of Gettier intuitions? Third, is the knowledge-first rejection of the analytic project best read as a tacit version of my diagnosis, or as a genuine alternative to it? I want the diagnosis supported by how the standard conditions actually behave in the stock cases, and an honest verdict on whether anything could end this debate -- a distinction, a formal result, a revisionary proposal -- or whether the counterexample mill should be expected to run indefinitely, and what, if anything, that would show about the concept we have been studying.
