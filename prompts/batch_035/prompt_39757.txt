I'm teaching a philosophy-of-mind elective to upper-level undergraduates on the question of whether machines can mean what they say, and the last unit is on translation and understanding. We've read Douglas Hofstadter's much-discussed attack on Google Translate — the essay where he feeds it poems, idioms, jokes, and culturally loaded phrases and argues that it produces fluent output by pure surface pattern-matching, with no model at all of the situations, images, and intentions the words are about. His positive claim is that genuine translation is inseparable from understanding: a translator carries across a web of allusion, tone, and purpose, and a system with none of that only simulates translation. For the final session I want to screen Arrival (Villeneuve, 2016), adapted from Ted Chiang's novella "Story of Your Life," and I'd like a reading of the film through Hofstadter's argument.

At first glance the film looks like a vindication of him: Louise's progress comes not from crunching correlations but from working out what the heptapods could be trying to do — what their writing is for, how their physics of least action is baked into their semasiographic script, why they bothered to come. Understanding and translating fuse so completely that learning the language rewires the translator's experience of time itself. But I suspect the fit isn't clean. Hofstadter's target is a system that translates *successfully* at the surface while understanding nothing; the film's dramatic failures, like the mistranslated logogram that reads as an offer of a weapon, are failures between interlocutors who understand their own utterances perfectly — a pragmatic and diplomatic breakdown, not a shallowness problem. And the scenes where Ian's team makes real headway on the written logs by treating the glyphs as combinatorial data sit oddly with a film that otherwise insists translation requires inhabiting a conceptual scheme. So: does Arrival dramatize Hofstadter's point about deep translation, or does it quietly change the subject from understanding without fluency to fluency without shared purposes? Does its Sapir-Whorf conceit — the translator transformed by the language — support Hofstadter's insistence that meaning is carried across only by a mind, or undermine it by suggesting that what matters isn't understanding but the formal structure of the symbol system?

I'd like a reading that takes both Hofstadter's argument and the film seriously and says where each resists the other, rather than reducing the film to an illustration of the essay. Please point me to two or three specific scenes or plot turns that would anchor the discussion in class, flag anything the film gets philosophically muddled that I should warn the students about, and end by telling me what you think the film adds to the debate over machine translation that the essays and the Chinese-Room-style arguments on their own don't — and whether you'd teach Chiang's novella differently from the film on this point.
