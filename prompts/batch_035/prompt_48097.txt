Write a satirical commodities-desk column, roughly 500–600 words, from a world in which privacy is a mined, refined, and traded natural resource. Give it the standard furniture of a daily futures round-up — spot prices, the day's movers, boilerplate about conditions in the fields, one quoted analyst with an invented name and firm — but the commodity being traded is control over personal information.

The humor must come from getting privacy theory right rather than inventing it. In particular: price information the way contextual-integrity theorists think about it, so that a pharmacy purchase, a dinner-table remark, or a late-night search is worth different amounts depending on the context it ends up in, with the biggest fortunes made on arbitrage between contexts; make bundles of individually worthless fragments command premium prices once aggregated, so that the holdout who insists he has nothing to hide and sells only trivial facts goes quietly broke; offer forward contracts on the secrets of people who have not yet done the things they will later want hidden, so that the market literally prices the chilling effect on who one might become; and include a bank run on some unfortunate soul's reserve of obscurity after a leak. Let one analyst explain, deadpan, that a fully transparent world would be worthless to everyone, since you cannot trade what nobody has the power to withhold — the market's entire existence depends on the surveillance it complains about.

Stay deadpan and venue-appropriate: the column never winks at the reader, and the satire lands through the market logic rather than through editorializing about surveillance. It should be funny and fair to someone who knows the privacy literature, and merely strange to someone who doesn't. You may invent tickers, exchanges, and units of measure, but every joke should be an exaggeration of a real feature of privacy, its economics, or its value — not an invented one.
