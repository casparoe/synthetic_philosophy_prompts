I'm a final-year PhD student in political science, and in two weeks I'm presenting a dissertation chapter at an interdisciplinary workshop on the long-term future of the international order. The room will be about twenty people: mostly historians of empire and grand strategy who have spent their careers on the rise and fall of great powers and who regard assigning a probability to hegemonic decline as somewhere between a category error and malpractice; two or three quantitative political scientists who are friendly to numbers but suspicious of priors pulled from history; and one retired intelligence analyst who produced national-style estimates for decades and has strong views about how badly governments have abused historical analogy.

The chapter's claim: any judgment about whether the United States will still hold primary-power status in 2075 should be anchored in a base rate from a reference class of comparable episodes, not in inside-view reasoning about this particular moment. I've coded around two dozen episodes since about 1500 in which a state held clear primacy — Habsburg Spain, the Dutch Republic, France under Louis XIV and again under Napoleon, Britain twice, the United States since 1945, and so on — and the distribution of episode durations is not kind to the assumption that the current one just continues. My further argument, and this is the part that will land badly, is that the historian's instinct to respond with distinguishing detail — nuclear weapons, global finance, the difference between territorial and maritime hegemony, the alliance network — mostly amounts to choosing the reference class after the fact so that it delivers the answer one already wanted, and that only fairly specific evidence should license a big move away from the base rate.

Help me get ready for the question period. What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of the chapter: the objections this particular room will actually raise — phrased as these historians would phrase them, with their vocabulary of periodization, contingency, and over-determination, not as a decision theorist would — ranked by how much trouble each would really cause me. I'd expect somewhere in the range of six to nine of them. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is thin — tell me which objections I should concede ground on rather than fight, because conceding some of them cheaply is probably how I survive the session. Then there's the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, the one that goes after the weakest joint in my coding scheme itself rather than at the general idea of reference classes — I want to see it stated plainly so I can at least rehearse an answer. Please keep the entire prep sheet under 800 words; I want something tight enough to actually drill from.
