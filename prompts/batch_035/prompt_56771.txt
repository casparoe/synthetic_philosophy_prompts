One student wrote:

"The Darwinian dilemma is an argument against moral realism developed by Richard Joyce in his work on the evolution of morality. It begins from the claim that our basic evaluative attitudes — disapproval of cruelty, a positive attitude toward reciprocity, and so on — were fixed by natural selection, and that selection favored them because of their effects on reproductive fitness rather than because they were true. Given that starting point, the realist faces two options. On the first horn, the independent moral facts themselves influenced which attitudes selection favored. There is nothing in principle wrong with this horn: selection is guided by moral facts in the same way it is guided by facts about nutrition, so the realist can simply accept it and hold that evolution led us toward moral truth. On the second horn, moral facts had no influence on selection. Then the specific contents of our moral beliefs were fixed by fitness considerations that are indifferent to moral truth: we would have had exactly the beliefs we have whether or not any of them were true. Since it would be an enormous coincidence if beliefs formed in this way happened to be mostly true, it is overwhelmingly likely that our moral beliefs are false, and realism is thereby refuted.

This is not a mere genetic fallacy. The genetic fallacy would be inferring that a belief is false from the mere fact that it has some causal origin, and the argument does not do that. Its point is rather that the origin in question has a specific feature: fitness would have produced the same attitudes whether or not they were accurate, and an origin of that kind is epistemically relevant in a way an arbitrary origin is not. Being told a mathematical truth by a stranger is also a causal origin of one's belief, but it gives no reason to doubt the belief.

The strongest realist reply is the third-factor reply. Moral facts supervene on natural facts, and selection did track natural facts, so by tracking the natural facts selection tracked the moral facts as well, and the correlation the debunker calls miraculous turns out to be explained. The wrongness of cruelty, for example, supervenes on facts about suffering; evolution gave us an aversion to suffering because suffering impedes survival and reproduction; so our belief that cruelty is wrong was shaped by the very natural facts that ground its truth. The main objection is that this gets the order of explanation backwards: it makes the wrongness of cruelty depend on the fitness effects of suffering, so that if suffering had happened to be fitness-enhancing, cruelty would have been right."
