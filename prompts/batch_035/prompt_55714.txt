**Exam question:**

"Mathematical platonism holds that numbers, sets, and other mathematical entities exist as abstract objects — mind-independent, not located in space or time, and causally inert — and that mathematical statements are true in virtue of how these objects are. In essay form: (a) explain the epistemological challenge to platonism associated with Paul Benacerraf's 1973 paper 'Mathematical Truth'; (b) explain the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument and why it is often taken to support platonism; and (c) describe one prominent nominalist response to either (a) or (b), and one serious objection that response faces. You need not take sides, but your explanations must be accurate, and you must keep distinct arguments distinct."

**Student's answer:**

"Platonism about mathematics says that numbers and sets exist as abstract objects: they are mind-independent, not located in space or time, and they do not interact causally with anything physical. This has two big attractions. First, it makes mathematics objective: if there are numbers, then 'there are infinitely many primes' is true regardless of what anyone thinks. Second, it fits the necessity of mathematics: theorems hold in every possible world, unlike ordinary empirical facts about water or electrons.

Benacerraf's 1973 challenge is the problem of causal access. Our knowledge of the world is causal: perception gives us knowledge of tables because light bounces off tables into our eyes. But abstract objects are causally inert, so no causal chain can ever connect us to them. Benacerraf concluded that platonism is therefore false, since we could have no knowledge of causally isolated objects. But this depends on the causal theory of knowledge, which hardly anyone accepts anymore, so the challenge dissolves and platonism is in the clear. Benacerraf also made a related point: on the Zermelo construction the number 2 is one thing, on the von Neumann construction it is another, and as Quine showed, any progression would serve equally well as the numbers, so there is no fact of the matter about which sets the numbers are. This is the same argument as the causal one, just put in terms of reference.

The Quine–Putnam indispensability argument says that mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories, and that we are ontologically committed to whatever our best theories quantify over. So we should believe in numbers the way we believe in quarks: they earn their place by doing explanatory work in physics.

Field's nominalism is the main response. In Science Without Numbers he rewrote Newtonian gravitation without any reference to numbers, using only spacetime points and regions standing in primitive metrical relations, and he showed that mathematics applied to the nominalized theory is conservative — it lets us derive nominalistic conclusions faster but adds no new nominalistic content. So Field agrees with Quine about what matters, namely what our best theories quantify over; the disagreement is just about what they in fact quantify over. And since Field's strategy has since been extended to the rest of physics, including quantum mechanics and general relativity, indispensability no longer gives us reason to believe in abstract objects.

Finally, Gödel's incompleteness theorems support platonism. They show that arithmetical truth outruns formal provability in any consistent, sufficiently strong system. Since truths require truthmakers, and the truths of arithmetic outrun anything we construct, there must be abstract objects serving as the truthmakers. Gödel himself was a platonist and held that we have a kind of causal contact with mathematical objects through a faculty of mathematical intuition, analogous to sense perception."
