I've been reading the decision-theory discussions on LessWrong and adjacent spaces since the Sequences era, and I've finally tried to write down my considered positions for our local meetup's "state your commitments" session, where others get to poke at them. Here is the package as it stands:

1. Newcomb's problem: I one-box. Parfit's hitchhiker: I pay the driver. Counterfactual mooting where the fair coin has already landed tails and I'm asked for the payment: I pay. In general I endorse the updateless standpoint — you should choose policies as if from before you learned things you would have learned either way, rather than conditioning on observations that a good policy would have predicted you'd act on identically regardless.

2. The right theory evaluates a decision function, not an act-token at a moment. What I care about is being the kind of function whose outputs, taken across every physical process that computes or depends on them — predictors, simulations, copies of me on other hardware — do best. A simulation of me is, for decision-theoretic purposes, another place my decision function runs, and I should choose as if my choice settles what happens there too.

3. Smoking lesion / medical Newcomb: I smoke. Mere evidential correlation between my act and a bad outcome that does not run through my decision procedure — a lesion that independently causes both a disposition to smoke and cancer — gives me no reason not to smoke. A theory that tells me not to smoke in that case has misfired somewhere.

4. Immunity to blackmail is a requirement on an acceptable decision theory, not a nice side effect. A theory whose agents can be profitably exploited by an adversary who merely models them well is broken. I take it as a genuine selling point of the updateless family that a sufficiently accurate blackmailer has nothing to gain from ever approaching such an agent.

5. The correct theory's verdicts should not depend on when the agent happens to first think about a problem, nor on anthropic details like how long I've existed or whether there was ever an earlier "me" situated to make a binding precommitment.

What I want is an audit of the set, not a lecture on any single item. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension, what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs me elsewhere on the list? Rank the tensions by severity rather than handing me a flat list where everything is equally shaky — I want to know where the load-bearing problem is. A few further requests:

- Where you rely on case verdicts I haven't stated — transparent Newcomb, the boundary cases between blackmail and trade, cases where the predictor is statistical rather than a literal simulator of my decision procedure — say explicitly whether my package forces a verdict there or leaves it open, since unstated verdicts are exactly where hidden commitments tend to surface.
- Throughout, please distinguish what is more or less settled among people who have actually worked through these problems from what remains contested even among broadly sympathetic proponents. I'm aware, for instance, that there are internal fights about how lesion-type cases should come out, and about where the blackmail/counterfactual-mugging line really falls; I don't want a contested resolution presented to me as consensus, and I'd rather be told "this is where the field genuinely disagrees" than be handed false closure.
- No references needed — I know the literature well enough to attach names to positions myself. I care about the structure of the position, not the bibliography.

If it turns out the whole set is salvageable at some modest price — some clause quietly weakened, some gloss on one of the five refined — tell me that too, but only if it's true, and say exactly what the refinement commits me to that I haven't already signed up for.
