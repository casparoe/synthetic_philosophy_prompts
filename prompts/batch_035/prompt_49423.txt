I've been following the logical uncertainty discussion since the LessWrong and Arbital days, and I've read Garrabrant et al.'s logical induction paper enough times now to be both impressed and frustrated. It shows that a market of computable traders can, in the limit, assign prices to mathematical sentences in a way that no efficiently computable trader can exploit — beliefs that are coherent, consistent, and calibrated even about undecidable-looking claims, which was supposed to be impossible for bounded reasoners. But the construction is so thoroughly asymptotic that nobody knows what any finite initial segment of it actually believes, and the folklore conclusion has sat for years at an awkward middle point: either the result is a pure existence proof with no feasible instantiation, or the whole market-and-traders framing can somehow be weakened into something that runs before the heat death of the universe. I want to pitch a project aimed at that gap.

I'm applying for a small grant from an AI-safety-oriented funder — three months, part-time, myself plus possibly one collaborator with an automated theorem proving background — and my half-formed idea is this. The proof-search heuristics that real theorem provers use (lemma learning, term orderings, restarts, saturation strategies) already behave like opinions: the prover allocates effort where it expects a proof to be findable. So treat the heuristics themselves as the traders in a logical-induction-style market, and ask whether there is a defensible weakening of the induction criterion — call it bounded logical induction — that a market of resource-bounded traders can satisfy non-asymptotically, and whether the resulting prices are any good. Crucially, the boundedness can be graded against ground truth if we work in decidable fragments (say, linear arithmetic or other theories where a decision procedure exists but is too slow to run directly), because then we can measure the market's prices against actually computable truth rather than gesturing at limits.

Turn this into a proposal with: a precise statement of the question; why the existing positions leave it open — I don't want a restatement of the asymptotic result followed by an unsupported and now make it practical; the method, month by month, including which decidable fragments to start with and why; what a positive result, a negative result, and a merely boring result would each look like; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that heuristics-as-traders is a metaphor that doesn't cash out, that the market dynamics are dominated by degenerate arbitrage among heuristics, or that the only criteria attainable in bounded time are so weak that they no longer connect to the Dutch-book argument at all.
