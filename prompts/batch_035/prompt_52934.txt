I'm a journalist who has covered science and medicine for about fifteen years, mostly epidemiology and public health. I think of causal questions the way my sources and my own reporting training have taught me: randomized controlled trials versus observational designs, confounders, effect sizes, adjustment, stratification, replication, pre-registration, and the reflexive caution that correlation is not causation. When I encounter the phrase "the study controlled for socioeconomic status," I have a concrete picture of what was done and what could still be wrong with it. I also have a working familiarity with things journalists pick up along the way: the Bradford Hill viewpoints that old-school epidemiologists still invoke, the occasional reference to instrumental variables when someone explains a natural experiment, and the notion that a randomized trial approximates what would have happened to the treated had they not been treated.

What I do not have is any serious training in metaphysics. I keep reading that philosophers disagree about what causation is — in particular, that there is a fault line between so-called counterfactual theories, associated with David Lewis and possible worlds, and interventionist theories, associated more recently with James Woodward and with Pearl's do-operator in statistics. From my side of the fence, "counterfactual" already means something quite specific: the outcome we would have observed under the alternative assignment. And "intervention" means something equally specific: a manipulation, ideally randomization. So my suspicion is that the philosophical debate might partly collapse into a distinction I already use without noticing, or might partly not translate at all — and I can't currently tell which parts are which.

Here is my request. Restate the debate between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation in the vocabulary I actually work in — trials, observational designs, confounding, exchangeability, adjustment, natural experiments, replication — as faithfully as you can, treating that vocabulary as a translation target rather than as the obvious home of these ideas. Then give me a residue report, structured so I can follow it. Which parts of each philosophical position map cleanly onto things I already distinguish? Which parts survive only in distorted form, and what exactly gets bent? And what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all?

On that last category, I want your honest assessment rather than a diplomatic one. I can already see one candidate: Lewis's claim that a counterfactual is true or false depending on similarity relations between entire possible worlds looks like it might have nothing to do with anything I could ever check, report, or even gesture at in a story — but I might be wrong, and if the closest-world machinery turns out to be quietly doing work that exchangeability assumptions also do, I want to know that. Similarly, the old question of whether causes must reliably precede their effects, and what to do about simultaneous or backward-looking cases, may or may not have a home in how trial timelines are described. And I have never needed to take a position on whether causation is a feature of the world or a feature of our models of it, though I notice that when I write "smoking causes lung cancer" I am doing something my sources would insist is more than summary statistics, and something that does not seem to depend on any particular model they used.

Finally, tell me where the residue points. If parts of the philosophers' disagreement have no expression in the working vocabulary of people who estimate causal effects for a living, is that because that vocabulary is missing something, because the disagreement is less substantive than it looks from the outside, or because it concerns a level of description that working scientists are entitled to ignore? I would particularly like your view on whether the interventionist insistence that a cause must be something one could in principle manipulate — which I gather rules out or complicates claims like "the laws of physics cause planetary orbits" — marks a real insight about what my sources are doing when they identify a cause, or a limitation dressed up as a criterion. I would rather be told that one of my two professional vocabularies is inadequate than be flattered about either.
