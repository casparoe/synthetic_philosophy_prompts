I'm asking you to write a eulogy, and I want it built on the philosophy of education my mother actually lived by, even though she never used the word 'philosophy' for it. She taught biology in secondary schools for thirty-eight years, and somewhere in her first years of teaching she came across A. N. Whitehead — the mathematician and process philosopher — and his short essay from the 1920s on the aims of education. What she took from it, and repeated to every student teacher she later mentored, were three ideas I've heard her voice a thousand times, always in her own words rather than his. First, that knowledge goes stale: it has to be used, tested, thrown into fresh combinations, or it's worthless — she liked to say that a student's head is not a pantry where you store things against a future that never comes. Second, that learning moves in cycles: a stage of half-wonder, half-understanding where everything is new and a bit vague; a stage of discipline and exact technique; and then a return to wider horizons once the technique has become second nature — and that forcing the discipline stage onto students who haven't had the wonder stage, or stretching the wonder stage indefinitely to avoid the hard part, ruins both. Third, that education has a single aim in all its subjects: a sense for the value of life as it is actually lived, here and now — not preparation for a distant examination, and not preparation for a distant adulthood. She took this seriously enough to redesign her whole curriculum around fieldwork, and to get in trouble with her head of department more than once for spending a spring term on what looked, on the timetable, like 'pond-dipping'. She also, as it happens, had no patience for the afterlife in any form: she was a cheerful, uncomplaining atheist, and when she died last month at eighty-four she left instructions that her funeral should contain 'nothing about her looking down on us', because, as she put it to my father years ago, 'that would be exactly the kind of dead idea I spent my life trying to prevent'.

The funeral will be attended by perhaps two hundred people: her family, a scattering of relatives, and a very large number of former students and colleagues, including several generations of the teachers she trained, some of whom will know exactly where her teaching philosophy came from and some of whom will only know they loved her. It should run about five minutes when read aloud. I'd like the eulogy to make her ideas do real structural work rather than sit as decoration — for instance, the cyclical idea could shape the speech itself (opening in the wonder stage of who she was, passing through the precision of what she actually demanded of her students, and widening back out), or the freshness-of-knowledge idea could shape the way her life is told, so that nothing in it is reported as inert fact. What I want you to avoid is the standard eulogy move where the philosophy gets name-checked in one paragraph and the rest is anecdotes. Equally, please don't turn it into a lecture on Whitehead for the two mourners who might enjoy one; most of the room never read him, and the words have to land for a seventeen-year-old former student who is grieving. If the process-philosophy background helps you — the idea that a person is not a static substance but a continuing process of experiences, always becoming and never finished — you may draw on it, but only if it earns its place. The hardest constraint is the one she set herself: no watching over us, no 'she's still with us in a way', no soft deism smuggled in at the end. Comfort has to come from something else. Write it so that she would have recognized it as true, and so that the teachers in the audience leave wanting to run their own next lesson differently.
