So I've been following the Anthropocene formalization fight for like a decade now, ever since a LessWrong-adjacent post about "concept ownership" pointed me at it, and the thing that keeps bugging me is that the March 2024 vote where the stratigraphers (the ICS/IUGS, the geologists who literally get to decide what counts as an epoch) rejected the Anthropocene as a formal unit did not end the debate at all. It was supposed to be the decisive move, and instead everyone just kept using the word like nothing happened — the Earth system people, the climate people, historians, half the papers I skim on arxiv. The Anthropocene Working Group had a whole proposal ready, mid-20th century start, pinned to the plutonium and bomb carbon spike from nuclear tests, and the Quaternary stratigraphy folks voted it down, and the counter-reaction was basically "ok, we'll keep using the term informally forever then." So the debate is stalled in a weird zombie state where the official decision exists and nobody cares.

Here's my working hypothesis, and I want you to stress-test it rather than just nod along: nobody in this fight actually disagrees about the empirical facts. Everyone agrees humans have massively altered the planet, everyone agrees on when the CO2 curve bends, when radionuclides show up, even the more exotic markers like the 1610 Orbis spike (that dip in atmospheric CO2 Lewis and Maslin attribute to reforestation after the Columbian exchange collapsed indigenous populations). The way I read it, the real disagreement is some mix of (a) a definitional dispute about what an epoch even is — the stratigraphers want a globally synchronous marker layer because that's what chronostratigraphy has always demanded, while their critics think demanding a crisp golden spike for a process that ramped up over centuries is applying geology rules to something that isn't really a geology question, and (b) a jurisdiction fight over who owns the concept, i.e. whether a committee of Quaternary geologists gets to be the mapmakers for what is basically an Earth-system-science-plus-humanities concept. Basically: verbal dispute dressed up as science, or two research programs answering different questions under one label, or is there a genuine substantive crux I'm not seeing?

Diagnose the disagreement for me — verbal, factual, methodological, turf war, some mixture — using what the sides actually say to each other, and tell me honestly what would even count as progress here. Like, is there any new data or marker that could settle it, or a conceptual distinction that would dissolve it (epoch-as-formal-unit vs epoch-as-concept, something like that), or is the right answer that the impasse is load-bearing for both sides and should be expected to continue? And if it's genuinely a jurisdiction fight, is there any principled way to decide who gets custody of a word that half of academia is already using in the looser sense? I keep going back and forth between "this is the cleanest example of a definitional non-disagreement I know" and "no wait, the synchronicity criterion is actually doing real work and the critics are just annoyed they lost the vote," and I can't tell which frame survives contact with what the participants actually claim.
