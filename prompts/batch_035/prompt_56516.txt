For thirty-five years I worked as a physical chemist, where nearly every practical question eventually reduced to putting quantities on a common scale: converting enthalpies, rates, and yields into comparable units, or better, into dimensionless ratios. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep returning to the idea that some values are incommensurable—that there simply is no common currency in which, say, the continued existence of a wilderness valley and cheap electricity for a poor region can be jointly expressed. My instinct as a measurer resists this, so I have improvised a diagnostic, and I would like your help applying and stress-testing it. Borrowing loosely from dimensional analysis, my "chaining test" asks: can one construct a finite sequence of intermediate options, each linked to its neighbors by a comparison I can make confidently and ordinally (this one is at least as good as that one), such that the chain connects the two original options? If such a chain exists and the links feel genuine rather than strained, I say the values are chain-comparable even if not expressible on a single scale; if every attempted chain breaks at some link where confident judgment simply gives out, I say the values are incommensurable in a stronger sense. I suspect philosophers would say my test conflates several distinct things—perhaps Ruth Chang's notion of two options being "on a par," or the difference between incomparability and mere difficulty of comparison—but I am not sure quite how.

Here is the task I would like you to perform. Please rank the following six hard choices from most to least amenable to my chaining test—that is, from the pair of values most easily connected by intermediate options down to the pair where you judge the chain most likely to break irreparably. (1) A hospital choosing between allocating a scarce ventilator to a young patient with low survival odds and an older patient with high survival odds. (2) A musician deciding between technical perfection and emotional rawness in a recorded performance. (3) A government choosing between damming a wild river for cheap hydroelectric power and preserving the valley untouched. (4) A parent choosing between an evening helping a child with exam preparation and attending a close friend's once-in-a-lifetime celebration. (5) A prospective academic choosing between a permanent post in a city she dislikes and a fixed-term post in a city she loves. (6) A person choosing between funding a year of additional life for herself in poor health and donating the same sum to a charity that reliably saves two strangers. For each, give a score between 0 and 1 for how well the chaining test applies, along with a sentence or two on where, exactly, you expect the chain to break or hold. Finally, tell me plainly whether you think the test itself tracks anything real about the values at stake, or whether it merely measures the ingenuity of whoever designs the intermediate options—in which case I may have smuggled a physicist's faith in common scales back into a domain where it does not belong.
