I am an MA student in philosophy preparing to write my dissertation (approximately 12,000 words, completed over one academic year with supervision). My half-formed idea concerns the aesthetic testimony debate, and I would like you to turn it into a polished dissertation proposal.

The familiar question in that debate is whether one can acquire justified aesthetic belief, or aesthetic knowledge, from testimony alone — from a friend's verdict, a critic's review, a star rating — given the widely felt pull of the thought that first-hand acquaintance with a work is somehow required. My suspicion is that the debate has quietly assumed a static, two-step model: testimony first, in the absence of acquaintance, and the question is then whether the belief formed at that first step is justified. But in real life, aesthetic belief formation is usually iterative. You hear a verdict, form a weak expectation, then encounter the work, and your encounter is itself shaped by what you were told — what you attend to, how much patience you have, what you anticipate at each moment, whether you watch it at all. Testimony is thus not a rival to acquaintance but an upstream input into it. I suspect that once the process is modelled this way, some standard positions in the debate will look like artefacts of the static model, though I am not yet sure which ones, or whether the argument simply collapses into an obvious point about expectation effects that aestheticians have already made in other vocabulary.
