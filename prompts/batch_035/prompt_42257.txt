I want the specification a theory of moral dilemmas must satisfy before I will trust any particular verdict it issues about a hard case. The theory is meant to be good enough to guide decisions in practice — the sort of dilemma a clinical ethics committee or a wartime triage officer actually faces — not merely to classify cases from the armchair; that matters because real agents have to live with, and answer for, the duty they fail to fulfill. Write the desiderata: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the formal constraints it must either honor or explicitly forswear, with the consideration behind each, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp.

Among the cases it must handle, include at least these: a conflict in which both duties remain binding after the choice, so that neither is cancelled or overridden — the feature that distinguishes the genuine phenomenon from Ross's conflicts of prima facie duties, where one consideration merely outweighs another; a dilemma the agent entered through earlier innocent choices, versus one imposed from outside, and whether the theory may treat these differently; and a case of two options of exactly equal choice-worthiness, to test that the theory does not collapse dilemmas into arbitrary picks between ties.

Among the distinctions it must not collapse: moral residue — what the agent still owes the party whose duty went unmet, in apology or compensation — versus mere regret; a genuine dilemma versus a case where one option is in fact better and the difficulty is only epistemic; and the structure of the option-set itself — in a dilemma, is each of the two options forbidden, each permissible, or something else? The theory must say which pattern constitutes the genuine article, and explain why that answer neither dooms the agent to wrongdoing whatever she does nor dissolves the dilemma into a free choice between equally acceptable options.

The formal constraints are where I most want care. If 'ought' aggregates across conjunction — from O(a) and O(b), infer O(a and b) — and whatever ought to be done must be possible, then genuine dilemmas cannot exist: the aggregation yields an obligation to do the impossible. So a theory that keeps both principles has to deny dilemmas outright, and a theory that keeps dilemmas must break aggregation or the possibility principle, owning the costs of the break — notably that failures of aggregation leak into innocent cases, where two jointly satisfiable duties are still naturally conjoined into a single obligation. Flag this tension explicitly, including whether some subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable; that is the part I most want to see. Then tell me which single requirement on the list excludes the most otherwise-plausible theories.

Finally, briefly and without extended exposition, score three standard positions against the list: the Rossian view that all conflicts are merely prima facie, the view that denies that ought implies can, and the view that rejects aggregation while keeping dilemmas. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard — the criteria are the deliverable.
