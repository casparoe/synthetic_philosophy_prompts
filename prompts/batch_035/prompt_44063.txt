I'm a novelist, and I've been circling Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor for two years now without landing anything. It's the old trade-off: people can't handle freedom, so take it from them and give them bread, certainty, and something to belong to — and be damned yourself for the mercy of it. Every time I try to write a modern version I end up with either a dystopia knock-off or a sermon. So maybe you can get me unstuck.

Write me a short story, roughly 800 to 1200 words, that does the Inquisitor's bargain in a fresh setting. Here's the one I want, and I'm fairly attached to it: a mid-sized software company that has, over about a decade, quietly become a total institution. Everything is provided — housing, meals, a clinic, scheduled fun — and the deal is genuine, the food is actually good, nobody is lying to anyone. The founder is the Inquisitor figure: she doesn't want worship, she wants people fed and calm, and she privately believes freedom is a luxury most people are relieved to put down, the way you put down something heavy at the end of a long day. The temptation material (bread, miracle, authority) is all there but translated: the miracle is the product actually working, the authority is the total absence of decisions, the bread is the bread.

The sticking point, and the part I most want you to get right: I don't want a Christ figure who walks in and wins the argument. That's the cheap move and Dostoevsky himself didn't take it — his Christ just kisses the old man and leaves. So my equivalent should be small and ambiguous. My current draft has an employee who keeps asking, politely, for the one thing the company won't give: the right to fail at something. She wants her project to be allowed to ship broken. Not freedom in the abstract, just permission to have a bad outcome that's hers. And I want the story to end without the Inquisitor converting, without anyone converting — maybe with the founder granting the request in a way that quietly hollows it out, or refusing it in a way that's more loving than granting it would be. You choose, but no twist ending, no narrator winking at me about how it's all really about Dostoevsky.

If it helps, the thing I keep failing to dramatize is this: the Inquisitor is sincere, and his cruelty is a form of service, and the people are not stupid and not oppressed and mostly grateful. The horror isn't the cage, it's that the cage is warm and the door is, as far as anyone can tell, not even locked. Make me feel both sides of that in the prose itself, in the texture of daily life, not in speeches. First person from the founder would be great if you can pull it off without making her a monster or a saint — she thinks of herself as basically a good administrator.
