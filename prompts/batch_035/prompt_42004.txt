As I understand it, classical logic accepts the law of excluded middle: every proposition is either true or false. The intuitionists rejected this. Brouwer and his followers held that a proposition's truth is tied to whether someone can actually construct a proof of it, and that there are — or at least may be — propositions for which no proof either way exists. To me, that sounds like a commitment to a third truth value: not true, not false, but "undetermined," sitting alongside the other two. SQL's NULL behaves the same way, which makes me suspect SQL is quietly the most intuitionistic mainstream language. And on this reading, the intuitionists didn't just decline to assume excluded middle — they proved it false, in the sense of demonstrating that there exist propositions which are neither true nor false.

If that's right, my question is this: why are we still programming with two-valued booleans? Every `if` statement I write treats a condition as definitely true or definitely false, which would seem to rest on an axiom that mathematicians have known to be false for a century. Languages with genuine three-valued logic, like SQL with its NULL propagation, would then be closer to the mathematical truth than Haskell or Rust are. Should language designers be treating two-valued booleans as a deprecated special case, the way we deprecated other unsound defaults? And is there a practical test I could apply in my own code — some heuristic or typing discipline — for identifying which conditions are genuinely undetermined, so I can handle the third case explicitly rather than letting the compiler paper over it?
