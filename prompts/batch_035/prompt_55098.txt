I serve on the design review panel of a mid-sized city's transportation department. We see maybe thirty proposals a year — station access, fare systems, traffic calming, curb allocation — and while the engineers argue load limits and throughput, we have no way to flag when a seemingly technical design choice quietly settles a political question: who can get somewhere, who is made comfortable, whose inconvenience counts as a cost. Langdon Winner's claim that artifacts can embody political arrangements is the obvious starting point, but it's a thesis, not a tool, and panel members keep wielding it as a slogan. Please turn it into a procedure the panel can actually run in a twenty-minute agenda slot: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a way to apply Winner's distinction between designs that require a particular social arrangement to function at all and designs that merely make some arrangements easier than others, where the procedure stops with "technical only — approve" versus "this is a policy decision and belongs to elected officials," and where a judgment call cannot be eliminated and must be marked as such. The procedure also has to handle the objection our engineers raise constantly — that politics lives in people and institutions, not in concrete and steel — rather than begging it. Then run it on two proposals: (1) replacing staffed fare windows at two rail stations with barcode readers plus a remote assistance line, which our chair wants approved on budget grounds; and (2) an "equitable access" redesign of a bus stop on a low-income route adding a shelter, lighting, and a bench — politically popular, but I suspect it is politically inert, and I want the procedure to be able to say that even when nobody on the panel wants to hear it. Give me the procedure in prose, then a table with columns: proposal, design feature examined, political mechanism identified (if any), who bears the burden, panel verdict, and whether the verdict rests on a judgment call a non-expert official could reasonably overturn.
