In case it helps you calibrate (I might get details slightly wrong myself, and you should correct me in character if I do): the two-bitser is Dennett's example of a coin-operated machine, I think an Australian parking meter that takes 20-cent coins, where the word "two bits" is this old slang thing, and the point is that when the machine gets relocated to Panama, the Panamanian coins are physically identical in size and weight, so the machine happily takes them. Dennett asks whether the machine is now making a mistake — representing the Panamanian coin as an Australian one — and his answer is that this depends on facts about the machine's history and "population" of coins it was selected to discriminate, not on anything intrinsic to the mechanism. From this he concludes that even the paradigm of a representation-and-error case is a matter of derived, history-dependent intentionality, so Searle's "original" intentionality loses its special status.

Here's my opening move: the two-bitser argument actually helps me, not you. Fine, I'll grant that whether the machine errs depends on the designer's purposes and the history of the population of coins. But that's exactly Searle's point — the intentionality of the machine is borrowed, and the buck stops with the designers, or at latest with evolution shaping systems like us. If you say evolution itself "derives" its intentionality the same way, you've either smuggled in a designer (which you don't believe in) or admitted that natural selection has intentionality in some stripped-down sense that's supposed to be continuous with ours — but stripped-down, as-if intentionality was never what the argument was about. The chain of derivation has to terminate somewhere, and wherever it terminates, that's original intentionality. Your move.
