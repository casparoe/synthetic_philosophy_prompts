I write historical fiction, mostly set in the 18th and 19th centuries, and I keep running into a pattern in how my writing group, my editor, and basically every reader I know hands out moral exemption to real historical figures, and I can't find a word for it. Whenever Jefferson and slavery comes up, someone deploys what I privately call the "everyone believed that then" move — the idea that a person can't be blamed for not seeing what nobody around them could see. Fine, I know that whole debate exists. But what I keep noticing is that the move's power comes entirely from an implied headcount, and the headcount is almost never true in the sense needed. Abolitionists existed, Jefferson corresponded with some of them, and at points he clearly did see at least part of it. So the defence quietly shifts from "he couldn't have known" (which is false) to "most people like him didn't act on it" (which is sort of true). And the shift only works in one direction: nobody accepts "most people then were casually antisemitic" as excusing Churchill's worse remarks, and nobody accepts "basically everyone was religious then" as excucing anybody's religion. So there's a list of vices where the era's average belief works as an excuse, and a list where it obviously doesn't, and I can't figure out what actually decides which list a thing goes on. Whether the dissenters were visible and being punished? Whether we think the belief is bad *because* that era held it?

There's a second wrinkle that's maybe the more interesting part. The counterfactual "if he'd been born in 1970 he'd think like us" is doing a lot of quiet work in these conversations, which smells to me like constitutive moral luck — but as I understand it moral luck is about the individual's circumstances, whereas here it's more like the *era's* average level of insight gets imported into each individual as a discount, even individuals who were demonstrably above the average. It's also not just the old justification/excuse distinction, because the person making the defence wants to say the belief was wrong AND he isn't blameworthy for it — but then they go right on blaming him for other wrongs from the exact same era, using standards he also couldn't have known. Same person, same century, two different moral time zones.

So: does this configuration have a name in the philosophy literature? I've bumped into moral luck and the justification/excuse thing while reading around, but neither seems to capture the move, especially the headcount structure and the puzzle of which beliefs the excuse is allowed to cover. What I want is a proper carving of it — what exactly the pattern is, how it differs from a plain "he didn't know" defence that's actually true, whether the one-way list of excusable vices has ever been explained, and if there's no established concept, then coin one and give me the boundary cases. I'd like to know whether the thing has already been named before I invent my own handle for it and get corrected at a book club.
