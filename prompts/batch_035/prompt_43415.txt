I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm preparing a piece that pushes back on the received wisdom that the sunk cost "fallacy" is always a fallacy. The proposition I need to pin down precisely: there exists at least one kind of real situation in which an agent's decision is made better — in expectation, by the agent's own lights — by counting sunk costs as a reason, where the improvement is not smuggled in purely through reputation or signaling. My own credence in that proposition is about 0.6, and it rests mainly on an argument I find only half-persuasive: past choices can encode information about one's reasons that is costly or impossible to reconstruct now, so a past investment is not always epistemically inert — your earlier self did a calculation you can no longer see. Give me your own number, then decompose it into terms such as: (1) the probability that rationality should be assessed at the level of the isolated, forward-looking decision — as the textbook analysis assumes — rather than at the level of whole policies or characters that others observe over time; (2) the probability that past investments sometimes carry decision-relevant information the agent genuinely cannot now recover, as distinct from mere bias toward consistency; (3) the probability that any cases surviving (1) and (2) are really cases of sunk-cost reasoning, rather than the label being stretched to cover something else. Assign each term a probability with one line of justification, combine them into your final number, and tell me which term the result is most sensitive to and what argument or observation would move it most. Finally, tell me whether you think part of this dispute is verbal — a fight over what gets to count as a "sunk cost" at all — and if so, where you would draw the line.
