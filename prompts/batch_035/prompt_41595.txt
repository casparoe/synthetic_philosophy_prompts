I'm finishing a seminar on the history of modern philosophy, and our last unit covered Molyneux's problem — the question, posed in William Molyneux's letter to Locke and taken up in the Essay, of whether a person born blind, who has learned to distinguish a cube and a sphere by touch, would upon gaining sight be able to tell them apart by sight alone, before ever handling them. The thing that struck me all semester is less the proposed answers than the shape of the question's afterlife: Berkeley predicting "no," Cheselden's boy in 1728, then a long run of sight-restoration patients in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, each one hailed as the decisive test and each one leaving the argument pretty much where it was — partly because the patients' eyes, brains, and histories never cooperate with the clean version of the question.

I want to close our final session with a poem that captures that structure. Write a villanelle. The two refrains should be, roughly, the two answers the question keeps receiving — the confident "yes" of the rationalist who thinks geometry transfers, and the empiricist "no" that says sight's spatial content must be learned from the hand. The form should do real work: the refrains return again and again the way the question itself is re-posed each time a newly sighted patient appears, and each return should feel less like progress than like the question wearing a new costume — armchair question about ideas in one stanza, empirical question about neuroplasticity and cross-modal correspondence in the next. Let the final couplet, where the refrains finally stand side by side, register whatever you take the honest ending to be: that the question has quietly changed shape, or that the answer keeps depending on what we decide the question meant. Someone who knows the problem should be able to tell which refrain is which and feel that the poem gets it right; someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem about a question that refuses to die.

Accuracy matters more to me than grandeur. The philosophical content should be rendered well enough that a Locke scholar wouldn't wince, and please keep historical specifics to the ones that are genuinely uncontroversial (Locke's reporting of the letter, Berkeley's prediction, the general fact of Cheselden's case). After the poem, add a short prose note — five or six sentences — separating what is settled in the history you've used from what is genuinely contested: for instance, whatever stance the poem's ending takes about what the actual sight-restoration cases show, the note should say plainly that philosophers and vision scientists disagree about how to interpret those cases and about whether the original question is even still well-posed, and should not present your poem's resolution as the received view. If the poem leans on any particular reading of Berkeley or of the modern literature, flag it there.
