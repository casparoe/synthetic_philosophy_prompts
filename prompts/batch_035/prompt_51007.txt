I got deep down a rabbit hole last week reading about Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project, the one where he tried to get a computer to understand letterforms, and it's messing with my head. His framing is that there's an abstract role (the essence of "a"-ness, so to speak) that every individual 'a' is an instance of, and separately there's a style that runs through a whole font like a vein, connecting all the letters of one typeface to each other. Sounds clean enough, but the more I stare at actual glyphs the less I can say what the 'a' role even is. So here are my gut verdicts, tell me what principle (if any) captures them. A double-story book 'a' and a single-story handwritten 'a' are about as different in shape as two 'a's can be, and yet both are unmistakably 'a's -- no hesitation there. But the schwa character 'ə', which in a lot of fonts is basically the same shape as the single-story 'a', is NOT an 'a' to me, it's its own thing. Related weirdness: rotate a lowercase 'e' 180 degrees and you get something nearly identical to an italic single-story 'a', and it's still not an 'a'. Meanwhile lowercase Greek alpha, which is literally the ancestor of our letter, doesn't read as an 'a' either when I see it in a math paper. Then there's this techno font I have where the 'a' is basically a triangle with a slash, barely a closed bowl at all, and it reads fine as 'a' in context. But a leetspeak '4' in "h4ck" feels like it's sort of doing 'a'-duty without actually being an 'a'. And a circled 'ⓐ' still feels like an 'a' to me somehow, even enclosed in a decorative ring.

The pair I keep chewing on: shape similarity clearly can't be the principle (schwa vs single-story 'a' kills it), but neither can lineage alone (alpha), and neither can "does the job in a word" (the '4'), and yet my verdicts all seem to pull on some mix of those. My tentative guess is that it's something like Hofstadter's role idea -- being an 'a' is belonging to a whole system of letter-roles where each one is partly defined by not being the others, plus having some history of use -- but I can't get that to explain why the schwa and the 'a' are different letters when they share a system AND nearly share a shape. Maybe the answer involves the style thing? Like the single-story 'a' carries the style-vein of a font that also shapes its 'e' and 'g', and that's what individuates it, whereas the schwa belongs to a different system (phonetic notation)? I honestly don't know.

So: is there one coherent principle that saves all my verdicts, or close, and if two rival principles each save most of them, tell me which one you'd bet on and why. Please also apply it to some cases I haven't listed -- a mathematical double-struck '𝕒', a hypothetical font where the counter (the little hole) of every letter is a miniature copy of that same letter, and a handwritten 'a' of mine that has slowly drifted over the years until it's now closer to my 'o' than to the font 'a' I learned in school -- and say what verdict your principle gives for each. Feel free to tell me my schwa intuition is just wrong, but if so you owe me an argument, not just vibes.
