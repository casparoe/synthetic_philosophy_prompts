I've been asked to lead a forty-minute session of our institute's journal club on what Dennett calls free-floating rationales: the reasons that selection installs in the world without any reasoner — the reason the heart beats the way it does, which was there for millions of years before anything, including the animals whose hearts they were, could so much as represent it, let alone state it. The audience is six experimental biologists and a historian of science. The biologists lean on functional language all day — an enzyme exists in order to catalyse a reaction, petals are coloured in order to attract pollinators — while being professionally trained to disavow that talk as mere shorthand for a selection history, and the historian will hear Aristotelian teleology the moment the word reason leaves my mouth. I don't want you to explain the idea to me; I want the plan for explaining it to them.

First, the order the pieces have to go in: in particular, what has to be established before I can introduce the term itself without its being heard either as teleology or as lab slang, and why that piece has to come before the others rather than after. Second, the single example that should carry the whole session. I'm torn between two candidates and I'd like your verdict on each: our craving for sugar and fat, whose rationale I like because it persists — and remains the correct answer to why we crave it, and can be misidentified (the Lamarckian just-so story is wrong in a way shorthand can't be) — even in environments where it now misfires; and the ultraviolet nectar guides on flowers, which did their work for ages before any human could see them, making the rationale look like a standing fact that awaited discovery. Tell me which can carry the whole session, what the winner must have and must lack, and whether the spider's web — the example everyone reaches for — is wrong for this audience, as I suspect it is, and if so exactly why. Third, the misconception map: not a list, but the order these will actually arise across the session, with the point in the sequence where I should head each one off. I can predict the teleology alarm, the deflationary reading that nothing new has been said beyond causal history, and the slide from there being a rationale to the process itself wanting or knowing something; I'd like your fuller map, including whatever the historian will raise that the biologists won't. Fourth, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud — I'm wondering whether to exclude the philosophy-of-biology literature on functions entirely, and whether to bracket the question of whether free-floating rationales are eventually supposed to ground meaning or mental content, but the call is yours. Finally, one question I could put to the club at the end that would tell me whether the distinction between a causal story and a rationale has actually landed, rather than whether they were merely entertained for forty minutes.
