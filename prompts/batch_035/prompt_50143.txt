I sit on the publications committee of a state archaeological institute that must sign off on the interpretive wording of final site reports before they go to press. We're stuck on a genuinely hard case, and I've been asked to draft the circulated recommendation. I'd like you to write that draft for me.

The case: excavations at a Late Neolithic settlement (a single-period village, houses with beaten clay floors, no written records of any kind) recovered a group of eleven fired-clay figurines from a deposit under the floor of one house. Most are standing figures with pinched heads and no faces; three were found headless with the breaks looking fresh rather than abraded; one was deposited inside a grain bin and another at the threshold stone. Domestic debris, querns, and charred grain were found throughout the house, so the occupation context is ordinary. The excavators propose that the published report describe the figurines as "images of a grain goddess" and the deposit as a "household shrine," and say the breaking of the figurines was "a ritual act of deliberate destruction before burial." Two committee members disagree: they hold that the most we can responsibly print is that figurines were made, used, deposited, and in some cases broken, since every claim beyond that rests on analogy to other prehistoric sites or to much later or distant cultures whose religious content we also only know through the same kind of circular reasoning. Our director has told the committee he wants a decision, not a compromise that hedges every verb.

Please draft the memo. Structure it under these headings, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. "The case before the committee" — ~120 words, stating exactly what wording is at issue and what the two positions are.
2. "The case for the excavators' reading" — ~250 words, making the strongest honest argument that the cult interpretation is warranted, including what the find's configuration (placement in the grain bin, at the threshold, fresh breaks) could legitimately support and what principle of inference from objects to beliefs it relies on.
3. "The case for the conservative reading" — ~250 words, developing the strongest version of the skeptics' position: not a blanket refusal to infer, but a principled account of why inferences about technology and subsistence are on firmer ground than inferences about what prehistoric people believed, and why the analogy to better-documented cultures imports the very content it is supposed to establish.
4. "Where the burden of proof lies" — ~150 words, on who should have to argue what: whether the excavators owe us a route from material evidence to religious content, or whether the skeptics owe us a reason the ordinary interpretive tools of our discipline (configuration of finds, spatial patterning, cross-cultural regularities) should be suspended here.
5. "Recommendation" — ~200 words, giving concrete final wording: one sentence as it should appear in the site report, one sentence for the public exhibit label, and a ruling on the phrase "ritual act of deliberate destruction" (accept, revise, or reject — with the actual replacement language).

The memo should be usable by colleagues who will vote on it, so the reasoning must be one they could accept or contest on the merits. Do not treat the conservative members as timid or the excavators as credulous; each position should come out of this looking like the serious view it is. No bibliography — cite no literature and name no scholars; the memo is to stand on the reasoning alone.
