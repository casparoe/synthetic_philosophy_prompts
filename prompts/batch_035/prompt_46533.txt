I'm a retired physical chemist, 67, and since I hung up my lab coat I've gotten deep into philosophy of mind, of all things. The thing I can't sort out is the difference between just processing information and actually experiencing it -- what the journals seem to call access vs. phenomenal consciousness. I've been going through famous psych experiments and writing down my gut verdict about whether the subject was conscious of the thing or merely processing it, and now I want someone to tell me if there's a principle underneath my gut. So: in that old Sperling-style experiment where a grid of letters flashes for a fraction of a second and people say they "saw them all" but can only name about four, I'm confident they were conscious of all the letters -- the experience overflows what they can report. A blindsight patient who reaches accurately for objects in his blind field while insisting he sees nothing: not conscious, full stop. Subliminal priming, where a flashed word speeds up your next decision without you noticing: not conscious, and I don't feel any pull the other way. The invisible gorilla experiment, where you're counting basketball passes and a person in a gorilla suit walks through and you swear you never saw it: not conscious, even though lots of it hit the retina and presumably got pretty far up the visual system. And a locked-in patient, who can't report anything except with an eye-movement alphabet: obviously conscious of everything, including things she currently has no behavioral access to at all.

The problem is these verdicts don't obviously add up. If overflowing the report in the letter-flash case still counts as conscious, why doesn't the gorilla -- which was equally "in front of" the subject and equally unreported -- count? My tentative rule was something like: it's conscious if a cue delivered right afterwards could have made it reportable, which saves the letters (cued row gets named) and arguably the gorilla (it's gone by the time you'd cue, so no rescue possible). But then there's the cutaneous rabbit -- taps at the wrist, then sudden taps near the elbow, and you feel phantom taps hopping up the forearm in between, because the brain "fills in" after the fact. The intermediate taps feel perfectly conscious, they were vivid, but they never existed as a state that could be cued-and-reported, since the brain only decided where they fell after the elbow taps arrived. So was the hopping conscious or not? I keep going back and forth. Same problem with reports from anesthesia of people who followed commands during surgery (squeeze my hand, and they did) but remembered absolutely nothing afterwards.

So that's my ask: is there one principle that gets all my verdicts, or close to it, and is it the cueing one patched up somehow, or something else entirely (a global workspace person tried to tell me everything hinges on whether a state is "globally available," which sounds right until I try to apply it to the rabbit)? If the principle has to overturn one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why that one is the weak link rather than the others. And then tell me what it says about the case that's really bugging me: a subject in one of those rapid-serial-presentation tasks who later shows above-chance forced-choice guessing about an item they say they never saw -- like a weak blindsight, but in a normal brain. Conscious or not, and what does your principle lean on to say so?
