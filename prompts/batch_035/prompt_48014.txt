I'm a high school junior writing an extended essay for an IB-style course on speculative reasoning in science, and my supervisor suggested I get outside feedback before the next draft. My essay attacks the "dark forest" solution to the Fermi paradox, the one from Liu Cixin's Three-Body trilogy that got popular online: the axioms that survival is a civilization's primary need, that matter stays constant while populations grow, plus the ideas that civilizations can't trust each other across interstellar distances (the chain of suspicion) and that a weaker civilization can leapfrog a stronger one technologically in a short time (the technology explosion). The standard conclusion is that the galaxy is silent because broadcasting is suicide and everyone is hiding.

My essay argues the deduction has a hole nobody in the online discussions seems to notice: the whole logic assumes a first strike is decisive, but at interstellar distances you cannot verify a kill. If you launch at a star system and the target civilization has dispersed, or hardened itself, or quietly survived the strike, then you have revealed your location, your capabilities, and your hostility to a survivor who now has every reason to retaliate — and decades or centuries of warning time to prepare. So the game isn't a one-shot hide-or-shoot choice; it's closer to Cold War deterrence with un-verifiable strikes, and deterrence logic predicts signaling, defense, and even communication rather than total silence. My second, weaker section suggests the hiding premise itself is doubtful, since a civilization at our level already leaks heat and radio, so the forest may never have been dark in the first place.

Have I mischaracterized the dark forest reasoning anywhere? And is my second section worth keeping or should I cut it? For each of your main criticisms, say how confident you are in it, and single out the point you are least sure of, since I have to defend this in a viva.
