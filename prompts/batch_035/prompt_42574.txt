I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about the philosophical debate over consciousness. For background I've been reading a trade book on the mind-body problem, and it summarizes Daniel Dennett's quining qualia argument from his 1988 essay in Consciousness in Modern Science roughly as follows: Dennett notes that qualia are standardly conceived as ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible properties of experience, and he then argues, via thought experiments about two coffee tasters — one whose taste receptors have changed over the years, one whose aesthetic preferences have simply shifted — that the tasters themselves can never tell which change has occurred, because their introspective reports come out the same either way. Since the reports are identical, the book says, Dennett concludes the experiences must have been identical all along, so any felt difference is a confabulation; from this he infers that we don't really experience anything in the first place, consciousness as we ordinarily conceive it is an illusion, and the hard problem of consciousness dissolves. The book adds that most philosophers of mind have since accepted this and moved on. Before I put any of this in print, please audit this summary claim by claim: which parts a philosopher of mind would endorse, which are defensible simplifications, and which are misreadings — in particular, whether the step from identical introspective reports to identical experiences is actually Dennett's inference or a distortion of it, and whether denying qualia as he defines them amounts to denying experience altogether. I'd also ask that you mark where the experts themselves genuinely disagree — for instance, about whether the argument succeeds, or about whether any notion of phenomenal character survives the quining — so that I don't present a contested position as the settled consensus in my article.
