I'd like your credence, stated as a number, that acausal trade is a real and rational phenomenon — that two agents with no causal interaction whatsoever (say, civilizations in causally disconnected regions of the universe) can each rationally bear real costs that benefit only the other, purely because each can model the other well enough that mutual cooperation becomes a kind of equilibrium neither could reach by ordinary bargaining. I don't want "it depends on your decision theory" as an answer; I want the number and the decomposition. Please assign a rough probability to each of the following and give one line of justification for each: (a) that some decision theory on which such cooperation is actually rational — not merely a nice thought experiment — is correct, and that we are in a position to act on it; (b) that real agents could achieve the mutual-modeling fidelity the argument requires, given that each side must simulate, formalize, or otherwise predict the other without any causal channel to check its work; (c) that the surplus from such trades would be large enough to matter once you account for the risk that your model of the far party is wrong and it defects while you pay; and (d) any further terms you think belong — for example, whether a common bargaining solution can even be located when the parties' utility scales, computational budgets, and priors are wildly different, or whether the whole thing quietly collapses into ordinary causal trade the moment any indirect causal link exists. Then give the combination, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and describe the argument or observation that would move that term the most. Please respond in American English.
