I'm a philosophy grad student and I've been chewing on a short essay idea about Moore that I want feedback on before I commit a term to it. The background, for context: in Principia Ethica Moore claims that the things of greatest intrinsic value are, roughly, personal affections and the enjoyment of beauty, and his method for backing this up is the isolation test — you conceive the state of affairs existing alone in the universe, stripped of every consequence it would ordinarily drag along with it, and ask whether its bare existence still seems good. Everyone knows that much. My angle is that if you apply his own test consistently, it backfires on the friendship half of his famous pairing, and that Moore gets away with it because he bundles friendship together with beauty so that the beauty half can do the reputational work for both.

Here are my draft notes so far: "To certify something as intrinsically good, Moore requires that you be able to conceive it in absolute isolation — consequences subtracted — and still judge it good. He puts loving someone and being loved near the top of the results. But run the test seriously on friendship. A friendship's value runs through its history: how you met, what you survived together, what you know about each other that nobody else does. Each of those items is either a memory, which is a causal residue of events, or a fact you discovered in the course of the relationship — in either case, something that happened. If consequences means anything in the test, surely things that happened are included. So honest isolation strips friendship down to two people standing in a mutual benevolent disposition, with no past and nothing learned, which is not friendship but charity between strangers. The verdict that friendship is intrinsically good then cannot have come out of the test; it must have gone in before the test, and the method is a costume worn by a pre-theoretical affection for affection. The aesthetic half, by contrast, survives isolation perfectly well: the beauty of a sunset owes nothing to any history, so the enjoyment of beauty really can be judged in a moment, alone. That asymmetry explains the famous pairing — beauty pulls the weight and friendship rides along."

What I want from you is blunt comments, the kind my supervisor gives me, specifically on three things. First, accuracy: is the memory-is-a-causal-residue move fair, or would a Moore scholar reply that the test subtracts the consequences of the state, not its causal preconditions — and if they'd reply that, is it a real reply or a rescue? Is there a respectable Moorean answer that says the isolated state should be described richly, history and all, and if so doesn't that make the test so flexible it certifies anything? Second, do the two cases really behave asymmetrically, or can someone who likes Moore find a history-dependence in the aesthetic case too (familiarity, a face you've looked at for decades)? Third, whether the closing explanation — the bundling claim — is an interesting thesis or just a conspiracy theory about a dead man's sentence. Also please tell me flat out if any part of this is just wrong about what Moore says, I'd much rather hear it now than in reviews later.
