I'm an undergraduate taking a bioethics course on the definition of death, and I sit as a student observer on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. This term we've been auditing the committee's past rulings in disputes over declaring death, and I'm writing my course paper on whether that record is internally consistent. Here is the record as I understand it. First, a man with a massive intracranial hemorrhage met every clinical criterion for brain death — no brainstem reflexes, a negative apnea test, an imaging study showing no intracranial blood flow — but when a nurse touched his arm he twitched, and his heart beat without any machine. His family insisted that a body that is warm, moving, and unassisted is alive. The committee backed the declaration of death and explained to the family that the movement was spinal. Second, a woman in a permanent vegetative state after a cardiac arrest breathed on her own, cycled through sleep and wakefulness, and would never regain consciousness; her family argued that the person they loved was gone and asked the committee to treat her as dead. The committee refused — she was alive. Third, a man left in a complete locked-in state by a brainstem stroke was fully conscious and communicated only by blinking but could not move, speak, or swallow; a relative pressed the claim that the lesion had destroyed most of his brainstem and that someone without a functioning brainstem should count as dead, and the committee refused again. Fourth, in a donation-after-circulatory-death case, the committee treated two minutes without any sign of auto-resuscitation after asystole as sufficient to declare death, dismissing the objection that the donor's skin and other cells remained metabolically active. For context, our jurisdiction, like most, follows the standard statutory formulation involving irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, or of circulatory and respiratory functions.

The case set for next week: a patient whose whole-brain workup is positive — no brainstem reflexes, a positive apnea test, absent intracranial perfusion — except that her posterior pituitary, an anatomical down-growth of the hypothalamus, continues to secrete vasopressin, so she has never developed diabetes insipidus and her sodium has stayed normal. Her intensivist objects that not all the functions of her brain have ceased. One attending agrees and will not declare; a second argues that the committee already dismissed spinal twitches in the first precedent, so a trickle of hormone from tissue that is arguably more gland than brain should not matter either; a third grumbles that this is what the hospital gets for applying a statute that says all functions when what anyone actually cares about is the brain's role in integrating the organism.

In American English, please tell me what the committee should decide if it wants to be consistent with its own record. Which precedent is the closest analog? Identify the candidate rules that fit the existing rulings — for instance, a strict reading on which any residual function anywhere in the brain blocks declaration, a whole-brain rule understood as the loss of the brain's role in integrating the organism, and whatever circulatory rule the fourth precedent embodies, including whether two minutes of permanence was being used there as evidence of irreversibility or as a substitute for it — and say what each rule predicts for the new case. Note that the second precedent appears to exclude at least one obvious candidate rule; I'd like you to confirm or dispute that. Then give your best guess at the ruling with your reasons, state which principle the committee would be adopting with each available choice, and name the further hypothetical case that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules from one another.
