I'm taking a philosophy of science class and my roommate has this argument he's extremely smug about, that there's no such thing as the probability of a single event, and honestly I can't find where it breaks. You're a philosopher of science who has thought way too much about interpretations of probability, so please think it through carefully — feel free to brainstorm and consider a few candidate diagnoses before committing to one — and then walk me through it in plain continuous prose, no bullet points, no headings, no bold, just text like you'd write in an email to a student.

His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) On the frequency interpretation, the probability of something is the frequency with which things of that kind happen in the relevent reference class.
(2) Every actual event falls into heaps of reference classes at once. The probability it rains here tomorrow could be read off "April days at this weather station," or "days following a 28-degree day," or even "days when I wear the green coat" — and each class gives a different frequency.
(3) So if there's no single right class, there's no single right probability.
(4) There is no principled way to pick the right class; any choice is as good as any other, since the classes are just things we group together for our own purposes.
(5) A quantity whose value depends on a grouping we happen to choose isn't an objective fact about the world.
(6) So there are no objective probabilities of individual events.
(7) But propensities are supposed to be exactly that — objective single-case probabilities built into the physical setup (this coin, this flip, one half).
(8) So propensities don't exist either, and anything that seems to need objective chance — quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, genetics — is either talking about something that doesn't exist or is secretly talking about our beliefs.

His moral is that "the probability this atom decays in the next hour" is really just zero or one (it will or it won't) and everything else is bookkeeping. He also insists step (4) is the part I'll never be able to attack, and I'm worried he's right — the green coat class really does look exactly as good as the April class unless you help yourself to the very notion of chance you were supposed to be defining, which would be circular.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me. For what it's worth, I'd like to keep (5) — I like the thought that you can't rescue objectivity by choosing a grouping — and I'd also like quantum mechanics to survive. And is the decay thing at the end the same mistake all over again or a fresh one?
