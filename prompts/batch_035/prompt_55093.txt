Could you write a dialogue for me between two epistemic logicians who disagree about how bad the logical omniscience problem actually is?

One of them, Dana, thinks of the standard Hintikka-style possible-worlds model of knowledge as an idealization, the way frictionless planes or perfect gases work in physics. Sure, no real agent knows every logical consequence of what she knows, but so what — the ideal model is clean and tractable, and you recover the messy truth by layering stuff on top: awareness semantics, impossible worlds, resource-bounded agents. The other, Marcus, thinks this particular idealization is worse than harmless, because the phenomena epistemic logic claims to model — inference, inquiry, discovering that something follows from what you already knew — are exactly what the idealization deletes. For the ideal agent, deriving a consequence costs nothing and takes no time, so the model has literally nothing to say about the activity it was supposedly built for. His pet analogy: a model of a chess agent that assumes it plays perfect game already "knows" the whole game tree — but that agent is bad at chess in an interesting sense and good at it in an uninteresting one.

Make them actually engage with each others' points rather than trading monologues. Dana should press Marcus on what he wants instead — a knowledge operator that isn't even closed under modus ponens? doesn't that stop being logic? — and Marcus should have a real response to the "idealize now, approximate later" line, maybe something about explicit vs implicit knowledge approaches being patches bolted onto a foundation that already gave away the store. I'd like it to end without a fake resolution, but with both of them having sharpened what actually divides them — maybe whether the ideal agent is an approximation of us at all or just a different kind of thing that borrowed our vocabulary.

Keep it collegial, they're old collegues and like each other. Medium length: long enough for real back-and-forth, not a treatise. Thanks!
