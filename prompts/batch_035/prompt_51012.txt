I teach a political philosophy seminar for undergrads (about 18 students, one 75-minute session) and we're coming up on the boundary problem — the question of who counts as a member of the demos, which annoyingly can't itself be settled by a vote without already presupposing you know who the voters are. I don't want to lecture this one, I want them to run into it. Design me an activity where they do.

Here's the rough shape I'm imagining, but feel free to improve it: they're the founding committee of some small self-governing entity (a student makerspace, a co-op, whatever works best), and their first official act is to write a membership rule — who gets a vote at the meetings. Then, once the rule is locked in, they get a stack of edge-case applicants to rule on: someone who works in the building but isn't enrolled, a landlord who owns the place but lives abroad, a guy who runs a delivery route through it every day and is definitely affected by their decisions, the neighbors who hear the noise, a member's kid who'll inherit her membership debts, and maybe something slightly weirder like an automated booking system everyone's schedules depend on. But make the edge cases ones where different plausible principles (being affected by decisions vs. being subject to their authority vs. having a stake in the institution's continuation) genuinely pull apart.

The crucial part: whatever rule they write has to then actually get applied — it decides who's in the room, so to speak, for the *next* vote, which should be on something they care about (control of a real budget, the date of something, real stakes even if small). And I want a moment engineered in where somebody their rule disenfranchised has to just sit there while a decision that affects them gets made — and the only way to fix the rule is a vote they're not entitled to participate in. The bootstrap problem should be felt before it's named. Nobody should hear the phrase "boundary problem" or terms like "all subjected to" vs "all affected" until the debrief.

For the debrief: map the specific moments of the activity onto the actual philosophical positions — where did "affected" balloon into absurdity, where did "subjected" leave out someone who clearly had a claim, who ended up locked in or locked out by a rule written before they arrived. Also tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing: they write something vague and bland like "members in good standing" to dodge the hard cases — tell me how to block that dodge), and be frank about which feature of the real problem an activity like this just cannot reproduce, no matter how I stage it.
