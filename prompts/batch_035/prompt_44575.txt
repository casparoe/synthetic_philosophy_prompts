I'm teaching an upper-level seminar this term called Death, Time, and the Self, and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one essay question on whether death is bad for the person who dies — the Epicurean challenge — but my students have all seen the standard deprivation dialectic (Nagel, Feldman) in intro courses, so a question they can answer by reciting the classic back-and-forth is useless to me. They're also all comfortable with Lucretius's symmetry argument about prenatal nonexistence, and a few of them know Parfit on the bias toward the future, so the question has to push past that material rather than just collect it.

Here's the case I want to build the question around — I invented it, but I think it isolates something real. A clinic offers what it calls "deferred annihilation": for a fee, they implant a device that guarantees you die instantly and painlessly in your sleep exactly ten years from the implant date, and they can prove the device works. Nothing about your health changes before then; your remaining decade is the decade you'd otherwise have, except it now has a known, fixed endpoint that you chose. I want the students to grapple with whether signing up is irrational if death isn't bad for you, whether refusing to sign commits you to saying death is bad for you, and — crucially — which premise of the Epicurean argument (the no-subject premise, the no-time-at-which-it's-bad premise, or the symmetry premise) is actually doing the work in their answer. The case is designed so that the symmetry argument gets weird here: everyone 'had' a fixed endpoint before the clinic existed, so what exactly did the implant change?

Please write (1) the question itself, answerable in about 1200 words over 48 hours, (2) a model answer of roughly 700 words that actually takes a position instead of surveying options, and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 marks. For the marking scheme I'd like a table with four columns: "must-hit move" (the specific philosophical step, named precisely), "full marks" (what a complete execution of the move looks like), "partial credit" (what a half-understood version looks like), and "marks". Also add a row or two for penalties — I specifically want students to lose marks if they quietly slide into talking about the badness of dying, or the grief of others, or the unfairness of early death, without noticing they've changed the subject; part of the test is whether they see they have to bracket all that and say so. Finally, please end with a JSON object with the fields "question_focus" (string), "positions_available" (array of strings listing the defensible stances), "separates_A_from_B" (one line on what an A answer does that a B answer doesn't), and "total_marks" (number). One typo I keep making in my draft instructions, apologies: where I wrote "the no-subject premise" I mean the claim that harm requires a subject who exists to be harmed — make sure the question phrases it so there's no ambiguity.
