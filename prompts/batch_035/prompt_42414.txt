Last month a friend of ours, Dev, who volunteers at a community science night, ran a pyrotechnic chemistry demonstration for a room of about forty people, including children. Dev had run the same demonstration several times before and had used a slightly larger charge this time because the venue was bigger. It went off without a hitch. Afterward, though, a retired chemist in the audience looked over the setup and told Dev, privately, that the safety margin had been much thinner than Dev evidently believed — not quite an accident waiting to happen, but closer than Dev would have accepted had Dev known. Two of my friends, Mara and Jules, witnessed this and have been arguing about it since, and they've asked me to stay neutral, which I've tried to do.

(1) Mara's opening position: nothing that happened afterward can change how blameworthy Dev was. Blame is fixed at the moment of decision by what Dev knew, chose, and risked at that moment. The demonstration went well, so if Dev was not blameworthy when planning it, Dev is not blameworthy now.

(2) Jules replied that this is too quick, but for a reason Jules insisted is not the usual one. Jules is not saying the near-miss makes Dev more blameworthy because bad outcomes make for more blame. Rather, the near-miss supplied evidence that Dev's estimate of the risk was badly wrong, and the quality of that estimate is itself something Dev can be blamed for. Someone who proceeds believing the risk is one in a thousand when it is really one in five is more culpable than someone proceeding with an accurate one-in-a-thousand assessment, even if neither is ever harmed — and we only find out which kind of person someone was when something almost goes wrong.

(3) Mara's response: this just smuggles outcome luck back in one step removed. The information about the thin margin only became available because the near-miss prompted the retired chemist to look. If Dev's blame depends on facts Dev could not access and that only surface through lucky or unlucky channels, then blame still ultimately tracks luck — Jules has moved the luck from the outcome to the discovery, but it is luck all the same.

(4) Jules countered that the channel does not matter: the same information could have arrived the next week via a safety bulletin or Dev's own recalculations, and Jules would say the same thing. What it tracks is the accuracy of Dev's judgment, not fortune. And, Jules added, we already blame people for negligence in exactly this way — Dev presumably had access to the technical specifications and either did not consult them or did not understand them, and that is blameworthy regardless of what happened on the night.

(5) Mara closed with a concession wrapped in a challenge: fine, blame Dev for not reading the specifications — say that plainly. But then the near-miss does no work at all. If Dev had read everything carefully and the margin was still unknowably thin, Mara insists the near-miss should add nothing to Dev's blame, and Jules has never quite said whether Jules agrees.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please go through the exchange move by move: say who is right at each step, not merely who wins overall; identify, if you can, what both of them are missing; and tell me which of them is more right overall. As you go, please say how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the point in your adjudication about which you are least sure.
