i'm on my city's commemorative review board — small city, so it's mostly statues, plaques and street names, and it's all volunteer work. we've got a case coming up next month that has us genuinely split, and i want to make sure whatever we recommend is consistent with what we've already done. here's our track record:

1. bronze statue of a civil war general, put up in the 1920s by a heritage group, honoring him specifically for his defense of slavery. we removed it.
2. campus building named for an early-1900s university president who's commemorated for basically building the institution — quadrupled the endowment, half the campus is his doing. turned out he held pretty vile racial views and said them out loud, but for his time and class this was unremarkable, nobody back then raised an eyebrow. we kept the name and added a plaque.
3. street named for a 19th-century industrialist honored for donating the land our hospital sits on — except his fortune came from brutal company-town labor, and there were strikes and newspaper campaigns about it while he was alive. we renamed it.
4. a wpa-era mural downtown depicting the city's founding, including some really ugly imagery of indigenous people. we kept it up with an interpretive panel, on the reasoning that the mural is a historical display, not an honor.
5. a park named after a still-living donor after a story broke about decades of workplace abuse at his company. we renamed it fast.

ok, the new case. there's a statue of an early 20th-century doctor on the courthouse lawn, honored for pioneering the serum that ended a regional epidemic and still saves lives here. newly digitized archives show the key trials were run on children from the local orphanage with no consent from anyone — and this was before research ethics as a thing even existed, so nobody at the time objected or even thought to. the breakthrough and the wrong are literally the same events; you can't carve them apart the way you maybe can with a founder whose personal views were bad but whose accomplishment was its own thing. there are still descendants of the orphanage kids in town, though no petition or anything yet.

so: what should we recommend if we want to be consistent with ourselves? i can see at least two different rules that fit everything we've done so far — something like "it matters whether the thing being honored is itself the product of the wrongdoing" (that would explain 2 vs 3) versus something like "it matters whether the wrong was contested at the time, with era-typical stuff getting a pass" (that would also explain 2 vs 3, differently) — plus some rider for living honorees to explain 5, and the honor-vs-historical-display thing from 4. walk me through which rules actually fit our record, what each one predicts here, which of our five decisions is the closest precedent, and what we'd be implicitly committing to whichever way we go. also, if you wanted to design one more hypothetical case that would split the plausible rules cleanly, what would it look like?
