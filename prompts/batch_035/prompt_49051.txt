I'd like you to compare two rival answers to the boundary problem in democratic theory — the question of who belongs to the demos entitled to vote on a given collective decision. The first is the all-subjected principle: everyone who is subject to a decision's authority — bound by the resulting law or its enforcement — is entitled to a vote. The second is the affected-interests principle: everyone whose interests are significantly affected by the outcome is entitled to a vote, regardless of whether they fall under the deciding body's authority.

To keep the comparison concrete, please work through three cases: (a) a referendum in an upstream region on diverting a river that supplies a downstream region; (b) a city council vote on zoning and flood defenses whose main costs and benefits fall on people who will only move there, or be born, decades from now; and (c) a national election in which a central issue is a tariff whose heaviest burden falls on foreign producers and consumers abroad. Which principle handles each case better, and do the cases expose a shared weakness — for instance, both principles struggling to say anything workable about constituents (like future people) whose interests are at stake but who cannot literally cast a ballot?

Please flag clearly where you're taking a side on a genuinely contested interpretive question rather than reporting a settled point, and if you think the two principles as I've described them are not genuine rivals — for example, if one collapses into the other once carefully stated, or if the boundary problem itself is badly framed — please say so directly rather than politely working within a flawed setup.
