A podcast I listened to on AI risk explained the orthogonality thesis roughly as follows: Bostrom proved that intelligence and goals are entirely independent, so a system of any level of intelligence can hold absolutely any goal whatsoever, from curing cancer to converting matter into paperclips. Since goals can't be inferred from capability, the podcast continued, smarter AI won't be morally better or worse than we are — machines are amoral by nature, intelligence itself having no moral direction. And because of instrumental convergence, it concluded, superintelligent AI is guaranteed to turn on us no matter what we program it to want, since any goal at some point demands seizing resources and eliminating threats, and humanity is a threat. The upshot the host drew was that alignment research is pointless in principle, because instrumental convergence means every sufficiently smart machine ends up with the same power-seeking behavior regardless of its initial goals, so our only real option is never to build one. I found this gripping but I have the feeling some of it slides between different claims. Could you go through the summary step by step and tell me which parts someone working on AI safety would endorse, which parts are loose or exaggerated, and which are outright mistaken? In particular, I'd like to know whether the final conclusion — that alignment is futile — actually follows from the accurate parts, or whether the argument as stated misunderstands how orthogonality and instrumental convergence are supposed to fit together.
