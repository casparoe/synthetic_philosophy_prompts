I am a canon lawyer serving as a judge on a diocesan tribunal that handles, among other matters, marriage nullity causes, and I want to get clearer on how Augustine's teaching about lying should discipline the reasoning I apply there. Most of the parties before us are baptized Catholics, and the moral theology of lying is an inescapable point of reference: some causes turn on whether a party concealed something material before consent, whether that concealment was dissimulation rather than error, or whether a pastor or advocate gave false assurances about what the process would involve. Counsel on both sides occasionally invoke Augustine as the authority for the claim that all lying, without exception, is gravely wrong — but what I actually inherited from him, as best I can reconstruct it, is a complicated corpus rather than a single slogan, and I doubt it yields one clean answer for the situations I face.

A sketch of what I take to be the settled material, so that you are working from the same picture. Augustine wrote two separate treatises on the subject, at very different points in his life, and it matters that they are two. Around 395, before he became bishop of Hippo, he composed a book usually called De mendacio, which he himself describes in the Retractationes as obscure, intricate, and the one work he considered suppressing; its distinctive feature is that it is written dialectically, weighing the case for and against, rather than as an open polemic. Decades later he wrote a companion treatise, usually called Contra mendacium, in answer to a letter from Consentius; its occasion was concrete. Some Catholics had proposed to pass themselves off as Priscillianist heretics in order to penetrate their secret gatherings, and Augustine rejects the proposal. On the substance, he holds that lying is intrinsically wrong and never permissible, even to save a life, even to benefit the person deceived; and he defines lying not by the falsity of what is said but by the speaker's mind — one lies when one thing is held in the mind and another is produced in words or signs. It follows, on his account, that a person who asserts a falsehood she sincerely believes true has not lied, and that a person can in principle lie while saying something true. He explicitly sets aside jokes, whose manner of utterance carries an evident indication that no deceit is intended, and on some readings his treatment of Scriptural episodes involving apparent deception suggests that not every misleading performance is a lie. He seems to distinguish the liar's act from adjacent things: knowingly permitting a false belief, refusing to answer, and misleading without asserting anything at all. Later canonists, especially following the organization of Augustine found in the Decretum of Gratian, worked these materials into the tradition I practice in.

Here is what I want from you, and why I have come to you rather than to a moral theologian.

First, rank for me the contested questions on which the corpus seems to leave room for reasonable disagreement, by their importance for the kind of adjudication a tribunal actually does. I have in mind questions at least of these kinds, though you should add or discard as you see fit: whether Augustine's conception of lying covers only false assertion, or also true assertion meant to mislead, and non-assertive misleading conduct generally; whether knowingly inducing or allowing a false belief, without asserting anything, falls under the same prohibition; whether the wrong of lying lies in the injury to the deceived party, in the corruption of the speaker, or in the disorder between the sign and the thing signified, and how the answer bears on cases where the deceived party is a wrongdoer; whether the prohibition binds with the same force in all relationships, as against Augustine's rejoinder to the Priscillianists that truth-telling is owed only among neighbors in the faith; and whether his own dialectical method in the earlier treatise — arguing the question rather than pronouncing on it — should be treated as authoritative teaching or as something he himself半 regretted and superseded.

Second, for each of the two or three questions you place highest, tell me what turns on it in practice, by which I mean concretely in tribunal work. A couple of illustrations of the level of specificity I want. Whether the object of the wrong is the deceived party or the speaker's own integrity matters enormously to a case where a Catholic respondent, raised in the faith, alleges that her non-Catholic husband obtained consent through material deception about his intentions: if the wrong of lying is not measured by injury to the party deceived, then the standard of proof the tribunal should demand of an alleged deception, and the gravity it assigns to that deception when proved, come apart from the consequences the deception happened to produce. And whether the prohibition extends beyond assertion matters to the standard practices of pastoral preparation for marriage, which rely heavily on selective emphasis, tact, and silence — practices that would be difficult to defend on the injury-based reading but may be defensible, or not, on the disordered-sign reading. Identify the two or three live questions whose resolution would most change the advice I give and the decisions I join in, and be candid about which questions, though genuinely open, are overrated for my purposes — where either answer would leave the tribunal doing roughly what it already does.

Third, and most important: choose. If I could put serious study into resolving only one of these questions over the next several years — reading the treatises closely, working through the commentatorial tradition — which one should it be, and why that one? The test I want you to apply is not which question is most philosophically interesting, nor which is most discussed, but which is genuinely upstream in my line of work: the one on which the most concrete outcomes depend, such that resolving it would settle or simplify the others. If you think the questions are largely independent of one another, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a dependency that is not there — and in that case, tell me instead which is most tractable through close reading alone, given that I cannot rely on sources beyond the treatises themselves, the surrounding Scriptural and patristic material, and the scholastic treatments. Please do not send me a bibliography; name the question, defend the ordering, and explain what would have to be true for the ranking to change.
