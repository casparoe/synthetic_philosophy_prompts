A law-school classmate of mine, Mira, has views on hate speech that our seminar keeps circling back to, and before we build our paper around them we want to know whether they amount to a coherent rule — especially since the paper compares the First Amendment approach with European dignity-based hate-speech law, and she's firmly on the American side. Her track record, each verdict being what she thinks the law ought to be: a pamphlet arguing that violent revolution will eventually be necessary — lawful; a widely published polemic asserting as fact that a religious minority is carrying out a secret plan to replace the native population — lawful; a blasphemous film portraying the majority religion's prophet as a fraud — lawful; a stand-up routine whose entire premise is cruel jokes about an ethnic group — lawful; a university declining to host the author of the replacement polemic — also fine, since a private institution may decline even though the state could not ban the pamphlet; a speaker telling an angry crowd, outside a hall where the target group is meeting, to go in and drive them out right now — unlawful; a cross burned at midnight on a Black family's lawn — unlawful; a website posting the names, photos, and home addresses of doctors, with the ones already murdered struck through — unlawful; a newspaper knowingly reporting that a named local man committed a crime he did not commit — unlawful. When we press her she insists the pattern is obvious, but she flatly refuses to state a principle.

The new case our paper turns on: a nationalist group has applied to march through the neighborhood where the minority it vilifies actually lives, carrying banners depicting that minority as vermin and chanting that it must be expelled, with no named individuals, no threat against any particular person, and no call for anything that crowd could do that afternoon. What will Mira say — lawful or unlawful? I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the rules her track record is consistent with: she could be running a viewpoint-neutrality rule on which only incitement, true threats, and defamation of identifiable persons fall outside protection, or she could be running something like an individualized-targeting rule on which speech loses protection when it is aimed at identifiable people or gatherings of them where they cannot avoid it, while publication aimed at the public at large keeps protection however vile. Both rules fit every verdict she has given so far, as far as I can tell, and they come apart on the march. So: predict her verdict, identify the candidate rules and which of them fits her record best and why, say which earlier verdict is closest on each rule, and propose the single further case — beyond the march — that would do the most to distinguish the rules from each other. Since our paper is a comparison, also tell me which of her earlier verdicts a European court applying a dignity-based hate-speech statute would be most likely to reach the opposite result on, and whether there is one case on which her rule and the European approach would be maximally opposed.

Please lead with your prediction of her verdict in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question.
