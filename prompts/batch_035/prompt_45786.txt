**Exam question:** Present the premises of Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, explain what the argument is supposed to show about non-reductive physicalism, and assess one prominent reply to it.

**Student's answer:** Kim's argument is an overdetermination argument. It rests on three claims. First, causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. Second, supervenience: no mental difference without some physical difference. Third, causal exclusion: where an event already has a sufficient cause, we should not posit a further, distinct sufficient cause of it.

Now suppose some mental event M causes a physical event P*. By supervenience, M has a physical base P, and by closure P is a sufficient cause of P*. So P* has two sufficient causes: M and P. The situation is like two marksmen firing simultaneously at a victim, either shot being fatal on its own. Since overdetermination of this familiar, symmetric kind cannot be widespread in nature, one of the causes must go: either M just is P, or M is causally idle with respect to P*.

The argument's target is not dualism but non-reductive physicalism: if mental properties are irreducible, they end up epiphenomenal. This applies with special force to Davidson's anomalous monism, which makes mental causation vacuous — a concession Davidson himself eventually made.

One might reply that the physical realizes rather than causes the mental, so there are not two causes competing for a single effect. But realization is a species of causation: the brain state brings about the mental state, and what brings something about cannot simultaneously stand as a rival cause of what it brings about. So the reply fails, and the upshot is that Kim has vindicated the type-identity theory: since either M is identical with P or the mental is epiphenomenal, and epiphenomenalism is absurd, the identity theory is true — and Putnam's multiple realizability objection to it was a mistake.
