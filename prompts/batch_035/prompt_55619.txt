Two friends of mine have been arguing for months, off and on, about what life actually is, and at the last dinner it reached a point where neither would give an inch. I have no background in science — I left school early and have only read popular stuff — so I could only follow so far, and I want to understand who was right at each step. Here is how it went, as best as I can reconstruct it.

It started when Daniel, who reads a lot of biology, said the essential thing about life is reproduction with heredity and variation: things are alive if they make copies of themselves, the copies mostly resemble the parents, and occasional differences let lineages change over time. He said this is close to how space scientists define life when they go looking for it on other planets.

Priya said reproduction can't be the mark of life, because plenty of undeniably living things can't reproduce at all: a mule, a worker ant, a woman past menopause, an oak tree I planted from a sterile hybrid. They're alive even though nothing copies them. What they all still do is keep themselves going — repair, feed, hold their insides different from their surroundings, push back against decay. She said that self-maintenance, not reproduction, is the heart of it: a candle flame and a crystal grow and even spread, but they don't maintain anything, they just happen; a cell does work to stay the way it is.

Daniel answered that Priya was looking at the wrong level. Individual mules and worker ants are alive because they belong to lineages that do reproduce with variation — the ant is a part of a system that copies itself, even if this particular ant is a non-copying part. He said the unit of life is the lineage, not the individual, so his definition survives her examples untouched.

Priya then posed something Daniel hadn't expected, which is where it got hard for me. She said: imagine a bacterium in a lab whose copying machinery has been made essentially perfect, so that when it divides, the daughters are always exact copies — no variation ever, so no possibility of evolutionary change, though everything else about it, the feeding and repair and dividing, goes on normally. Is it dead? It seems absurd to call it dead. And on the other side, take a crystal growing in a solution and splitting when it gets too big, with impurities now and then changing how a shard grows, which its own shards inherit — that's a lineage with copying and variation, but no self-maintenance whatsoever. She said Daniel's definition classifies the crystal as alive and the bacterium as dead, and that this is exactly backwards.

Daniel had two replies. First, he said heritable copying has to be informational, not mere structural repetition: a flame and a crystal pass on their form without passing on anything like instructions, whereas a cell passes on a genetic system that spells out how to build the next one. Second, about the perfect-copying bacterium, he said it still has the machinery for variation, only disabled, and having the machinery is what matters — like a person who is temporarily sterile is still a member of a reproducing kind.

Priya said that reply proves too much. If having the machinery, even switched off, is enough, then a virus is alive whenever it's outside a cell — it carries full instructions for copying itself and simply lacks the factory — and a freeze-dried culture in a warehouse, dormant for decades, would be an ecosystem sitting on a shelf. She said Daniel keeps shifting between what a thing does and what it's connected to by descent or potential, and that a definition of life that can't be applied to a single organism in front of you, without tracing its ancestry or its capabilities, is a definition of something else — of history, maybe, or of evolvability — rather than of being alive. Daniel said every real boundary case will embarrass any definition, that this is true of every concept we use, and that a definition of life should tell us where life came from and where else we might find it, not just sort the objects already on the table.

Could you go through their exchange step by step and tell me who is right about what — including whether the whole disagreement is just two words being used for different purposes, or whether one of them is actually drawing the better line around the same thing? And if you think both have hold of something, I'd like to hear where exactly the right division falls, especially on the two cases Priya invented: the error-free bacterium and the splitting crystal.
