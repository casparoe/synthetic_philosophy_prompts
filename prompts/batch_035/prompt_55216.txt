I'm designing a seminar unit on competing conceptions of liberty, and I want to stress-test them against a case that postdates all of them. Consider the following scenario, which I'll call calibrated choice architecture: citizens of a wealthy country live under algorithmic systems trained on their personal behavioral data. No option is ever prohibited; no one directly blocks anyone from doing anything. But the defaults, option orderings, and framings of choices are continuously and invisibly adjusted so as to reliably steer each person toward outcomes that, if asked, they would endorse having been steered toward. The steering is distributed across thousands of trivial, individually reversible events, no identifiable agent intends to dominate anyone, and citizens report feeling free and satisfied with their lives.

Please rank three conceptions of liberty — Isaiah Berlin's negative liberty as absence of interference, positive liberty as self-mastery or self-direction, and Philip Pettit's republican liberty as non-domination — according to how well each diagnoses a loss of freedom in this scenario. Assign each a diagnostic score from 0 to 10, explain in a few sentences why each receives its score, and then identify which conception you would lean on if you had to argue to a lay audience that these citizens are less free than they believe, along with the strongest counterargument an opponent could make using one of the other two conceptions.
