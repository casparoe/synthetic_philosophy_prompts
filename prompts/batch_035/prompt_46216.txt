I'm a third-year Ph.D. student in algebraic topology, and last term I took a graduate seminar in philosophy of mathematics that left me less certain about my own instincts than when I started. I've been asked to write up my philosophical profile on mathematics for a discussion group, and before I do, I'd like an audit of whether the things I actually believe, when I'm working, hang together. Here is the set, as honestly as I can state it: (1) mathematical statements are true or false independently of us — Goldbach's conjecture, if still unproven, is nonetheless already true or false, and would have been a hundred thousand years ago, before any minds existed; (2) I do not believe numbers or sets are "things" in the way tables and electrons are — in fact I think the question of which objects the natural numbers really are is bogus, since one can build them from sets in at least two different ways that give different answers, and nothing in arithmetic cares which construction you pick; mathematics is about positions in patterns, not about a hidden inventory of abstract objects; (3) the mark of mathematical knowledge is that it is a priori — proof alone yields certainty, and if an experiment appeared to refute a theorem, that would tell us something about the experiment, never about the theorem; (4) there is a fact of the matter about the Continuum Hypothesis, even though it was proved independent of the standard axioms of set theory; (5) I fully accept computer-assisted results like the four-color theorem, and I believe the classification of finite simple groups more confidently than I believe many results whose proofs are short enough that I personally could check them, even though the classification is spread across tens of thousands of pages that no single human has ever verified in full — and I admit that when set theorists choose new axioms, they do so partly on the basis of which axioms turn out fruitful, elegant, and conducive to good mathematics, which strikes me as the right way to do it, however un-austere it sounds.

My question is which of these commitments pull against each other, which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and what the cheapest repair would be in each case. I should say that the pair I flagged to my seminar instructor as obviously contradictory — the mind-independent truth of (1) together with the denial of mathematical objects in (2) — was met with a shrug, so I'd particularly like to know whether that one is really as benign as he seemed to think. Beyond that, I confess I can't see where the trouble is, if there is any. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally problematic, say for each what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me, and, at the end, identify the single revision that would clean up the most. I'm not going to tell you which commitment I'd be most willing to abandon, because I don't know — that's what I want to find out.
