I teach intro philosophy of mind at a community college, 18ish students, one 75-minute session. Next week is Avicenna's flying man and I want them to actually feel the argument instead of taking notes on it. The setup as I present it, roughly: imagine being created all at once, fully formed, suspended in mid-air, blindfolded, limbs splayed, falling through a void with no contact with anything, no sound, nothing at all. And the claim is you'd still affirm your own existence — that you'd be aware of yourself without being aware of any limb or organ or external thing. Fine on paper, but on paper it dies in five minutes to the first smartass.

My half-formed idea so far is a staged float: lights off, earplugs, lying on the floor while I narrate, for real minutes, before anyone hears the name Avicenna. But I suspect that's too easy to poke at, since nobody in my classroom was created yesterday. They all have decades of sensory memory telling them they have bodies, so if they still feel self-aware in the dark, that might show nothing beyond the fact that memories persist — which is exactly the objection I want them to reach, but on purpose, not by accident. So design me the full activity with that baked in. Maybe split the class: some get a narration that keeps the present-tense sensory setup (you are floating, you feel nothing, you hear nothing) and others get one that tries to strip the past too (you have just been created, you have never seen or touched anything), so when we come back to the room there's something to compare and argue about. Or do something better if you see it — I'm not attached to the float.

What I need: rules, a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, what the facilitator says and when to shut up, how to run the debrief so the moments people actually had get mapped onto the parts of the argument (the suspension, the absence of sensation, the affirmation that survives it), and so they end up fighting about whether the felt certainty in the dark proves anything about the self — not about whether lying on the floor was relaxing. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (students falling asleep is one I already forsee) and how to handle each thing. And be honest about which part of Avicenna's thought experiment no classroom version can reproduce at all, so I know what I have to supply myself in the debrief rather than pretend the activity showed it.

Write it as plain prose like you're emailing a collegue — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous text.
