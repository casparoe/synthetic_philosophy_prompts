My friend Dana and I have spent years arguing, mostly over coffee, about when you're allowed to trust your own thinking and when you have to back off because of something you know about your thinking. Neither of us has any philosophy training; we just keep finding cases. I've got a file of Dana's rulings, and next week we're doing a new one, so I want a prediction before I bring it to her.

Her rulings so far. First, the pill case: you took a drug and later read a solid study showing that people who took it have a seventy percent chance of forming a false memory of that evening; you vividly remember leaving the party at eleven. Dana said the memory is dead weight — your confidence should drop to match the study, and she added that saying "I remember leaving at eleven, though my memory is probably false" is not a state a person should be in; pick the second half and drop the first. Second, the bill case: you and an equally good friend recheck the arithmetic on a shared dinner, both get different totals, both recheck twice, nobody budges. Dana said each of you is entitled to your own figure; you don't have to meet in the middle, because the rechecking is its own evidence and you did it. Third, the interview case: you come out convinced you aced it, and a friend with a long track record of your past predictions tells you that you always think interviews went better than they did. Dana said lower your confidence some, but not all the way down to the friend's track record, because your detailed impressions of how the conversation went still count for something. Fourth, the wine case: you know from experience you can't tell good wine from bad, a label and a sommelier say it's excellent. Dana said the sommelier wins outright, no hedging. And fifth, the forecast case: the weather station is seventy percent accurate, a farmer with a bad knee is eighty-five percent accurate, and they disagree about tomorrow's rain. Dana said trust the knee — what matters is the track record, not the pedigree of the source.

The new case: a student gets an exam back, is sure the grader made an error on question four, and rechecks her own answer three times; each time it still looks airtight to her. But the professor keeps records, and they show that students in exactly this position — students who were confident and rechecked and still felt sure — turn out to be wrong ninety percent of the time. The stats aren't about overconfident students in general; they're specifically about confident recheckers. What will Dana say? My sense is that her instinct for "the rechecking is its own evidence" from the bill case pulls one way, and her instinct from the pill case — that you shouldn't believe something while also believing your belief is probably wrong — pulls the other, because here the rechecking is already baked into the ninety percent. So: predict her verdict, lay out the different rules that could fit all five of her earlier rulings (I can see at least two — something like "you only bow to statistics about your thinking when you can't directly inspect the thing itself," and something like "all of it is just evidence to be weighed, and rechecking matters only insofar as it's new information"), say which rule you'd bet on and why, and tell me what further case I should put to Dana that would best split those rules apart — and whether the student case itself already does it.
