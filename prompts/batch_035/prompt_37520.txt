Some background first so it makes sense: I recently got out of a two-year stretch working for a boss who was, in hindsight, really good at making you doubt yourself — denying things he'd said, telling you you'd agreed to stuff you hadn't. I've been doing a lot of thinking (plus a bit of therapy) about how anyone stays anchored in what they know, and in the middle of that I noticed a pattern I can't find a name for, and its bugging me.

The pattern, two examples:

1. My therapist suggested I write down interactions with him as they happened, so I'd have something to check my later doubts against. Then it hit me that to trust the notes over my later doubts, I have to trust my past self's perception and honesty — the exact things the notes were supposed to be checking. And there's no bootstrapping out, because any further check stands on the same legs. Weirdly, this doesn't feel like a problem in practice. Nobody treats it as unresolved.

2. Arguing with my brother, who's gotten very into a podcast host who says you should question absolutely everything. Someone challenged that guy once and he replied that to doubt your own eyes you'd need to look at something, and to doubt your own reasoning you'd need to reason. My brother took it as a devastating comeback, but the guy kind of had a point — except it clearly isn't a normal reason. He didn't produce evidence. The doubt just has nowhere to land.

So in both cases there's some commitment that (a) can't be defended with evidence, (b) doesn't work like a premise — people don't use it in arguments so much as argue from inside it — and (c) isn't dogma either. The podcast guy updates on plenty of things, nobody is being stubborn; if you pushed them they'd say sure, in principle I could be wrong, and then carry on completely unchanged. It's less a belief they hold than the floor they're standing on. When it gets doubted, the doubt isn't answered, it's more... refused — but not defensively, more like asking whether chess is a legal chess move.

I know about the regress problem and that foundationalists have their properly basic beliefs, but this doesn't seem like that. A basic belief is still supposed to get justification from somewhere (an experience, whatever), and the things I'm describing don't get any justification at all — they're more like whatever lets justification happen in the first place. I also considered self-sealing reasoning, but these people aren't immune to argument in general, only to this one kind of question.
