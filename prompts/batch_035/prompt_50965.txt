I run a discussion group at a reentry program — guys in their twenties to fifties, most of them didn't finish high school, plenty smart, and every one of them has had a parole officer, judge, ER nurse or cop decide they were lying or exaggerating when they weren't. Next month I want to do one session on epistemic injustice — the testimonial and hermeneutical kinds — but with no jargon and no readings, 50 minutes, a whiteboard. Plan it for me, don't just explain the view: what has to come first and what rides on it, whether testimonial injustice can be taught before hermeneutical or the other way around, and what example should carry the whole thing — is the classic one about people who suffered sexual harassment before anyone had the words for it right for this crowd, or would something like a patient whose chronic pain keeps getting read as drug-seeking do better work, and why? Then the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form on the spot — I'm guessing "anytime somebody doubts you, that's an injustice" and "the fix is to just believe everyone" — and exactly where in the sequence I need to head each off, plus what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so. End with one question I can ask that would show me they've got the difference between being wrong about a person and wronging them epistemically. Keep it under 650 words — I'll be reading this off my phone at 8am.
