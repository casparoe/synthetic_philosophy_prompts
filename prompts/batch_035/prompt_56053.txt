I'm a high school senior, and over the summer I read my way into philosophy of mind and convinced myself of a position I now want stress-tested. The position is a version of Dennett's real patterns idea, made more precise than he usually makes it: a pattern is real if and only if, for some fixed class of prediction tasks, adopting a higher-level stance (the intentional or design stance rather than the physical stance) lets you get the right answers while using strictly less information than the full physical-level description of the system would require. On this view, beliefs are real in the same sense thermostats and genes are real: they are compression wins. The twist I've added, which I haven't seen anywhere, is that I think the compression has to be measured relative to an explicitly stated question class — so reality of a pattern becomes a three-place relation among the system, the stance, and the list of predictions you care about — and I've half-convinced myself this triadic reformulation is what saves the view from its standard problems.

I want to know what would count against this position, and I want the answer to lead with its bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be concrete about which intuitions, cases, and results the position stands or falls with. A few things I've already started worrying about, which you should address but shouldn't limit yourself to: (1) trivially regular systems — a pendulum, a screen saver, the little shapes in those old animation experiments that people instinctively describe as chasing and hiding from each other — where a higher-level description is vastly shorter but where I feel uneasy saying the pattern is real in the same way my belief that it will rain is real; (2) whether the measure of descriptive shortness is too observer-relative, since how long a description is depends on the language or machine you measure it in, and the wiggle room might be exactly as large as the controversial cases; (3) whether making reality relative to a question class means two people with different interests could correctly disagree about whether the same belief exists, and whether that is a cost my view must pay or a bullet it can bite. I'd also like you to tell me which objections a committed defender of this kind of view would concede as genuinely counting against it, and which they would wave off — and, honestly, whether you think my triadic amendment is doing real work or whether it's just an epicycle that lets the view survive anything, which would make it unfalsifiable in a way that should worry me even for a philosophical position.
