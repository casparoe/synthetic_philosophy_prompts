I'm grading midterm exams for a survey course on classical Chinese philosophy. The students are second- and third-year undergraduates with no background in logic or linguistics; the course has covered the Warring States "disputation" debates, including the Zhuangzi's report of Hui Shi's theses, the "White Horse" dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long, and a selection from the Mohist Canons on names. Below is the exam question as it appeared on the paper, followed by one student's answer, reproduced as written. Please assign a mark out of 25 and then write the comments you would put on the returned exam: a point-by-point assessment that justifies the mark, so that the student can see exactly which steps earned credit and which lost it. Where a passage is right for the wrong reason, or wrong in a way that is easy to miss under time pressure, say so explicitly. The comments should be usable as marginal and summary notes, not a general impression of the answer.

The exam question (25 points):

The dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long defends the thesis that a white horse is not a horse. (a) Reconstruct, in your own words, the two main lines of argument the dialogue offers for this thesis: the line about what the terms fix — that horse, by itself, marks out the shape while white marks out a color — and the line about which animals may be brought — that a yellow or a black horse will satisfy a request for a horse but not a request for a white horse. [10 points] (b) A common reaction is that the thesis is a cheap equivocation on the word is: read as predication it is plainly false, read as identity of expressions it is trivially true. Assess that reaction: does the ambiguity explain what is going on in the dialogue, or does something else do the work? [10 points] (c) Gongsun Long and Hui Shi are conventionally grouped together as the School of Names. Is that grouping apt? [5 points]

The student's answer:

(a) Gongsun Long's case rests on two lines of reasoning. The first concerns what each word does. The word horse, by itself, marks out the shape: something is a horse in virtue of its form, and no color is thereby specified. The word white, by itself, marks out a color. Put them together and you have a compound expression fixed by both color and shape, whereas the simple word is fixed by shape alone. Since the compound is fixed by something the simple word is not fixed by, the two are not the same expression, and the thing named by the one is not the thing named by the other. That is the thesis: a white horse is not a horse. The second line concerns which animals will do. Asked for a horse, one can bring a yellow one or a black one; asked for a white horse, one cannot. So the animals that answer to horse and the animals that answer to white horse do not form the same group.

(b) The usual diagnosis is that is is doing double duty. On the predication reading, the thesis is flatly false: of course a white horse is a horse. On the identity reading, it is trivially true: the expression white horse is not the expression horse, which no one ever doubted. Sliding between the two readings creates the appearance of paradox, and once the slide is noticed the dialogue deflates into a lesson about ambiguity.

But the dialogue is more than a pun, because Gongsun Long sets out a threefold division of names in the course of it: unrestricted names, which apply to everything; classifying names, which apply to the things of a kind; and private names, which apply to a single thing. Against that background, the thesis is not a pun but a substantive claim about how classifying names carve things up. And the two arguments in (a), which can look like different moves, are on this showing the same move made twice: the shape-and-color argument says in the vocabulary of naming what the yellow-and-black argument says in the vocabulary of bringing — namely, that the class of white horses is a narrower class than the class of horses. Once we see that the dispute concerns the placement of a class within a wider class, we can see what the disputers were really arguing about, and why the interlocutor keeps losing.

(c) The label School of Names is a later librarian's category, not a self-description: it was fixed by Han bibliographers, long after the disputers, and it lumps together people who did not think of themselves as colleagues. Hui Shi, so grouped, argued, as the Zhuangzi reports, that heaven and earth form one body and that one should care for all things alike; his bent is toward the one, with the many dissolving into it. Gongsun Long's bent is the opposite: separating the white horse from the horse, separating the hard and the white. The grouping is apt only in the thin sense that both cared about the relation of names to things.
