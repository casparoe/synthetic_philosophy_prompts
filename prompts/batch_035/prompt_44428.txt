I'm organizing a small workshop on what pain asymbolia and related dissociations show about the badness of pain: three philosophers, two neuroscientists, and a pain clinician, with funding for an initial meeting and two follow-ups over eighteen months. My working suspicion is that the debate has stalled because everyone agrees asymbolia involves a decoupling of the sensory dimension of pain from its affective-motivational dimension, but no one agrees on what that decoupling is supposed to establish about value: whether pain's badness resides in the raw feel, in the aversive reaction to it, in what the aversion tracks, or somewhere else entirely.

Please treat asymbolia as one member of a family of cases rather than a clinical curiosity: patients who report intense pain while remaining indifferent to it, sometimes even amused; the full-blown nociception of nonverbal infants and many animals, whose affective character cannot be probed by self-report; and drug effects and placebo responses that leave the sensation intact while draining its aversiveness. The question of what these cases have in common, and what each shows, is part of what I want prioritized.

What I need from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Identify the three or four open questions in this area most worth the workshop's time, ranked, with the ranking defended along the following lines: for each question, say why it is upstream of the others — which live debates about, for example, the moral status of animals, neonatal analgesia, or the construction of welfare measures tacitly presuppose an answer to it; whether it can be advanced by conceptual analysis alone or requires empirical input the workshop could realistically arrange; and what kind of evidence over the next few years would legitimately move it up or down the list.

I also want a frank section on which alleged implications are overrated or simply do not follow. I am particularly suspicious of the quick inference from pain-without-distress is possible to distress rather than sensation is what makes pain bad, and of the mirror-image inference that asymbolia shows pain's badness was never intrinsic to pain at all. Tell me whether these suspicions are warranted on the current state of the debate, or whether I am just protecting a non-reductive view of pain's badness that I happen to hold. If a question you rank highly would mainly be settled by neuroscience rather than philosophy, say so plainly — the worst outcome for this workshop would be two days of conceptual throat-clearing followed by a call for more data nobody in the room can gather.
