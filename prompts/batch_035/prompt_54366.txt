I direct grantmaking for a small family foundation. We make roughly thirty grants a year, none large enough to fund a project outright but each large enough to matter, across three areas the charter names without ranking them: early-stage medical research, music education in under-resourced schools, and land conservation. The board requires every decision to be defensible in a paragraph to a rejected applicant; I have one part-time analyst and about twenty minutes of committee time per shortlisted proposal.

Our difficulty is not ranking within a category — we can usually order the medical proposals against one another, the music proposals against one another. It is comparing across categories: a pilot study of a cheap diagnostic against a proposal that would keep a rural school's music program alive for three years. Some years we agonize over such pairs; some years the loudest trustee prevails. I have come to suspect the agonizing is sometimes a category error (there was a common currency all along, and we should have computed) and sometimes the only honest response (there was no currency, and no amount of deliberation will manufacture one). I cannot currently tell which situation we are in, and I would like a procedure that can.

Please write a decision procedure the committee can run in a meeting, grounded in what the philosophy of value actually says about incommensurability and hard choices — Raz on how options can be excluded from comparison before any weighing occurs; Chang on parity, including her test of whether a small improvement in one option would decisively tip the balance, and on what follows when it would not; and the distinction between a tradeoff that is painful but has a common currency and a comparison in which no currency exists at all. The procedure should specify, in this order: gatekeeping questions that stop it with a no (mission fit, basic competence — so that value comparisons occur only between proposals that have already cleared the bar); tests for whether a given pair is commensurable, incomparable, or on a par, phrased so a non-philosopher can apply them in five minutes; and what to do in each case — when to compute, when the correct instrument is a lottery or a rotation between categories and why that is not an abdication of responsibility. It should end by marking the point at which the procedure runs out and hands the decision to the committee because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts, together with a sentence on what the committee is doing at that point — making a reason rather than discovering one, if that is the right way to put it.

Then run it on three comparisons. First, two medical research proposals whose expected benefits are both expressible, roughly, in life-years. Second, the diagnostic pilot against the rural music program. Third, a music education proposal against a land conservation proposal in which I privately want the music one to win. Report honestly where the procedure gives an answer I will not like — my guess is that it will sometimes tell us a tie was broken by lot, and that the paragraph to the rejected applicant must say that rather than pretend the winner was better. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
