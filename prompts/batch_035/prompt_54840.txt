I've been reading about how defeats become national myths and I want a careful case analysis of the best example I know: the Battle of Kosovo. The facts, since neither of us can look them up: in 1389 a Serbian army under Prince Lazar fought the Ottomans under Sultan Murad on the Kosovo field. Murad was killed, Lazar was captured and executed, the battle itself was basically a mutual bloodletting, and Serbia ended up under Ottoman control anyway. The myth built on it afterwards is much stranger than the battle -- in the epics, a grey falcon from Jerusalem offers Lazar a choice between an earthly kingdom (victory) and the Kingdom of Heaven (defeat and martyrdom), and he chooses the heavenly one, so the loss becomes a chosen sacrifice; Miloš Obilić, who killed Murad, becomes the saint-hero; and Lazar's son-in-law Vuk Branković becomes the arch-traitor even though historians think he actually fought to the end. Six hundred years later, to the day, Milošević stood at a monument on that same field and gave a speech to a crowd of maybe a million people that everyone now reads as foreshadowing the Yugoslav wars.

So analyze this as a case study in lost causes and national myths. What work does the myth do that the actual battle couldn't -- and why is a defeat better raw material for that than a victory would be? Then stress-test the obvious parallels: the Confederate Lost Cause, which had a real political program (white supremacy, Redemption) that needed laundering after Appomattox, and Masada, which was a marginal episode elevated centuries later partly through excavation and staged IDF ceremonies. Does Kosovo fit either pattern, or does the "heavenly kingdom" move -- retroactively converting defeat into deliberate sacrifice -- make it something structurally different from both? And tell me honestly where the mythmaking frame goes silent: it explains why 1989 was emotionally available, but I don't think it can explain why it happened when it did or why the specific actors used it, and I want you to be precise about what it does and doesn't account for rather than forcing the theory to speak.
