I'm an undergraduate currently taking an intermediate metaphysics course, and we have just begun the unit on the problem of universals. I believe I have found a simple argument that settles the entire debate, and before I build my term paper around it, I would like to test it.

My reasoning runs as follows. Ockham's razor instructs us not to multiply entities beyond necessity. A realist about universals posits two kinds of entity — particulars and universals — whereas a nominalist posits only particulars. An ontology with strictly fewer entities is always better, other things being equal; and here the other things are equal, because both sides are trying to explain the very same facts about attribute-agreement (that two objects can both be red in the same way, that distinct things can share a nature). So the razor proves nominalism: the problem of universals is not an open question at all but a solved one, settled by a straightforward count of entities. What I cannot understand is why our textbook still presents the debate as live rather than as a historical curiosity, and why contemporary philosophers keep publishing on it.

I have a second, related thought. As I understand the position, every realist about universals is a Platonist who holds that universals exist in a separate, non-spatiotemporal realm that we could never observe or visit. If that really is the realist's view, then it looks empirically empty from the start, and my argument from parsimony isn't even needed to dismiss it.

Is my argument sound, or have I misdescribed the dialectic somewhere? If I have gone wrong, I want to know precisely where and how badly. As you answer, please say how confident you are in each of your claims, and explicitly single out the point at which you are least sure — I would rather know in advance where even the standard textbook story is contested than walk into my term paper with a false sense of security.
