Analyse the Sally Clark case — the English solicitor convicted in 1999 of murdering her two infant sons, on the strength of prosecution evidence that included the paediatrician Roy Meadow's figure of one in 73 million for the chance of two unexplained infant deaths in a family like hers, with her conviction quashed a few years later after medical evidence favouring her had come to light — as a case study in epistemic injustice. I know the broad outlines; what I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a loose gesture at the concept. Identify where, precisely, Fricker's testimonial injustice is supposed to live here: is it the jury discounting Clark's own account, the court inflating Meadow's credibility qua expert, or something in between, and does the concept even apply when the injustice runs through the over-weighting of one speaker rather than the downgrading of another? Consider whether hermeneutical injustice fits at all — the mothers' accounts of sudden infant deaths arguably lacked adequate interpretive resources, or arguably did not — and be candid if the theory strains there. Then say what the episode exposes that the theory is silent on: the prosecutor's fallacy and the misuse of statistics seem to be a failure of institutional epistemology rather than of justice as Fricker frames it, and I want your view on whether that is a gap the concept should be extended to cover or a category error. In short: run the analysis in both directions — what epistemic injustice illuminates about the case, and what the case does to the theory.
