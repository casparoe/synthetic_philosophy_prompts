I'm not a philosopher or a doctor, just someone who got to thinking after a family meeting about organ donation, and I've managed to puzzle myself in a way I can't untangle. Here is what struck me. In the hospital, the standard for declaring someone dead so that their organs can be donated is that their entire brain, including the brainstem, has permanently stopped functioning — even though a ventilator is keeping the heart beating, the skin is warm, and the body looks asleep. So in that setting, the medical and legal systems seem to operate on the idea that the person is gone once the brain is gone, even though an awful lot of the body is still ticking along. Yet at the same time, if a patient has a living will, or a bank account, or a criminal charge pending, none of that follows the beating heart or the warm body — it all follows whatever the brain supported: memories, decisions, personality. And I recently read about companies that offer to freeze people's heads after death, on the theory that everything that matters about you is in the brain and the rest is replaceable. All of this reminds me of an old thought experiment I stumbled across, where a philosopher imagines his brain being removed from his body, kept alive in a vat, and connected back by radio — and then puzzles over which lump of matter he should point to when he says the word "I."

So my question is this: taken together, what view of the self do these practices — brain-death organ donation, legal identity following the brain rather than the body, and the head-freezing business — actually presuppose? Is it the crude-seeming claim that the person just is the brain, a lump of tissue, so that you are wherever your cortex is? Or could these same practices be justified by a more abstract view, on which you are your memories and personality and the brain matters only as their carrier — in which case a whole-brain transplant and, say, scanning and copying those contents might come apart in strange ways? And is there a genuine inconsistency lurking here, given that we declare a warm, beating body dead because its brain is gone, yet everyone would agree that if that living brain were moved into another body, the person would have gone with it? It feels like the practices point in one direction while common sense points slightly sideways, and I can't tell whether that's a real tension or just me confusing two different questions.

I'd like a definite answer, not a menu of possibilities. Commit to what you think the implicit theory behind these practices is, say whether the practices strictly require that theory or would survive under a rival one, and tell me plainly whether you think the tension I described is real or illusory. Please answer in American English.
