I've been reading and occasionally posting on rationalist forums and blogs for over a decade, and for the last few years I've helped run a small online forecasting community — a few hundred members posting probability estimates on geopolitical, technological, and scientific questions over multi-month horizons. Something keeps nagging at me, and I want to turn it into a fundable project.

The phenomenon: our members have all read the standard material on information cascades — the sequential-decision models where each person observes predecessors' choices, partially infers their private signals from them, and can end up rationally ignoring their own signal once the public evidence pile grows heavy enough. These people know about cascades the way a dieter knows about calories. And yet I'm fairly confident we still get them: a respected early forecaster posts a confident estimate, three or four people visibly shift toward it, and within a week the community median has converged on a number that, as far as I can reconstruct the underlying evidence, isn't supported by it.

My half-formed idea for the research question: everyone seems to assume the fix for cascades is either (a) making participants more aware of cascade dynamics, or (b) restructuring who speaks when — simultaneous anonymous estimates, batch updates, prediction markets instead of open discussion threads. I suspect both fixes leak, for one underlying reason: in any real community, the timing and visibility of a contribution is itself an informative action, so the sequential structure can never be fully sterilized. The lurker who waits three days before posting, the veteran who quietly edits an old estimate rather than posting a new one — these are actions that successors can read, and the community can cascade on timing and participation rather than on content. Call it a meta-cascade. I don't think I've seen this analyzed, though I could be wrong; don't take my word for the state of the literature.

I'm applying for a nine-month grant from a foundation that funds work on improving collective epistemics, and I need a two-page proposal.

Take the role of a formal epistemologist with a background in microeconomic theory and experience turning half-baked forum intuitions into tractable research questions. Before drafting anything, brainstorm carefully: sharpen my question until it has a determinate answer, decide whether the timing-as-signal idea can actually be modeled or whether it dissolves under scrutiny once you push on it, and consider whether there's a better framing of the whole problem that I've missed. The proposal should then state the research question precisely; explain what the standard results on rational herding and cascade fragility leave open about it; lay out the method — what I would actually do, month by month, across formal modeling, agent-based simulations, analysis of our community's own timestamped forecast data, and possibly a small pre-registered experiment if time allows; specify what a genuine result would look like on each side of the question; and give an honest account of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the timing effect is real but too small to matter in practice. Somewhere in the proposal, make the case for why a foundation should fund a partly formal, partly philosophical project rather than simply paying for a platform redesign that forces simultaneous estimation. Do not include a bibliography and do not cite specific papers by name — describe the relevant positions and results at issue in prose, and I will track down references myself.
