Please analyze the Gulf of Tonkin incident of August 1964 as a case study in Hannah Arendt's account of lying in politics — but with a specific twist, which I'll state at the end.

First, the salient facts, so that we work from the same base. On August 2, 1964, the USS Maddox, on a signals-intelligence patrol near the North Vietnamese coast (in a region where South Vietnamese commando raids had just occurred), exchanged fire with three North Vietnamese torpedo boats; that clash was real. On the night of August 4, the Maddox and the Turner Joy, in rough weather and poor visibility, reported a second attack; the ships fired on radar returns, and the task-force commander reported an engagement. Within hours he cabled that the reports may have been mistaken and urged a full evaluation before any action was taken, but the administration proceeded as though the matter were settled. Relying on communications intercepts that were later shown to have been misread and, in the NSA's own declassified historical study, skewed toward the desired conclusion, President Johnson and his advisers treated the phantom attack as fact. Within days Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution nearly unanimously — over the dissent of only two senators — on the strength of that report, and it became the legal basis for the escalation of the war. The second attack never happened, and this was eventually conceded, including by Robert McNamara decades later.

Arendt's framework, as I understand it, distinguishes the traditional political liar — who knows the truth, conceals it, and thereby keeps a foothold in the world he is deceiving — from the modern practitioner of organized lying, who replaces the factual world with an image and is in danger of coming to believe his own fabrications, losing the capacity to distinguish truth from falsehood precisely in the domain where he has been doing the replacing. She also claims that the modern image-making liar is prone to a specific failure: the fabricated world, being more coherent than the real one, eventually becomes more convincing to its maker than reality, so the liar's ability to judge — to respond to unforeseen events — quietly disintegrates. She thought the truth-teller in politics is always at a structural disadvantage, but also that lies have a self-consuming character.

Now the twist, and my actual questions. It is tempting to file Tonkin under Arendt's heading of organized lying, and I don't want the standard lesson — "the government lied, and the war followed" — which any op-ed can deliver. What strikes me as hard about the case is that it is not obvious there was a liar in Arendt's sense at all. The sailors fired on weather; the commander reported honestly and then honestly doubted; the NSA analysts doctored the record toward a conclusion many of them probably believed; the President, who was privately skeptical about what the ships had actually seen, nonetheless spoke of the attack with certainty that he may not have experienced as a lie from the inside. The distortion seems to have been produced by an interlocking set of self-deceptions distributed across an organization, with no one occupying the position of the liar who knows the truth and replaces it with a known falsehood. So I want you to be disciplined about the mapping: who, if anyone, is the liar here; what exactly is the lie; where the truth that gets replaced is held or not held at each link in the chain; and where secrecy ends and lying begins. Then tell me which features of the episode Arendt's framework genuinely illuminates, and where it is silent or strained — in particular, whether her theory presupposes an identifiable image-maker that bureaucratic self-deception of the Tonkin kind lacks, and if so whether that is a boundary case her theory anticipated or a phenomenon it never considered. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does Tonkin show about her theory? Does her thesis that the modern lie ultimately destroys the liar's own judgment survive a case in which the judgment seems never to have been intact to begin with — where the image was believed at the moment of its manufacture? And if Arendt needs a third category between the traditional lie and organized image-making — call it distributed self-deception, or the confirmation of what everyone wants to have been the case — say what would have to be added to her account to accommodate it, and whether the addition is a friendly amendment or a concession that her account of the modern political lie was built around the wrong exemplar. I care more about the honesty of the mapping, including the places where you have to conclude that the theory simply does not speak, than about any particular verdict.
