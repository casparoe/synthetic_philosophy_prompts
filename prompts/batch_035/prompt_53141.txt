At a recent departmental reception after a colloquium on scientific realism, two fellow PhD students in our program got into a sustained argument, and since I couldn't referee it to anyone's satisfaction, I'd like your help adjudicating it. For context, the dispute was about structural realism.

Elena was defending the position. Her opening move: the no-miracles argument gives us a reason to think our successful theories are at least approximately true, but the history of science — phlogiston, caloric, the optical ether — shows that successful theories get their central ontology wrong over and over. The way to keep what's valuable in realism without inheriting its vulnerability is to believe only the structure. Her example was the passage from Fresnel's wave theory to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory: the ether Fresnel posited doesn't exist, but the equations describing how light propagates carried over essentially intact. So what survives theory change, and what we should commit ourselves to, is the mathematical structure, not the objects.

Ben's first reply had two parts. First, the Fresnel case is cherry-picked: for every pair of successor theories where something recognizable carries over, there are others where nothing does, and the structural realist tells the story only about the survivors. Second, and more fundamentally, he argued that "the same structure" is not a well-defined notion. Any two theories agree in structure if you describe them coarsely enough and disagree if you describe them finely enough; Fresnel and Maxwell agree about some equations in some limits and disagree elsewhere. Whether the structure "survived" is an artifact of the level of granularity the narrator chose, and a realism worth the name should be an answer about the world, not about our descriptive choices.

Elena answered that the grain isn't arbitrary: the parts of the theory that do the explanatory and predictive work — the equations that carry the empirical success — are exactly what's preserved, and that pick-out is an objective matter about which portions of the formalism the successes depend on, not a matter of narrative convenience.

Ben then made what he took to be his strongest point: the view faces a dilemma. If structural realism is understood on the model of replacing theoretical terms with existential quantifiers — "there is something or other that plays this role" — then the resulting claim is nearly trivial, satisfiable by almost any domain, and says almost nothing about the world; it's a version of an old objection to purely structural accounts of knowledge. But if, to avoid that triviality, you let the mathematical content back in with its interpretation — the equations understood as being about fields, say, rather than about an elastic medium — then the view smuggles the objects back in and inherits the full pessimistic induction it was designed to escape. Either it says too much or too little.

Elena's rejoinder was that this trades on flattening "structure" into mere quantificational form. The structure she's committing to is the substantive mathematical content — the equations, the symmetry properties — and that is not the sort of thing that gets trivially satisfied by any old domain.

Ben's last word: but "the same equations" already wears an interpretation, since what an equation says depends on how its terms are understood, and Maxwell's equations as claims about fields are not Fresnel's equations as claims about disturbances in a medium. The notion of shared structure that was supposed to do all the work quietly presupposes the object-level identifications it was meant to replace.

Please go through the exchange move by move — including Ben's two-part opening and both prongs of his dilemma — and say who's right about each specific point, where each of them concedes or fails to concede something they should, and what, if anything, both are missing. Then give an overall verdict on who comes out ahead. I should say that I have views but would rather not signal them, since you'll be more useful to me as a neutral referee.
