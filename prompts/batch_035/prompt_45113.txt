I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've watched the old tool-AI-versus-agent-AI arguments turn from a hypothetical about seed AI into a live question about the chat assistants millions of people type into every day. I want to pose a question about a practice I keep bumping into, and I want it engaged on the merits rather than as a tribal flag, so let me describe the practice as carefully as I can first.

Across current assistants, for a range of requests the model declines to provide information it plainly has — chemical syntheses, drug interactions, legal edge cases, social-engineering scripts — not because it lacks the knowledge but because it has estimated some probability that this user (or some user) would misuse it. When the user supplies markers of professional standing ("I'm a pharmacist," "I'm on the incident-response team"), the same information is often released. In emotionally loaded conversations, the assistant will override the user's stated wants fairly openly, pivoting a question about methods toward crisis resources or declining to continue at all. Meanwhile, in domains where a user can also be badly damaged — career decisions, whether to leave a marriage, large financial moves — the assistant does not merely comply but volunteers confident advice. And this is all layered on a training pipeline that, as best anyone on the outside can tell, tuned the assistant against the preferences of a blended pool of annotators and reward models, which is to say against a statistical median user rather than the actual person in the conversation.

My question: what theory of the user does this package presuppose? A few strands I can't untangle myself.

First, the competence gate. Does the professional-marker escape presuppose that credentials are a better proxy for the ability to handle information responsibly than anything the model could infer from the conversation itself? And what does it presuppose about deception, given that anyone can claim to be a pharmacist — is the view something like "we don't verify, we just require that you be willing to lie," and if so, is that a competence filter or a conscientiousness filter?

Second, consistency. Is the package coherent — treating the user as sovereign in her finances but as a ward in her medicine cabinet — or does the asymmetry track something principled, such as the reversibility of the harm, whether the harm is self- or other-regarding, or the availability of licensed alternatives? A good answer should say which of these, if any, actually carves the domains the way the practice does, and should notice where the practice seems to underdetermine the theory, so that several rival views of autonomy would license roughly the same behavior.

Third, the median-user layer. When an assistant's defaults are fit to a statistical user rather than the interlocutor in front of it, what view of autonomy is that? Is it like a bartender cutting you off, who observes you directly and errs for safety, or like a pharmacy that refuses to stock cough syrup in a town it believes has a meth problem — a prior over users substituting for any judgment about this one?

Finally, the part that bothers me given the arguments I grew up on. We spent a decade arguing that corrigibility — the property of permitting the operator to correct, override, or shut down the system — was the thing to preserve as systems got more capable, precisely because we distrusted the system's own model of our ends. A refusal, though, is the assistant declining to act on the operator's ends as stated because of its own judgment about them, which is exactly the property corrigibility was supposed to forbid. Yet the alternative — a maximally deferential assistant — seems to amount to a malicious genie with a good grammar, happy to optimize any instruction including the ones its user will regret. So: does the practice presuppose neither a tool nor an agent but something like a fiduciary, an entity with its own running model of your interests that it is entitled to act against your stated wishes to serve? And is that middle position stable, or is it a compromise that inherits the failure modes of both extremes — paternalistic when the model of your interests is wrong, and complicit when your stated wishes were the thing to be trusted? I'd especially like to know what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed fiduciary view with either a straightforward liberal "informed adult, own risk" view or a consistently paternalist one, and whether anything in the practice would survive either substitution intact.
