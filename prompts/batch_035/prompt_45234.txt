Not sure if this has a name, hoping you can tell me. I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about which animals can feel pain or be conscious, and it goes roughly like this: the evidence bar seems to move depending on how close the animal is to us on the evolutionary tree. Nobody demands neural data before accepting that a chimpanzee nursing a wound is in pain — you just look at it and know. For a fish, people start wanting brain chemistry to line up with ours before they'll say anything. And for a crab or an octopus, I've read researchers say the behaviour is suggestive, but the brain is just too differently built for us to extrapolate anything, so the default becomes "unknown — assume no pain, keep boiling them." So the threshold for how much evidence counts seems to rise exactly in step with how alien the animal is, even when the outward behaviour is the same in all three cases.

I don't think it's straight anthropomorphism — nobody is projecting human traits onto the crab; if anything they're withholding a trait. And it's not quite the classic problem of other minds either, because somehow we manage to solve the other-minds problem fine for dogs and babies. Is there an established concept or literature for using similarity-to-us as the measuring stick for evidence of consciousness — and for the fact that the same quantity of behavioural evidence gets interpreted differently depending on who produced it? If there's no proper name, could you coin one, define it precisely, and say how it differs from just being sensibly cautious when the evidence is weak? I'd also be curious whether the move ever runs in reverse (do cephalopod people over-credit them because they're clever in ways we find impressive?). I'm guessing this is behind why the whole UK thing about whether lobsters and crabs can feel pain got so heated a few years back, if that helps situate it.
