My cousin works in communications for a nuclear regulatory agency, and at Thanksgiving she got talking about the marker systems several countries have designed for deep geological waste repositories — since the waste stays dangerous for tens of thousands of years, people have seriously worked on how to warn whoever might stumble on the site in the far future: huge earthen berms, fields of granite spikes, messages carved in multiple current languages, pictograms of a figure digging or drinking from a well (one recurring worry being that the pictures might get read as a map to treasure, or as instructions, rather than as a warning), and one expert panel apparently even floated the idea of engineering cats that change color near radiation, with songs and legends to carry the meaning down the generations. I've been reading about longtermism for an ethics course this semester, and it hit me that there's a philosophical question sitting right under all this that my cousin just waves off as an engineering problem.

So here's what I want untangled. The marker program treats people ten thousand years out as somebody we owe something to: we can't communicate with them properly, they can't consent or complain, they may not even share a language or a religion with us, and yet we spend real money and effort protecting them from our garbage. What does that practice actually presuppose about the moral status of future people? Does it require the strong longtermist claim — that influencing the far future is the most important thing we could be doing — or does it get by on something much weaker, like a plain duty not to dump serious harms on whoever comes after us? I've read that strong longtermism is a claim about priority, not just about future people counting at all, and I honestly can't tell which one the markers need.

There's a second thing that bugs me too: the whole design assumes the future finders are different enough from us that language will fail and institutions will vanish, but similar enough to us that they'll be curious, that they'll dig, that skull imagery and spikes will trigger something like dread. Is that combination stable, or a contradiction we never have to face because nobody will be around to report the failure? And why the 10,000-year-ish horizon at all — under a genuinely strong longtermist view, wouldn't the marker program be a rounding error next to the question of whether the repository exists in the first place, so that adopting longtermism officially would push the agencies to spend the money somewhere else entirely? If you can sort out which parts of the practice genuinely need a theory of our obligations to future people, which parts run on ordinary prudence, and what the agencies would actually have to change if strong longtermism won the internal argument, I'd really appreciate it.
