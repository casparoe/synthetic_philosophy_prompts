I'm a novelist, and for the last three years I've been slowly writing a book with a Grand Inquisitor figure in it — a soft-spoken man who takes away people's choices "for their own good" and is honestly, sincerely kind about it — and I keep going back to the source: Ivan's poem in The Brothers Karamazov, the one he recites to Alyosha in the tavern. My writing group has now argued about that chapter maybe a dozen times and we are stuck exactly where we started, and I've started to wonder whether we're even having one argument. The editor in the group insists the Inquisitor is obviously a devil-figure, that Christ's silent kiss at the end is Dostoevsky's answer, and that reading the Inquisitor as "winning" the exchange is a category error — the poem is Ivan's projection of his own rebellion, and Dostoevsky dismantles it later through Zosima and through what actually happens to Ivan. My co-writer thinks that's wishful: the kiss is a gesture, not an argument, the Inquisitor's case about the three temptations — bread, miracle, authority — never actually gets rebutted anywhere in the novel, Zosima's counter is warm soup compared to the Inquisitor's steel, and Dostoevsky, who understood demagogues better than almost anyone, knew exactly how strong the case he'd written was. A third friend doesn't care who Dostoevsky agrees with and thinks the whole thing is an empirical claim about human beings — that most people most of the time will trade freedom for security and certainty — which she says history has basically run as an experiment for us, with ugly results, and which she thinks settles it in the Inquisitor's favor whether Dostoevsky liked it or not.

So my working hypothesis, and I want you to tell me if it's right, is that we're not having one stalled argument, we're having three at once: a literary question about whether the Inquisitor speaks for Dostoevsky or for Ivan, a psychological question about what people actually want, and a normative question about whether a happiness you didn't choose is worth anything — and each of us keeps answering our own question and thinking we've answered the others. The editor treats textual evidence as deciding the normative question; my co-writer treats the weakness of the novel's rejoinder as evidence Dostoevsky agreed with the devil; friend three doesn't think authorial intent matters at all. Diagnose this for me properly: is the impasse substantive, verbal, or just three different questions wearing one cassock? Does anything in the rest of the novel — Ivan's conversation with the devil, his brain fever, Alyosha's kiss at the end of the chapter itself — actually move the needle, or does it all just restate each side's prior? And be honest with me about whether there's any such thing as progress here, or whether every generation rediscovers the Inquisitor and re-fights him because the disagreement is about bedrock intuitions that no argument reaches — because that actually matters for my book, since I have to decide whether my Inquisitor gets answered by anything or just by weather.
