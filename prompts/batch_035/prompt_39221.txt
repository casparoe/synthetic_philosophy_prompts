The debate over Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness is now three decades old, and in my experience of it nobody has changed sides. The atheist side insists that a perfectly loving God would ensure no one is ever in a position of nonresistant nonbelief — open to relationship but lacking belief — while the theist side replies variously that such nonbelief may not really exist once cognitive biases and self-deception are accounted for, that we are poorly placed to judge what a being of that scale would have reason to do, or that human freedom gives God sufficient reason to withhold obvious evidence. Both camps publish, both accuse the other of not engaging, and the dialectic appears to run in circles. My working hypothesis is that the standoff is not about divine love at all: it is a disguised disagreement about burden of proof — about how much of an explanation a theist owes before the evidential situation is no longer surprising — and the debate persists precisely because each side thinks the other is smuggling in its own standard. Is that diagnosis right, or is the dispute instead a genuine first-order disagreement about the psychology of nonbelievers, or two groups answering different questions (whether God exists versus whether the evidence is what we'd expect) under one heading? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a sharper category than nonresistant nonbelief, an agreed standard for theodicies, or nothing at all.
