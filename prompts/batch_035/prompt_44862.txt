I'm designing a seminar unit around a diagnostic question about the sorites: is the vagueness of our predicates an artifact of perceptual indiscriminability — the thought, most developed in Williamson's epistemicism, that the boundary of a vague predicate is unknowable because our judgments carry a margin for error set by our inability to tell adjacent cases apart — or would the paradox survive even where discrimination is, in the relevant sense, perfect? I want a thought experiment engineered to put maximum pressure on the first half of that question.

The specification: construct a scenario in which (i) there is a concrete series of cases spanning a vague predicate — something ordinary and realistic, e.g., a trained sensory panel at a cider house calibrating "dry," though you may choose your own setting of that grain; (ii) every adjacent pair in the series is discriminable — the panelists can reliably say, given two samples side by side, which one is sweeter, at every single step along the series; and (iii) the predicate nevertheless behaves vaguely — samples at the ends are uncontroversially dry or not dry, while somewhere in the middle the panel's non-comparative judgments (is this dry, yes or no?) begin to waver, with no panelist able to identify, even in principle, where dry ends. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph, with no impossible psychophysics (pairwise detection of small differences is an ordinary fact about trained tasters) and no appeal to fatigue, distraction, or sample degradation — those would smuggle an indiscriminability rationale back in by the back door.

The paradox has to be regenerated, not stipulated. So after the scenario, lay out the sorites argument explicitly: from the sample everyone calls dry, in increments of the smallest sweetness difference the panel can detect, to the sample everyone calls sweet, with each conditional step presented for acceptance or rejection. Then show why the usual rationale for accepting the tolerance premise — that adjacent cases can't be told apart — is unavailable here, since each step is exactly as large as the panelists can tell apart. The wavering in (iii) must fall out of the scenario, not be asserted of it; a case that meets the letter of my specification by stipulating a hidden perceptual gap, or by quietly making the predicate precise and recasting the wavering as mere interpersonal disagreement, fails.

With the case built, work through these deliverables:

1. The epistemicist's options. A Williamson-style margin-for-error principle says that knowing "this one is dry" requires the sample to remain dry across an indiscriminable neighborhood. In this case there is no indiscriminable neighborhood — so what, if anything, still explains the unknowability of the boundary? Consider the repair that the margin attaches to the category judgment rather than to bare discrimination, and say whether that reply is convincing or is really an admission that discrimination never grounded the boundary in the first place.

2. What a supervaluationist and a degree theorist would each say about this series — one clear paragraph each — and whether the case flatters, embarrasses, or leaves untouched their accounts. If a contextualist treatment (on which the extension shifts as one marches through the series) has an easier time here than elsewhere, say why.

3. An honest note on which feature of the case is doing the work. My guess is the split between comparative discrimination (which side-by-side sample is sweeter?) and non-comparative categorization (is this dry?), which the case lets come apart completely — but tell me if I've mislocated it. Then say what happens if that feature is removed: if the increments shrink back below the detection threshold, does the case collapse into an ordinary perceptual sorites, or does something of the point survive?

4. How a defender of the discrimination-based diagnosis would attack the case as constructed — for instance, by arguing that pairwise discriminability is not the notion her margin-for-error principle ever invoked, or by reinterpreting the mid-series wavering as performance noise rather than semantic indecision — and whether any of these counterattacks would survive being made precise.

The case is for advanced undergraduates who have already seen the standard formulations, so assume familiarity with the major positions and spend your words on the engineering, not on surveying them.
