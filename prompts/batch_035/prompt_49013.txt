A few weeks ago I watched a video about this psychology experiment I havent been able to stop thinking about. A CEO is told a new program will make money but will also harm the environment as a side effect, and he says he doesn't care at all about the environment, he only cares about profit. He goes ahead, the environment gets harmed, and something like 8 out of 10 people say he harmed the environment *intentionally*. But when the setup is flipped — the program will help the environment as a side effect, he still doesn't care, he goes ahead, it helps — most people say he did NOT help intentionally. Same guy, same "I don't care," opposite answer depending on whether the side effect is good or bad. The video called it the Knobe effect. I brought it up at a BBQ last weekend and my two friends got into it pretty hard.

Ravi said it shows that our concept of intention isn't a neutral description of what's inside someone's head — it's morally loaded, so "did he do it intentionally?" is partly a blame question wearing a descriptive costume. Sam said that's way too much to hang on one experiment: people just refuse to let the CEO off the hook, so they reach for "intentionally" as a hammer to punish him with, and loose talk doesn't change what a word means any more than people saying "literally" when they mean the opposite changes what "literally" means. Ravi fired back that if a word's meaning just is how people use it, then usage that shifts with blame means the concept really is tangled up with morality, and Sam doesn't get to retreat to some private clean version of "intentional." Sam said of course he does, that's literally what making a mistake with a word is — and besides, in the help case people aren't even using the word wrong: intention requires aiming at something, and the CEO wasnt aiming at anything, just at profit. Ravi asked why that standard doesn't apply in the harm case, since he wasn't aiming at the pollution either, and it kept going in circles like that until someone turned the music up.

Please referee this move by move — who's right about each specific point they made, not just who wins overall. And here's the part I really want you to commit on: while they were arguing I made up a test case. Suppose the harm version is exactly the same, except the damage lands on the CEO himself — say the pollution happens to wreck his own family's lake house and knock down his own bonus, while the shareholders still profit fine. If people still say he intentionally harmed the environment, that seems to support Ravi, since blame doesn't really apply to hurting yourself. If people suddenly flip and say no, that seems to support Sam, since it looks like they only said "intentionally" before because the word was doing punishment work. Tell me how you think people would actually answer this variant and what it would show, then take a definite position on whether the Knobe effect reveals something about the concept of intention itself or just about how people vent blame with their word choice — no "it depends" hedging, I can get that from Sam.
