Exam question: "Suppose you discover that someone you regard as an epistemic peer — equally intelligent, equally informed, and equally careful — disagrees with you about some question. What should happen to your confidence in your own answer? (a) Distinguish the general conciliationist position from Adam Elga's Equal Weight View, and describe a case in which they come apart, i.e., a case where a conciliationist could reasonably endorse an unequal split. (b) State the self-undermining objection to conciliationism, and explain the conciliationist's best-known reply to it. (c) Explain the charge that steadfast views are dogmatic or arbitrary, and how the steadfast theorist tries to answer that charge."

Student's answer: "(a) The conciliationist view of peer disagreement is Adam Elga's Equal Weight View. Elga's idea is that when two epistemic peers disagree, each should give equal weight to both opinions: if I'm 80% confident and my equally informed, equally careful friend is 40%, I should move to 60%, and so should she. Suppose my friend and I each independently calculate the tip on a dinner bill and get different totals; since we know at least one of us must be wrong and neither of us can tell which, the rational response is for both of us to suspend judgment about the right amount. On the other side, Thomas Kelly's Right Reasons view also tells you to make a comparison: you ask whose initial reasons were stronger and go with the better reasons. In that sense the Right Reasons view is really a form of conciliationism in disguise, since both views make your post-disagreement credence depend on a comparison between you and your peer. (b) The self-undermining objection is that if conciliationism is true, we get far too much skepticism: conciliationism is itself disputed among philosophers, so conciliating about it would leave us unable to believe it, and the same would go for nearly all of philosophy. The conciliationist's standard reply is that this case is special — the arguments for conciliationism are so strong that once you have worked through them, you cannot rationally resist the conclusion — so the view is entitled to exempt itself from its own rule. (c) Steadfast views are accused of dogmatism because they let you shrug off a peer's disagreement without giving it any weight: whatever the peer says, you just keep your original credence. Kelly escapes the charge only by insisting that your reasons were better all along, which is question-begging, since that is exactly what the peer disputes."
