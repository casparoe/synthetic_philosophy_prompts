I have been reading a popular book on the history of evolutionary debate, aimed at a general audience, and its chapter on the adaptationism controversy summarizes Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin's famous 1979 paper, "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm," roughly as follows:

Gould and Lewontin's argument was that the adaptationist programme — the habit of breaking an organism into discrete traits and explaining each as something natural selection shaped for a purpose — is not merely overzealous but untestable. For any trait whatever, an adaptive story can be invented after the fact, and since such stories can always be spun, they can never be refuted; the programme therefore falls short of genuine science. The paper's positive contribution was the spandrel, named for the tapering spaces between the arches and the dome of San Marco in Venice: a feature that is a mere byproduct of the body's construction, with no adaptive function of its own and no consequence for fitness, which selection accordingly ignores. (Architectural literalists complain that the San Marco spaces are technically pendentives rather than spandrels, but the metaphor has stuck.) Gould's other celebrated example, the panda's thumb — a wrist bone repurposed into an instrument for stripping bamboo — is a spandrel in exactly this sense: a structure selection never built for the job it now does. The chapter concludes that, in the wake of Gould and Lewontin, evolutionary biology should presume that any complex trait is a byproduct until a demonstrated adaptive function is established, a presumption that the tellers of just-so stories have conspicuously failed to overcome.

I do not know the field well enough to tell which parts of this to trust. Two things struck me as possibly off. I half-remember that Gould's own account of the panda's thumb treats it as a strikingly imperfect, jury-rigged solution — evidence of contingency in design rather than of the absence of design by selection — which seems like a different point from the one the chapter attributes to him. And the closing advice sounds less like removing a bias than like installing one that runs in the opposite direction, which I would not have expected from a paper I have elsewhere seen described as a plea for pluralism.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a working evolutionary biologist would accept as a fair rendering of the 1979 paper, which parts a defender of Gould and Lewontin would object to and on what grounds, and whether the chapter's concluding methodological advice follows from anything accurate in the summary? Along the way, I would especially like to understand the distinction the paper draws between a trait's evolutionary origin and its current use, and the difference between complaining about how adaptive hypotheses have typically been tested and claiming that they cannot be tested at all. Finally, is the parenthetical aside about pendentives correct?
