I write a column on science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and I have been asked to draft a recommendation on how we should moderate the comment threads beneath our long-form essays. Most of the discussion is excellent. But one group of commenters has organised itself around the view that our recent series on the genetics of intelligence should never have been published, that its author should never write for us again, and that anyone defending it is an apologist for its supposed conclusions; they vote down opposing comments, chase dissenters off the thread, and have begun writing to our advertisers. In effect, they use the freedom of the thread to argue that this sort of thread should not exist. Our editor is reluctant to remove anything: he holds that the answer to an argument is a better argument, that readers can tell the difference, and that a paper which polices its threads betrays the principle it is meant to embody. The colleagues who run the threads tell me that if this carries on, the only people left commenting will be the campaigners, and that our openness will have produced a space no open-minded reader wishes to enter. Which way should my recommendation fall, and how do I justify it — including to readers who will accuse us, in the same breath, of cowardice and of becoming the censors we said we would never be?
