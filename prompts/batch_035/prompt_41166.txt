Two researchers have been arguing for a year about the gender-equality paradox: the repeatedly reported pattern that countries scoring higher on standard indices of gender equality show larger average sex differences in personality, values, and occupational and educational choices, with the best-known and most contested case being Stoet and Geary's 2018 finding of lower women's shares of STEM degrees in more gender-equal countries — a study later corrected and disputed, even as related work reports similar patterns for values and personality. One researcher, drawing on evolutionary psychology, holds that the pattern chiefly reflects the freer expression of relatively stable sex-typed preferences once external constraints loosen; the other holds that it chiefly reflects the persistence and reconfiguration of social structures — stereotypes, norms, labor-market organization — under conditions of formal equality. Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should produce. It should contain: (1) the claims both can sign, including about what a cross-national correlation does and does not establish, about what the common equality indices actually measure and fail to measure, and about the epistemic status of the contested STEM finding; (2) the residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a cross-sectional pattern licenses any claim about what happens within a country as it becomes more equal over time, and another is whether the operative mechanisms run through individual choices at all rather than through markets and institutions — and, for each crux, a concrete kind of study or evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of their dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive — for instance, over the word "choice" — say which part and why. Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions.
