I keep running into a distinction that must have a name, but I can't find it because I don't know what to search for. I'm a small-animal veterinarian who writes the anatomy entries for a clinical reference, and the word "function" seems to carry two different readings that agree in the textbook cases and come apart everywhere else. On one reading, the answer is historical: the heart is for pumping blood because pumping by ancestral hearts is why descendants have hearts at all. On the other, the answer is current: the heart is for circulating blood because circulating blood is what it does now to keep the organism alive. The readings diverge in at least four ways I've noticed. (1) A beneficial mutation that appears today has no history of contribution, so on the historical reading it seems to have no function yet, even though it is currently doing all the work. (2) Feathers were plausibly favored first for insulation and only later became load-bearing for flight, so the two readings give different answers to what feathers are for, and differ again about a vestigial structure with a long history and no present contribution. (3) A heart murmur is exactly what the cardiologist's diagnostic procedure depends on the heart contributing, and I would never write that the heart's function is to make that noise — but I can't explain my refusal without saying "function relative to whom," which seems to give the game away. (4) When we write that an organ is "functioning normally," the dysplastic hips of a breed whose extreme conformation was deliberately selected for are the historical reading's nightmare: they were literally selected for, yet we file them as malfunction. I'm told philosophers of biology have had this fight, but I don't want just the stock terms — I need to know what exactly each reading is committed to, where each one breaks (especially on malfunction and on traits whose useful effect is new), and which reading, if either, a clinical reference should standardize on. If the two readings I've described actually conceal a third or fourth, I'd want to hear about that too.
