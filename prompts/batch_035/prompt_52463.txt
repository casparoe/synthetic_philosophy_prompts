My political philosophy reading group is doing collective responsibility this term, and I've been assigned the bit on state apologies. I want to run a real case through the speech-act theory of apology and see if it survives contact. The case: Australia in 2008, Kevin Rudd's parliamentary apology to the Stolen Generations — the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children removed from their families over most of the twentieth century under assimilationist policy, which the 1997 Bringing Them Home report had called genocidal and recommended reparations for. Howard had spent a decade refusing to say sorry (his line was that present generations shouldn't feel guilt for past ones), the apology was one of Rudd's first acts as PM, it passed with bipartisan support, members of the Stolen Generations were in the gallery, and — this is the part I care about — it came with no compensation scheme attached.

The theory to apply: an apology is a speech act with felicity conditions, roughly — an agent acknowledges a specific wrong, accepts responsibility for it, expresses regret, and commits to not repeating it, all addressed to the wronged party. Then there's the collective-responsibility layer: who is the "agent" when a state apologizes? The corporate-agency view says the state itself is a moral agent that can wrong and apologize in its own right; the membership-inheritance view says responsibility somehow transmits to current citizens who mostly weren't born when the removals happened; and the skeptic says the whole thing is category error, more like condolence than apology. Map the case onto all of this: who is the speaker, who bears the responsibility being accepted, who counts as the addressee given that many direct victims were dead by 2008 and the gallery was full of descendants, and what the absence of reparations does to the "commitment" condition — is it an impure apology, or does the pure/impure framing not even apply to states?

Then run it the other way and tell me what the episode does to the theory. Does the fact that it visibly mattered to people regardless of the philosophical puzzles count as evidence that the speech-act model is missing what apologies actually do (identity-reconstitution, standing, acknowledgment of moral standing in the first place)? Where is each view on collective responsibility just silent here rather than wrong? I'd rather hear "the theory has nothing to say about X" than a forced answer. Keep it under 800 words — the mapping first, then the breakdowns.
