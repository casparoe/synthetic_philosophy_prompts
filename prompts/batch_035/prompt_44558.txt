You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory and the psychology of rational choice, writing for a well-informed but non-specialist audience.

I am a science-and-ideas journalist preparing a piece on the sunk cost "fallacy," and my working thesis is that the textbook treatment is too quick: that there are realistic cases in which honoring sunk costs is not merely excusable but positively rational. To test the thesis, I need a constructed case, not a survey of the literature.

Please begin with a short brainstorm of candidate mechanisms — reputation with others, self-signaling, information carried by past spending, intertemporal commitment — and state which one you will rely on and why the others are ruled out by the constraints below. Then construct a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. A single agent, deciding alone. No audience, no reputation effects, no external enforcement, and no third parties whose expectations or reactions matter.
2. The sunk expenditure itself must do the work. The case must not rely on the agent inferring from "I have already spent this much" to "continuing must be worth it," nor on the past spending merely tracking information the agent could have possessed anyway.
3. A realistic setting — a career decision, a home renovation, a long-running research program — no science fiction, no idealized agents, no exotic psychology.
4. The agent who honors the sunk cost must end up better off by her own lights — evaluated over her whole life and against her own settled values — than an otherwise identical agent who ignores sunk costs.

After the scenario, verify that it meets each specification: walk through the reasoning of both agents, identify exactly which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdict if that feature is subtracted. Then present the strongest objection a critic would raise — in particular, whether the critic could redescribe the case as one in which the "sunk cost" is really something else (a commitment device, an investment in a future self), and how you would respond. Close by telling me, as a journalist, whether this case is a genuine crack in the textbook treatment or a curiosity that survives only under my constraints.

Please think carefully, take the specifications seriously rather than approximating them, and write in polished American English.
