My friend and I keep arguing about van Fraassen's Reflection principle -- the idea that if you're sure your future self at time t will assign credence r to some proposition, your credence now in that proposition, conditional on that, should also be r. She thinks a suitably restricted version of Reflection is a genuine requirement of rationality; I'm starting to suspect it's either false (because of the counterexamples) or true-but-trivial. Give me your actual credence that some properly restricted Reflection is a real rationality requirement, decomposed: how much rides on whether the counterexamples (the person who knows a drug will make him overconfident, someone anticipating her own fatigue or wishful thinking) can be handled by principled restrictions rather than just hand-waving them away; how much on whether Reflection is really a synchronic coherence condition on your current beliefs about your future beliefs, in which case it might be true but not about diachronic anything; and how much on the thought that if all the restrictions do their work, what survives is just the unexciting claim that you shouldn't expect to outpredict your own future evidence. Give me a number and one line of justification for each term, then combine them, and tell me which term the final answer is most sensitive to -- and what would actually shift it.
