I'm taking an introductory philosophy course, and we've just finished the unit on death. Rather than accept one of the stock positions, I've been trying to sort out my actual verdicts on a spread of cases, and I'd like your help finding the principle behind them — or being told honestly that there isn't one.

Here are the verdicts. A woman who dies suddenly and painlessly in her sleep at 40, with every prospect of a good life to 85: her death is very bad for her. The same sudden death at 95, for someone who had a week to live and a hard week at that: not bad for her at all, or barely. The eternity she didn't exist before birth: not bad for her, even though it deprived her of just as many years as an early death would have — this is the symmetry point Lucretius made, and I keep endorsing it even though my other verdicts seem to rest on deprivation, which looks symmetric. A man who spends his last forty years in an irreversible coma: I think the coma was bad for him during those decades, even though he experienced nothing, which suggests harms don't require an experiencing subject; but I can't get that to sit with my feeling that death itself, which also removes the subject, is not bad in the way pain is bad. A person whose life was on a clear downward slide — chronic pain, losses piling up, no realistic prospects of reversal — who dies: I don't think the death is bad for her, which suggests deprivation only counts against net future goods, not gross ones. But then a serene 80-year-old who has no future-directed desires left, who wants nothing more from life and is content: I still think his death a year earlier than it happened was somewhat bad for him, which pulls against any desire-based gloss and back toward something like objective goods. Finally, the pre-natal one again but harder: I hold that nonexistence before birth isn't bad, and I also hold that a frozen person suspended between two stretches of conscious life isn't harmed by the gap. Yet if a gap of nonexistence flanked by consciousness on both sides isn't bad, and pre-natal nonexistence is just a gap flanked on only one side, it's hard to see what makes an early death the bad one — and yet I'm confident an early death is bad in a way the others aren't.

So: is there a single principle that captures these, or a small set of principles that divide the labour cleanly? State whatever you propose precisely, tied each clause to the case that motivates it, and then tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't mentioned — say, the death of someone who would have lived on, but only in a permanently drugged state she would have hated; or whether the badness of the 40-year-old's death is located at any time at all, given that before it she exists and after it she doesn't. And be blunt about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save, and which of the two it is — the pre-natal verdict or the early-death verdict — that has to go if no accommodation works.
