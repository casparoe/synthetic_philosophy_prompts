At our last reading-group meeting, on philosophy of social science, two members spent the better part of an hour on whether social explanations must bottom out in individual action, and the rest of us could not tell who had won by the end.

It started when Dev brought up Durkheim's finding that a society's suicide rate stays remarkably stable across decades even though the individuals composing the society die and are replaced, so the rate seems to be a fact about the social group rather than a summary of facts about particular people. He added an example of his own: bus bunching, where buses on a route cluster into pairs even though no driver wants this and no driver intends it, and the pattern re-forms day after day regardless of who is driving. Regularities like these, he said, are the explananda of social science, and they are regularities of aggregates; explaining them by citing what individuals do is answering a different question — as strange as explaining the operation of a piston by citing the trajectories of molecules rather than the pressure on it.

Mara replied that Dev had conflated two things: what a fact is about and what fixes the fact. The suicide rate is fixed entirely by individual acts of individual people; bus bunching is fixed by individual drivers' decisions, each made in ignorance of the others. Nothing at the group level exists over and above people acting, she said, so any correct explanation must trace the regularity back through individual decisions and the situations individuals are in — otherwise you get the old circularity in which social facts are explained by further social facts and nothing ever touches ground.

Dev made a concession and then a move. The concession: fine, the total individual facts fix the aggregate facts; nothing happens in society that is not happening to and by individuals. The move: that concession concerns what exists, whereas his claim concerned what should appear in our best explanations. Compare, he said, the gas laws once more: temperature is fixed by molecular motion, and there is nothing in the gas over and above molecules, yet the right explanation of why the piston moves cites pressure, not the trajectories of some 10^23 molecules, because the molecular story, though complete, is not explanatory at the scale of the question. Aggregate-level explanation is not a confession of ignorance; it is the appropriate resolution for aggregate-level questions. So "bottoming out" is ambiguous: explanations must bottom out in what exists, but they need not bottom out in what they cite, and Mara had quietly traded the first reading for the second.

Mara was not impressed by the gas analogy, for a reason she thought actually cut in her favor: molecules do not read thermometers. People, unlike molecules, respond to the aggregates they compose. The unemployment rate alters how individuals search for work; a run on a bank is caused by beliefs about what other individuals are about to do. So whenever an aggregate variable appears to do causal work, the real work is done by individuals' beliefs about the aggregate, and the individualist can redescribe every one of Dev's cases in those terms. If the "appropriate resolution" were anything more than a bookkeeping convenience, there should be aggregate-level causes that cannot be redescribed as individual beliefs plus situations, and she challenged him to produce one.

Dev's reply was that the same individuals, differently arranged, produce different aggregate outcomes. His favorite illustration: two populations with the very same distribution of individual dispositions can cascade differently depending on how those dispositions are wired together in networks of trust and imitation; the structural fact that generates the difference is a fact about the arrangement, and it is not a fact about any one individual, since it remains fixed while every individual changes. If Mara's doctrine has to dismiss that as a bookkeeping convenience, so much the worse for the doctrine.

Mara's last word: a fact about an arrangement is still a plural fact — a fact about all the individuals and the relations among them — and nothing in Dev's example requires a group agent or a group mind. Individualism, she said, never claimed that individuals are to be explained one at a time; it claimed only that individuals are the sole agents there are.

At that point we ran out of time. Both had given ground somewhere, and I honestly could not say who came out ahead, or whether the whole exchange turned on a single issue. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right about which specific point, where each of them gave ground, and what, if anything, both are missing? If it is possible, also say what the disagreement would look like once restated so that it no longer appears to be a disagreement at all.
