I am currently enrolled in a second-year epistemology course in which the seminar method is almost entirely built on cases: Gettier-style scenarios against the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge, fake barn cases about environmental luck, Frankfurt-style cases about moral responsibility, and trolley cases in the ethics lectures we share with another course. The professor presents these cases, polls our intuitions, and treats agreement in the room as evidence for or against the theory under discussion — a failure of intuition is presented as a failure of the theory. At the same time, the same department runs a well-advertised seminar on cross-cultural philosophy, and I have read that experimental philosophers have found that verdicts about some classic cases — including whether a Gettier subject counts as knowing — differ systematically between respondents from Western and East Asian cultural backgrounds, and that other intuitions shift with socioeconomic status or with the order in which cases are presented.

My question is what this pedagogical and philosophical practice presupposes. Does the method of cases presuppose that intuitions are uniform across competent thinkers, or merely that they are truth-tracking in whatever population happens to be in the room? Can it survive the cross-cultural findings as it stands — perhaps via the thought that philosophy students, like experts in other fields, converge on trained verdicts — or does the reliance on majority intuition in the seminar quietly presuppose a claim about which population's intuitions carry evidential weight, a claim nobody has defended? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the case method, distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, saying where the practice is underdetermined so that several theories of intuition's evidential role would license it, and telling me concretely what would have to change — in the seminar's procedure, in the exam questions that reward the "correct" intuitive verdict, or in how cases are selected and taught — if the practice were rebuilt on the assumption that intuition is local rather than universal. Finally, please assess whether the inconsistency I sense between the department's enthusiasm for cross-cultural philosophy and its unexamined reliance on one culture's intuitions is a real philosophical tension or merely an institutional accident.
