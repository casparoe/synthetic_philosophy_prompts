I'm a philosopher of science on the faculty of an agricultural university, and I sit on the review panel that must decide the design of our experiment station's next five-year laying-hen breeding program. The panel is split over two proposals, and of all things the disagreement has turned on the units-of-selection debate. Proposal A applies individual-level quantitative genetics: each hen's own performance is measured, breeding values are estimated with standard mixed models, and — added after consultation with the quantitative geneticists — the models are extended to include indirect genetic effects, i.e., the influence a hen's genes have on her cagemates' performance, so that socially harmful but individually profitable traits are not blindly favored. Proposal B discards individual records as the primary criterion: whole cages are scored as units, and the next generation is bred from the best-performing cages, with cage composition arranged to maximize relatedness among members. Its designers invoke the experiments from the 1990s in which selecting at the group level dramatically reduced feather-pecking and cannibalism, and they argue that the station's persistent failure to fix these behaviors through individual selection is itself evidence that the selection we need has to operate at the group level.

One faction on the panel claims B is nothing but A in different bookkeeping: on the Price equation, they say, the split between within-group and between-group components is a matter of accounting convenience, and arranging relatedness within cages just means B is kin selection under another name — funding B would reward rhetoric over substance. The other faction replies that the disagreement is causal, not notational: whether selection acts at the group level determines the shape of the long-term response curve, how quickly harmful social traits are purged, and how robust the outcome is when the station later scales up to larger, less-related groups — precisely the conditions under which the two schemes should be expected to come apart empirically.

I have to circulate a recommendation before the panel's next meeting, and I want it to be a genuine decision document rather than a diplomatic hedge. Please draft the memo I'll circulate: it should recommend one scheme (or a concrete hybrid or conditional plan, if you genuinely think that's best), with a time frame and interim evaluation points at which the panel could revise course; it should engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument rather than dismissing it; and it should make explicit where the philosophical dispute about units of selection does and does not bear on the practical choice — in particular, whether the equivalence point, even if granted in full, really has the deflationary consequence the first faction claims for it. Assume the panel members are sophisticated readers: they will notice hand-waving, whether about group adaptation or about gene-centrism.
