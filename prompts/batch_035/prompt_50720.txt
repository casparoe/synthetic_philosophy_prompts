So at a dinner party last weekend two of my friends got into it and I still can't decide who was right. My friend Daan is Dutch and my friend Yuki is Japanese (grew up in Osaka, been here for like 6 years), and it started because a third friend asked us all if her birthday cake was good, when honestly it wasn't, it was kind of dry. Daan just told her, straight up, "it's a bit dry actually." Yuki was horrified and after we left the two of them went at it for an hour and I got stuck in the middle.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Yuki said Daan's bluntness was just selfishness dressed up as honesty — the tiny cost to him of saying "mm, it's nice" versus the hurt he caused her, and he chose his own comfort in "being real" over her feelings. Daan said the exact opposite: the white lie is the selfish move, because it buys you a smooth evening by quietly letting your friend keep believing something false, and she'll bake the same dry cake for the next twenty people. (2) Yuki then said white lies aren't even really deception, because everyone knows the code — when someone asks "is my cake good" the answer "it's good!" just means "I care about you," and the listener knows that, same way nobody thinks a person waving is literally claiming to see them. It's not lying, it's another language. Daan said fine, it's a code — but codes break the moment you mix audiences, which is exactly what happened at that table, and besides, if "it's good" can mean either "it's good" or "I'm being polite," then you've made the honest sentence unusable, and people who actually want to tell the truth about cake now have no words for it. (3) Then Yuki, who I honestly thought was losing till then, made a move I'm still chewing on: she said Daan's directness is also "saying more than the words" — telling someone flatly "your plan is bad" doesn't just transmit the opinion, it signals you didn't think the relationship was worth softening for, and the listener hears that signal whether you meant to send it or not. So being blunt is not neutral, it's a choice with moral weight too, just a different one. (4) Daan said but at least his signal is true — he genuinely doesn't see cushioning as respect, he sees it as treating people like they're fragile, which he finds insulting. And Yuki said, so your "honesty" is itself just your culture's setting — you call it truth, but plenty of people in your office would experience it as aggression, so who's counting what the words "really" mean?

I'm not asking you to referee feelings here, I'm asking about the arguments. Can you go through their four moves and tell me who's right at each step? And if there's something both of them are missing that would actually dissolve part of this, I'd love to hear it — because from where I sat it looked like they were each half right the whole way down and just talking past each other. Also, at the end, if you had to say who's more right overall, who would it be? (And is there actually any principled answer on whether white lies are wrong, or is it just culture all the way down, which is a thought I find kind of depressing honestly.)
