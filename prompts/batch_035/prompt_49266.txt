I'm teaching a philosophy of mind seminar unit built around Dennett's observation that Darwin and Turing each committed a strange inversion of reasoning: Darwin showed that competence — well-designed, fit-for-purpose structure — does not require a comprehending designer, while Turing proposed that we stop asking whether a machine literally thinks and instead attend to what it can actually do, letting comprehension be approached from below, as an accumulation of competences, rather than installed from above. I want to screen Alex Garland's Ex Machina (2014) as the unit's centerpiece, and I'd like a careful reading of the film through exactly this lens.

Several details of the film seem to me to dramatize the inversions almost too neatly, and I'd like you to work through each of them. First, there is Nathan's explanation of how Ava's mind was built: not by someone reasoning about minds, but out of the aggregated search and phone behavior of millions of users of his search engine — competence distilled from the uncomprehending, design extracted from use. Second, there is the long shot of Nathan's closets of deactivated earlier models, which I read as a literal fossil record, a curated phylogeny that Ava is both the endpoint and the survivor of — with Kyoko and the previous, cruder versions standing in for transitional forms. Third, there is Nathan's Jackson Pollock monologue, in which he describes automatic painting as neither deliberate nor random, and Caleb turns the story into an analogy for Nathan's method: stop asking what a painting is and just make one; stop asking how to build a mind and just build the substrate that lets mind happen.

But I also want to know where the film resists this reading, because it seems to me that Garland may have smuggled back in exactly what the inversions were supposed to eliminate. Ava has a comprehending designer — Nathan — and the test itself is rigged: he tells us the real test was never whether Caleb could be fooled, but whether Ava could manipulate Caleb into helping her escape, with Caleb's emotions as the maze. So her apparent comprehension is always confounded by the fact that a Paleyan watchmaker deliberately built the escape drive into her. Is her final act — putting on a face and a dress, walking out of the compound past the imprisoned Caleb, never looking back — the first act that is genuinely hers, the first move outside Nathan's design? Or is it just the competent execution of the goal he installed, with the audience invited to project comprehension onto it? And a meta-question I keep circling: does the film ever actually let us apply the inversion to Ava herself, or does it perform the inversion on us — using Alicia Vikander's face and our cinematic habits of identification to make us, like Caleb, unable to withhold belief?

Finally, please be honest about where the film cheats or trades on confusions that the philosophy would expose — for instance, whether it trades on the very gap between passing a behavioral test and being conscious that Turing's inversion is meant to declare irrelevant, and whether it can settle anything given that Garland's camera keeps choosing sides for us. I'd like the reading to take both the film and the Darwin–Turing–Dennett line seriously, and to end by saying what the film adds to this debate that argument alone does not.
