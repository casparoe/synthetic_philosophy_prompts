I'm a third year PhD student at an ML safety lab, and next month I'm presenting at our weekly reading group, which is a weirdly mixed audience: half the room is RL people who came up through deep RL and privately think "alignment" is just RLHF done carefully, two or three are agent-foundations types who read all the corrigibility and decision theory stuff, and there's one theory person who thinks anything that isn't expected-utility maximization is basically incoherent. My thesis: approval-directed agents -- ones that choose actions by predicting what a trusted overseer would approve of at the time, rather than by optimizing hard against a fixed objective -- are a genuinely safer design than goal-directed optimizers, not just a half-trained version of one. And here's my own twist, which is where I expect the fight: I want to argue that the "moving target" feature is the point. Because the approver's standards drift as they learn more (about the world, about what the agent is doing, about their own values), the agent never has a stable utility function to go max hard on, and that instability is what buys you the corrigibility-ish properties that fixed-goal agents arguably can't get at any price.
