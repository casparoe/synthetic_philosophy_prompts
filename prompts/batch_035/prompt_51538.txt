I teach philosophy of science at a small state school, and every summer I co-teach the interpretation module at an archaeology field school — my job there is basically to stop the students from writing "ritual purpose" on anything they can't otherwise explain. The students are undergrads with basically no stats background and no lab access out in the field, they each produce a couple dozen interpretive write-ups over the summer (which I grade), and a few times a year the local museum sends me draft label copy to vet before it goes on the wall for the next decade. So the stakes range from a grade to something the public will read as fact for years.

What I want is a decision procedure the students can actually run in ten minutes on an interpretive claim about an object — "this figurine represents a fertility goddess," "this pit was a ritual deposit," "these grinding stones were used to process ochre." Ground it in whatever epistemology genuinely applies here: inference to best explanation, the underdetermination problem, analogical reasoning and how it fails, the way "ritual" becomes a trash can category for the unexplained, the difference between claiming a function and claiming a meaning. Order the questions, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark where the procedure terminates in "don't make the claim," where it lands on "make it but with this qualifier," and where it has to hand the thing back to a human because it's a judgment call that can't be checklist-ified. Be honest about which steps are just dressed-up intuition.

Then run it on three real-ish cases from last summer: (1) a clay figurine with exaggerated sexual characteristics that the museum wants to label a fertility goddess; (2) a grinding stone with red residue that the students want to say was used for ochre processing; (3) a pit of disarticulated animal bones, some burnt, which half the class wants to call a ritual deposit and the other half says is ordinary butchery waste that got scavenged and trampled. I want at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I suspect it's going to tell me the fertility goddess label has to go, and I want to see how it justifies that, beacuse the museum director is going to fight me on it. Also tell me what the procedure can't settle at all — presumably the part where somebody decides how much interpretive risk a public label should take, which sounds like a values question rather than an evidence question, and I'd like that said out loud.
