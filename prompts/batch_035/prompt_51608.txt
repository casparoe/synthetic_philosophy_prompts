I am a novelist working on a story meant to dramatize the money pump argument without ever reading like an illustration from a textbook. The protagonist is a collector — of prints, perhaps, or watches — whose preferences are pairwise sensible but jointly cyclic: he prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. A shrewd dealer senses this and trades him around the cycle, each time extracting a small sweetener, until the protagonist holds exactly what he began with, minus the sweeteners. The difficulty I want the story to honor is the philosophical one: at no single moment does he make a mistake he could have recognized. Each trade, considered alone, is an improvement by his own lights; only the sequence, seen from outside, drains him. So I would like the reader kept suspended between two judgments — that he is irrational, and that nothing he did, one choice at a time, was wrong — which is the tension between evaluating a life decision by decision and evaluating it as a whole. No character should explain what is happening; the pattern should emerge for the reader precisely as it never does for him, since seeing the cycle in advance is the one thing that would have saved him. Around 1,500 words; the number of trips around the cycle is yours to choose, and the sweeteners should stay small enough that each one is trivially worth it.
