I am a novelist, and my current manuscript has accidentally turned my kitchen table into a philosophy seminar. The book turns on a late reveal: a woman the reader has followed for three hundred pages under one name is disclosed, in the final chapter, to be the same person as a girl who appears only in another character's memories, always under a different name. I have been arguing for a year with two old friends — one a committed Millian about proper names, one a committed Fregean about sense — about whether the reveal can possibly do the work I want it to do. The Millian says that if the two names refer to the same person, the reveal sentence is strictly speaking as uninformative as an identity I would never dream of writing, namely a character being told her own name; the Fregean retorts that my whole chapter is a monument to the difference between the two, and that the Millian owes my readers an apology. They have now been talking past each other for so long that I have imposed a condition: before either of them says another word to me, they must produce the joint statement from an adversarial collaboration, and I am delegating the drafting to you.

Please write that document. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. I would expect this to cover at least the following, though you should decide the exact formulations: the bare existence of an informativeness asymmetry between trivially true identity sentences and informative ones about the same referent; the failure of substitution of names in belief-reporting contexts; and whatever they can jointly say about how fictional names behave, which matters to me professionally, since most of my characters' names refer to nothing at all.

2. A disagreements table, with each residual disagreement phrased so that the party holding the view would accept the wording of its own side — neither vacuous nor slanted. Where the disagreement is about what to say concerning my reveal chapter, give each side's verdict in its own terms.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one of my friends. I suspect one crux is whether cognitive significance belongs to names themselves or to thoughts and sentences containing them, and I suspect another is what empty names force each side to say — but work these out properly rather than trusting my suspicions.

4. For each crux, at least one case, argument, or test that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance. Feel free to use cases from my trade: a character unmasked mid-scene, a false name an impostor answers to, a character known to the reader only through another character's possibly unreliable memories, or the old fairy-tale device where speaking a hidden name dissolves an enemy's power.

Finally, since I have suffered through a year of this, indulge me on one point: where you judge that part of their dispute is merely verbal — a fight about the word "meaning" rather than about anything in my manuscript — say which part, and say why, in a section both of them could sign without gritting their teeth.
