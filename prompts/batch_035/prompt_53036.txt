I run a small decision-theory blog and want to build an interactive quiz titled "What Kind of Probabilist Are You?" for readers who have encountered the sharp-credences debate only in passing. The question the quiz must sort people on is: must rational credences be single real numbers, and, if they need not be, what should govern action when they are not? The quiz must sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which labels they like, so no question may ask anything like "do you think credences are sets of probabilities?" — every question must be a concrete choice or a commitment the reader can endorse in plain language.

Please build it as follows. First construct the logical space from branching questions, not from a list of schools: roughly, (a) is there always a determinate answer to what your credence in a given proposition is; (b) if not, do the behavioral signatures of "ambiguity aversion" live in the credence itself or in the utility/risk side; (c) for those with non-sharp credences, how is a set or interval of probabilities converted into a choice. Then locate the actual positions in that space, and note which answer combinations nobody in the literature occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

The quiz should have ten questions, each presenting a case with two or three plain-language options. The following cases must appear among them: (1) an Ellsberg-style two-urn choice (known 50/50 composition vs. unknown) at stakes small enough that utility is effectively linear, so risk attitude in the ordinary sense cannot be the explanation; (2) a follow-up testing whether the preference for the known urn survives when the play is repeated many times with the unknown urn's composition fixed once at the start — ambiguity should persist across repetition in a way ordinary risk aversion does not; (3) a dilation case: the reader is happy with credence 1/2 for a coin they know is fair, then learns the result of an independent, ambiguous device, and conditional on that result their credence in the coin spreads toward the extremes — ask whether this is a reductio, a benign consequence, or a misuse of the formalism, with options phrased as reactions rather than positions; (4) a bid-ask spread case: the least the reader would accept to sell a small-stakes bet on an ambiguous event and the most they would pay to buy it are some distance apart, and the question is what, if anything, rationally occupies that gap; (5) a second-order-probability case: whether the reader would model their uncertainty about a probability as itself a sharp distribution over probabilities, and whether they find it a problem that the resulting overall credence then pins down a single fair betting price; (6) at least two cases in which the imprecise decision rules come apart behaviorally — e.g. one act is optimal relative to some probabilities in a set but not the worst-case-minimizing one, versus an act that only dominates when whole intervals are compared — presented as concrete menus with the properties of each option described in words, so a reader picks a meal without knowing which rule they are endorsing. The remaining questions are up to you, but each must do diagnostic work.

The leaves must include: the orthodox sharp Bayesian; the hierarchical Bayesian who pushes the non-sharpness up a level; the view that absorbs ambiguity attitudes into the utility side (Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility); imprecise probabilists split by decision rule — E-admissibility in Levi's sense, maximin over the set of probabilities, and interval-dominance or permissive views; and an eliminativist leaf for readers who deny there are credences to be sharp in the first place. Where two leaves cannot be separated by the quiz — for instance, a plain maximin-over-probabilities rule versus Gilboa–Schmeidler-style maxmin expected utility, or E-admissibility versus Levi's full view with its secondary security level — flag this explicitly and say what further case, if any, would drive the wedge.

Please return the whole thing as a single JSON object with the following fields. "branching_logic": an array of objects, each with "node", "question" (the substantive question encoded at that fork, in one sentence), and "children". "questions": an array of ten objects, each with "id", "case" (the concrete scenario), and "options", an array of two or three objects each with "label" (a letter) and "text" (the plain-language answer). "scoring_key": an array of objects, each with "leaf" (the position's name, which may appear here but never inside the questions), "thesis" (one sentence), "signature" (the option letters that route a reader there, with wildcards where questions are non-diagnostic for that leaf), and "awkward_commitment" (the consequence this leaf has to bite hardest). "unoccupied": an array of objects, each with "signature" and "diagnosis". "separation_failures": an array of objects, each with "pair", "why" (why the quiz cannot tell them apart), and "wedge" (a further case that would, or a statement that none would). Before the JSON, give no preamble; after it, nothing.
