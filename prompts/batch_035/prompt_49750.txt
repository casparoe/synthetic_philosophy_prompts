I sit on the grantmaking board of a mid-sized foundation that takes effective-altruist ideas seriously, and our next meeting takes up a proposal that has split the staff. Before I argue my side, I'd like an outside view on what our precedents commit us to. Our decisions to date: (1) we funded insecticide-treated bed nets through an established distributor, citing randomized-trial mortality data; (2) we funded a campaign that won signed cage-free commitments from three egg producers, with no direct welfare measurements but auditable contracts; (3) we rejected a grassroots land-tenure coalition whose members argued their impact was large but unquantifiable; (4) we rejected a graduate's request for support while she trained for a high-paying finance job and pledged half her income, on the ground that someone else would take the job either way; (5) we funded a fellowship placing analysts in health ministries, on the argument that a few salaries could influence budgets a thousand times larger than their cost.

The new proposal: three years of support for a small team trying to change how major aid agencies measure success, replacing output counts with a broader wellbeing measure. There would be no verifiable milestones for years; if it works, it could redirect billions; if it fails, there would be nothing to show.

What decision would be consistent with our precedents, and which precedent is the closest analog? More than one rule fits our track record — at least one under which we fund this and one under which we don't — so please spell out the candidate rules, say what each predicts here, and identify a further case that would tell the rules apart. Finally, one board member claims our record shows measurability bias dressed up as rigor: does this proposal, oddly, give us the cleanest test yet of that accusation?
