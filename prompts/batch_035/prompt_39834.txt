I'm a high school junior writing my extended essay in philosophy, and I want to open it with a question my supervisor says I'm not allowed to hedge on: whether the chatbot my classmates use every day actually has beliefs, in the same sense that they do. I can't settle for "it depends on your theory" in the essay, and I don't want it here either. So here is what I'm asking of you.

Give me your real credence, as a specific number between 0 and 1, that a current large language model — the kind answering this prompt, not some hypothetical future system — literally has beliefs. Then show me how you got there by splitting the question into the sub-claims it depends on and putting a rough probability on each. I have a sketch of what the decomposition might look like, and I'd like you to use it, correct it, or add terms I've missed: (1) the probability that taking the intentional stance toward a system can be more than a predictive convenience — that it can track something real about the system rather than just being a face we put on it; (2) the probability that having beliefs requires a certain kind of rational integration — beliefs that interact with each other and guide action over time — and, if it does, the probability that a system whose entire output is predicting the next token in a conversation satisfies that; (3) the probability that the whole question is partly verbal, so that some of the disagreement between yes and no is about how we should use the word "belief" rather than about what's inside the machine. For each term, one sentence defending the number you chose, then combine them into your final credence.

Two further things, because my essay needs to take a stand and so should you. My teacher says the question is like asking whether a thermostat believes the room is cold — obviously no, and the only interesting work is explaining why the analogy fails when we scale up. My own idea, which I haven't found in anything we've read, is that it's more like asking whether viruses are alive: a genuine boundary case where nature doesn't contain the answer and our purposes decide it. I want your verdict on which framing is better and which one your credence should move in response to — don't tell me both have merit. And then tell me which term in your decomposition you're least confident in, and what specific evidence or argument about language models would move that term the most. If my classmates asked you a follow-up designed to expose the weakest joint in your reasoning, what would they be pushing on?
