I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and this week's meeting ended in a standoff between two of my doctoral students over counterfactual mugging. I shall set out the exchange as it occurred and would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

For context, the scenario we were discussing: a predictor with a flawless record approaches you before a fair coin is flipped and announces that it has predicted how you will respond if approached afterwards. The coin lands tails. The predictor then says: the coin came up tails, there is no money on the table, but I predicted you would hand over £100 in this exact situation; if the coin had landed heads, I would have shown you this promise and given you £10,000. Now pay up. So the question is whether to pay £100 in the world where you receive nothing and are merely told about a hypothetical windfall in the other branch. The same seminar had earlier discussed the related XOR blackmail case: a blackmailer who can foresee whether your house will be burgled and can also detect whether the processor in your laptop is flaky, and who sends you a letter demanding £1,000 exactly when one but not both of those things is true — so the letter's arrival is strong evidence that either your house will be burgled or your processor is failing, and the payment does nothing to avert the burglary.

The exchange went as follows. First, Ada said she refuses to pay in the mugging: the coin has landed tails, the £10,000 exists nowhere in her world, and handing over £100 is a certain loss. Decision theory, she insisted, is about what to do given the information you actually have when you act; the preferences of a hypothetical counterpart in an unrealised branch cannot bind you, any more than the dead can be paid wages on behalf of the living. Second, Ben replied that she is reasoning "updately" rather than "updatelessly": before the coin flip, any sensible person would have paid £100 for a half chance at £10,000, and the whole point of acting on the policy you would have endorsed ex ante is that predictors can condition on your disposition rather than your later whim. He added that Ada already one-boxes in Newcomb's problem, so she accepts that the qualities of agents like her can be worth money; refusing the mugging is just two-boxing with the second box placed in another branch. Third, Ada drew the Newcomb line differently: there, she said, the correlated good sits in the very world she inhabits and she walks out of the room with it, whereas no version of her ever collects the £10,000; moreover, she argued that Ben's ex ante endorsement, taken seriously, would have him pay the blackmailer, since from behind a veil of ignorance the letter's arrival looks like terrible news worth £1,000 to act on. Fourth, Ben denied this: the right ex ante policy in the blackmail case is never to pay, because one's decision is not tied to the burglary in the way it is tied to the predictor's behaviour in the mugging — the blackmailer merely observes both facts, and no output of one's decision procedure changes either of them — so the contrast between the two cases is not a embarrassment but the very thing that shows the updateless framework is discriminating rather than indiscriminate. Fifth, Ada pressed on the underlying logic: in the tails world, the claim that had her decision procedure output "refuse", the predictor would not have offered the money is a counterfactual with a false antecedent about a mathematical function's output, and she professed not to understand the truth conditions of such counterlogical conditionals, whereas causal reasoning never needs them. Sixth, Ben answered that she needs them anyway, since even in Newcomb the claim that the opaque box would have been empty had she two-boxed is precisely a counterlogical about the predictor's model of her, so her official causal theory cannot underwrite her excellent instincts in that case.

I confess I could not tell them which of them was right at which step, and the seminar ran over time. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right at each of the six steps and why; assess whether Ada can coherently be a one-boxer who refuses to pay in the mugging, or whether she must abandon one or the other; explain what the XOR blackmail comparison actually establishes about the coherence of Ben's position; and identify what, if anything, both students are missing.
