I'm writing a position statement on distributive justice for a graduate seminar that has spent the term on luck egalitarianism — we've read the Dworkin material on equality of resources, Cohen's critique of it, Anderson's objections, Arneson's responsibility-catering view — and before I circulate it to the group I want a consistency audit rather than a survey of the literature. The six commitments I've arrived at are: (1) the fundamental aim of distributive justice is to neutralize differential brute luck — inequalities traceable to unchosen circumstances are unjust, while inequalities that arise from genuine choices are not, in themselves, unjust; (2) preferences are by and large the person's own, so someone who develops expensive tastes — champagne rather than beer, mountaineering rather than walking — has no claim to extra resources to satisfy them; (3) the right test for whether a bad outcome counts as brute luck is a hypothetical insurance test — if a prudent person starting with the same resources and opportunities would have insured against it cheaply, the resulting loss is option luck and the claim to rescue is weak; (4) the state should nonetheless guarantee everyone an unconditional decent minimum — emergency care, basic income — including people whose losses flow from their own choices, because abandoning the imprudent is harsh in a way no conception of equality can accept; (5) upbringing, class, and education shape people's dispositions toward prudence and risk, so the responsibility of someone raised in deprivation for their later imprudence is genuinely diminished, and this should modulate what we think they are owed; and (6) in the case of two patients with identical injuries — one hurt in an unprovoked attack, one hurt in a solo motorcycle crash she chose to ride helmetless — the second can permissibly be charged higher co-payments or given lower priority for scarce reconstructive surgery.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each genuine tension is — rank them rather than treating them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs the overall view. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. For what it's worth, I honestly don't know where the pressure is, if anywhere; my private fear is less that the set contains a flat-out contradiction than that I'm tacitly running two different conceptions of responsibility without noticing, and I'd rather have that exposed than be reassured that the package is coherent.
