During my working life I was an analytical chemist, and much of my career was spent worrying about detection limits: at what concentration does a substance count as present, given that every instrument produces noise and every calibration curve has a fuzzy edge? Now that I am retired I have begun reading philosophy seriously, and I have discovered that the problem I spent decades handling pragmatically is a special case of the ancient sorites puzzle — and that there are two contemporary theories of vagueness that appear to give strikingly different verdicts about it. Epistemicism, as I understand it, holds that there is a precise cutoff for any vague predicate; we simply cannot know where it lies. Supervaluationism instead holds that the predicate itself is indeterminate: there is no fact of the matter about the exact cutoff, though statements like "either a concentration of 7.3 ppb is detectable or it is not" come out true on every admissible precisification.

I would like you to compare these two theories as they apply to a concrete case drawn from my own experience. Suppose a regulatory threshold states that a contaminant is "not detectable" in drinking water, and suppose an instrument's response grows smoothly from pure noise into clear signal across a range of concentrations. A laboratory must decide whether a sample at some intermediate concentration is detectable, and different stakeholders (the regulator, the water utility, an advocacy group) each pick a slightly different statistical criterion for what counts as signal emerging from noise. Please explain how the epistemicist and the supervaluationist would each analyze this situation: what does each theory say about whether there is a single correct concentration at which detectability begins, and how would each characterize the disagreement among the stakeholders? Along the way, I would appreciate it if you could evaluate which theory sits more comfortably with a scientific mindset — I confess that the epistemicist's sharp but unknowable boundary strikes me as odd, since in my experience vague boundaries in measurement seem to reflect the world's own tolerance rather than merely our ignorance, but I am aware that intuitions formed at the bench are not automatically good philosophy. Feel free to conclude with a considered judgment about which theory handles this case better, and why.
