I'm a philosophy PhD student (dissertation on Nietzsche's late stuff, mostly the 1888 notebooks, so yes I'm one of those) and I've run into "what doesn't kill you makes you stronger" twice in one week and it's driving me up the wall. First my dad, who's deep into marathon training and has it basically as a mantra about ice baths and hard weeks, and then a "resilience in graduate school" workshop my department made us attend, where the facilitator used the line to argue that stress and overwork are actually good for you and we should stop pathologizing them. I know the line is loosely Nietzsche, and I know from my own reading that around when he wrote it he was completely obsessed with diet, sleep, climate, digestion -- Ecce Homo is basically half him ranking cities by whether their air agreed with him and giving you his opinions on coffee. So there's this funny tension where the poster version sounds like a cheerleader for hormesis while the guy who wrote it was a semi-invalid micromanaging his meals, and then collapsed permanently like a year later.

Here's what I want: take the one-liner and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, as a list I can actually use. I'd guess some are near-tautological, some are empirical (and probably false in the cases people care about -- I vaguely recall the trauma literature being pretty grim about whether people come out of near-death experiences stronger), some are about selection effects (the ones it killed aren't around to object), and maybe some are more Nietzschean, like a claim about what "strength" even means -- possibly something like the capacity to metabolize experiences without getting resentful or sick, given how much he leans on digestion metaphors. For each reading tell me whether it's trivial, defensible, or straight up false, and, importantly, whether it would actually support the use it was put to -- because my dad's training-stress version and the workshop's "your burnout is a gift" version need very different claims to work, and I suspect the workshop one is unsupported on every reading.

Also, if you can say something about whether the original context helps: is the aphorism even making a claim about adversity at all, or is it more of a boast/taunt in the middle of a book where he's being maximally smug about his own constitution? And is it a cheap shot to point out that the author was in a sanatorium within months -- like, does that falsify the claim, or just show he didn't think it applied to literal brain disease? I want to be able to explain to my dad and to the workshop facilitator (in very different registers) what exactly they're committing to when they repeat it.
