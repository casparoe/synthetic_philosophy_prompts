My friend and I have been fighting about dirty hands in politics for like six years, and last night was another three hours of it, so I finally want the document we should have produced ages ago. She's on the Walzer side: sometimes the right political act is also, all-things-considered, a wrong one — the leader who does the lesser evil has still done something wrong and ought to feel the guilt, and that's the whole point of the dirty hands literature (Walzer's famous essay from the early seventies, the Machiavelli background, the usual). I'm on the other side: if it's genuinely the lesser evil, then it's simply right, full stop, and the guilt she demands is dishonest sentimentality — a politician who did the best available thing should say so plainly, and calling it "dirty" muddies the water. What we can never settle is where exactly we part ways, so last night we stopped arguing conclusions and tried to find the cruxes instead. What we've got so far:

1. Whether there's a real moral remainder once the lesser evil is done — is the guilt appropriate, misplaced, or appropriate-but-not-required?
2. Whether political office puts you in a genuinely different moral position (answerable for everyone's welfare in a way private citizens arent) or whether it's just ordinary morality with bigger stakes.
3. Whether wrongdoing the public can never find out about differs in kind from wrongdoing done in public view.
4. Whether "it was the lesser evil" is a complete justification or only a partial one that leaves something unaccounted for.
