I'm a few years into a comp neuro PhD and I'm caught between my advisor and a committee member who loves to needle her, and I genuinely can't tell who's right. My advisor treats the free energy principle basically like physics: any system that persists far from equilibrium has to keep minimizing free energy / prediction error, and this scales all the way from a bacterium climbing a nutrient gradient to me sitting here typing this. The committee member's favorite move is to ask about a candle flame — it holds its shape, it "resists" dissipation in some loose sense, and if you're in the mood you can draw a Markov blanket around it just fine — and then ask, if the principle covers the flame and the sand dune and my brain equally, what exactly does it rule out? His line is that anything compatible with every outcome is a schema, not a theory, though he says it less politely at committee meetings.

Write a dialogue between the two of them at, say, a poster session where they've run into each other and can't leave. The defender should get real arguments, not vibes: that persistence under perturbation is a genuine constraint (a system that doesn't model its sensory states doesn't stay a system for long), that the formalism does forbid certain dynamics, and that the skeptic's complaint could be leveled at natural selection in 1859 too. The skeptic should press on the actual weak spots: the slide between "surprise" as a technical quantity and as a folk word, Markov blankets being drawable wherever you like and therefore not picking out anything, and whether the bacterium is doing anything a ball rolling downhill isn't, once you phrase both in the principle's vocabulary. They should actually respond to each other's points — no strawman on either side, and no polite convergence at the end.

Format: exactly five exchanges, numbered 1 through 5, each turn a sentence or two (they're both fast talkers who hate long speeches), no introduction or conclusion. End on the sharpest point that's still open when the poster session ends — don't resolve it for them, that's my job to figure out. Oh and it can be a little casual in register, they know each other well, but keep the technical content tight. One of them can mis-speak once ("the flame minimizes its surprisal" — wait, no, that was a slip) since that kind of thing happens in real conversations and both of them pounce on it.
