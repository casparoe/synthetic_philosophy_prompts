I work in risk management at an asset manager, so the vocabulary I actually live in is VaR, expected shortfall / CVaR, coherent risk measures (monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity, subadditivity — I've internalized the Artzner-style axioms), distortion functions, mean-CVaR frontiers, and the general habit of thinking about risk as a functional you apply to a P&L distribution, with utility functions as a separate thing you apply to wealth. Lately I've been reading decision theory as a hobby and I keep getting the feeling I've seen it all before. Specifically I've been working through Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility framework, where you keep standard expected utility machinery but weight utilities by a function r on cumulative probabilities, so the probabilities effectively get distorted before you aggregate (a risk-averse r treats the better outcomes as less likely than they are, or something like that — the point is the distortion happens on the probability side, and by choosing r you can represent the Allais preferences as rational rather than as a bias to be explained away). Her bigger claim, as I understand it, is that expected utility maximization is too demanding as a standard of rationality: an agent whose preferences violate the sure-thing principle / independence axiom isn't thereby irrational, they just have a risk attitude that EU theory has no room for, and part of her argument is that EU conflates beliefs with risk attitudes in a way that muddies things.

So here's the ask: restate Buchak's position and the surrounding debate — the defense of Allais-type preferences, the pushback from people who think independence is constitutive of rationality, the beliefs-vs-risk-attitudes point — in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Distortion risk measures, spectral risk measures, whatever genuinely fits. Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: what maps cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what has no expression at all in how we talk about risk in finance. My working suspicion is that r is basically just a distortion function and her theory is a spectral risk measure applied to utilities, so the math is something we've had on the shelf for decades — but I half expect the residue to live in the normativity, because when I say expected shortfall is a good measure I mean it's subadditive and backtests well, not that a person who prefers a different one is failing at rationality. So which is it: is the philosophers' disagreement substantive and my vocabulary just has no slot for it, or does it dissolve once you see the math is the same? And be honest where the translation is doing work the original didn't intend — e.g. if reweighting by r on cumulative probabilities of utilities isn't actually the same family as the probability distortions we use (which act on the loss distribution directly), say so rather than papering over it. I'd rather come away knowing my framework is missing a concept than feeling smug about having invented decision theory first.
