I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing on practical rationality — my thesis is on everyday indecision, so yes, I'm the person still standing in the supermarket aisle at closing time. I've got a chapter draft stuck on what I think is a real clash between two ways of reading Buridan's ass: the donkey placed exactly between two identical haystacks that, on the classic telling, starves because it has no reason to prefer either one.

View one, which I'm calling the paralysis reading: the case shows that where an agent's preferences and evidence yield no strict ranking, practical reason simply issues no verdict, so the agent fails to act — the ass dies not because it is irrational but because rationality is silent in the face of a tie. View two, which my supervisor keeps pushing: rationality permits arbitrary tie-breaking, so the ass's actual mistake (if it has one) is treating an indifferent choice as if it still demanded a justifying reason; the tie can be broken by a coin toss, by whichever haystack it glanced at first, by anything at all, and the resulting action is no less rational for it. My supervisor claims the disagreement is mostly verbal — the two views are describing different situations, one where the options are genuinely tied and one where they aren't — but I suspect there's a proper fight underneath about whether deciding is itself an act that can require a reason. That is: even after the coin toss, the ass needs a reason to *stop deliberating*, not to pick the left haystack, and that reason cannot itself come out of the ranking. A friend over in economics muddied things further last week by saying that in her world indifference is routine and nobody starves, because any tie-breaking convention works as well as any other — which makes me wonder wether the paradox only afflicts agents who think deliberation has to terminate in a justification.

So: compare the two readings properly. Say what each of them takes the ass's error to consist in, if it has one at all; assess whether the "deciding is a second-order act" point can be folded into either reading or whether it genuinely splits them; and work out what the convention-based answer from decision theory does to the case.
