At a reading group on the philosophy of mind, two fellow students got into a dispute about whether introspection can be trusted, and I'd like a verdict on it rather than my own half-formed opinion. Maya argued that introspection is largely unreliable: the classic confabulation findings from the 1970s showed that people confidently report reasons for their choices that demonstrably were not the actual causes, and later "choice blindness" experiments went further — when subjects' stated preferences were secretly swapped, many not only failed to notice but improvised elaborate justifications for choices they had never made. If we confabulate that fluently, she said, our introspective reports are post-hoc storytelling, and the feeling of direct inner sight is itself part of the illusion. Leo replied that this overgeneralizes: the findings concern retrospective reports about the causes of cognition, which is exactly where introspection is weakest, whereas reports of ongoing experience — an itch, a mood, a mental image — are not inferential reconstructions and are rarely shown wrong; no experiment has demonstrated that we invent our current pain or anger. Maya countered that even "ongoing experience" reports are not raw data: we misclassify boredom as tiredness, anxiety as excitement, aesthetic interest as attraction, and since there is no instrument to check the mind's eye against, we can never catch these errors from the inside — introspection is uncalibrated and unfalsifiable, so trusting it is trusting a ruler that marks itself. Leo's final move was that this standard would doom all knowledge: perception was equally uncalibrated until we built instruments, and we did build them for the mind too — convergence between subjects, correlations with neural activity — so the right conclusion is that introspection is locally unreliable at causal self-explanation but broadly reliable at phenomenal report, which is a difference in degree, not a blanket verdict. Neither would concede. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five steps, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or wrong, and why, and then say who is more right overall. Take a definite position throughout rather than presenting both options as defensible.
