My friend has this whole framework in her head about which countries count as democratic backsliding, and shes very insistent that her calls aren't just "regimes I happen to dislike" -- but she's never actually spelled out the rule, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer it for awhile now. Here's her track record as I know it:

- Hungary since 2010: backsliding, and by now she says it's not really a democracy anymore ("competitive authoritarian" is her phrase). Courts packed, media bought up by government allies, constitution rewritten without a single opposition vote.
- Poland after 2015: backsliding, but still a democracy -- she was pretty smug when the opposition won in 2023 and took over, like that vindicated her somehow.
- Turkey: backsliding, then breakdown. Her line is that elections there are still held, but they're "real events, not real choices."
- The US, 2016-2020: attempted backsliding that failed. Courts held, the press stayed free, the election happened and the loser left. She got weirdly precise about how this is NOT backsliding in her book, since nothing actually degraded, just a near miss.
- A classic military coup that hands power back within a year: that's "breakdown," a separate category -- backsliding, in her usage, has to be incremental and done by elected incumbents.

She's coming to dinner next week and I want to spring a new case on her. Let's call it Veridia. In Veridia, elections are impeccable -- international observers love them, and two parties have alternated every eight years for three decades. But each government, on taking office, quietly adds a couple more judges to the constitutional court, plants loyalists in the civil service, and turns the state broadcaster into a party organ. Over thirty years the courts and the media have become thoroughly politicized -- and yet the ruling party just lost an election and is leaving office without a fuss. So: will she say Veridia has backslid? Is it still a democracy?

My hunch is yes on the first and yes on the second, but I genuinely can't tell whether that's because she's running a two-part rule (elections decide if you're a democracy; the other institutions decide if you've backslid) or because she thinks the ballot box can still correct anything, in which case maybe Veridia barely counts as backsliding at all. Those two readings come apart somewhere, right?
