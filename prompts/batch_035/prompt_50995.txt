Next term I'm teaching a seminar on moral philosophy through drama, and I want to build a unit on Confucian role ethics around King Lear. The pairing looks natural — a play about a father, daughters, filial duty, and a kingdom collapsing — but I want a real reading, not a decorative one, so let me state the lens as I understand it. On the Confucian view, a person is constituted by roles and relations rather than being a pre-social atom; conduct is governed by li, the shared ritual forms that make action intelligible, rather than by universal principles; virtue takes root in the family and is meant to extend outward to the state; and order requires that names match realities — that a father be a father and a king a king. Read the play against that, with attention to particular scenes.

Start with the opening love test as a corruption of li: Lear converts the ritual expression of affection into a public auction, and when Cordelia answers with "Nothing" and Lear retorts that "nothing will come of nothing," there are two live readings — either hers is the filial act of remonstrance, loyal to the substance of the role while refusing its corrupted form, or it is evidence that genuine love outruns any role and cannot be performed on demand. Later, when Lear asks a steward who he is and is answered with "My lady's father," the play stages a crisis of rectified names: Lear holds the title of king without the power, and the roles are losing their grip. Then tell me what to do with the storm scene. Is Lear, stripped of every role, discovering a pre-social humanity — which would refute the Confucian relational self — or is the storm a Confucian diagnosis: the disorder that follows when the great relationships fail? And what of the Gloucester subplot, where Edmund attacks inherited roles as mere convention against nature and the play makes him a villain? Does that vindicate role ethics, or does the fact that both fathers are ultimately saved by children acting outside every sanctioned form complicate the vindication?

I would like you to take a stance on whether the play endorses, refutes, or transforms the Confucian picture; to say honestly where the lens strains; and to end with what the drama shows that argument alone cannot — for instance, whether Lear's fatal conflation of the father-role with the king-role tests the family/state analogy on which Confucian political thought depends. No quotations are needed beyond the ones I have given; refer to scenes by description, and do not bring in secondary scholarship or citations.
