Every year in my metaethics seminar I hit the same wall: I teach them Hume's point that no 'ought' follows from 'is'-premises, then Moore's point that 'good' can't be defined in natural terms, and the students reasonably assume their two ways of saying the same thing. They're not — one is about inference, the other about definition — but lately I've been wondering how much daylight there really is between them once you push on both. So here's what I want from you: your credence that at least one non-trivial valid inference exists from a purely descriptive set of premises to a genuinely normative conclusion. And no, a hypothetical 'if you want X, you ought to take the means' doesn't count straight off — unless you want to argue those are genuinely normative, in which case that itself becomes a term in your decomposition.

Decompose it however you think it should go, but I'd want to see at least these terms: (1) whether the gap is just the trivial logical point that a conclusion can't contain vocabulary absent from the premises unless meaning links them, and if so, whether any such link is possible that isn't either analytic in a way Moore-ians will reject, or itself normative, which would beg the question; (2) whether the Cornell naturalists sidestep the whole issue by treating moral claims as ordinary empirical claims, and whether that's a way across the gap or just a denial that there was ever a gap to cross; (3) whether the naturalistic fallacy does any independent work at all once you've settled the inference question, or whether Moore's argument only ever had bite against definitions. Give me a number for each with a line of justification, then show me how they combine into your overall credence.

Then two more things. First, tell me which single term the overall number is really riding on — I suspect for most people it's (1), but I'd like to see if that's right. Second, what argument or kind of evidence would move that term the most? For the record I'm sitting around 0.25 myself, but I've never actually forced myself to lay out the terms like this, which is honestly part of why I'm asking you.
