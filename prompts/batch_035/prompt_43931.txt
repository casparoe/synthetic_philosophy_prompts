I just moved cities for my first proper job and signed my first grown-up lease, and it's got me weirdly philosophical. Everyone involved seems lovely though — the landlord replies within hours and had the heating fixed the same week I mentioned it, and my manager is genuinly supportive. When I said at dinner that I wasn't too worried because the people all seem decent, my friend cut me off with the line that a slave with a kind master is still a slave, and that instead of trusting people's characters I should have negotiated the actual clauses. Things got a bit heated lol, and I walked home annoyed, partly because comparing my lease to slavery felt off, but also with the nagging sense that she was onto something and I couldn't say what.

My other housemate, who does politics, muttered at the time that the proverb connects to "there being like three concepts of freedom, not two" and then refused to elaborate, which was not helpful. So now I want the proverb itself taken apart, not the dinner argument relitigated. What are the distinct precise claims "a slave with a kind master is still a slave" could be making, laid out from the near-trivial to the radical? Like, on one end there's the boring reading that legal status doesn't change with the master's temperament, and on the other something much stronger, maybe that your security shouldn't ever depend on someone else's discretion at all, or even that the kindness itself is part of the problem. And one more thing while you're at it: where does the analogy between my situation and actual slavery break down, if it does? Is there a reading of the proverb under which deploying it about a rental agreement is just rhetorical overkill, or does the strongest reading survive even that?
