In a philosophy of language seminar I'm taking, we spent two sessions on Wittgenstein's notion of a language game, along with the companion picture of words as tools and utterances as moves within a practice — including the observation that the instructor stressed, that 'game' itself is a family-resemblance concept with no common essence, and that the rules of a language game are mostly not written down anywhere but show themselves in what we do. The topic has become unexpectedly practical for me: my roommate, a computer science student currently fine-tuning large language models, has seized on the seminar material to try to settle a running argument between us about whether these systems understand anything. His claim runs as follows: a language is a game, a game is a structure of players taking turns under shared rules in pursuit of winning, and an LLM is therefore not a participant in our language at all — it is at best a move-generator or a board, like a chess engine that emits strong moves without itself playing chess, for anything, against anyone. It has no form of life, no stakes, no turn that is owed to it; so whatever it does, it isn't meaning.

My instinct is that he is leaning on the analogy much harder than it can bear, but I find I can't articulate where it bends without merely asserting that he has read Wittgenstein wrong, which convinces neither of us. So I'd like a proper audit. Could you lay out the correspondence table first — what is supposed to play the role of the players, the rules, the moves, the winning condition, the referee or rulebook — and then mark where the mapping breaks? In particular, I want to see which features of games in the ordinary sense (fixed constitutive rules, an explicit rulebook, a winning condition, a terminal state) lack counterparts in linguistic practice, and which features of language games Wittgenstein was actually pointing at (rule-following that need not be explicit, the background form of life, the absence of a single thing all games have in common) the chess-engine picture quietly drops. Then tell me whether his conclusion — that an LLM cannot mean anything because it is not a participant — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time; and if the latter, what you would offer as a better comparison, if there is one.
