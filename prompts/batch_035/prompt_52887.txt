I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar at a small college — fifteen students, one 90-minute session — and I want to run the session before they read anything about the method, so that the activity does the conceptual work first and the reading lands afterward. Design a two-stage classroom activity that makes heterophenomenology, Dennett's proposal that a science of consciousness should treat subjects' verbal reports as texts to be interpreted while remaining neutral about whether they faithfully describe anything, feel like a serious methodological stance rather than a dismissive stunt.

My half-formed twist, which you should build on, improve, or reject with reasons: some of the subject reports the class will interpret should be fabricated. In stage one, a handful of volunteers leave the room and undergo a staged experience I control — I'm imagining a short, deliberately ambiguous video, or a setup engineered to produce misperception, something in the neighborhood of inattentional blindness. Each volunteer then writes a short first-person report, but I also write several reports myself: some from volunteers who in fact saw nothing of what they'll claim to have seen, some entirely invented. In stage two, the remaining students get shuffled packets of transcripts and must build the best predictive model of the unseen subjects they can — what the subjects will say if re-questioned about details, how they will perform on a follow-up judgment task — using only the texts, under an explicit rule that they may not ask whether a report is accurate. I reveal the confabulations only in the debrief.

The deliverable I need: complete rules and materials, including your recommendation for what the staged experience should actually be (it must be runnable with a laptop and no lab equipment); the split between volunteers and interpreters; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; a concrete feedback mechanism so interpreters discover which of their predictions failed; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the real dispute — why treating reports as data to be interpreted differs from treating them as windows, the sense in which the subject's described world gets handled the way a theorist handles a fictional world that one may still reason about, and what all this is meant to do to the idea of privileged first-person authority.

Two further requirements. First, be honest about which feature of the actual method the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion — my own worry is that by authoring the confabulations in advance I have smuggled in more third-personal control over the data stream than real heterophenomenology ever has. Second, tell me predictably what will go wrong the first time I run it (volunteers leaking the twist, interpreters refusing to engage once they suspect some packets are fake, the class concluding the whole exercise is a parlor trick rather than a method) and how to handle each case. And beyond the patchable failures, I want you to say what would change your mind about the design itself: which outcome — for instance, if interpreters reliably spot the confabulations, or if the genuine reports turn out to be no more predictive than my invented ones — would tell you the activity is teaching the wrong lesson, and what the redesign would look like. I would rather know where the design is most likely to fail than have it defended.
