so my roommate is an econ grad student and at a party last weekend she ran Shubik's dollar auction on us -- the setup where the auctioneer sells a dollar to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder ALSO pays their bid and gets nothing. two of my friends got up past four dollars before someone physically restrained the guy holding the paddle. afterwards she claimed the whole thing is a proof that sunk cost reasoning is actually rational, and I've spent the week trying to write down why she's wrong and I keep failing. here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it: (1) rational choice should depend only on the consequences of the options available to you now, since money already bid is gone whichever way you go. (2) suppose I'm the second-highest bidder, in for n cents. if I stop now, my outcome is locked in: I pay n and get nothing. (3) if I bid one more cent, then either the other bidder stops and I win the dollar, which is strictly better than stopping no matter how big n is -- winning leaves me at 100 − (n+1) instead of −n, and 100 − (n+1) > −n reduces to 99 > 0, always true -- or they bid again and I'm in the same structural position one cent deeper, which is no worse in kind. (4) so at every node, one more cent is either strictly better than stopping or just a repeat of the same choice. (5) iterating (4): a rational player in my position always bids. (6) but the same argument holds for the other bidder, so neither of us ever stops and we both pay without bound, which is worse for each of us than quitting at literally any earlier moment. (7) therefore (1) is false: what you've already sunk is sometimes a reason to keep going, and the auction is a little machine that proves it.

the reason I can't just laugh this off is that step (3) really does look like plain arithmetic. at 90 cents, bidding 91 to win a dollar beats eating a certain 90 cent loss; even at 150 cents, bidding 151 and winning loses me 51 instead of 150. and I keep noticing the same shape in real escalations -- my uncle's firm kept funding a doomed lawsuit "because we're already in for two hundred thousand," and honestly if you'd asked him at each funding vote whether another 10k was the best shot at not eating the 200k, step (3) logic is basically what he'd have said. but the conclusion proves way too much. if (7) were right then no one could ever rationally exit a war of attrition, a price war, a bad lawsuit, any fight with exit costs -- escalation of commitment would be the norm of rationality rather than the textbook bias, and my roommate should advise everyone to never drop out of anything. so: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd much rather keep (1), it's one of the few things I actually believe, but if keeping it means telling my friends they should have quit at 15 cents the moment they were second bidder, I want to see exactly where that advice comes from and why it doesn't collapse into (5). please write your answer in American English.
