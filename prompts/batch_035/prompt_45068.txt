Two members of a public-policy reading group I attend got into a dispute about pure time preference — the practice of counting a unit of future well-being as worth less than the same unit of present well-being, merely because it occurs later. The occasion was a proposal to spend a large sum now on maintaining a seed vault and long-lived warning markers above buried nuclear waste, benefits of which would fall mostly on people alive in two centuries and beyond. They wrote their exchange up for the rest of us to consider.

(1) Aldana argued first that any positive pure rate of time preference is indefensible: a person harmed by a failed vault in 2225 suffers exactly as much as a person harmed today would, and discounting that suffering just because it is distant is present bias elevated into public policy — the same vice as procrastination, but with other people's money and lives.

(2) Björn replied that the procrastination analogy fails, because procrastination wrongs one's own future self, whose interests one has already taken on as one's own; whereas there is nothing incoherent in a community caring somewhat more for people near it in time, just as we tolerate caring more for compatriots than for foreigners, or family than than strangers. A modest time preference is no more arbitrary than those familiar partialities.

(3) Aldana answered that the partiality analogy proves too much: we already condemn temporal partiality whenever it runs the other way — a council that quietly shifts costs onto future ratepayers for present convenience is accused of short-termism, not excused by an appeal to natural partiality toward one's own era. If closeness in time is a legitimate ground for discounting the claims of the distant, it is an equally legitimate ground for inflating the claims of the near, and we do not grant that.

(4) Björn then shifted ground to uncertainty: a benefit scheduled for 2225 may simply never arrive — the vault may fail, or the civilization that would have benefited from it may not exist — and a fully time-neutral agent should still discount far-future benefits by the probability that there is no one around to receive them. Exponential discounting, he said, need not express contempt for the future; it can be an honest accounting of the chance that there is no future to account for.

(5) Aldana conceded the uncertainty point but denied it showed what Björn wanted: discounting for the possibility that a benefit never arrives is not pure time preference at all, since we would apply the same haircut to a present benefit that carried the same chance of miscarriage. She added that Björn's own reasoning actually points to a discount rate that falls over time: if we are unsure how large the annual risk of non-delivery is, then over very long horizons we must average across the possibility that the risk is low, and the low-risk possibilities come to dominate the far future, so distant benefits should be discounted less steeply than any constant exponential rate implies.

Neither would budge on whether a positive pure time preference is ever defensible. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five contributions, say whether the argument is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify the verdict briefly. Then state who is more right overall, and identify anything of importance that both of them missed.
