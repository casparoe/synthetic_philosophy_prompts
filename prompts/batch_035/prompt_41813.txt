I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and my editor has greenlit a long-form feature on the Sleeping Beauty problem — the one where Beauty is woken once if a fair coin lands heads and twice if it lands tails, remembers nothing between awakenings, and is asked her credence in heads on waking. Every previous explainer I can find rehearses the halfer answer, the thirder answer, and a couple of the standard objections, and then discreetly changes the subject. Before I start booking interviews with philosophers on either side, I want to be sure I actually know what would count as resolving the thing, rather than just what the factions say. So what I'd like from you is a specification, not a scorecard: the desiderata that any adequate treatment of the problem must meet.

To give you a sense of the level I need: I already understand that a mere intuition about the original case settles nothing, so the desiderata have to be more demanding than "gets the textbook scenario right." I'd want to know, for instance, whether a solution owes us an account of how Beauty's credence should move when she's told it's Monday — I've noticed that halfers and thirders who condition on that information seem to end up in different places, and I don't know whether that's a fatal symptom for one camp or an artifact of how the experiment is described. I'd also want to know what a solution owes us on the decision-theoretic side: if Beauty is offered the same bet on heads at each awakening, or scored on her forecasts by something like a proper scoring rule, is agreeing with the right betting behavior a requirement or merely a bonus? And there are the anthropic extensions I'd rather my piece didn't ignore — the versions with biased coins, the ones where the number of awakenings tracks something in the world like how many observers there are, the pressure the problem puts on whether self-locating evidence should be treated like ordinary evidence. I don't need you to adjudicate these; I need to know which of them any serious answer must handle and which are optional exercises one camp happens to enjoy.

So please write me the list of requirements: the cases a solution must get right, the distinctions it must respect (say, between Beauty's credence and the fraction of awakenings at which heads was true, or between a well-defined answer for Beauty and one for an outside observer), the constraints it can't violate — like consistency with the coin being fair, or with Beauty's rationality not depending on information she lacks — each with the consideration that motivates it. Mark which desiderata you'd defend as non-negotiable and which you suspect are prejudices of the halfer or thirder camp dressed up as adequacy conditions. Most importantly, tell me if some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if the desiderata themselves force you to pick a side, that's exactly the paragraph my article needs. And note which single requirement does the most to knock out otherwise attractive views; I want my readers to come away understanding that the interesting question is not "heads or tails math" but what it would even mean for this dispute to be over.
