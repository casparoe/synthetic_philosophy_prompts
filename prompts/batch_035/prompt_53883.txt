My PhD supervisor has strong and stable views about how names and natural kind terms work, and I have been keeping a running list of her verdicts in the hope of reconstructing her underlying theory. Her rulings so far, in her own words:

1. Hesperus is Phosphorus: necessary, but knowable only a posteriori; someone fully apprised of ancient astronomy could still coherently believe they are distinct.
2. Water is H2O: likewise necessary and a posteriori. It is epistemically possible that the clear, drinkable liquid in the lakes should have turned out to be some other compound, but not metaphysically possible that water should not have been H2O.
3. Neptune, which Leverrier introduced as a name for whatever was causing the irregularities in the orbit of Uranus, did not thereby become synonymous with that description. The sentence Neptune causes those irregularities is a posteriori and contingent: the planet might have been deflected before having any such effect and would still be Neptune. Likewise, Neptune is the planet causing the irregularities in the orbit of Uranus is contingent.
4. The standard meter stick S is one meter long at time t, where the unit was fixed by stipulating the length of S at t, is contingent and a priori: S could have been heated and slightly lengthened at t, and the sentence would then have been false.
5. Aristotle taught Alexander: contingent and a posteriori, even though Aristotle is a rigid designator; Aristotle might never have entered a classroom.
6. Gold has atomic number 79: necessary and a posteriori, on the pattern of the water case.

This term she has agreed to adjudicate three new cases, and I would like to predict her answers before she delivers them.

Case A. I stipulate a fresh name, Julius, for the person, whoever that is, who actually invented the zip, assuming for the sake of the case that there is exactly one such person. Is Julius invented the zip a priori or a posteriori, and necessary or contingent?

Case B. Vulcan: the name Leverrier later introduced for a planet inside the orbit of Mercury, posited as the cause of Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession, until it turned out there is no such planet and the anomaly was explained by general relativity. Did Vulcan ever refer to anything? Is Vulcan does not exist a priori or a posteriori, necessary or contingent? And is there a possible world in which Vulcan exists?

Case C. Madagascar: as the standard story goes, the name was originally used for a portion of the mainland, then transferred through a traveler's or cartographer's confusion to the island, with the island usage eventually winning out. During the transition, did the name refer to the mainland portion, the island, both, or neither, and what does the eventual victory of the island usage show about what fixes a name's reference in the first place?

What I want from you: predict her verdicts on A, B, and C, including the modal and epistemic status she will assign to each sentence at issue. Then identify the two or three rules that fit her entire track record, and say at which of the new cases those rules diverge and where they agree. Tell me which rule you would bet on her running, and why — what in her earlier verdicts or manner of giving them tips the balance. Finally, since the rules may not be fully separated even by A through C, describe the further case, whether a stipulation, a baptism gone wrong, a shift of usage, or something else, that would best tell the surviving rules apart.
