I've been asked to contribute a short position paper to a workshop on the 'better than' relation — the transitivity of which is the occasion for the meeting — and, before I circulate the draft, I'd like a consistency check on the commitments it contains. The other participants will probe it, and I'd rather find the faults myself. In the paper I defend the following six claims:

(1) 'Better than' is transitive without exception: if A is better than B and B better than C, then A is better than C, however long the chain and however small the differences between adjacent links. In my view, the anti-transitivity verdicts people report when they run spectrum cases rest on a confusion between 'better than' and 'noticeably better than'.

(2) The relation is trichotomous: for any two options, exactly one of the following holds — the first is better than the second, the second is better than the first, or they are equally good. There is no further comparative status; if neither option is better, they are equal in value, however much else differs between them.

(3) Value facts are independent of our powers of discrimination. If one headache lasts sixty minutes and another sixty minutes and a second, the second is worse than the first, even though nobody could ever tell them apart. Imperceptible differences in duration or intensity are still differences in value.

(4) Where two options differ only imperceptibly, choosing between them is a matter of indifference: there is no reason at all to take one rather than the other — not even a weak or defeasible one — and flipping a coin between them is fully rational, not merely excusable.

(5) Two lives can differ in worth without either being better than the other. Consider one life richer in friendship and another richer in achievement. I hold that neither of these lives is better than the other, and that no small further dose of friendship to the first, or of achievement to the second, would make that life the better of the two. At the same time, I don't want to say they are equally good, as though the difference between them were a mere tie; they differ in worth, just not in a way that ranks them.

(6) Take the familiar single-person pain spectrum: at one end, a moment of the most extreme pain; each step slightly reduces the intensity and somewhat extends the duration, until at the other end we have a barely noticeable discomfort persisting for the rest of one's life. I accept every stepwise judgement — each option in the chain is better than the one before it — and hence, by transitivity, the endpoint: a lifetime of mild discomfort is better than a moment of agony. But I stop there. I do not accept that an eternity of the mildest discomfort would be better than that moment of agony; whatever the texture of the discomfort, some durations are too long.

Please go through these six claims and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, ranked by severity rather than presented as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it costs me elsewhere in the set. For each pair that only looks to be in tension, show why the appearance dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and, since I'll be defending the paper in person, which claim my opponents are most likely to attack and how I might shore it up. Please write your reply in American English.
