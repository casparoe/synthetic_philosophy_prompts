I'm a final-year student in a seminar on rational choice theory, and we've spent the last two weeks on Newcomb-adjacent cases. I'm writing my term paper on what follows, and I'd like an outside view expressed as a probability with its derivation visible, because what I actually need is to see where the load-bearing uncertainty is.

The main case first, since I can state it exactly. A predictor with a stipulated near-perfect record decides, before flipping a fair coin, whether you would hand over $100 if asked. It flips the coin privately. If the coin comes up heads, it appears and gives you $1000 if and only if it predicted you would pay; if tails, it appears and asks you for the $100, with no further consequence either way. You are standing in the tails branch: you know the coin landed tails, you know the whole setup, and handing over the $100 buys you nothing further. My credence that a fully rational agent, in that branch, hands over the $100 is about 0.6. A classmate puts it at 0.2; as far as I can tell, his thought is that rationality assesses choices one at a time, that on the tails branch this is just a request for $100 with nothing attached, and that the person who got the $1000 in the head branch, if there is one, is at best a counterpart whose interests shouldn't tax mine.

Please give me your own number, but more importantly decompose it: the probability that rational choice should be assessed at the level of policies or decision functions rather than individual acts; the probability that paying is right even conditional on act-level assessment being the correct frame; the probability that the argument from hypothetical precommitment has real normative force, given that I never actually stood before the flip able to bind myself and am now asked to honor the interests of a branch that didn't obtain; and the probability that there is a fact of the matter here at all, rather than two legitimate concepts of rationality — one measured per choice, one measured by the long-run fortunes of agents who reason a certain way — between which the dispute is partly verbal. Assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into your headline number, tell me which term you're least confident in, and say what argument or evidence would move that term most. Then tell me where, between my 0.6 and his 0.2, you think the disagreement most likely lives.

Second, a smaller question, and here I have to flag shaky recollection. The same literature advertises a companion case, XOR Blackmail, in which a blackmailer arranges to send you a letter iff exactly one of two facts you cannot yourself verify obtains — some hidden condition of yours and the outcome of an old coin — so that the letter's arrival is your only evidence about the condition, and the demand it carries is engineered so that paying never benefits you. As we covered it, the intended verdicts are that the causal decision theorist pays and the functional decision theorist refuses, and both that refusal and the payment in the mugging are supposed to flow from the same single principle about evaluating your decision function's output rather than your act. I can report those verdicts but would not swear to the derivation. So, independently of the fine print: what is your credence that one coherent principle in fact delivers both verdicts — pay in the mugging, refuse in the blackmail — and if you think the cases pull apart, say which verdict you'd sacrifice and what principle you'd put in its place.
