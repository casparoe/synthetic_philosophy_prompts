I have defended illusionism about phenomenal consciousness for years: there are no phenomenal properties, only our reliably misrepresenting our own internal states as having them. But I have lately been pushed by a colleague into a fallback position I half-invented to blunt her objection — that while rich qualia like the "fire-engine redness of red" are illusory, there is a thin residual seeming, something like the mere seeming-that-there-is-something-it-is-like, that is not itself an illusion. I worry this move saves the view only by quietly collapsing it into a weak realism that would embarrass me at a conference. What would genuinely count against my position — not the standard Nagel-and-Jackson retread, but the introspective phenomenology a view like mine predicts we should find (say, judgments about experience that fragment in ways misrepresentation would not), cases it cannot describe, or results showing it has merged with its rivals? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest verdict on whether my residual-seeming gambit has insulated the view into unfalsifiability, or worse, made it no longer a position worth holding.
