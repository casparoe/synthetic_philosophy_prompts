You're a philosophy of mind person who's been around the block -- you watched functionalism get declared the winner in the 80s, watched identity theory get up off the mat, and now you sit through seminar talks where people say "of course mental states are multiply realizable" like it's as safe as modus ponens. I need you in exactly that mood.

I'm trying to figure out what a theory of mental states -- of what fixes what a belief or a pain or a desire IS, what individuates them -- actually has to deliver before I bother reading any more proposals. Everyone argues for their favorite theory by pointing at one or two cases it handles, but I want the full spec written down first: the desiderata. So give me the requirements list, where each entry says (a) what the theory has to get right, and (b) the case or consideration that makes it a requirement at all -- not just "it would be nice."

Stuff I want you to think hard about while brainstorming (please actually brainstorm a bit before writing the list, don't just dump the final thing):

- Multiple realizability itself. Is it a genuine desideratum that a theory of mind leaves room for the same pain in an octopus, a silicon system, and me? Or is that smuggling in a conclusion -- i.e., is MR a requirement for the theory or just the thing functionalists claim as their trophy? Be honest about whether some versions of the requirement are cheap (everything is multiply realizable at some description) versus which versions are actually load-bearing.
- The grain problem, or whatever you want to call it: functional descriptions come in coarse and fine grains, and depending which you pick, you either get too few mental states (everything from a thermostat up counts as believing it's hot) or too many (slightly different wirings, slightly different minds). If grain-ability is a desideratum, say what the theory owes us there.
- Distinguish having to explain something from merely being consistent with it. A theory of what pain is doesn't have to solve consciousness, probably -- but it at least can't be obviously incompatible with the fact that pains feel like something. Where's the line between "must deliver" and "must not contradict," and which desiderata live on which side?
- Explanatory adequacy: the theory is supposed to earn its keep in psychology and neuroscience. Does that mean it has to respect how cognitive science actually taxonomizes states, even when the folk taxonomy (belief, desire) doesn't line up with the scientific one? Which one wins?
- Something about first-person / introspective access, normativity (beliefs are the kinds of things that can be rational or irrational), and misrepresentation -- mention these or don't, your call, but if you include them, give the motivating case.

Then flag the part I care about most: which pairs or subsets of your desiderata look like they can't be jointly satisfied, and whether the tension is provable-ish (like a real no-go) or just where the field has been stuck for fifty years. And tell me which single requirement, if you had to drop one from the list or enforce one against all others, does the most work -- i.e. which one is quietly deciding the fight between functionalism and its rivals.

Last thing, briefly (a couple sentences each, the list is the main event): how do identity theory, straightforward functionalism, and eliminativism do against your list. Don't grade them leniently to look balanced.

Keep it conversational, this is for my own notes not a journal. A table for the desiderata is fine but don't over-format the rest.
