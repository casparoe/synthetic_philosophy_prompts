I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in epistemology on the testimony debate, and I'd like you to write it as a decision tree. The debate is the old one between reductionists and anti-reductionists: when a hearer accepts something she is told, must her justification be reducible to some independent, non-testimonial ground (her own observations, induction from her track record of who has been right, background checks on this particular speaker), or is testimony a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory, such that some default of trust stands until defeated?

The tree's starting point should be a commitment everyone in the room will grant: something like the fact that the overwhelming majority of what a person believes about history, science, geography, and the personal lives of people she has never met is held on the basis of what others have said, directly or at several removes, and that she could not independently verify more than a tiny fraction of it without abandoning most of what she knows. From that shared starting point, each branch node should be a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — concrete cases wherever possible rather than abstract profession of a label.

Please build the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of famous names. That is, decide what the genuine diagnostic questions are and what combinations of answers they allow, and only afterward locate the actual positions in that space. The branch questions should include, at minimum: (a) whether the demand for a non-testimonial justification of testimonial belief is satisfiable in principle, or whether satisfying it would require using the very kind of evidence it was supposed to ground — reading the credibility studies, asking around about who can be trusted — so that the project is self-undermining in practice if not in principle; (b) whether a hearer needs a general positive reason for trust in advance of any particular act of acceptance, or whether only background monitoring for defeating evidence is required; (c) whether whatever default of trust exists is an a priori entitlement or an empirically earned, revisable habit; and (d) — this is the angle I most want in the handout — whether the source of an utterance must be an agent capable of belief and reasons at all. As a sorting probe for (d), use machine-generated assertion: a search-engine snippet with no identifiable author, a large language model's answer produced by no one in particular, a GPS instruction. I want to see whether students' views about when they may accept such outputs without checking track down to their branch answers, and the handout should say where on the tree someone lands who trusts the GPS but insists testimony proper involves an asserter making herself answerable.

The leaves should include, at least: a global reductionism of the kind traditionally associated with Hume, on which the hearer needs a standing inductive case for the general reliability of speakers; a local reductionism of the kind associated with Jennifer Lackey's discussion of Elizabeth Fricker, on which no general positive reason is needed but the hearer must monitor each exchange for signs of unreliability; an anti-reductionism of the kind associated with Coady and with Burge's notion of entitlement, on which acceptance of testimony is justified by a non-evidential a priori right; and at least one hybrid or default-trust view. If a combination of branch answers has no standard defender, mark the leaf "unnamed" rather than inventing a school.

Three further requirements. First, for each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender most regrets having to say. Second, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate (for instance, if the tree cannot distinguish an a priori entitlement from a deeply entrenched acquired habit, say so plainly rather than papering over the gap with a question that no honest student could answer differently in the two cases). Third, note any combination of branch answers that your questions allow but no leaf occupies, and diagnose briefly why nobody is there: is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored?

Constraints on form: the audience is undergraduates with one prior epistemology course, so no jargon may appear in a branch question without a gloss in ordinary words, and the gloss must not covertly assume the answer. Keep the tree to no more than seven branch questions and no more than eight leaves. Each branch question should be answerable in one of two or three discrete ways, and the handout should end with a short scoring key mapping each complete path to its leaf. Please do not include a bibliography or citations; where I've associated a position with a philosopher's name, keep the association if you're confident of it and describe the position fully enough that the handout would survive my deleting the name.
