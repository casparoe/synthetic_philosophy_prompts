I'm a journalist who has spent fifteen years covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. I have no academic training in philosophy or historiography, but I've absorbed the working vocabulary of a newsroom: sourcing standards, the single-source rule, anonymous sourcing, the wall between news, features, and opinion, the corrections policy, the choice of lede, house style, the editorial meeting where framing gets decided, and the ideal of reporting that adopts no standpoint of its own. I've been assigned a long piece on whether history can be objective — the old dispute that runs from Ranke's nineteenth-century ambition to show the past as it actually was, through E. H. Carr's argument that facts only become historical facts through the historian's selection, with his image of them as fish on a fishmonger's slab from which the historian chooses, to Hayden White's claim in Metahistory that historians impose narrative forms — romance, tragedy, comedy, satire — that the evidence itself underdetermines.

Restate this debate, as faithfully as you can, in newsroom terms. Which of Ranke's ambitions corresponds to the wire-service ideal, and does that ideal survive contact with what newsrooms actually do? Which of Carr's points are already institutionalized in story selection and the editorial meeting? Which of White's points correspond to feature writing and its narrative arc, and where does that correspondence break down? Then give me the residue report: which parts of the historians' debate have clean newsroom counterparts, and what the newsroom's own answers — corrections, sourcing standards, the news/features wall — would say about them; which translate only with distortion; and which have no counterpart at all. I suspect White's claim that the choice of narrative form is a poetic rather than an evidentiary act is one of these, since newsrooms treat story structure as a craft convention, not a commitment. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it shows the newsroom vocabulary is missing something or whether the historians' claim is less substantive than it looks. Finally, a test case: I'm planning to structure my article as three generations of historians gradually learning humility, with White as the endpoint. Tell me what my outline is doing in White's own terms, and whether a newsroom-trained reader would notice.
