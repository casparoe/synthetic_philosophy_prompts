I'm a novelist, a few chapters into a book where Kurt Gödel turns up disoriented in present-day Princeton (long story — the neighbourhood watch finds him in the woods near the Institute, and I'm keeping how he got there vague on purpose). My editor says the Gödel scenes read like every other 'great genius marvels at smartphones' bit, and honestly she's right. What I want is for his reactions to be *his*, and specifically his reactions to cosmology after 1978, since the man spent years on that rotating universe solution — the 1949 one he dedicated to Einstein for his 70th birthday, with its closed timelike curves and its negative cosmological constant, which he used to argue that the passage of time is basically an illusion.

So here's what I'd love from you: how do you think Gödel would react to (1) dark energy and the accelerating expansion, especially the irony of the cosmological constant, the one Einstein supposedly regretted, coming back from the dead; (2) the problem of time in quantum gravity, where people write down an equation for the whole universe with no time variable in it whatsoever — would he see that as vindication or as physicists fumbling toward something he thought was obvious; (3) rotating black holes, i.e. the Kerr solution, where the same closed timelike curves he found show up all over again; and (4) the fact that the expanding-universe picture is now treated as settled, given that I've read Gödel himself never really accepted the expansion — was that stubbornness or something principled in him?

For each one, could you ground the prediction in what we actually know about the guy — his Leibniz-style conviction that the world is rational and every question has a real answer, his theism and belief in an afterlife, his reported view that understanding the world would eventually require some other kind of perception than the senses, his habit of telling all this to people like Hao Wang in conversation while publishing almost none of it — and please be honest about where you're extrapolating versus where the record is solid, since I don't want my novel to be confidently wrong about him. No invented quotes, paraphrase instead. And if you could end with one short bit of dialogue between him and a fictional cosmologist character at an observatory, where they genuinely talk past each other, that would definately be gold.
