I'm a PhD student in computer science working on causal discovery — I spend most of my time thinking about structural causal models, identifiability of interventional quantities, that sort of thing — and this term I'm sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar on causation to see what the philosophical literature can offer. We read Lewis's counterfactual analysis and then Woodward-style interventionism back to back, and I came out more confused than when I went in. Everyone in the seminar talked as if these are clearly rival theories, but when I try to articulate what actually separates them, the distinction keeps dissolving in my hands.

Here's my confusion in more detail. On the Lewisian side, "c caused e" goes via the counterfactual "if c had not occurred, e would not have occurred," and the truth of that counterfactual is settled by similarity relations between possible worlds: the closest worlds without c are ones where a small miracle keeps history broadly intact up to just before c, and where we do not backtrack — we don't adjust the past in light of c's absence. On the interventionist side, we ask whether setting X surgically — via something like the do-operator — would change Y, holding the rest of the causal structure fixed. But to my eye the surgical intervention just *is* the no-backtracking stipulation in different notation: a surgical intervention is precisely one that changes X without disturbing X's ancestors or any other variable in the system, which is what the small-miracle, hold-the-past-fixed convention secures for Lewis. A classmate insists the difference is substantive, while a visiting speaker last week more or less said the two frameworks are intertranslatable modulo bookkeeping, and neither side gave me a worked example that made the contrast bite.

What I'd like is a careful explanation of where the two approaches genuinely come apart, if in fact they do. Three threads seem promising to me, though feel free to add or correct: (a) the way each secures non-backtracking — closest-world similarity as a metaphysical fact versus Woodward's requirement that a genuine intervention variable be possible and not be "fat-handed," which can fail in systems where X cannot be varied without disturbing other causes of Y (a common upstream driver, or something like a conservation constraint tying X to Y's other parents), and I'm unsure what Lewis has to say in exactly those cases where interventionism goes silent; (b) the type/token asymmetry — counterfactual dependence was offered as an analysis of actual, token-level causation, while the interventional analogue seems naturally to be a claim about variables in a model, relative to which variables we've chosen to represent, and it's unclear to me how each framework then handles the classic trouble cases like late preemption with a backup, where the effect would still have occurred through another route, or the failures and successes of causal transitivity; and (c) whether the deepest difference is that one theory is doing metaphysics (what causation is, in nature) while the other is doing something more pragmatic or model-relative (what it is to have causal knowledge of a system). I'd be grateful if you could anchor the discussion in one or two concrete scenarios — the sort you could draw as a small DAG or tell as a story about a short circuit, a backup wire, and a fire — where the two frameworks either deliver different verdicts or, more interestingly, explain the same verdict in ways that reveal different commitments. I'm not looking for a slogan-level contrast of the kind I could get from an encyclopedia entry; I'm looking for the load-bearing differences.
