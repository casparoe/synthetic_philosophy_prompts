I've just retired after thirty-some years as a sedimentologist, which mostly meant staring at grain size distributions, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been reading philosophy and have gotten properly stuck on vagueness and the sorites paradox. Maybe you can sort me out. Please write your answer in British English.

Here are my actual gut verdicts, as honestly as I can give them:

1. One grain of sand is not a heap. Two million grains dumped out of a lorry is a heap. No hesitation on either.
2. A few thousand grains piled up — I'll just call that a heap, flat out, with a shrug. I would never say it's "60% a heap" or "sort of true that it's a heap". A pile doesn't come in percentages, that seems mad to me.
3. But I also flatly refuse the sorites step: take one grain away from a heap and you still have a heap. Every time. So there's no sharp cutoff, and yet (per point 2) no degrees either. I'm told these two can't both be right, and I don't see why not.
4. If you show me a colour patch exactly midway between orange and red, I'll say "orange" and move on, but I'd also say there's genuinely no fact of the matter there. Whereas with the heap I catch myself feeling like there IS a fact of the matter, I just can't put my finger on it. Annoying.
5. My professional instinct says the whole thing is solved by stipulation, the way we solved it in the lab: the sand/silt boundary on the Wentworth scale sits at 1/16 mm, some committee agreed on it decades ago, and nobody has lost sleep since. And here's the thing — once that convention is in place, whether a particle is sand or silt feels like an objective fact about the world, not a choice we made.
6. Except a colleague's lab used a slightly different cutoff (they worked in φ units, and there were always little conversion spats), and I want to say both labs were perfectly correct, each relative to its own convention. Which makes me wonder whether the sand/silt fact was ever "in the world" at all, and then whether "heap" could be true full stop even after stipulating.

So my tentative theory, from a lifetime of calibrating instruments: vague words are like uncalibrated thermometers — the world is perfectly precise, the words are just out of adjustment, and once you calibrate them by stipulating, everything is exact. But then why does truth seem to float with the calibration (point 6) rather than getting fixed by it?

Can you find one principle that gets all six verdicts, or close? If not, which of my verdicts has to go? And I'd especially like to know what the theory says about two cases I haven't worked out: (a) if you march me along piles from 10,000 grains down to 1, one grain at a time, and force a yes/no each time, what should I say and when? and (b) the famous textbook case of ring species — herring gulls circling the Arctic where each neighbouring population interbreeds with the next, but the two ends don't interbreed with each other. Is there a fact of the matter whether the end populations are one species or two, and does my calibration picture help there or make it worse? Be honest if my "no cutoff AND no degrees" combination is just incoherent and I'm fooling myself.
