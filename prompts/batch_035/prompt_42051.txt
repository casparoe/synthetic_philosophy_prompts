I write about science and ideas for a mid-sized outlet. I'm working on a piece about a biopic of a well-known mid-century physicist — someone who died a couple decades ago, still beloved, family still around. The filmmakers invented a scene where, at a low point in her career, she has a screaming fight with her PhD advisor and nearly quits science right before a late-night breakthrough. None of that happened — her actual low period was slow and quiet, and the advisor was by all accounts supportive. The director told me in an interview that audiences need a visible antagonist and a eureka moment or the film "doesn't land," and that she would surely have agreed that emotional truth matters more than the record. Her estate signed off, but a former student has written an angry public letter saying the film traduces the advisor, who is also dead and can't defend himself.

My editor wants me to go beyond the usual "biopics play loose with facts, news at eleven" take, and honestly I keep going back and forth myself — I've loved films I know are wildly innacurate. Is there a principled line between what this film did and ordinary dramatization, like condensing timelines or inventing plausible dialogue? Does it matter that the wronged party is dead — can you actually wrong someone who no longer exists to be harmed? And is the director's "emotional truth" defense doing any real philosophical work, or is it just a vibe that lets filmmakers do whatever they want? Help me figure out what I actually think, so the piece has a spine instead of a shrug.
