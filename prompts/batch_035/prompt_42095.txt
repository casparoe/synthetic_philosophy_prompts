I quit smoking about two years ago (not a humblebrag, it was mostly becuse cigarettes got so damn expensive) and ever since Ive been kind of obsessed with how fast smoking went from totally normal to basically shameful. My parents' generation smoked in hospitals, on airplanes, at their desks. Now the couple of smokers left at my office huddle outside by the loading dock like they're doing something criminal. Around the same time I quit I reread Kwame Anthony Appiah's popular book on moral revolutions — The Honor Code — where he tries to explain how practices like dueling, footbinding, and the Atlantic slave trade actually died. His whole pitch is that moral arguments by themselves never do it: you need honor to flip (the practice stops conferring respect and starts costing it), plus material interests lining up on the right side, and the arguments mostly show up after the fact to tidy things up. He leans on Bernard Williams for the dueling analysis, something about dueling dying out when it stopped doing honor-work, though I confess I forget the exact mechanism.

So here's my question: take the fall of smoking and run it through that model. The rough timeline as I understand it: the 1964 Surgeon General report on smoking and health, the secondhand smoke findings piling up in the 80s and 90s, smoking banned on US domestic flights around 1990, California banning it in bars in 1998, New York City in 2003, and now basically everywhere indoor in the Western world. Map that onto Appiah's three pieces — argument, interest, honor — and tell me which one was actually doing the work, and when. Was it even a moral revolution at all, or just a hygiene regulation plus forty years of people watching their relatives die of lung cancer? And here's the part I keep chewing on: unlike dueling or footbinding, smoking never actually ended, it just got reclassified from a glamorous universal habit into a lower-class vice. So does the case confirm the honor thesis, or does it show the thesis only explains the respectable version of a practice dying, and the unrespectable version just gets pushed outside the building?

If you think the honor story holds up here, spell out what it would even look like — who lost honor, who gained it, whether there was something like the anti-footbinding pledge dynamic he describes (where people bound their reputations together by promising not to bind their daughters' feet / not to marry their sons to such families). And be honest about where the model goes silent or misdescribes things — the secondhand smoke point in particular seems to make this a harm-to-others argument from early on, which is not really how his cases work. A few paragraphs is fine, I'd rather have careful mapping than a lecture.
