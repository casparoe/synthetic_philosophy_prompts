I've taught the philosophy of mind survey for most of my career, and each year, teaching the dreams unit, I have drifted further into a view I once presented merely as a curio: something close to Norman Malcolm's position in his book *Dreaming* (1959). My rendering of the view has three commitments. First, dream reports are not memories of experiences that occurred during sleep; they are, roughly, narratives composed in the course of waking and reporting. Second, nothing that happens in a sleeping brain is properly assessed as an experience, because experience requires a kind of contact with the environment — responsiveness, availability to intersubjective checking — that sleep, as we actually use the concept, suspends. Third, and most Malcolmian, the overwhelming felt certainty that dreams are experiences is a grammatical artifact of the dream-report idiom rather than a datum that any theory must respect. Colleagues treat this position as refuted by now-standard material: lucid dreamers who signal to experimenters with pre-agreed ocular movements; the finding that dreamers can incorporate external stimuli, such as a sprayed mist of water, into reported dream content; Dennett's old point that a dream report can encode temporal information about the night, as when a report seems to run through material corresponding to events that a recorder shows occurred at different times before waking. I want to know, seriously, what would count against me.

Please construct a map of the view's exposure rather than a defense of it. Which observations would be decisive, and which merely awkward? I would like the defeaters sorted into kinds — empirical observations, structural arguments (e.g., that the view collapses into or is parasitic on a rival like Dennett's account, on which dream narratives are composed at the moment of waking and inserted into memory as if remembered), cases the view cannot describe, and consequences it is committed to that may prove unacceptable. For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of the Malcolm-style position would concede it as counting against the view, qualify the view to absorb it, or dismiss it — and, where the move is dismissal or qualification, what the qualified view has lost. Pay particular attention to the definitional move at the heart of the position: if sleep is characterized partly by unresponsiveness to surroundings, the view risks becoming unfalsifiable by construction, since any apparent counterexample involving a responsive sleeper can be reclassified as a case of brief waking. Assess honestly whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind, or whether some definitional protection is legitimate here in a way it would not be in, say, a theory of temperature. Where it helps, invent hard cases for me: a sleeper whose breakfast-time report incorporates two recorded environmental sounds in the correct order, twenty minutes apart, is the sort of thing I lose sleep over, so to speak.

Please present your answer as a JSON object with the following fields. "overall_exposure": one of "fatal", "serious", "moderate", "well_insulated", with a one-sentence justification. "defeaters": an array of objects, ordered from most to least damaging, each with the fields "name" (short label), "kind" (one of "observation", "argument", "case_the_view_cannot_describe", "consequence", "structural_collapse"), "description" (two or three sentences), "severity" (one of "fatal", "serious", "merely_awkward"), "defender_response" (one of "concede", "qualify", "dismiss"), and "cost_of_response" (what the view loses if the response is qualification or dismissal). "insulation_assessment": a string assessing the unfalsifiability worry and whether it discredits the view. "least_discussed_defeater": the objection I am least likely to have already heard in the literature on this debate. "what_would_move_me_most": a string identifying the single consideration that should most shift my credence, and in which direction.
