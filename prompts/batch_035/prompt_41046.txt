Our philosophy of mind reading group has been stuck on eliminative materialism for what feels like forever, and I'm starting to suspect the debate isn't really going anywhere and maybe never was. The setup as I understand it: the Churchlands said propositional attitudes like beliefs and desires are the theoretical posits of folk psychology, folk psychology is a bad theory (it's stagnant, it can't handle sleep, mental illness, creativity etc.), and bad theories get eliminated, not vindicated by smooth reduction -- just like phlogiston didn't turn out to be a refined version of oxygen. So no beliefs, no desires, done. Against them you've got people like Fodor arguing that belief-talk is our best explanatory tool and that posits individuated functionally don't get eliminated just because the implementational details get messy, plus Dennett's line that the intentional stance carves out real patterns even if you never find beliefs inside the skull.

Here's the thing though. Nobody has moved in forty years and I have a hypothesis about why: the debate only looks like an empirical bet about neuroscience, but neuroscience lost interest. Nobody in actual cognitive science is waiting around for the replacement vocabulary that "eliminates" beliefs -- and worse, the trendy frameworks like predictive processing keep inventing things (expectations, priors, generative models) that look suspiciously like beliefs with new names, which the anti-eliminativists count as a win and the eliminativists count as no such thing. Meanwhile both sides keep talking past each other because they can't agree on what it would take for something to "be a belief" in the first place -- one side wants a natural kind, the other just wants explanatory success, and each thinks the other's criterion begs the question. And there's a sneaky bit where the eliminativist has to explain why she asserts her own theory, which she usually does by saying she has good reasons to believe it.

So what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it a disguised verbal dispute about what counts as a real mental state term, a genuine empirical dispute about the future of cognitive science that's just badly underdetermined right now, or two different projects (ontology vs. methodology) wearing one label? I want a real diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says, not a survey, and I especially want you to evaluate my hypothesis that the empirical core of the dispute quietly dissolved without either side noticing. And then tell me honestly: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here -- a distinction, a result, a reformulated question -- or is this just an ill-posed debate that will be decided by fashions in neuroscience rather than arguments? If you think my reading group should just dissolve the topic and move on, say that too.
