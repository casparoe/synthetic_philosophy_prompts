A friend of mine who recently finished a philosophy MA and now writes a blog about reasoning and bias sent me her summary of the debate over higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I would like a careful check of it before I lean on it for a reading-group essay. Her post reads as follows: "Epistemic akrasia is the epistemologist's analogue of weakness of will: believing p while at the same time believing that your evidence does not support p. In the practical case, everyone agrees that akrasia — acting against your own better judgment — is irrational, and the standard view has always been that epistemic akrasia is irrational in just the same way: it is, after all, believing something you know you should not believe. Recently, though, a minority have defended the akratic believer. Their argument is that we sometimes know things on the basis of misleading higher-order evidence, and since knowledge entails rationality, the akratic believer comes out fully rational in those cases. Their opponents reply that this would license steering: if you could rationally hang on to p while believing your evidence does not support p, you could simply dismiss higher-order evidence whenever it was inconvenient. What everyone on both sides agrees on is that higher-order evidence is special — it is evidence about what your other evidence supports, and so it cannot be treated as just one more input to be weighed alongside the rest. And either way, the akratic agent is in a bad epistemic spot: one of her two beliefs has to be irrational, so the debate is only about which one, and whether she can help having it." She closes her post with a thought of her own: that these cases reveal the problem is really recursive, because forming any belief on the basis of perception already involves taking your own faculties to be reliable, and that taking is itself something further evidence could undercut — so every ordinary belief is at least latently akratic, and epistemology should give up on demanding consistency between what you believe and what you believe your evidence supports, settling instead for rationality that comes in degrees.

I am inclined to sympathize with the defenders she mentions — it does seem harsh to call the person who knowingly keeps a well-supported belief irrational — but I suspect she has flattened some distinctions somewhere, and I do not know the literature well enough to say where. Please go through her summary part by part: say what an epistemologist working on higher-order evidence would accept as accurate, what they would call a simplification a popular treatment can be forgiven, and what they would flatly disagree with. Pay particular attention to whether the arguments she attributes to each side are the arguments those sides actually run, and whether anything she presents as common ground is in fact the crux of the dispute. Then assess two further things: whether her claim that one of the akratic agent's two beliefs has to be irrational really is something both sides accept, and whether her closing recursive observation about perception follows from the accurate parts of her summary, or whether it changes the subject.
