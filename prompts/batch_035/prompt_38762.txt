I'm taking a philosophy of mind course this term, and while the readings disagree about what consciousness is, they all lean heavily on the word "illusion" without ever saying what gets to count as one. So I've been keeping a list of cases and marking down my gut verdict on each — illusory or not — trying to see whether my gut is tracking something principled. My tentative rule is this: an experience is an illusion when it attributes a property to something that doesn't have that property, and otherwise it's veridical. But I've found that my verdicts don't obviously follow this rule, and I'd like help figuring out what principle, if any, they actually add up to.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. Stubbing my toe: real, full stop — the hurt isn't misattributed to anything, there's just pain. The Müller-Lyer lines, which I know are equal but stubbornly look unequal: illusion — the lines lack the property they seem to have. The green afterimage floating on the wall after I stare at a red square: illusion — nothing on the wall is green. So far the rule works. Then it starts to wobble. My uncle, after his leg was amputated, had years of pain in the foot that was no longer there; structurally that looks exactly like the afterimage (pain attributed to a limb that doesn't exist), yet I can't bring myself to call his pain an illusion the way I call the green patch one — it was real pain, he needed real treatment for it. My peripheral vision seems rich and detailed until you show me the change-blindness videos, where huge changes go unnoticed; I'm comfortable calling the richness an illusion, but it's a stranger one, because it's not that some object lacks a property — it's that the "rich visual field" itself doesn't seem to exist anywhere until I probe it. Then there's the case of getting absorbed in a book while the fridge hums: when the hum stops, I realize I'd been hearing it for an hour, and my instinct is that there was nothing it was like to hear it until I noticed it — which suggests phenomenology comes and goes with attention, and that unsettles me. Finally, reading a French sentence I understand versus one I don't: I'm convinced there's something it is like to understand it, beyond any imagery or inner speech, and not an illusion — but I can't say what property is being attributed to what object there at all.

So my questions are: does some principle — mine repaired, or a better one — capture these verdicts, or at least most of them? Where my rule and my verdicts conflict, like the phantom limb, which should give, and why? What would the resulting principle predict about cases I haven't listed — tinnitus, the feeling that you've been somewhere before, the felt effort of doing mental arithmetic in your head? And does the principle ultimately slide into illusionism about phenomenal consciousness generally? A classmate of mine calls himself an illusionist and says that once I accept that the unequal look is real as a look while the lines are equal, I've admitted the look isn't a property of anything out there — and that the same reasoning should apply to the "hurtness" of my stubbed toe, which he says is just as much a construction as the green afterimage. I want to know whether my own verdicts, taken seriously, force me to agree with him, or whether there's a principled place to stop short.
