At our last lab meeting, the director shut down a debate about whether to continue a five-year data-collection project that is roughly 80% complete but has so far produced nothing usable, saying "don't throw good money after bad" as though it settled the matter. I suspect the proverb is doing less work than she thinks: it seems to slide between several distinct claims — that past investment is never evidence about future returns, that an unfinished project's outputs are worthless unless finished, that continuing after losses is always escalation of commitment, or just the truism that rational decisions depend on marginal costs and benefits going forward. Please lay out the precise theses the proverb could be expressing, from the trivially true to the substantive and contestable, and for each say whether it is plausible and whether it would actually support ending our particular project (where the remaining work is small relative to what has been spent, and finishing is the only way to salvage any return). Also indicate whether any reading supports the opposite conclusion — that a sunk cost can sometimes be a legitimate reason to continue.
