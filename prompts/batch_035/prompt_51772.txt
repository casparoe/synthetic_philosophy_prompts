I am a novelist drafting a chapter in which two estranged brothers restore their late father's fishing boat plank by plank over the course of one winter, while a neighbour quietly salvages the discarded timbers and rebuilds the original hull in his own barn. I want to open the chapter with a sestina that does the metaphysics rather than merely describe it: the six end-words should name the ship's parts or aspects — a keel, a plank, a nail, a name, and so on — and their recurrence in a new position each stanza should enact replacement without loss, the same words persisting through the poem exactly as the ship persists through the restoration. The envoi, which must use all six end-words in three lines, should be the moment the brothers first see the neighbour's reassembled boat and each claims the original for a different reason. The poem should not resolve the identity question, since my chapter refuses to; but it should make a reader feel precisely why the claim pulls two ways — toward continuity of form and function, and toward continuity of matter — and the envoi should let both brothers be, in some sense, right. Accuracy matters to me: someone who has taught the Ship of Theseus, including Hobbes's addition of the second reassembled ship, should recognise the puzzle working in the form and find the poem correct rather than decorative.
