write me a sestina on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the old objection that the pleasure of eating a peach when you're hungry, the pleasure of an itch being scratched, the pleasure of a proof clicking into place, of music, of orgasm, don't seem to share anything except the word "pleasure," so the hedonist owes an account of the one property all these episodes supposedly have in common, and just saying "they're all pleasant" starts to sound like naming rather than saying anything. use the form to do the philosophical work: the six end-words have to keep coming back in new company, exactly like the property the hedonist claims recurs in wildly different experiences, so the reader should start to feel the question — is the recurrence in the world or just in the word? one of the end-words should be something like "again" or "more" — a word that could naturally cap a line about literally any pleasure — and let it slowly start to feel suspicious, like it's doing all the work while meaning nothing determinate, which is precisely the critics' complaint. the other end-words can be concrete (peach, ache, dusk, whatever). each of the six stanzas should live inside one specific pleasure, rendered sensually enough that a hedonist would claim it and a critic would say: fine, but look how little it has to do with the others. no philosophy vocabulary in the body of the poem at all. the envoi is the only place the question is allowed to surface, and it shouldnt be answered — the objection is a demand for an articulation, not a proof there's nothing to articulate, so leave the hedonist exactly one honest move at the end (the direction-of-fit move, the family-resemblance move, whatever you think is strongest) without endorsing it. someone who knows the objection should recognize it stanza by stanza; someone who doesn't should just get a good poem about wanting things again. no mention of bentham or mill or pushpin vs poetry, it cheapens it
