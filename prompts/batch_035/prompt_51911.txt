I hold a position in the methodology of economics that I'll state as crisply as I can: the question of whether a model's assumptions are realistic is, for purposes of scientific appraisal, simply not the right question. A theory or model is to be judged by the accuracy, scope, and fruitfulness of its predictions, and there is nothing amiss — indeed something right — about a theory that achieves great predictive power while resting on assumptions that are wildly false as descriptions of the world. This is, roughly, the position Milton Friedman put forward in his 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics: significant theories will tend to have descriptively false assumptions, and demanding realism of them betrays a misunderstanding of how science works. I take the strongest form of the view to say not merely that unrealism is excusable but that the unrealism of assumptions is largely irrelevant to assessment — a point critics have called the F-twist, and which Alan Musgrave tried to dissect in a 1981 paper distinguishing assumptions read as negligibility claims, domain claims, and heuristics. I know Musgrave's move, and I think it largely defuses the standard objection: once we see that most so-called unrealistic assumptions are really statements that certain factors are negligible within a domain, the unrealism ceases to be an objection at all. So my question is not whether the F-twist has critics — it plainly does — but what would genuinely count against the position.

I want the deliverable to be a map of the view's exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically: which conceivable findings, arguments, or methodological developments should move me? I'd like you to consider at least the following lines of attack, and any others you think are stronger. First, the epistemology of prediction itself: given that models are tested against data under ceteris paribus clauses and that a defender can always redescribe the theory's domain so as to accommodate failures, is it even possible in principle for predictive performance to falsify the view — and if predictive success is this elastic, hasn't the view bought its invulnerability at the price of its substance? Second, the selection problem: since a model makes many predictions and a theorist can, after the fact, emphasise the ones that succeeded, what would count as a fair test of predictive power, and what observations would show that apparent successes were obtained that way? Third, the use of models where prediction is unavailable — welfare analysis, policy advice, counterfactual claims about regimes we have not observed. If a model's assumptions are unrealism-tolerated because predictions are good, what happens when we use the model precisely where its predictions cannot be checked, such as in the Lucas critique's setting, where the policy intervention itself changes the structure that generated the past predictions? Does the fact that economists themselves often demand realistic assumptions of behavioural parameters when doing policy counterfactuals concede too much to the critics? Fourth, underdetermination: where two models with incompatible assumptions fit the same observed record, and fit is all that matters, on what basis does the discipline choose — and is that basis something the F-twist, as I hold it, can accommodate without quietly reinstating assumption-realism as a criterion?

Beyond these, tell me which objections a sophisticated Friedmanite would concede as genuine defeaters versus dismiss as confused, and whether the position as I've stated it — with Musgrave's negligibility/domain/heuristic apparatus doing the defensive work — has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability, and whether that is disqualifying for a methodological thesis of this kind, or whether, as some argue, methodological theses are not themselves the sort of thing that needs falsifiable content. I'd also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not: what should look different in the actual practice of a science whose practitioners accept it compared with one whose practitioners demand realistic assumptions — and whether the history of economics since 1953 looks more like vindication or refutation on that score. Finally, be honest if you think the view in its strongest form is false but a modest descendant of it survives: I'd want to know exactly which amendment does the work and what it costs.
