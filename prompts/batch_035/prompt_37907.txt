I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and my metaethics reading group spent last Thursday on the Frege–Geach problem. The discussion ended in a standoff between two of my colleagues, and I found myself genuinely unable to referee it, so I'd like a careful, point-by-point adjudication. The exchange, in order:

(1) Marta opened with the classic version of the objection. On a simple expressivist treatment, an unembedded utterance of stealing is wrong expresses disapproval of stealing. But in the conditional if stealing is wrong, getting your little brother to steal is wrong, the antecedent is not asserted, so no disapproval is expressed there. Yet the antecedent plainly means the same thing in both contexts, and the modus ponens argument built from the conditional plus the unembedded antecedent is obviously valid. So a theory that ties meaning to whatever attitude is expressed in assertion cannot give a compositional semantics for embedded occurrences, and cannot explain the validity of the inference.

(2) Jonas replied with a Blackburnian move. To accept the conditional, he said, is to have a higher-order attitude against the combination of disapproving of stealing while failing to disapprove of getting your little brother to steal. Someone who accepts the conditional and the antecedent but rejects the consequent is left with a fractured sensibility — an inconsistent set of attitudes — and that inconsistency plays exactly the role that inconsistency of belief plays in explaining why the inference is valid.

(3) Marta said this concedes her point rather than answering it: the account of what the conditional expresses is not built up compositionally from the meanings of its parts; it is stipulated independently, tailored case by case so the right arguments come out valid. Whatever-it-takes semantics isn't semantics.

(4) Marta then added what she took to be the harder variant: negation. If stealing is not wrong is read as expressing the mere absence of disapproval, then someone indifferent to theft counts as accepting it, and it no longer conflicts with stealing is wrong; if instead it expresses a positive attitude like approval, then negation is no longer operating on content but flipping us between distinct attitudes, which is not how negation behaves anywhere else. Jonas answered that some expressivists handle this by letting the negation operate inside the object of the attitude — being for not-blaming, say — rather than on the attitude itself. Marta replied that this treats attitudes as having structured, proposition-like contents that can be negated and embedded, thereby reintroducing precisely the representational machinery expressivism was supposed to replace.

(5) Jonas then floated hybrid expressivism as the way out: moral claims express both beliefs and conative attitudes; the belief component composes with negation and conditionals in the ordinary way and handles all embedding; the problem dissolves.

(6) Marta responded that on that picture the belief component does all the logical work, so the hybrid theorist must either say the attitude contributes nothing to truth-conditions — in which case the view is just realism with a remark about the psychology of assertion bolted on — or say it contributes something, in which case compositionality returns in full force.

(7) Jonas's final move was a step back: maybe the expressivist should stop trying to mimic truth-conditional semantics and instead characterize validity for moral discourse as preservation of coherence among attitudes, admitting that a general logic of attitudes is a research program rather than a finished theory. Marta said a program whose central unsolved problem is this old, and whose main results are worked examples rather than theorems, is not obviously a live program.

Please adjudicate each of the seven moves: say who is right about which specific point, with a brief justification, and indicate where a move is right in part and mistaken in part. I'd especially like your view on three things: whether move (2) actually explains the validity of the modus ponens argument or only redescribes what a consistent set of attitudes looks like; whether Marta's compositionality demand in (3) is a fair requirement or quietly assumes a truth-conditional standard that the expressivist is entitled to reject; and whether the negation issue in (4) is genuinely separable from the conditional problem in (1) or just one face of the same objection. Finally, tell me whether either of them is missing a move that would substantially reframe the dispute, and who comes out more right overall.
