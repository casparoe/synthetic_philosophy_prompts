I've been around the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and nuclear deterrence is the one topic where I think our toolkit gets the least traction, precisely because the standard analysis looks airtight until you push on it. Here is the shape of my problem. Deterrence works by threatening a retaliation that has no remaining point: once enemy missiles have already destroyed your cities, launching yours in reply achieves nothing — the deterrence has already failed, and the strike is pure loss. A perfectly rational adversary should see this, discount the threat accordingly, and attack whenever the first strike was tempting to begin with. The familiar repairs are Schelling's threat that leaves something to chance, where you don't threaten to carry through, only to keep running a risk of carrying through, and the brinkmanship reading on which leaders rationally manipulate the appearance of partial loss of control; Kahn's doomsday machine just makes the needed irrationality automatic. But as far as I can tell, every repair either reintroduces the irrationality at one level down or makes the mechanism so noisy that it becomes unclear it deserves the credit it gets for the long peace.

My credence that the absence of great-power nuclear war since 1945 is mainly owed to deterrence working roughly as theorized — as opposed to luck, or to factors the theory doesn't include — sits around 0.8 and has been drifting down. A friend whose judgment I rate puts it at 0.3. His version: we've passed through a handful of genuine coin flips (Cuba, the 1983 false-alarm episodes, assorted border incidents), and a combination of survivorship bias and the anthropic shadow — the observation that we could only ever find ourselves in the worlds that dodged the bullets — makes the mechanism look far safer in the historical record than it is.

Give me your own credence on the proposition, and decompose it into the terms you think actually generate the number. Some candidates, though you should add or discard as you see fit: the probability that the long peace reflects a real causal effect of deterrence rather than survivorship, given what is known about the frequency and severity of the near misses; the probability that the credibility paradox is genuinely dissolved by the risk-transfer mechanisms, as opposed to real-world deterrence quietly depending on leaders being something less than perfectly rational, or on domestic incentives to be seen avenging a loss; and the probability that over a long enough horizon, accidental or unauthorized launch dominates deliberate escalation in the total risk — in which case the theoretical dispute about rational threats is largely beside the point. Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, which term my friend and I most likely disagree about given our 0.8 and 0.3, and what evidence, obtainable in principle by people like us, would move your number the most.
