I'm a novelist — my most recent book turns on a fictional regional museum sitting on a collection of Benin bronzes looted in the 1897 punitive expedition — and I've been invited to run a single session of a graduate seminar on colonial provenance and museum ethics: about 14 students, 90 minutes. I know how to stage a scene, not how to run a seminar, so I'd like you to work as a designer-facilitator with a philosopher's command of the restitution debate. Begin by briefly sketching two or three candidate designs and saying why you reject all but one; then give the full design.

What I want the students to feel, rather than be told, is this: the parties to a restitution dispute often agree on all the facts — what was taken, when, by whom, where it sits now — and still cannot resolve anything, because each side holds a different framework for deciding who is entitled to what: legal title validly acquired under the law of the time, the universal museum's claim to stewardship for all humanity, and restitution as repair owed to a particular despoiled community. And beneath even that: that "who has standing to decide" is itself the contested question. So the rules, briefs, and incentives should be built so the group collides with the deadlock themselves, before anyone names it.

Please specify: roles and confidential briefs (trustees, the origin country's national commission, the royal court as a rival claimant, a mediator, and at least one party I haven't thought of); a staged twist mid-session; materials; a timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments onto the frameworks; what will predictably go wrong the first time; and an honest note on which feature of the real dispute no classroom exercise can reproduce and I must supply in the debrief.

Conclude with a machine-readable last line of the form \answer{...} containing, in one short phrase, the single rule or constraint on which the whole exercise turns — the element that, if removed, collapses it into a generic debate.
