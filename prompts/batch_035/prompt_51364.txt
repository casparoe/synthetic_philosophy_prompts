Background: I spent 37 years as a physical chemist, mostly thermodynamics and kinetics, and now that I'm retired I've gotten really into decision theory as a hobby. I think I understand the basic money pump story: if your preferences form a cycle (you prefer A to B, B to C, and C back to A), then a sharp trader can start you at A and shuttle you around the loop, charging a small fee at each step, until you're right back where you started except poorer. It's presented as the decision-theoretic version of a perpetual motion machine, and I get why people find that damning — in my world a cycle that produces net work out of nothing violates a conservation law, so "cyclic preferences leak money" feels like it should be just as final.

But here's what bugs me, and none of the pop books I've found address it. What exactly has to be true of the agent over time for the pump to actually go through? The victim has to accept each trade one at a time, using the same local reasoning each time, without noticing the pattern or without being able to do anything about it even if they do notice. But a smarter agent could just refuse all trades once they see a cycle coming, or even pay a small fee up front to not be offered the sequence at all. It seems like the argument quietly assumes rationality is evaluated trade-by-trade rather than as a plan or policy. My nephew, who does econ, brushed this off by saying the trader will just design the sequence so each step still looks good to you, but that doesn't answer it as far as I can tell — if refusing is available at any node, why does having cyclic preferences doom you rather than just having cyclic preferences *plus* a habit of grabbing every locally attractive trade?

So what I'd like you to explain: (1) which assumptions about the agent's behavior across time the standard money pump actually needs, (2) whether the real lesson is "your preferences must be acyclic" or something weaker/stronger about consistency between what you choose now and what you'll choose later — is diachronic consistency a separate requirement from plain transitivity of preference? (3) which specific failures the pump is even aimed at (intransitivity vs. violating the independence axiom vs. ordinary time-inconsistency like preferring to start a diet tomorrow), since people run the same argument against all three and I suspect they shouldn't. And finally whether the folk version — "cyclic preferences = free money for anyone who finds you" — survives once all these caveats are on the table, or whether the pump was carrying that conclusion the whole time. Feel free to use tables or worked examples, that's how I'd want it in a paper anyway.
