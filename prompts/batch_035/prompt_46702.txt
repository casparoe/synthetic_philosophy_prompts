A friend of mine serves on hiring committees at a mid-sized software company, and we recently got into a debate about how they make decisions. Each candidate is interviewed by a panel, and every interviewer separately files a hire / no-hire recommendation; the candidate receives an offer only if a majority recommends hiring. My friend is enthusiastic about this procedure. She says she once read about a result going back to the eighteenth century concerning collective voting: if each member of a group is more likely to be right than wrong about some question, and the members form their opinions independently of one another, then a majority vote among them is more reliable than any single member, and the reliability climbs toward certainty as the group grows. She takes this to show that the panel system is not just better than any individual interviewer but increasingly trustworthy the more interviewers are added, and she has been pushing the company to enlarge its panels from five to nine members on that basis.

Something about her confidence bothers me, though I've had trouble pinning down exactly what. The interviewers aren't strangers reasoning from scratch: they all read the same packet of materials about the candidate, they were trained on the same evaluation rubric by the same person, several went through the same graduate program and share similar tastes, and they've sat on many panels together, so over the years they've converged on shared views about what a strong hire looks like. When one of them misjudges a candidate — say, by overweighting enthusiasm and polish — it seems plausible the others make the same mistake for the same underlying reason. The company has also had cases where the panel was unanimous and confident, yet the hire turned out badly, which my friend dismisses as bad luck.

I'd appreciate your assessment of the situation. Is my friend's appeal to the mathematical result sound as applied to her committee, or is there a hidden assumption her setup fails to satisfy? If the assumption fails, what follows — is majority voting among partially like-minded judges worthless, still an improvement over a single judge, or does it depend on quantitative details? And is there anything the committee could change in how it elicits and aggregates the individual judgments that would get closer to whatever the result actually requires? I'm not looking for platitudes about the value of diversity; I want to understand precisely where the mathematics stops applying, and what, if anything, can be done about that.
