I am a novelist three years into a speculative novel about a far-future biosphere in which engineered lineages of animals and people drift apart over ten thousand generations, and I have discovered that I cannot keep my own story straight about what a species is. I keep making snap judgments in the margins, and I want to know whether those judgments add up to a coherent position or whether I am a lumper and a splitter at once. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of biology who has spent serious time on the species problem and its competing approaches — interbreeding-based accounts, phylogenetic lineage accounts, morphological and ecological ones — and who will not flatter me. Before you answer, please take a moment to brainstorm freely and consider several candidate principles; do not settle on the first one that fits most of the cases.

Here are my verdicts on cases, some drawn from my draft chapters:

1. Lions and tigers produce ligers in captivity, nearly all sterile. I count lions and tigers as distinct species without hesitation.
2. Domestic dogs and wolves interbreed freely and produce fertile offspring, yet I want to write them as separate species. The dog is a character in my novel in a way the wolf is not, and taxonomically it feels right.
3. A ring species circles a mountain range: each neighboring population interbreeds with the next, but the populations where the ring closes do not interbreed at all. My verdict: it is one species. I feel no temptation to split it, even though the end populations satisfy my own criterion for separation.
4. Coyotes, wolves, and domestic dogs now form a hybrid swarm across much of North America, with fertile coywolves breeding in the wild. My verdict: this is not one new species; it is a mess — and I do not know what makes a mess different from a species.
5. In my novel, an engineered line of humans becomes reproductively isolated from the baseline population within a few centuries — no viable offspring at all — while remaining visually and culturally almost indistinguishable, and members of the two groups marry and raise adopted children throughout the book. My verdict: they are the same species. My editor, who has read more biology than I have, says this is indefensible.
6. I am confident Tyrannosaurus rex was a species, though no interbreeding test could ever apply to it.
7. Bdelloid rotifers have gone without sex for tens of millions of years; each asexual line is as different from its neighbors as a named sexual species would be. My verdict: they are still one species, or at any rate not millions of species.
8. A lineage in my novel arises as a fertile hybrid of two engineered lines and breeds true thereafter, never back-crossing. My verdict: genuinely a new species, immediately, from the first hybrid.

Is there a single principle that captures all eight verdicts? If not, give me a small set of candidates and be precise about what each one says, which of my cases motivates each clause, and how each handles the cases I have not yet considered — in particular, how I should treat Neanderthals and modern humans, whose lineages interbred to a limited degree, and how my judgment should change if my engineered humans in case 5 remained interfertile but stopped choosing each other as mates. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts you would overturn, in which direction, and what it would cost the novel. I am more attached to internal coherence than to any single judgment, but I want to know exactly what I am giving up.
