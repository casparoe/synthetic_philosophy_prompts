I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum since the mid-2010s, and over the past few years the slogan that we may be living at the "hinge of history" — that this is the moment when the long-run future is most up for grabs — has filtered down from the big longtermist books into ordinary meetup conversation, where it now does a surprising amount of argumentative work. Last week someone used it to move from a general concern for future generations to a concrete funding claim: that marginal donations to existential-risk work, especially AI safety, dominate marginal donations to global health, because what happens in the next few decades will shape trillions of future lives. Another attendee waved this off with the observation that every generation thinks it's the hinge of history — early Christians, Victorians, people who lived through the Cuban Missile Crisis all felt the same way. Neither of them seemed to notice that "hinge of history" can be precisified into several quite different theses, and that the funding argument only works on some of them.

Separate the slogan into its distinct precise claims, ordered from weakest to boldest, and give each a verdict: trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but doubtful, or straightforwardly false. As I understand the cluster, the candidate readings at least include: (1) the influence reading, that the present century has more causal influence over the long-run future than any other century; (2) the time-of-perils reading, that existential risk is unusually concentrated in this century rather than spread evenly across history; (3) the lock-in or plasticity reading, that values and institutions are about to ossify in a way that will persist across cosmic time, so there is a short window before the future stops being shapeable at all; and (4) what you might call the early-history reading, that we are simply so early in humanity's story that a small number of people now can affect an astronomically large future — a reading on which every early century is a hinge, making the claim far less distinctive than it sounds. If you see readings I've missed, add them to the menu.

Finally, tell me which reading the meetup arguer actually needed for his conclusion: it seems like "unusually high influence over the future" plus "the future is enormous" still doesn't yield "donate to AI safety rather than bednets" without some further claim about which risks are tractable at the margin and on what timescale. And assess whether the "every generation thinks this" reply engages any of the readings on the menu, or whether it is only a claim about human psychology — does it show the hinge belief is probably false, show only that we'd be biased to believe it whether or not it's true, or do neither? Lay the whole thing out as a structured list I can bring to the next meetup.
