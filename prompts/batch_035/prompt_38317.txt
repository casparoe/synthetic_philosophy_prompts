My sister has firm and, as far as I can tell, consistent opinions about which sad works she enjoys, and I have started keeping a list of her verdicts:

1. *Oedipus Rex* and *King Lear* are among her favorite works; she rewatches both by choice.
2. A formulaic dying-lover film reduced her to tears; she called it "emotional junk food," says she did not enjoy it, and resents it slightly for working on her anyway.
3. Sad songs about heartbreaks she has never come close to suffering: she plays them on repeat and loves them.
4. Live news coverage of a stranger's drowning: no enjoyment, obviously.
5. A close friend's first-person account of his mother's death, told over dinner: deeply moved, glad to have heard it, but she would not call the experience enjoyable.
6. The final scene of an opera sung in a language she does not speak, with only a plot summary in hand: moved and delighted.

Next week she is watching three things with me, and I want your predictions. (a) A beautifully made documentary about a 1907 mining disaster with no living survivors, which will leave her in tears. (b) A found-footage drama so convincing she will believe throughout the runtime that it is real, with the fiction revealed only in the closing credits. (c) An interactive film in which her own earlier choices doom a child character she has grown attached to.

I care less about the yes-or-no answers than about the rules she seems to be running. Sketch several candidate rules that could plausibly account for her record — for instance, one that turns on the events being fictional or otherwise safely distant, one on which what she enjoys is her own compassionate response rather than the sadness itself, one on which sadness is pleasant when welcomed and unpleasant when imposed. Check each candidate against all six verdicts, refining or discarding as needed, and say what each surviving rule predicts for (a) through (c). Pay attention to whether your rule makes her enjoyment depend on what she believes the case to be or on what it actually is; the found-footage drama should force the issue. Then give your best guesses with reasons, and finish by proposing the single further case that would best discriminate among the candidate rules — one where they genuinely diverge, if (a) through (c) do not already. You may draw on the classic philosophical treatments of the paradox of tragedy if you find them helpful, but name-dropping is not required; what matters is the fit between rules and verdicts.
