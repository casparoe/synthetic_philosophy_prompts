In our philosophy of science reading group, a fellow member keeps wielding the word underdetermination as though it named a single settled fact: the evidence, she says, underdetermines theory, and therefore the choice between rival scientific theories is never forced by data alone — it is, at bottom, a choice of convention, taste, or politics. Last week she used this to argue that since Copernicanism did not fit the observations appreciably better than a sufficiently elaborated Ptolemaic system, the eventual acceptance of heliocentrism must be explained by something other than evidence. I suspect the word is doing several different jobs at once and that only some of them would support her conclusion, but I lack the map to say which.

Please lay out, as an ordered list I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise theses that the slogan the evidence underdetermines theory could be expressing. I would like the menu to run from readings that are near-truisms to readings that are strong and controversial, and it should at least distinguish: (i) the minimal claim that at any moment the available evidence fails to single out exactly one theory; (ii) the holist, Duhem-Quine-style claim built on the idea that our statements face the tribunal of experience not individually but as a corporate body, so that any apparent refutation can be absorbed by revising auxiliary assumptions elsewhere; (iii) the stronger existential claim that for any theory and any body of evidence there exists a genuinely distinct rival that is empirically equivalent to it; and (iv) the strongest claim that theory choice is therefore never rationally constrained by evidence at all — that anything goes. Include any further readings you think belong between or beyond these. For each reading, say whether it is true or false, whether it is substantive or trivial, and — this is the part I most need — whether it actually entails anything like my colleague's conclusion about heliocentrism or about conventionality. In particular, I want to understand where the burden falls: does the holist reading even purport to describe a plurality of rival theories, or only the revisability of a single one, and is the step from there is an empirically equivalent rival somewhere in logical space to the rival is a live scientific option a legitimate inference or a slide? Note also where the Copernican example is doing independent work, since it may involve pragmatic virtues like simplicity that are not themselves evidence in her sense. Conclude by telling me which reading she is most plausibly invoking when she uses the word to end an argument, and what a good pressing question would be for each of the two or three most defensible readings.
