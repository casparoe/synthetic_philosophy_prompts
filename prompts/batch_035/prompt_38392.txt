I am a sixth-form student who runs my school's philosophy society, and I have been asked to lead a session for about fourteen pupils aged 15 to 17, in a single 45-minute lunchtime slot with only chairs and a whiteboard available. Design a structured activity for that session on the question of whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading someone — the intuition many people have that saying something false to someone is somehow worse than saying something true that you fully expect to make them believe something false.

The pupils should run into this intuition themselves rather than hear it from me first: no lecture up front, and no philosophical vocabulary until the debrief at the end. My idea is to exploit the gap between what a sentence literally says and what a listener naturally infers from it, so I would like the activity built around a strict rule that participants must never assert anything they believe to be false, whilst still being set communication tasks that can only be accomplished by getting someone to draw a false conclusion. Please include at least one comparison condition in which outright lying is permitted, so the group can notice whether the two practices feel different from the inside, both to perform and to be on the receiving end of, and so the debrief can ask whether the rule-bound participants actually behaved better or merely felt cleaner. I would also like some tasks where the misleading serves an obviously good end, such as protecting a friend's secret or sparing someone's feelings, and at least one where it serves the participants' own interest, so we can see whether the felt difference survives when the motive changes.

Please provide the rules and materials, the tasks themselves, a timeline that fits the slot, a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between assertion and implicature, and an honest note on which part of the philosophical question the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, whatever the difference consists in for long-run trust or for the liar's own character — and which I will simply have to supply in discussion. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
