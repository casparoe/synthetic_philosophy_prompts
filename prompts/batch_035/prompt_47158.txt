I work on self-locating belief, and I have an argument that I cannot refute but also cannot believe. It starts from a verdict about a variant of the Sleeping Beauty problem that seems to me obviously correct, and it ends with what looks like armchair cosmology. I would like to know which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

The setup, stretched to make the numbers vivid: Beauty is put to sleep; a fair coin is tossed; if it lands heads she is awakened once, if tails she is awakened a thousand times; her memory of each awakening is erased, the room is identical on every occasion, and nothing in what she experiences reveals which awakening it is.

(1) When Beauty finds herself awake in the room, her credence that the coin landed heads should be about 1/1001. Conditional on tails, there are a thousand times as many subjectively indistinguishable centered possibilities that her evidence could be located at, and nothing she knows favors any one of them over any other, so indifference among them puts almost all the weight on tails. This is the standard thirding reasoning with the arithmetic made conspicuous, and to me the verdict seems right.

(2) What does the work in (1) is just the counting: a hypothesis earns more credence the more maximally specific, subjectively indistinguishable possibilities it supplies that are consistent with the evidence. The coin entered only by setting the prior odds; after that, the argument consulted nothing but how many awakenings each branch contains. It did not consult the fact that there was a laboratory, or a protocol, or that the thousand awakenings are all episodes of one person rather than experiences of a thousand people.

(3) The reasoning therefore transfers to any case in which rival hypotheses differ only in how many observers there are in one's epistemic situation — including cases where the rival hypotheses concern different possible worlds, or different regions of the actual world, and where the observers are distinct people rather than one amnesiac revisited.

(4) Cosmologists sometimes consider two theories that fit all current data equally well and are comparable in simplicity, but which differ in how many observers exist: on one, the universe is finite; on the other, it belongs to an ensemble so vast that enormously more observers are, at this moment, in exactly my evidential situation — reading a passage like this one at a desk like this one. By (2) and (3), I should already assign the second theory a credence overwhelmingly greater than the first, before any test that could distinguish them has been run.

(5) And the principle iterates. For any empirically open question, I should favor whichever answer holds on the theory that multiplies observers, and my confidence should grow without bound with the number of observers the theory posits, however gerrymandered the theory that does the positing. Before the data arrive, I can be nearly certain of the observer-rich answer.

This is where I balk. A philosopher who announces, ahead of any experiment and on no astronomical grounds, that the universe is almost certainly one of an ensemble — on the ground that if it were, more people would be having this thought — does not seem to me to be responding to evidence. He seems to be presuming on his own existence, and the principle that licenses it appears to prove too much. Yet I cannot locate the false step, and every denial I have tried carries a price:

If (2) is false, then something in the Beauty case besides counting must generate the verdict in (1), and I cannot find a candidate that does not wreck (1). I have tried: the awakenings all occur within one possible world (but the ensemble theory can place all of its observers in a single world); the awakenings belong to one subject (but the usual defense of (1) already runs through cases where Beauty is copied rather than woken, and is supposed to survive that); there is a chancy device with known frequencies (but the frequencies did no work beyond fixing the prior, and I could replace the coin with any process that leaves the prior odds unchanged).

If (3) is false, I need a principled boundary between uncertainty about where I am within a world and uncertainty about how large the world is, and I do not know how to draw one that does not look ad hoc.

If (1) is false, I am a halfer, and in my stretched case Beauty's credence in heads stays at 1/2 no matter how many awakenings tails adds — a million, a trillion — so that the number of future awakenings is evidentially inert; and the halfer must also accept, at least on the usual conditioning, that when Beauty is told on an awakening that it is Monday, her credence in heads rises to two-thirds, drawing information about the coin out of a calendar remark that appears to carry none.

For what it is worth: I would most like to keep (1), and I suspect the slippage is somewhere between (2) and (4), but I cannot name it. Please tell me which step to give up, explain why that step looked innocent, and state plainly what the view that keeps the rest must then say — including what it must say about the case that originally motivated it.
