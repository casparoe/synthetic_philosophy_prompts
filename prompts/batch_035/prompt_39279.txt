I'm a PhD student in comparative literature, writing a dissertation chapter on twentieth-century fiction and the philosophy of time, and I want to avoid the comparison everyone reaches for. When Slaughterhouse-Five is taught, the Tralfamadorians are read as straightforward eternalists: they see all moments at once, they hold that no moment can be changed, and their shrug at death — the dead person is still alive in plenty of other moments — is taken as a dramatization of the block universe. What I want instead is to read the novel through Borges's views on time, and I suspect the fit is both tighter and stranger than the eternalist reading, because in "A New Refutation of Time" Borges also denies the reality of time, but by a route that ends somewhere very different. His argument runs through Berkeley and Hume: if only what is perceived exists, and if the self is a bundle rather than a persisting subject, then no moment needs any other, succession is superfluous, and time is an illusion. But the conclusion he draws is not that all moments are equally real; it is that the individual who would pass through them is refuted along with time, and the essay closes with the rueful admission that the world's reality is untouched and that the arguer is still stuck being Borges, made of the very time he has just dissolved.

So read the novel through that Borges rather than through the block universe. Specifically: (1) Where does the novel dramatize the eternalist version, and where would Borges's route deliver a different verdict — is Billy's serenity available to a self that has been dissolved along with time, or only to a self that persists through it? (2) The Tralfamadorians and Borges both start from the self-sufficiency of moments — no moment needs another — yet they draw opposite conclusions about the self. Does the novel notice this, or does Vonnegut borrow the premise without paying its cost? (3) The Tralfamadorian doctrine works in the novel as a technology of looking away: the advice to concentrate on the pleasant moments, the answer to every why that the moment is simply structured as it is. Borges insists his refutation yields no such anesthetic. Do the two men converge on fatalism as a psychological necessity, or do they split precisely there? (4) Where does the novel resist the reading — the narrator's flat insistence near the opening that he really was there, the anger that keeps surfacing, the serenity prayer installed as the epigraph, the very few moments when Billy actually weeps — and does that resistance show Vonnegut quarreling not with time itself but with the doctrine about time? Finally, be candid about where my reading breaks down, tell me which scenes the chapter should center on, and say whether the novel manages to add something to Borges's debate about time that his essays cannot.
