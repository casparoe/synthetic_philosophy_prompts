so I keep TA-ing intro ethics, and every year when we hit the Euthyphro some student asks the thing I can never answer cleanly: ok, maybe piety isn't pious because the gods love it, but then what IS the independent standard doing, and does every domain have one? lately I've been cataloguing my gut verdicts on Euthyphro-shaped questions across different areas, and I want to know whether there's a single principle underneath them or whether I'm just conflating a bunch of unrelated things. here are the verdicts:

1. if God commanded torturing children for fun, it would STILL be wrong — divine command doesn't constitute moral truth, there's an independent standard God is (at best) tracking.
2. whether some contract is legally valid is, I think, fully constituted by what the legislature and courts say. there's no independent standard of legality the lawmakers could be getting right or wrong. an unjust law is still a law, full stop.
3. ...except I also have the intuition that a sufficiently monstrous legal system — say a state whose constitution openly declares one ethnic group has no rights at all — produces things that are in some sense not really laws. I hate that I think this given #2, but there it is.
4. chess: the knight moves in an L because the rules say so. there's no independent fact of the matter the rules are tracking — en passant got added centuries into the game's existence and nobody thinks players before that were playing "wrong chess."
5. word meaning: 'elm' means elm purely because of how the community uses it. a community that used it for beeches would thereby make it mean beech. no independent standard, and no authority either — it's constitutive all the way down.
6. math: the opposite. if every mathematician alive agreed tomorrow that the continuum hypothesis is true, that agreement wouldn't make it true. mathematicians track, they don't constitute.
7. money: a banknote is money purely because it's treated as money, and apparently you don't even need an authority — bitcoin is money and no one issued it.
8. grammaticality: 'he don't know' is grammatical in certain dialects because their speakers say it is, even though no academy rules on it. constitutive, like meaning.
9. aesthetic merit: if every critic on earth declared some painting great and I just couldn't see it, I'd feel pressure to defer, but the way I'd feel pressure is that they're perceiving something real I'm missing — not that their consensus is what makes it great. so: independent standard, unlike grammar, even though both involve communal response.
10. back to law: precedent. when a court extends an old doctrine to a new kind of case, I have it that they're discovering what the law already required — even though on my #2 story, law is constituted by institutional decisions, and this decision wasn't made until now.

my tentative principle is something like: a domain has an independent standard iff disagreement in it can be genuine — i.e. two communities can both follow their own rules and one still be getting it wrong. but I think that principle gets grammar wrong (two dialects aren't in "genuine disagreement"), and I can't tell if it saves #3 or just buries the problem.

so: is there a principle that captures most of this? which of my verdicts have to go — I already suspect #3 and maybe #10, and possibly #9 if the critic thing is really just sociology with extra steps? state it precisely, tell me which cases motivate which clauses, and tell me what the principle predicts about a case I haven't listed: is social class like that? is being 'classy' or 'trashy' constituted by elite opinion (money-style), tracked by it (aesthetics-style), or something else entirely? asking because I genuinely can't tell which side my own reactions put it on.
