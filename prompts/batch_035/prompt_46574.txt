I've been recording my verdicts in cases from the paradox-of-deontology literature, hoping they'd add up to a principle, and I've failed, so I'm asking you to do the extracting. Here are the cases and what I believe about them.

(1) Strategic bomber: a pilot destroys an enemy munitions plant, foreseeing but not intending that some civilians in the adjacent housing will die — permissible.
(2) Terror bomber: a pilot bombs the civilians themselves, hoping that terror shortens the war and saves more lives than are lost — impermissible, even when the arithmetic favors it.
(3) Diverting a runaway trolley from five onto one trapped on the side track — permissible.
(4) Pushing a bystander off a footbridge to stop the same trolley — impermissible.
(5) Shooting a rampaging attacker who is about to kill five and can only be stopped by lethal force — permissible.
(6) A sniper kills a tyrant who is about to launch a massacre of thousands — permissible, and I'd say permissible even if the tyrant is not himself currently pulling any trigger, so long as the massacre is imminent and otherwise unstoppable.
(7) Killing an innocent bystander as the physical means of stopping that same tyrant — say, shoving him off a ledge onto the tyrant — impermissible.
(8) A case I invented and can't stop chewing on: a police unit can stop a mass murderer's imminent killing spree only by framing and executing an innocent man, fabricating evidence that convinces the murderer the spree has been avenged and will only bring retribution on his family. The innocent man is not harmed physically as a means of stopping the spree; he is wronged as a means. My verdict: impermissible, and yet I notice it feels closer to the line than (7), which makes me suspect something other than means-based harm is doing work in my verdicts.
(9) The chain case: a cartel rule holds that whoever kills one of ours, we kill ten. A defector is about to kill one cartel member, and the only way I can stop him is to kill a different cartel member first, triggering my own execution under the same rule and thereby deterring the defector (he wants to live). I feel pulled toward permissible here in a way I don't in (7), and I don't know what distinguishes them.

My tentative principle was something like: constraints against harming the innocent don't yield to numbers, but they are sensitive to whether what is being prevented is other violations of the same constraint — preventing a murder can license a killing that preventing a death from disease could never license. But I can see this proving too much. A rule permitting constraint violations whenever they prevent more violations of that same constraint seems to license a security state that executes a few innocents on fabricated charges whenever deterrence math favors it, and it seems to make deontology self-undermining: if the ground of the constraint is that violations are especially bad, a permission to violate whenever that reduces total violations looks like the system guaranteeing what it forbids, on the installment plan.

So, the questions: Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these nine verdicts, stated precisely enough that it delivers a determinate answer on each? What exactly distinguishes (5) and (6) from (7), and does the distinction survive (8) and (9)? Does my tentative principle, repaired, give verdicts I'd accept on cases I haven't listed — for instance, the loop case where diverting a trolley works only because it kills the one person on the side track (his body stops it from looping back around); a case where the bystander in (7) freely and informedly consents to being shoved; or a probabilistic choice between certainly killing one innocent and imposing a one-in-five chance of death on each of five innocents to stop the tyrant? And be honest about which of my verdicts cannot all be saved: if the best principle overturns one, name it, explain what looked attractive about the verdict — I suspect some of my intuitions track a supposed moral difference between using someone's body and using someone's social standing or trust, and I'd like to know whether that difference is real or a familiar distinction under a new description — and tell me what I'm conceding if I give it up. I'd rather keep (2) and (7) than (6) and (9), but I want to know the price either way.
