I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group that's spent most of the past year on the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response to it, and I've hit a wall that feels structural rather than just "the problem is hard." On one side we have the naive realist / disjunctivist line: in a genuine veridical case you're literally aware of the worldly object itself as part of your conscious episode, so veridical seeing and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, and the "highest common factor" that the classic argument says they share simply doesn't exist. On the other side the intentionalist or conjunctivist line: veridical and hallucinatory cases share everything mentally relevant and differ only in whether the world cooperates, and naive realism is just refusing to accept the lesson of the illusion cases. We've read the classic stuff, Austin included, and at this point everyone in the group can state both sides cold and run all the standard moves — the screening-off thought about causation, the common-kind assumption, the point about hallucination being defined by its intrinsic indistinguishability from perception. And still nobody in the group has budged an inch in months, including two of us who switched sides and switched back.

My working hypothesis, which the group has more or less laughed off, is that this isn't one disagreement at all but a bundle: maybe it's partly verbal, a fight over who owns the phrase "mental state"; partly a real methodological dispute about what a theory of perception is supposed to explain (why hallucination deceives you vs. how perception puts you in touch with the world — the sides each think they're winning and they're winning different games); and partly one bedrock intuition — about whether introspective indistinguishability fixes mental sameness — that neither side can argue for without begging the question. But I honestly can't tell whether that's right or whether it's a genuine metaphysical fight that's just extremely hard, and whether the famous "silence of the disjunctivist" about hallucination is a real explanatory gap or just a refusal to play the conjunctivist's game.

So give me your diagnosis, not a survey. Exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion: identify the distinct sub-disagreements you think are actually bundled into this standoff (verbal ones, methodological ones, bedrock-intuition ones), say which of them is doing the real work in keeping it alive, and in your fifth point tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction somebody should draw, a question somebody should stop asking — or else argue that no progress should be expected and why.
