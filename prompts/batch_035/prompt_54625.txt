I'm a freelance literary translator, mostly French and German prose and poetry into English, and for most of my career I treated Hofstadter's worries about machine translation as an interesting sideline rather than something that concerned me. That changed three weeks ago: my largest publisher informed its translators that, beginning next year, all manuscripts will be pre-translated by a neural MT system, with human translators retained only to post-edit the output at a fraction of our current rates. I objected in the meeting, and my objections were essentially Hofstadter's — that fluent output on ordinary prose demonstrates almost nothing about whether a system understands what it is translating, and that the real test is the kind of constrained literary translation he built his book about translation around, where the task is to carry meter, wordplay, register, and a certain lightness across languages rather than to decode the propositional content. To my surprise, the pushback landed well enough that the publisher's parent company, which runs the MT research group, has invited me to give a guest lecture to that group's weekly seminar: thirty minutes of talk followed by twenty minutes of questions.

The audience is about fifteen people: graduate students and research scientists who build large-scale MT and language-model systems, plus two senior professors who have spent their careers on statistical and neural translation. These are people who think in terms of training distributions, human-evaluation metrics, context windows, few-shot prompting, and scaling curves, and who are, by default, convinced that the remaining gaps are engineering problems that scale and more data will close. Some will know that Hofstadter himself spent decades building AI models of analogy-making, so I expect at least one of them to point out that the man whose banner I'm carrying was himself an AI optimist. I want to concede up front that post-editing machine output for straightforward commercial prose is often perfectly fine — my claim is about depth, not average output quality — but I need to be ready for everything else. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way an MT researcher would phrase it (invoking COMET scores, emergent capabilities, multimodal grounding, whatever you think they'll reach for), each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — for me that's the one about whether my position is anything more than a threatened craft defending its rents, and what evidence could actually get me to concede the point. Please write the prep sheet in American English.
