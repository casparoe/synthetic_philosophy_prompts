As a novelist, I have staked a decade's work on Francis Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis, and I want to know precisely what would count against it.

Let me state the wager as I hold it, because the thesis is nearly always caricatured. It is not the claim that events stop, that newspapers become boring, or that conflict ceases. It is the claim that history, in the Hegelian sense, is a single directional contest among principles of political legitimacy, that this contest has a discoverable endpoint, and that liberal democracy coupled with market capitalism is the terminal arrival — not because it is perfect, but because it has no principled rival, only deviations and relapses. The engine is thymos, the desire for recognition: isothymia, the wish to be recognised as an equal, is satisfied by universal and equal recognition, while megalothymia, the craving to be recognised as superior, is the recalcitrant residue, the source of the last man's restlessness and of Fukuyama's own unease about the endpoint. On this reading, Kojève's post-historical animal — well fed, comfortable, occupied with snobbery and tourism and private life — is a novelist's subject par excellence, and that is precisely what I have written: small contemporary novels of recognition and its ironies, in which nothing of political principle can any longer be at stake, because the wager is that only the private registers remain open.

Now tell me what would falsify the wager. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest account of which of them Fukuyama's defenders would themselves concede as counting against the thesis and which they would dismiss as events within its terms. China's authoritarian capitalism — is that a principled rival or a deviation? Democratic backsliding in places long counted as consolidated? Theological politics, which Fukuyama already had to argue around? A long durable stagnation in which liberal institutions formally persist but generate no recognition anyone believes in? I am particularly interested in whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability: whether every reversal can be redescribed as a relapse into pre-historical struggle, every bad decade as a detour on the same road, so that nothing short of an actual successor principle to liberal democracy could ever count — and, if so, whether that insulation is a defect in a thesis of this kind or simply what a claim about directionality must look like.

And attend to the wager from within my craft, because I suspect my books are the thesis's auxiliary evidence in a way I have not examined. If the large historical canvas were to become narratively necessary again — if the novel were to demand war, revolution, and the collision of principles rather than divorces, careers, and reputations — would that be evidence of anything, or merely a change of fashion, or my own failing nerve? Please answer in British English.
