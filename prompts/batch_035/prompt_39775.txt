I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and for the last three years I've run a monthly discussion meetup in the back room of a public library — twelve to fourteen people, ninety minutes per session, mostly programmers, one astrophysics grad student, a couple of curious spouses. The group is comfortable with Bayes, has been through the anthropics discussions, and has absorbed the usual slogans, but we've never done the thermodynamic arrow of time, and I want to do it properly next month.

Here is the problem. Everyone in the group can recite that entropy explains why you can remember yesterday but not tomorrow. Almost nobody has felt the structural point underneath that slogan: the microscopic dynamics are time-symmetric, so the arrow has to live somewhere else — in a boundary condition about the early universe — and, more disturbingly, our evidence for that boundary condition is itself made of records and memories, which only work because of it. The claim is self-supporting in a way that should bother them once they see it. I could lecture at this for ninety minutes and they would nod along and retain the slogan.

Take on the role of a physicist who has taught undergraduate statistical mechanics and has a track record of designing participatory demonstrations — the kind who believes an idea has not been taught until the audience has been surprised by it. Think carefully before committing: brainstorm two or three candidate framings in a few sentences each, say why you are rejecting all but one, and only then write up the full design.

The design itself should meet these constraints:

1. No lecture up front, and no naming of the Past Hypothesis, typicality, or anything else from the literature until the debrief. The group should collide with the puzzle before it is labeled.
2. Concrete rules, materials, and timings that I could run from your reply alone. I have decks of cards, poker chips, a whiteboard, a phone tripod for photographing states, and access to a print shop.
3. A payoff scheme in points or chips, no real money. Somewhere in the design, participants should be shown only the current coarse-grained state of a system and have to bet on its past versus its future — that contrast is where I expect the asymmetry of inference to hit them, and I want them to discover that guessing the past requires a premise they never actually observed.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal pieces: time-reversal symmetry of the microdynamics, macrostates as coarse-grainings, why the low-entropy boundary condition is doing all the work, and why records and memories inherit the arrow rather than creating it.
5. A note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including the inevitable participant who computes exact card probabilities and the one who wants to argue about Boltzmann brains instead — and how I should handle each.
6. An honest section on which features of the real idea the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief. I already suspect card-shuffling, being a dissipative, human-driven process, has no analog of phase-space volume preservation, and that fifty-two cards make the statistics far chunkier than any real system — but tell me everything else the simulation quietly fakes.

Finally, a stretch question: someone in my group will eventually ask whether the entire past could be a fluctuation, given all of the above. Include a short scripted way to open that up in the last ten minutes without letting it eat the session.
