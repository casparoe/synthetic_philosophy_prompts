i teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of religion — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and next week we hit Molinism and middle knowledge. The problem is that on paper it all sounds like scholastic bookkeeping, so i want a classroom game that makes them feel the problem from the creator's side, not the debater's side. The thing they should run into through the rules, before anyone names it: to actualize a world containing free creatures, the creator apparently needs to already know, for every possible setup of circumstances, what each free creature WOULD freely do in that setup — but nothing seems to make those claims true or false before the creatures exist and choose. They should discover that the hard way, not be told it up front.

Roughly what i have in mind (improve freely): one student, or a small "creator committee," has to pick a world to actualize from a menu of options i hand them. The menu describes circumstances — who exists, what resources there are, who meets whom, what temptations are in play — but never choices. Everyone else plays the creatures. Creation only "succeeds" if the creator's written description of the resulting world, choices included, matches what the creatures actually go on to do; if anything mismatches, we rule that the intended world couldnt be created, and the creator is stuck with the messy unplanned world the creatures in fact produce. At least one round should have creatures deciding in the moment, on a private card, after the creator has already committed in writing; and maybe a contrasting round where creatures get to secretly pre-commit, so the group can see how that changes what the creator can know. Payoffs can be silly points, definitely no real money. If they start negotiating with the creator mid-game ("would you betray me if i put you in this spot?"), dont stop it — i actually want to see what they think settles those answers.

Afterwards i need a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the three moments of knowledge (natural, middle, free), names Molina only at the end, and confronts the grounding objection head on: in our game, where exactly did the "true counterfactuals of freedom" come from, and was that source legitimate or did we smuggle it in? Also give me your honest list of ways this goes sideways on a first run (creatures conspiring purely to foil the creator, someone refusing to choose so the creator cant write anything true, a creature pre-committing in their head and announcing it to make the creators life easy) and how to handle each one. And be straight with me about which part of the actual doctrine the game cannot reproduce and ill have to supply by talking — the game happens in time, for one thing, and the creator in my classroom is a student with a sheet of paper.
