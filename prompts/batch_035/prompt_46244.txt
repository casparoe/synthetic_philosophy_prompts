I'm four years into a novel and it's finally working: three braided storylines, a fishing family outside Calcutta in 1943, a smallholder in the west of Ireland in the late 1840s, and a production-team accountant in Henan through 1959-61. Same structure in each third, deliberately: the rains fail or the harvest goes wrong somewhere, but there's still food moving through each place — boats of grain, exports, requisitions — and the ones who starve are the ones who lost their claim on it. The fisherman who had to sell his nets. The tenant whose crop went to the rent. The village that reported fantasy yields and then had its real grain carted off on the strength of the fiction.

Somewhere in year two a friend pressed the Sen line of argument on me, that famines are about entitlements collapsing rather than food disappearing, and that they're political events you can pin on people rather than natural disasters you blame on weather, and honestly I recieved it like a gift. It's the novel's spine now. The trouble is I never stress-tested it, I just liked the shape of it. So: what would actually count against it? I want the full list, ranked — what would have to be true for the entitlement view to fail, which of those things are plausibly true, and which a Sen-style defender would concede as a real problem versus wave off as a case the theory already handles.

A few things I keep chewing over and want addressed head on. Ireland had MPs at Westminster and a (mostly) free press, and the famine happened anyway — is that a genuine counterexample to the claim that functioning democracies with a free press don't let famines happen, or is there a wrinkle that saves it? The Great Leap famine killed people at a scale the other two don't approach, and yet the starving mostly wasn't intended — it was catastrophic recklessness plus a system that punished telling the truth — so does calling it a crime, rather than an institutional failure or something like manslaughter, smuggle in a theory of collective responsibility I can't actually cash out, and if so whose hands am I putting blood on? And what about sieges and blockades, where the food really does run out for everyone — Leningrad, say — do those count as famines for these purposes, and if they do, doesn't the food-availability story get some of its footing back?

Last thing, the novelist's question, which I care about as much as the philosophy one: fiction can live with moral mess in a way an essay can't. Tell me whether the novel actually needs the thesis to be true, or whether it just needs a protagonist who believes it and is wrong in ways the reader can see. I'd rather settle that now than in the reviews.
