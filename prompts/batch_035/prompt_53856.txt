My flatmate is doing a seminar on Hume's Of the Standard of Taste and he's convinced the essay secretly refutes itself, and honestly I can't find the broken step. He lays it out like this:

1. Hume's whole essay is supposed to answer the relativist. The relativist says beauty is just in the sentiment of the spectator, so there's no disputing about taste. Hume's key move is the joint verdict thing: the same Homer who was admired in Athens and Rome thousands of years ago is still admired in Paris and London, while the authors who were neglected back then stay neglected. He also throws in that one about whoever would assert that an epic poet of the last century is equal to Milton can't be taken seriously. From this Hume concludes there's some quality in the works themselves that's the source of the durable verdicts.
2. If durable cross-century verdicts have a cause in the works, then "this work is beautiful" attributes a real property of the work, and Hume wins against the relativist.
3. But watch what the property turns out to be. It's whatever feature of the work makes normal human sentiment respond in a certain way. It's exactly the same kind of property as being funny: whether a joke is funny is fixed by whether people with functioning ears laugh, and if the same joke had been getting laughs across every culture for three thousand years, we'd say the same thing about it that Hume says about Milton.
4. "Funny" is not objective in the sense the relativist was denying. Nobody thinks the durability of laughter underwrites a fact of the matter in the way facts about, say, the boiling point of water are. If comedy fans in every century prefer some jester to some other jester, that settles what's funnier, but it's still "just sentiment" all the way down.
5. So the property Hume has bought with the joint verdict argument is only response-dependent objectivity, and if that counted as objective then the relativist concedes nothing, since relativists are fine with "objectivity" that's just patterns in sentiment persisting over time.
6. Therefore either the joint-verdict facts don't hold (Milton's durability is just fashion that happened to stick), or they hold but are metaphysically weightless, and Hume's standard is a standard in name only.

He swears the conclusion is that the essay just collapses into the relativism it was supposed to head off. I have the feeling there's an equivocation somewhere between step 4 and step 6, something about whose sentiment is doing the work, because Hume makes a big deal in that essay about the judges needing practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and all the rest of it, and my flatmate's version just uses "people respond" as if any old response counts. But I can't quite make the charge stick. Which step would you reject, and what do you have to pay for rejecting it? For what it's worth I'd be sad to give up step 2, but I care more about knowing whether step 4 and 5 are sneaking in a weaker sense of "objective" than the one the relativist actually denied.
