One practice I find philosophically puzzling is the institutional life of modern cryptography, and I would like an analysis of what it presupposes. Consider the following, all of which is common ground in the field. Government standards bodies publish encryption standards whose security rests on unproved mathematical conjectures: that factoring large integers is intractable in practice, or that certain lattice problems admit no efficient algorithm. Security agencies have set binding timetables for migrating public infrastructure to new schemes on the strength of two further conjectures at once — that large-scale quantum computers will eventually be built, and that the replacement lattice problems will withstand classical attack indefinitely. Meanwhile, the same community runs public contests with cash prizes for breaking its own assumptions, and celebrates each newly factored challenge number as progress. And the formal apparatus is telling: a modern security proof is typically a reduction — a rigorous conditional showing that breaking the scheme would solve the underlying hard problem — so the practice demands mathematical rigour for the conditional while accepting conjecture for the antecedent, and then manages that conjecture socially, through open publication and decades of failed cryptanalysis, rather than mathematically.

Here is what I want you to do. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this practice. Does it presuppose platonism about arithmetic — that it is already determinately true or false, prior to anyone's proof, that an efficient factoring algorithm exists — or could it survive on a more deflationary view? Does the reliance on 'hardness' treat computational complexity classes as something like natural kinds, with polynomial-time feasibility functioning as a surrogate for a law of nature, so that the practice quietly modalises a mathematical question into a physical one? Is the confidence in unproved hardness best read as induction from a long history of failure, as something a priori about the structure of the problems, or as neither — a purely pragmatic commitment that happens to be manageable by the community's social procedures? Address the asymmetry directly: the migration timetables bet that one technological contingency (scalable quantum computers) will obtain while another (a clever classical algorithm for the lattice problems) will not, so what view of technological possibility and of our ability to foresee our own future mathematics does that wager presuppose? And say whether the apparent inconsistency is real: a community that demands proofs of correctness for algorithms while basing its entire enterprise on conjectures, and that simultaneously treats hardness as bedrock and as something expected to erode at the edges.

Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with, and identify where it is underdetermined — several rival philosophies of mathematics could each license it, and I want to know which parts of the practice, if any, would discriminate between them. Then close with two counterfactuals: what, if anything, in the practice would actually change if a proof of P ≠ NP arrived tomorrow (would it be any real news to the cryptographers, and if not, what does that show about what their commitment was actually to?), and what the practice reveals about itself in the scenario where the conjectures are simply never settled either way, so that cryptography goes on indefinitely resting on the difference between what is provable and what is merely unhappened.
