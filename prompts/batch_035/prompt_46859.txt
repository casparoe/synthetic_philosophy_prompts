Consider the family of paradoxes comprising the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. In the lottery case, a fair million-ticket lottery supports credence 0.999999 in each proposition of the form "ticket n will lose," yet the conjunction of all of them contradicts the certain proposition that some ticket wins. In the preface case, an author has carefully checked each chapter of her book and is justified in believing each chapter is error-free, yet she is also justified in believing—on inductive grounds about books like hers—that at least one chapter contains an error. These cases are standardly taken to threaten a triad of principles: (A) sufficiently high credence in a proposition suffices for justified outright belief in it; (B) justified outright belief is closed under conjunction, so that justified belief in each of p1 through pn entails justified belief in their conjunction; and (C) one is never justified in believing an outright contradiction.

I want your credence that the correct resolution of this family of paradoxes abandons principle (A)—that is, that high credence, however high, does not by itself amount to justified outright belief—rather than abandoning (B), abandoning (C) in some restricted way, or declaring that no single principled resolution is available. Please do not stop at a bare number. Decompose your estimate into sub-questions you take to be genuinely probabilistic, which should include at least the following, plus any others you think belong: (i) the probability that there is a stable distinction between outright belief and mere high credence that does not itself collapse into a sorites of thresholds; (ii) the probability that conjunction closure for justified belief can be given up coherently without dismantling too much of ordinary reasoning, as Kyburg's lottery-driven dissent from closure attempts to do; (iii) the probability that the lottery case and the preface case have a unified solution at all, given that the preface author's individual beliefs are based on specific evidence rather than bare odds; and (iv) the probability that a knowledge-based or safety-based restriction on (A)—say, requiring that belief be sensitive or safe in the way lottery beliefs are not—can exclude the paradoxical cases without excluding too many ordinary ones. Assign a rough probability and a one-line justification to each term, then show how they combine into your final credence.

To sharpen the last point, consider a variant I will call the reviewer's paradox. A journal referee independently verifies each of 500 distinct factual claims in a manuscript, ending with credence 0.995 in each individual claim, but she also knows that manuscripts making 500 claims of this kind almost always contain at least one false claim. She is thus inclined both to accept the manuscript's claim-set wholesale and to assert that at least one claim in it is false. Unlike the lottery case, her confidence in each claim does not derive from a single probabilistic mechanism, and unlike the preface case, the defeating evidence is statistical rather than self-deprecating. In your decomposition, indicate whether this case, as described, raises or lowers the probability that (A) is the principle that must go, and why.

Finally, tell me which single term in your decomposition the final credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of case would move that term the most. I am especially interested in whether you think the instability of threshold views—the fact that "justified belief" seems to tolerate marginal drops in credence one at a time, yet not all at once—is a decisive cost, or merely a familiar and tolerable vagueness.
