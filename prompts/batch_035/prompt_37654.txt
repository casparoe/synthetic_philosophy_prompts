For the past two years, my political philosophy seminar has hosted two visiting lecturers who argue past each other every session: one a committed telic egalitarian, who holds that inequality is intrinsically bad whenever some are worse off than others, regardless of how the inequality arose or what anyone's absolute level is; the other a committed prioritarian, who holds that benefits to the worse off carry greater moral weight, but that equality has no intrinsic value at all. Their standing flashpoint is the leveling-down objection: in a two-person society where one person is blind and the other sighted, telic egalitarianism seems to imply that a world in which neither is cured is, in one respect, better than a world in which the blind person is cured, since the former world is more equal. The prioritarian insists that no outcome can be even pro tanto better if it is worse for everyone or better for no one; the egalitarian replies that some values are impersonal and that "better in one respect" need not mean "better all things considered."

Please draft the joint statement these two should be able to sign after a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should include the formal ground they share — e.g., that a costless transfer from a better-off person to a worse-off person is an improvement; that both reject a view on which only the sum of well-being matters; and that the leveling-down objection, whatever its ultimate force, at least puts the burden on the egalitarian to explain what kind of value inequality's absence is. But go beyond the obvious: they should be able to agree on more than either of them currently realizes, including on which versions of egalitarianism are untouched by the objection and what that shows about the dialectic.

2. A disagreements table in which each remaining disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the egalitarian would accept as a fair statement of her view and once in wording the prioritarian would accept as a fair statement of his, with a note on which disagreements are substantive rather than verbal. Pay particular attention to the "in one respect" hedge: if the egalitarian's claim is only that leveling down makes an outcome better in an inequality-respecting way that never gives anyone a reason to bring it about, say plainly whether the two disputants should treat that as a substantive disagreement or as a dispute about how to use the word "better."

3. A cruxes section. Two candidates they should address explicitly: (a) whether any form of impersonal or non-person-affecting value is coherent, or whether all betterness must be betterness for someone; and (b) the continuity worry — as the prioritarian's weighting of the worse off is made steeper, her verdicts approach the egalitarian's, including in cases where a vanishingly small benefit to the very worst off must be purchased at an enormous cost in total well-being. For each crux, describe at least one case or argument both parties would accept as genuinely bearing on it — e.g., a fixed menu of two- and three-person distributions with stated well-being levels, so both must commit in advance — and record each party's prediction about how they would respond to it and what, if anything, would lead them to abandon or revise their position. Where a crux is such that no case could bear on it because the two sides would simply interpret the case differently, say so, since that itself is an important discovery.

Do not attempt to adjudicate who is right; the deliverable is a document both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than resolved.
