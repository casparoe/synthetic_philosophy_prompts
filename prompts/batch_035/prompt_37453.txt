I want to adjudicate between the whole-brain, higher-brain, and circulatory definitions of death, but first I need the specification: what must a definition of death, adequate for law and clinical practice, actually deliver? Write the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it. A definition will have to locate a moment in what is otherwise a gradual loss of function; it will have to be applicable at the bedside by clinicians without philosophical training; and it will have to handle the cases that pressure every candidate — the brain-dead pregnant woman whose body was sustained for weeks until the fetus was viable, which suggests integrated bodily function survives brain death; the anencephalic newborn and the patient in a permanent vegetative state, who force a choice between a consciousness-based criterion and the conviction that such patients are still alive; and donation after circulatory death, where a waiting period of a few minutes is accepted on the ground that function will not in fact return, not that it could not, resting on the distinction between permanent and irreversible loss. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp masquerading as a demand — I suspect the insistence that a definition not be disjunctive, and that it leave current transplant practice undisturbed, are of the second kind. Finally, flag which pairs of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied; that result matters more to me than a verdict, so a single sentence on how the three definitions fare is enough.
