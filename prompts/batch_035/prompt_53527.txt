I run a Thursday-night philosophy discussion group that meets in the back of a secondhand bookshop — a couple of retirees, a nurse, two CS grad students, none of them philosophers. Next session is on knowing-how vs. knowing-that, Ryle's distinction, and I've got 90 minutes and a whiteboard. Don't explain the distinction to me; plan how I should explain it to them.

A few constraints from past disasters: if the central example is a skill the audience doesn't have, half the room goes into spectator mode. So the carrying example has to be something every person in that room can actually do — and please don't use riding a bicycle. Someone tried that at a session I attended and got "it's just muscle memory," and the whole evening went sideways. You pick the example, but tell me what work it has to do, and what it must not do.

There are two hard cases I want slotted in somewhere, you decide where: the concert pianist who loses the use of her hands but insists she still knows how to play, and the plain fact that "I know how to get to the station" looks like it's just knowledge of a fact. And I keep going back and forth on whether to bring in the intellectualist stuff at all — the recent-ish line, Stanley and Williamson or whoever, that knowing-how is secretly a kind of knowing-that after all. Make that call for me: in or out, and why. Don't quote anyone at me, I just want the position.

Misconceptions: I can already predict "Ryle thinks skills are mindless" and "knowing how is just knowing that plus practice." Where exactly in the sequence should I head each one off? And is the "category mistake" framing worth using, or is it jargon that loses a bookshop crowd?

Give me one closing question that would show me they actually got it rather than nodding along, and name one thing I should openly refuse to explain at this level.

Format: start with the bottom line in your first sentence — the carrying example and what comes first, full stop — then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me. Take definite positions on everything; if you hand me a menu of options and tell me to choose, I will be annoyed. And write the reply in British English — half the group are Brits and I'd definately never hear the end of it otherwise.
