I teach a pretty mixed grad seminar on rational choice in the fall (philosophers, a couple of econ students, one very stubborn law person), and we just hit the value-of-information material, I.J. Good's old result that an expected-utility maximizer should never turn down costless information that's relevant to her decision. Good topic, went fine. But then this week at a faculty dinner a colleague from the econ department dropped the slogan version: you can never be worse off knowing more — full stop, no qualifiers — and used it to bat away a whole conversation about whether patients should be able to refuse the results of a genetic test they've already consented to (i.e. do the test but not be told, say for a family study). His reasoning was basically: if the information is free, taking it dominates, so anyone who doesn't want to know is just being irrational, and we should treat that refusal like any other irrational preference. He said it with the confidence of someone quoting a theorem, and honestly I couldn't immediately say which parts of that are the theorem and which parts are him.

I want to walk into our next department lunch ready. Can you take the bare slogan and separate out the distinct precise claims it could be making — the trivial ones, the actual theorem-level ones (with their premises visible: Bayesian, expected utility, costless, actable-upon, evaluated *before* you know what you'll learn), and the strictly false ones. For each reading, say whether it's plausible and, more importantly, whether it gets my colleague his conclusion about refusing to know — because I suspect the theorem doesn't even apply to the dinner-table case once you notice that utility can depend on your beliefs themselves, that people might not be expected-utility maximizers (Ellsberg and Allais style), and that there are those weird cases where getting information for free makes things worse even for a decent Bayesian (evidential blackmail, learning the date of your own death when you can't do anything about it, Ulysses wanting the sirens plugged). I'd like a menu with verdicts I can basically recite at lunch, not a defense of one reading. And since I'll be mouthing off about this in front of actual decision theorists eventually: tell me which of your verdicts is most likely wrong and what argument or case would flip it — I'd rather know where I'm soft now than find out over dessert.
