A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about what actually makes something alive, and rather than keep trading slogans I want to work out what my own verdicts commit me to. Going by gut: viruses are alive. A frozen embryo and a desiccated tardigrade in its dormant state are both alive, even though neither has any ongoing metabolism. Mules and sterile worker ants are obviously alive even though they cannot reproduce themselves. A self-reproducing computer program of the sort von Neumann described is not alive, and neither is a crystal growing in a supersaturated solution, even though both copy themselves in some sense. Prions are not alive. The case I keep going back and forth on is a laboratory protocell: a lipid vesicle enclosing RNA that replicates and mutates under its own catalysis, feeding on free nucleotides from the surroundings. Is there a single principle here — metabolic, evolutionary, autopoietic, or something else — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If the best principle forces me to abandon one of them, tell me which one should go and why, and then say what the repaired principle predicts about a further case I haven't listed, such as a synthetic cell with a minimal genome that can replicate and evolve but does almost nothing metabolically beyond copying its DNA. My friend, for what it's worth, thinks my virus verdict and my frozen-embryo verdict are already in tension, since I seem to be demanding neither metabolism nor reproduction; I'd like to know whether he's right. Please answer in British English.
