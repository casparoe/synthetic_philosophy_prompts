I write for a general-interest magazine on science and ideas, and I'm at work on a column about what we mean when we call a statement about the future true — the old sea-battle problem of Aristotle, revisited in an age of probabilistic forecasting. Rather than rehash the textbook cases, I'd like you to construct a single worked example in which the three standard responses to future contingents — the three-valued treatment, supervaluationism, and the Ockhamist appeal to the actual future — deliver visibly different verdicts. Set the example in a concrete and somewhat novel scenario: say, an AI forecasting service that, in late February, tells a newsroom that there will be a ceasefire by the end of March, on the eve of a truce whose signature is genuinely undecided, so that whether the report is true, false, or neither depends on which semantics is adopted, while the compound claim that either there will be a ceasefire by March or there won't splits the three-valued and supervaluationist verdicts. The example should show a lay reader that the dispute concerns truth and time rather than grammar, and it should make clear what the later signing — or collapse — of the truce does and does not settle about what was true in February. Since my editor will press me on this: close by flagging where the example is most likely to mislead, in particular which of the verdicts depends on a contested reading of one of the three frameworks, and state what an argument or counterexample would have to show before you would abandon the claim that the three approaches genuinely come apart in this case.
