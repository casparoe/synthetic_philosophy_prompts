I edit foreign-policy commentary, and I have come to distrust a genre of argument I nevertheless cannot do without: the appeal to historical analogy. Munich 1938 is invoked to shame anyone counseling accommodation; Vietnam is invoked against any open-ended commitment abroad; the "Thucydides trap" is used to frame a rising power and a ruling power as drifting toward war almost by structural necessity. Each of these analogies has been used both to open inquiry and to close it, sometimes within the same paragraph. Before I take the next one seriously, I want the standard written down. Please write the desiderata that any adequate method for evaluating historical analogies in policy argument must meet.

Cover at least the following, and add anything you think belongs. First, the cases the method must handle without embarrassment: Munich, Vietnam, and the rising-power framing — including, for each, where the analogy was apt and where it misled, since a method that only ever says "analogies are dangerous" is as useless as one that only ever approves them. Second, the distinctions it must respect. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the characteristic failure of a bad analogy is not that it is wrong about resemblance but that it is right about the wrong causal layer: analogies tend to match in vivid, narratively available respects — a defiant leader, a small country under threat, a great power feeling its position slipping — while the variables that actually drive outcomes (logistics, alliance credibility, fiscal capacity, domestic institutional resilience) sit in a layer the analogy never mentions. If that is right, the central requirement on the method is that it make this layer mismatch visible rather than leaving the reader to notice it on their own, which, third, connects to the psychological results it must be consistent with: judging likelihood by similarity and overweighting vivid precedent are documented habits of mind, so the method cannot presuppose an unaided reasoner who spots surface resemblance and discounts it. Fourth, the practical constraints: the method must be usable by a decision-maker or commentator in minutes, not by a historian over months, which I suspect is where the real tension lies.

For each requirement, state the case or consideration behind it; mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp — I have in mind the historians' preference for the uniqueness of contexts versus the political scientists' preference for generalizable patterns, but tell me if that dichotomy is itself too crude; and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I particularly want your honest view on whether the requirement of speed and the requirement of causal-layer scrutiny can coexist: if they cannot, the list itself becomes an argument that analogy has a legitimate place in generating hypotheses but none in justifying conclusions, and I would want to know whether that is the right way to put the point. Give me the specification, not a reading list.
