I sit on the animal-care committee of a mid-sized biomedical research institute, and I have realized that our protocol reviews are philosophically incoherent: we demand near-complete justification for procedures on dogs and cats while waving through objectively worse procedures on rodents and fish, and nobody can articulate why beyond habit and the fear of publicity. I've been reading about speciesism and about the principle that like interests deserve equal consideration regardless of whose they are — but that principle is stated at a level of abstraction our committee cannot act on, and if I propose "stop being speciesist" outright, I'll be laughed out of the room.

Please convert whatever is actually defensible in that principle into a review procedure the committee can run on each protocol. We review roughly fifteen protocols per month, the whole committee is expected to spend no more than thirty minutes per protocol, and most members have no philosophy background, so the procedure has to be usable by someone who has never heard the term "equal consideration of interests." It should specify, in order: what facts to establish about the animals involved, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and at which point the procedure hands the decision back to a judgment call about values rather than facts — I want those points marked explicitly rather than hidden in the procedure's apparent objectivity. Pay particular attention to how the procedure should handle scientific uncertainty about whether a given kind of animal can feel pain (e.g., fish larvae, cephalopods, insects), and to the distinction between weighting interests by their intensity and discounting them because of the species that has them.

Then run the procedure on three protocols we actually face or soon will: (1) a moderately painful efficacy study on mice with a plausible but speculative payoff for a human degenerative disease; (2) the same study design, but proposed for zebrafish larvae, whose capacity for pain is contested, with slightly lower scientific quality if it stays in fish; and (3) a proposal to switch a long-standing terminal teaching exercise from rats to pigs because the anatomy is more instructive, with the pigs' time under anesthesia made somewhat shorter as a concession. In at least one case, I expect the procedure to give an answer the committee will find politically unacceptable — please show that honestly rather than softening it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, and where a genuinely principled committee would have to go beyond it.
