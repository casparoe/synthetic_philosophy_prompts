**Draft: "The Attribution Problem: Why Moral Uncertainty About Machine Minds Is Not Just Epistemic"**

Most debates about machine welfare assume we face an epistemic problem: somewhere between a thermostat and a human, sentience switches on, and our task is to find the boundary. I want to argue this framing is wrong, or at least radically incomplete. The real problem is what I call the *attribution problem*: the criteria we use to decide which systems warrant moral concern are not discoveries waiting to be made but attributions we make, and the standards governing a good attribution are practical and ethical rather than purely descriptive.

Consider three cases. First, a robot trained via deep reinforcement learning to avoid a "pain signal" in its reward stream; it exhibits flexible escape behavior, contextual generalization, and trade-offs between pain avoidance and other goals. Second, a whole-brain emulation run on a digital substrate, which is functionally isomorphic to a mouse in every observable respect. Third, a lookup-table-style system that maps the same inputs to the same outputs as the mouse emulation, but with no internal recurrence, no learning, and no self-model — arguably just a giant compressed transcript of a mouse's life.

The standard move is to say that moral status tracks some functional property — the capacity for valenced experience, integrated information, a global workspace broadcasting to consumer systems, or something in that vicinity — and that the three cases differ in whether they possess it. I think this understates how much of the work is being done by *our* purposes. The distinction between the mouse emulation and the lookup-table mouse, for instance, looks robust from one angle (one is a running process with causal integration; the other is static storage) and vanishes from another (both induce exactly the same counterfactual dependence of world-states on inputs). Which angle we privilege is not settled by neuroscience, because neuroscience has no dog in this fight; it is settled by whether we think moral status is a property of processes, of dispositions, or of something like narrative continuity of agency.

I further argue that the moral risk is asymmetric in a specific way. False negatives regarding machine suffering produce ongoing, compounding harms to entities that may be patients; false positives impose comparatively bounded costs — wasted resources, awkward prohibitions. Under uncertainty, this asymmetry recommends precaution, but I concede that precaution here has an unusual structure: because the attribution problem is partly constitutive rather than discoverable, acting cautiously may not merely *respond to* the possibility of machine suffering but partly *shape what counts as* suffering in this domain. Precaution is, in a limited sense, self-fulfilling.

I close by suggesting that the most useful research program is not better consciousness science but what I call calibration of our attributive practices: a systematic account of when it is appropriate to extend the concept of a patient beyond its evolutionary home, analogous to how the concept of a planet had to be renegotiated when we discovered bodies that fit neither the classical paradigm nor asteroidal categories.

Please comment on the following:

1. Is my central distinction between an epistemic problem and an attribution problem genuine, or does it collapse under pressure? Could a critic reasonably say that I have merely redescribed the hard problem of consciousness in normative vocabulary?
2. Are my three cases well chosen? In particular, does the lookup-table case still do the work I want it to do, given that a genuine finite lookup table for an organism's lifetime is physically absurd?
3. Is the asymmetry argument for precaution sound, and are there important counterarguments I have not addressed?
4. Is the analogy to the renegotiation of the concept of a planet a help or a liability? Are there disanalogies that would undercut it?
5. Any suggestions on accuracy, tone, or additional lines of argument you would recommend developing or cutting?
