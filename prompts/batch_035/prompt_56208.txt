I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training — the closest I came was an abandoned political theory minor — and I arrived at Foucault sideways. I build content-moderation tooling, and a colleague once remarked that our queue of flagged accounts and escalating warnings felt like something out of Discipline and Punish, so I read it. Somewhere in that period I also fell into the internet's recurring argument about whether Foucault's whole project is self-undermining. You probably know the shape of it. The critic says that if Foucault is right that knowledge and truth are products of power relations, then his own critique of prisons, psychiatry, and the rest is just another power move with no authority behind it: he can give us empirical insight into how power works, but no standpoint from which to call anything bad, and when he does call things bad — normalized, dominated, subjectified — he is smuggling in values that his own theory says he has no way to justify. The Foucauldian replies that this rests on a misreading: genealogy never claimed there is no truth, only that what gets accepted as true is settled by institutions, procedures, and disciplines of verification; and it is not obvious that a critique needs foundations standing outside the world it criticizes. The critic then mutters something about crypto-normativism, and the cycle restarts. Versions of this have been running since at least the 1980s — Habermas and Nancy Fraser made early ones, Foucault never answered at length — and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch. What strikes me is that both sides seem sincerely convinced the other has simply failed to read.

My working theory, which I want you to treat as a hypothesis rather than confirm out of politeness, is that much of the disagreement is verbal in a particular way: the two sides use the word truth to pick out different things, with the critics hearing a claim about correspondence (nothing is really true) and the Foucauldians hearing a claim about regimes of verification (nothing gets accepted as true without institutional machinery), and something similar happens with the word foundation. On that reading, the charge and the rebuttal pass each other by. But I suspect it is not entirely verbal: underneath there may be a real question neither side engages head-on, namely whether an explicitly unfounded critique can bind anyone who does not already share its commitments, or whether it reduces to persuasion by rhetoric. I cannot tell from the secondary literature I've managed to wade through whether that question even has an answer.

So here is what I want from you: a diagnosis, not a survey. First, lay out as concretely as possible what each side is actually committed to — I have sketched the positions as I understand them, and since I'm reconstructing all of this from secondhand reading, correct me where I've garbled them. Second, tell me whether my verbal-disagreement hypothesis survives contact with the actual positions, and if there is a residual substantive disagreement, characterize it precisely: is it semantic, is it about what a critique needs in order to obligate anyone, or is it bedrock political conviction dressed up as methodology? Third, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction someone could sharpen, a reformulated question, a specific kind of work — or, alternatively, argue that the debate is structurally endless and explain why. Throughout, please say how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of; I would much rather know where the soft floor of your diagnosis is than receive a seamless lecture.
