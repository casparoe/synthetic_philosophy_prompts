I edit the outreach blog of a university ethics society, and for our autumn series on political obligation we want an interactive feature: a quiz titled, in effect, "Which view of the ethics of voting do you actually hold?" The point is to sort readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which labels they find congenial, so no question may name any position, theorist or school, and none may simply ask whether one has a duty to vote. The intended audience is an interested general readership with no philosophical training; assume the scenarios, not the vocabulary, do all of the work.

Please produce the following:

1. **Eight questions**, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options. The scenarios must include, among others: casting a ballot in a seat so safe that the outcome is beyond doubt; a two-candidate race in which the lesser evil is, from the reader's point of view, still seriously bad; a referendum the reader considers illegitimate; a small committee ballot in which the reader's vote is plausibly pivotal; deliberately spoiling the ballot; a compulsory-voting scheme backed by a modest fine; voting while knowing almost nothing about the candidates; and tactically voting for a party one dislikes in order to block a worse one.

2. The options must vary along **three dimensions that come apart independently**: whether turning out is obligatory at all; what one may or must do with the vote once one has turned out; and what the vote is *for* — its causal effect on the outcome, its expressive or endorsement content, or its status as participation in a collective act. Design at least one question so that two readers who agree there is a duty to vote can still diverge on what the duty is a duty *to do*.

3. A **scoring key** mapping answer patterns onto at least six positions, which must include at minimum: the civic-duty view (turning out is itself obligatory, whatever one does with the vote); the instrumentalist view (there is no freestanding duty to vote, and the ethics of any particular vote scales with its expected effect, so the small committee and the safe seat should be treated differently); the expressive view (a vote is a speech act, and what matters is what one thereby endorses, not what one causes); the clean-hands view (one may vote only for what one judges genuinely best, never for a lesser evil); protest abstentionism (declining to participate can be the more communicative or less complicit act); and the view that the insufficiently informed are obligated to stay home. Where a coherent combination of answers has no standard name, describe it and flag it as unoccupied or thinly occupied, with a sentence on why — whether it is unstable, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

4. A short **separation analysis**: which pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly pull apart (for instance, if the civic-duty view and the expressive view give the same answers on the compulsory-voting question, say so rather than hiding it), and which single question does the most diagnostic work and why.

5. A paragraph of **design notes** explaining, for each question, which two positions it was built to split — a question on which the named positions agree is a wasted question.

One last requirement: the scoring key must not force a label onto everyone. A reader whose answers form a mixture should receive a mixture diagnosis, with a note on where their views sit in tension.
