I'm taking an early modern philosophy course, and we've just reached Berkeley. My seminar partner is a genuine believer — not a strategic devil's advocate — and last week she reconstructed for me what the secondary literature calls the master argument. I went home, tried to write it out as numbered premises so I could see exactly where I resist it, and then made the mistake of running the same pattern on cases far from trees. Now I have an argument that I can feel in my bones proves too much, but I cannot locate the flaw. Here is my formalization:

(1) When I try to imagine or conceive of a tree existing entirely unperceived — no mind aware of it, not even as an image — I find I cannot do it: the moment I make the attempt, the tree is present to my mind, and so I have perceived it after all.
(2) What I cannot imagine, I cannot conceive; what I cannot conceive, I cannot meaningfully suppose to exist.
(3) So I cannot meaningfully suppose that a tree exists unperceived.
(4) This inability is not a quirk of my psychology but a limit of the notions involved, so it applies to every mind whatsoever: no one can meaningfully suppose that a tree exists unperceived.
(5) A state of affairs that no one could meaningfully suppose is one that cannot figure in any intelligible hypothesis; and there are no facts that escape all intelligible hypotheses.
(6) So it is neither a fact nor even a possibility that a tree exists unperceived: trees depend essentially on mind.

Each premise looked tolerable in the seminar room. But then I tried the same pattern elsewhere. I cannot think of a natural number that no one has ever thought of, since the only way to fix on a candidate is to think it — so by (2) through (5), there are no unthought numbers; every number has already been thought, by someone. I cannot conceive of the Jurassic, a stretch of time before any mind existed, without my mind's doing the conceiving — so nothing predates consciousness. The schema delivers undiscovered species that cannot meaningfully be supposed to await discovery and unproved theorems that cannot meaningfully be supposed true, which seems mad. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I have a vague suspicion the trouble sits somewhere between (1) and (3), in some gap between failing to imagine a tree that exists unperceived and something the argument needs instead, but I can't name it. My partner's fallback is that the analogies fail because numbers, times, and species aren't trees — that the argument is meant to run only on sensible things — but I can't see what in the six steps licenses stopping at sensible things, especially given that the Jurassic case seems to be about sensible things (rocks, ferns) if anything is. For what it's worth, I'd very much like to keep both (5) and the claim that the universe is billions of years older than any mind; if I have to give one of them up, I want to know exactly what I'm paying.
