I am a third-year PhD student in political philosophy preparing an application for a nine-month dissertation completion fellowship, and I need a two-page research proposal. Please write it in British English.

My topic is repugnant markets — the standard trio of kidney sales, vote selling and price gouging — and my half-formed idea is that the philosophical debate runs together two quite different objections that need to be prised apart. The first is an objection about the transaction: it exploits the seller's desperation or the buyer's emergency, and if you removed the desperation (a well-compensated, entirely free and informed seller) the wrong would disappear. The second is an objection about the practice: admitting the good into the market changes what the good is, so that the wrong persists even where no one is exploited. Call these the token objection and the type objection. My hunch is that the cases sort differently than the literature assumes: vote selling seems wrong even when the seller is wealthy and uncoerced, which suggests a type objection, whereas price gouging seems to many people to lose its sting once genuine scarcity rationing is at issue, which suggests the objection was a token one all along. Kidney sales sit awkwardly in the middle.

The proposal should do the following. State the research question precisely — something like: which of the standard repugnant markets, if any, are wrong for reasons that would survive the elimination of exploitation and desperation, and what does that tell us about the structure of the corruption objection? Explain why the existing positions leave this open: the corruption objection as usually stated (that markets degrade the meaning of certain goods) is asserted at a level of generality that makes it unfalsifiable, while the exploitation objection is precise but cannot explain the residual unease in cases of uncoerced, well-remunerated exchanges. My contribution is meant to be a sorting test: for each market, ask whether the wrong survives the removal of desperation, and use the pattern of answers to build a taxonomy that both sides of the debate could accept.

Set out a month-by-month method: roughly three months constructing and stress-testing the sorting test against objections (including the worry that intuitions about uncoerced sales are contaminated by the actual background of desperation in the real-world practice); three months applying it to the three case studies, including the question of whether markets in one good can corrode norms in adjacent goods — the crowding-out phenomenon familiar from the blood-donation literature — and whether that crowding-out is a token effect at scale rather than a genuinely type-level wrong; and the final three months addressing the hardest objection I can anticipate: that type objections violate liberal neutrality, since if all parties consent and no third party is harmed, the mere change in a good's meaning seems to be no one's business. The proposal should say what would count as the project failing — in my view, failure is showing that the type objection reduces without remainder to compounded token effects, in which case the dissertation would need to become a defensive argument for that reduction — and should be honest about scope: the fellowship is nine months and I have already drafted a chapter on exploitation, so the proposal should show what is realistic in that window rather than promising a complete theory of commodification.

Please do not include citations or a bibliography; I will supply references myself. The tone should be that of a serious funding application: confident but not grandiose, with the conceptual work visible in the framing rather than buried in jargon. Assume the assessors are philosophers, but not specialists in markets and commodification.
