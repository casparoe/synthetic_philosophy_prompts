Write an essay on whether non-empirical confirmation is real confirmation, in the specific form Richard Dawid defends it for string theory. Here's the setup I want you to actually engage with, not just gesture at: Dawid's three arguments are (1) the no-alternatives argument -- the fact that a large community of smart people has tried hard and failed to find a rival theory to string theory is itself evidence there isn't one; (2) the unexpected explanatory coherence argument -- string theory keeps turning out to explain things it wasn't built to explain, like certain patterns in black hole thermodynamics, and unforced explanatory success is a classic sign of being on the right track; and (3) the meta-inductive argument -- in past cases where a physics research program displayed these same non-empirical virtues before being tested, the theory tended to come out vindicated once tests arrived, so the non-empirical signs have a decent track record.

Here's the wrinkle I want front and center, and it's the part you should spend the most ink on. A critic could argue that the three arguments are not independent pieces of evidence at all but one argument wearing three hats, such that stacking them double-counts. The track record in the meta-inductive argument was a track record of theories that eventually got tested, so it only transfers to string theory if string theory is relevantly similar -- which is itself the thing in question. And the no-alternatives argument measures the search space of theories by the imagination and incentives of a particular community in a particular funding environment, which cuts less ice when the field itself decides what counts as a live alternative. On the other side, a defender can reply that ordinary empirical confirmation also involves screening off, background assumptions, and judgments about reference classes, so demanding that non-empirical evidence meet a standard empirical evidence never meets is unfair.

You should also say something about whether the situation changes when the string landscape plus eternal inflation enters the picture -- if the theory's "predictions" get filtered through anthropic selection effects in a multiverse, does that count as the kind of explanatory coherence that signals truth, or does the flexibility of anthropic reasoning actually undercut the coherence argument?

Reach a verdict. Something conditional is fine (e.g., "the arguments work as Bayesian evidence but can't carry the weight string theorists put on them," or "two of the three survive but the meta-inductive one doesn't"), but don't end on a shrug -- I want you to defend a specific position about when, if ever, a theory can be rationally believed without empirical tests, and justify why you draw the line there and not somewhere else.
