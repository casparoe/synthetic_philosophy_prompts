I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of physics course, and my roommate — a math major who has been on a Zeno kick all semester — has produced an argument he claims shows that supertasks are impossible and that, in the end, nothing ever moves. We have been going back and forth at the kitchen table for a week and I cannot find the exit. Before we meet again I want a careful diagnosis in hand, so let me set you up properly: you are a professional philosopher who works on infinity, supertasks, and Zeno's paradoxes. Please think through the argument step by step before answering — he is very good at pouncing on loose phrasing, so I need precision, not vibes.

The setup is the standard Thomson lamp: the lamp is switched on 30 seconds in, off at 45 seconds, on again at 52.5 seconds, and so on, so that infinitely many switchings are scheduled before the minute is up. His argument runs as follows. (1) A task is a task only if there is a fact of the matter as to whether it has been performed; an "assignment" that leaves no fact of the matter about its completion is not a task but a verbal formula. (2) The super-task of performing all those switchings is complete at the one-minute mark only if the lamp is then, definitely, on or off. (3) The lamp's state at the minute mark would have to be the outcome, or limit, of the sequence of states leading up to it: on, off, on, off, ... — the lamp's whole story consists of its states, and the state at the minute mark is the last chapter of that story; there is nothing else on the scene for it to depend on. (4) The sequence on, off, on, off, ... has no limit. (5) So there is no fact of the matter as to whether the lamp is on or off at the minute mark. (6) So the super-task has not been performed at the minute mark, and by (1) it was never a task at all. (7) But every ordinary continuous process is likewise an infinite series of tasks completed in finite time: a falling stone traverses the first half of its path, then half the remainder, then half of that, and so on — infinitely many sub-tasks before it lands. (8) If the lamp's infinite series cannot be completed, no infinite series of tasks can, since what defeated the lamp was infinitude itself — the lack of a final member — and every infinite series of tasks lacks a final member. (9) Therefore no continuous process ever completes: the stone never lands, Achilles never passes the tortoise, and motion itself is an illusion. He closes with: "Zeno was right; we just refused to work out the mathematics of what we claim happens."

I have hunches but no arguments. Step (5) feels to me like it slides from "the sequence doesn't settle the matter" to "nothing settles the matter," but when I pressed him on it he asked what, other than the lamp's prior states, the state at the minute mark could even depend on — and I had no answer. Step (8) also feels too fast, but he points out that it really does seem to be infinitude as such that breaks the lamp, and every infinite series has that feature. So: which step or steps should I reject, why did each of them look so innocent, and what does rejecting each one cost? In particular, if I give up (5), am I committed to the lamp's state at the minute mark being a brute fact — and is that a price a reasonable person can pay? And if (8) goes, what exactly is the difference between the lamp and the stone, stated precisely enough that he cannot turn it back on me?
