So my dad died in March and, in his infinite wisdom, the will just says to split the estate between us four however we all agree (it literally says something like "I trust you kids to sort it out" — thanks dad). Quick facts: I'm doing okay, my sister is a corporate lawyer, my brother is on disability and his care costs eat nearly all his income. My first instinct was to weight his share, since the money obviously does more for him. My philosophy-major friend pushed back with the separateness of persons thing: there's no big collective "us" whose happiness gets maximized, we're four separate people, and treating us as containers you pour utility into until the levels come out right is exactly what's supposed to be wrong with utilitarianism. Equal split, he says, is the non-aggregating, respectful option.

I tried to build a counterargument and now I'm worried it proves way too much. Here's the chain as I have it:

(1) Each of us experiences only our own life; nobody, no super-person included, experiences the four of us together.
(2) So the total welfare of a group is not anyone's good — a sum with no owner.
(3) If a total is nobody's good, then facts about that total can't be anyone's reason for choosing, since a reason has to be good *for* somebody. So totals are out as reasons.
(4) But interpersonal comparisons have the same defect. Saying my brother gains more from a dollar than I do is comparing two goods that live in two separate lives, and there is no standpoint from which both are given together — the comparison stands to the total the same way one column of a sum stands to the sum. If the whole isn't a real quantity, neither are relations between its parts.
(5) Then nothing can be said for any division over any other, including the equal one: "it treats your lives equally" is also a comparison nobody is positioned to make, and "he needs it more" is exactly the aggregation my friend says is illegitimate.
(6) So the choice is arbitrary — coin-flip territory — and my friend's own favorite answer collapses along with mine.

I don't believe (6). There obviously are reasons here, starting with my brother's needs. But I can't see where the chain breaks. My hunch is the move from sums to comparisons somewhere between (3) and (4) — that "nobody's good" might work against totals without working against comparisons — except I can't explain why a comparison would be a real fact if the total its a comparison about isn't. If I had to choose, I'd rather keep comparisons real than keep the anti-aggregation point — my brother's getting the bigger share either way, I just want to know what I'm actually paying for that. So: which premise do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost? And does whatever I give up also damage the Rawls-and-Nozick style objections to utilitarianism, or does it only cost my friend and me here?
