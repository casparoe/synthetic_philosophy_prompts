I'm a second-year PhD student in moral philosophy, coming into it from an economics master's, and I need to settle on a dissertation topic within the literature on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the family of results showing that if each individual's preferences satisfy expected-utility axioms, and the social ordering satisfies a Pareto condition and a sure-thing principle, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. My sense is that the field has clustered around a few recurring open problems, but I don't have a good feel for which of them actually deserve three years of my life.

The candidates as I see them: (1) the status of the individualist premises — whether ex ante Pareto and the social sure-thing principle can be defended in a way that doesn't already presuppose the additive conclusion they generate, or whether the theorem is circular in the way its critics charge; (2) the interpersonal comparability problem — expected-utility theory only pins down each person's utility up to positive affine transformation, so it's unclear the theorem supplies the common scale that summing requires, and whether this is a technical fix or a fatal gap; (3) the ex ante/ex post tension, where Diamond's fairness objection seems to show that the axioms that deliver utilitarian ex ante aggregation force indifference to outcomes anyone with egalitarian intuitions should care about distinguishing; (4) the "weighted utilitarianism" reading, where the weights are unconstrained, and whether any principled basis for equal weights is available or whether the theorem has silently delivered something much weaker than utilitarianism; and (5) whether the framework extends to domains where its premises look shakiest — population ethics, discounting in social cost-benefit analysis, aggregating across species, or aggregating under moral uncertainty. There's also a deflationary position I keep encountering: that once you see what the axioms actually assume, the theorem has no substantive import for ethics at all, and the literature is a monument to mistaking a representation result for a normative argument. I can't tell whether that position is a serious contender for being true or just a mood.

What I want from you is a prioritization, not a survey. Rank three or four of these as dissertation-worthy for someone with my profile — comfortable with the formal machinery, but needing a topic that also satisfies a committee split between decision theory and political philosophy — and defend the ordering explicitly: which questions are upstream of which others, which are tractable in three years versus open-ended, which are neglected relative to their importance, and which are actively overcrowded or, in your judgment, overrated. Tell me which of the remaining candidates you'd drop and why, and whether the deflationary position deserves treatment as a live thesis or just a section in the introduction. Finally, what would move things up or down the list: if someone published a convincing response to the comparability objection next year, or a decisive result on ex ante/ex post conflict, what would that do to the viability of the other topics?
