I'm grading final exams for the first time as a TA in a large introductory ethics course, and I've now gone back and forth for two days on one particular answer, so before I commit a grade I'd like a genuinely careful second opinion. The exam question read:

"J. O. Urmson, in his 1958 paper 'Saints and Heroes,' argued that the traditional division of actions into the obligatory, the forbidden, and the merely optional is inadequate to the moral facts. Explain the fourth zone of the supererogatory that he proposed, why classical act utilitarianism appears unable to leave room for it, and whether Kant's doctrine of imperfect duties provides supererogation with a home."

And here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Urmson's point is easiest to see with his example of the soldier who throws himself on a grenade to save his squad. That act is not forbidden, and it is not obligatory either, since jumping away would be perfectly permissible. But it is not merely optional the way choosing tea over coffee is, since it is the finest act of which the soldier is capable. So Urmson adds a fourth category: acts that are good to do, not wrong to omit, and praiseworthy when done. Strictly speaking, though, supererogatory acts fall within the zone of the optional, since both are permissible to omit; they differ only in that supererogatory acts attract praise, while indifferent ones attract none. So the fourth zone is really a subdivision of the third.

"For the act utilitarian, the right act is always the one that produces the most utility. So in the grenade case the soldier is obligated to jump, and jumping away is wrong; the utilitarian cannot praise him for what he does, since what he does is simply his duty. The same holds for charity: the utilitarian is obligated to give until further giving costs more utility than it delivers. Urmson took this to show that act utilitarianism is inadequate as a theory of duty, and I agree, since a theory that cannot praise the grenade-jumper cannot be right.

"Kant, by contrast, handles supererogation easily through his imperfect duties. Perfect duties like the prohibition on lying admit no latitude, but imperfect duties like beneficence demand only that we adopt the maxim, leaving us free to choose occasions. So for Kant, anything we do for others beyond the strict minimum is supererogatory: praiseworthy but not required. And because of this latitude, no particular failure of beneficence can ever be criticized; Kant can condemn only a total indifference to others' welfare, never any specific omission."
