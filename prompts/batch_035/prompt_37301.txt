In a philosophy-of-religion survey course I'm taking, our professor recommended an episode of a (non-academic) podcast that covered Wittgenstein's views on religious belief. The host's summary went roughly like this:

'Wittgenstein's big idea was that the meaning of a word is its use, and that human practices come bundled into what he called language games, each with its own internal rules. Religious language is just one more game. Statements like "God exists" or "there will be a Last Judgment" are not hypotheses that could turn out to be true or false; they are expressions of how the speaker feels about the world — commitments, comforts, attitudes toward life. In this Wittgenstein essentially arrived at the same place as the logical positivists' emotivism, just by a different route. And since religion and science are different games played by different rules, the two can never actually conflict: no possible discovery about the world could count against a religious belief, which means that, for Wittgenstein, faith is simply immune to rational criticism from outside. The believer and the scientist are never disagreeing about anything.'

The professor thought the episode was mostly well done but warned us that a couple of things in it were 'conflations that Wittgenstein spent real energy denying.' I also recall reading that there is a charge, usually labeled 'Wittgensteinian fideism,' aimed at some of Wittgenstein's *followers* rather than at Wittgenstein himself, and I'm not sure whether the podcast's summary is a fair rendering of Wittgenstein's own position or is really an unattributed rendering of what later philosophers made of it.

Which parts are accurate about what Wittgenstein said but then overstated by the closing inference?
