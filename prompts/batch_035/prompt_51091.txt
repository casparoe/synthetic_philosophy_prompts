I work on safety evaluations of neural networks, and for the past year and a half a disagreement in my group has been completely static. I have begun to suspect that arguing harder at the object level is the wrong move, and that we should instead figure out what kind of disagreement we are actually having.

The context: we want to build what people in our area call mechanistic anomaly detectors — monitors that watch a deployed model and flag inputs on which the model is not running the same mechanism it ran on the training distribution. The motivating thought is that if a model was safe on the training distribution because it was executing some benign procedure, then inputs that push it into a different procedure are exactly where our training evidence runs out, and we would like to catch that before the output rather than after. Everyone in the group accepts this motivation. The disagreement is about what "the same mechanism" means, and it has hardened into two camps that no longer engage.

Camp one holds that mechanism identity is a causal-pathway notion: the mechanism an input triggers is the subgraph of the network — the components and their interactions — causally responsible for the output on that input, so two inputs run the same mechanism when the same subgraph, traced by something like activation patching or path decomposition, carries the work. On this view our job is essentially reverse engineering: identify the circuit on the training distribution, then watch for inputs where the causal flow goes elsewhere. Camp two holds that this is a hopelessly strong standard — we cannot reverse-engineer frontier models in time, and, worse, features in real networks appear to be superposed and polysemantic, so "the circuit" may not be cleanly delineated to begin with. They propose a statistical proxy: define mechanism sameness over internal representations rather than over an interpreted decomposition — two inputs run the same mechanism if the model's activations on one fall within the distribution of activations the other inputs produce, with the detector trained on activations directly. Camp one replies that the proxy is precisely what the motivation rules out: a statistical anomaly can flag a merely novel but benign input, and can miss a genuinely new mechanism that happens to produce familiar-looking activations, so the proxy detects out-of-distribution-ness, not new mechanism. Camp two replies that "new mechanism," unexplicated in causal terms, is a phrase rather than a criterion, and that camp one's account has never been made to work on a network where features are superposed: if a single weight participates in many mechanisms, the "same subgraph" relation may simply not exist at the granularity at which it is being demanded. And there we sit, eighteen months in.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the camps are answering different questions under one heading. Camp one is answering a theoretical question about decomposition — under what conditions is it true at all that a trained network factors into mechanisms with identity conditions? — while camp two is doing detection engineering under a deadline, and the fight persists because each camp's standard of success is invisible to the other. Camp one would count a fully worked example on a small network as progress; camp two would not. Camp two would count a detector with respectable precision and recall on held-out anomalies as progress; camp one would not. But I can also see a reading on which this is a substantive empirical question in disguise — whether superposition and polysemanticity are permanent facts about trained networks or artifacts of scale and training that cleaner models will lack — and a reading on which it is a merely verbal dispute about the word "mechanism" that a stipulation could dissolve.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Ground the diagnosis in what each camp actually says, as I have described it. Say whether the impasse is verbal, empirical, a difference in standards of evidence, or two questions under one name; and say what would count as progress under your diagnosis — a crisp definition, a benchmark, a formal result about when decomposition is possible, a worked case study, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. Do not survey the field; I want the structure of the disagreement itself. Finally, tell me plainly where your diagnosis is most likely to go wrong: which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would move you to a different account of what we are stuck on.
