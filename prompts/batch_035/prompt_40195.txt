I'm drafting a short essay on whether the notion of a "national character" is coherent, and if so, under what conditions it is acceptable to generalize about peoples. I would be grateful for comments on the argument. I am especially interested in three things: (a) whether the central inference from institutional character to national character is sound, or whether it involves a quiet slide between senses of "character"; (b) whether the distinction drawn near the end, between an explanatory register and an accusatory register, can survive the leak the essay itself concedes in its final paragraph; and (c) whether the essay's position is genuinely a third alternative, as it claims, or just a re-dressing of one of the two positions it rejects. Please also point out anywhere the prose is doing work the argument cannot support.

The draft follows.

---

Do Nations Have Characters?

Attitudes toward national character tend to split between two camps. The first treats it as a natural fact: peoples have temperaments, roughly as breeds of dog have temperaments. The second treats it as a superstition: there are only individuals, and talk of national character is at best a statistical summarizer, at worst the grammar of bigotry. I want to defend a third position: national character is a real attribute, but not of the people who make up the nation; it is an attribute of the nation as a persisting institutional structure. This relocation of the attribute, I will argue, dissolves some — though not all — of the moral objections to it.

Start with individual character. Whatever character turns out to be, it cannot be a hidden inner essence that we introspect; it is an inference from a long pattern of conduct. To call a man stingy is to summarize a history: he tips reluctantly, itemizes shared bills, gives grudgingly at weddings. The summary has counterfactual force — it licenses prediction about cases we have not observed, and it explains actions that would otherwise look arbitrary. Character, so understood, is a higher-order fact about the shape of a person's conduct: not any particular deed, but the form that recurs across the deeds.

The question, then, is not whether sixty million people share a soul. It is whether there are higher-order facts of that kind above the level of the individual. And we already speak as if there are, without embarrassment, about institutions. A bureaucracy can be timid: it escalates every decision upward, buries inconvenient findings, and postpones whatever can be postponed. A firm can be greedy, a journal can be fussy, an army can be brave. Nobody supposes this requires a group mind. The timidity of the bureaucracy is a pattern produced by its structure — its promotion rules, its exposure to blame, the precedents its clerks have inherited — a pattern that no individual member need intend or even notice. If institutions can have characters without anyone supposing they have inner essences, then the question about nations is only whether a nation is the right kind of persisting structure to support the same sort of pattern.

A nation plausibly is. It outlives every member; it regulates an enormous share of the circumstances in which characters form — school curricula, conscription, honors systems, codes of civility; and, like a bureaucracy, it rewards some dispositions and starves others. A filter that operates over centuries, deciding which habits are comfortable to hold and which are costly, does to a population something like what habituation does to a single person. When a country is punctual, that punctuality is not carried in the blood; it is maintained every morning by trains that run on schedule, appointments that are kept, teachers who mark lateness. It is, in this respect, like the stinginess of our friend: a form imposed and re-imposed by structure, revisable in principle, stubborn in practice, and false rather than incoherent when the structure changes and the habit has not.

The familiar moral objections now need sorting rather than answering wholesale. The objection that generalizing about peoples is unfair assumes that such generalizations are addressed to someone — that they accuse. But attributions in the institutional register are not addressed to anyone, because there is no subject at that level to receive an accusation. When we say the ministry is dilatory, we are not insulting the clerks; the sentence is not about the clerks, though it is true only because of how the clerks habitually behave. Equally, though less obviously, misplaced is pride. To be gratified by the depth of one's national character is a category error of the same shape as a clerk taking satisfaction in the ministry's dilatoriness. The compliment, like the insult, is misdelivered — there is nobody at the address.

One objection remains, and I do not think it can be met on the essay's own terms, only acknowledged. The institutional register leaks. Generalizations about ministries are overheard by no one; generalizations about nations are overheard by their members, who receive them as descriptions of themselves, resent them or treasure them accordingly, and sometimes perform them into existence. A true characterization of a national character can thus help make itself truer, and the practice of asserting these characterizations is not innocent even when every assertion in it is true. This does not show the notion of national character is incoherent. It shows that truth is not the only standard the practice has to meet. I leave it there.
