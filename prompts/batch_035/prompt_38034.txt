I've been editing Wikipedia on and off for a few years and something about the sourcing rules keeps bugging me. The core idea is basically that what goes into an article is what reliable sources say, not what's true — even if you're personally certain of something, you can't add it until it's been published in a source the community treats as reliable. Last month I ran into the practical version of this. A novelist's widely reported birthdate is wrong: the error traces back to one decades-old interview, and every "reliable" source just propagates it. I have a copy of her actual birth certificate. Doesn't matter — for claims about living people, primary documents are basically off limits, and me reading a document and drawing a conclusion is textbook original research. So the wrong date stays and my correction gets reverted.

But here's what makes it feel inconsistent. In the same stretch of time, I watched editors happily strip a piece of tabloid gossip out of an article even though it ran in a source everyone agrees is reliable — BLP policy says be careful with potentially reputation-damaging stuff regardless of source quality. And a few years back the community decided by vote to stop treating the Daily Mail as a reliable source at all, which means "reliability" itself is something editors determine by consensus. So consensus of sources governs one minute, editorial judgement about gossip governs the next, and reliability turned out to be a community decision rather than a fact about the sources.

So my question: what theory of truth does this whole setup actually presuppose? Is it basically Peirce's view, where truth is whatever inquiry would converge on in the limit, with reliable sources functioning as our proxy for the ideal community of investigators — so my birth certificate doesn't count because lone investigators aren't yet the consensus? Is it closer to James, where the true is more like what works or pays off for a community's purposes, so a widely repeated birthdate is doing everything truth needs to do? Or is the system trying to presuppose nothing about truth at all — just a deference rule for a group of amateurs — and failing at that?

I don't want a Wikipedia-policy explainer. I want a real answer: reconstruct the implicit theory of truth the practice needs, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it's merely compatible with (my suspicion is that several quite different theories would license the same rules), point out where the practice leaves the theory genuinely underdetermined, and tell me whether the tensions I noticed are real inconsistencies or just the same theory applied at different levels. And then say what would actually have to change if the community swapped in a straightforwardly Peircean convergence view — would my birth certificate finally get to count, or would it still lose to the bad consensus?

After the prose part, please give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "implicit_theory" (one-sentence best reconstruction of what the practice presupposes about truth), "strictly_presupposed" (array of claims the practice can't function without), "merely_compatible" (array of theories of truth it fits but doesn't require, each with a few words on why it fits), "underdetermined" (array of points where the practice leaves the underlying theory open), "tensions" (array of objects with "tension" and "verdict", where verdict says whether it's a real inconsistency or resolvable, one line each), and "what_would_change" (array of objects with "if_view" and "changes", where one entry is the Peircean convergence view and the others are theories from your "merely_compatible" list).
