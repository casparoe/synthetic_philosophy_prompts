I sit on the scientific steering committee of a mid-sized institute for the neuroscience of consciousness, and at next month's meeting we must settle, for the whole upcoming funding cycle, how the institute treats first-person data. The occasion is a concrete dispute between two investigators planning a joint study on perceptual awareness under binocular rivalry. One, trained in the neurophenomenology tradition descended from Varela, wants to recruit trained meditator-observers, give them a two-month introspective refinement phase, and treat their first-person reports as primary data — in particular, as authoritative enough to overrule the experiment's neural classifier when the two disagree about whether a subject was aware of a stimulus at a given moment. The other wants to move the study to a no-report design on the grounds that verbal reports are too noisy and theory-laden to carry any weight at all, and would relegate them to an appendix. The committee, not the investigators, has to decide the methodological policy before the study is preregistered, and I have been asked to circulate the written opinion that frames the vote.

Draft that memo for me. Its natural backbone is Dennett's heterophenomenology as laid out in Consciousness Explained, and I want you to use it seriously rather than as a label: explain what the method actually commits us to operationally — treating the subject's reports as texts to be interpreted, constructing a neutral catalog of the subject's "heterophenomenological world" without deciding in advance whether its contents correspond to real inner items, and granting the subject neither authoritative inner access nor the status of a mere faucet of behavior. Then translate those commitments into concrete, enforceable policy language: under the recommended policy, what exactly happens when a subject's report conflicts with the neural measure; whether the meditators' introspective training is a legitimate part of the protocol or a contaminant; what counts as decisive disagreement; and what the preregistration documents must say about the evidential status of reports.

Your memo should recommend one of three options outright — adopt heterophenomenology as the institute's standing methodology for first-person data, adopt a hybrid that grants trained observers some privileged evidential role, or leave the status of reports to case-by-case adjudication — and it must do more than restate Dennett. The committee's most skeptical member (herself sympathetic to the meditator proposal) will argue that heterophenomenology begs the question: by fiat, it awards victory to the third-person point of view and builds the insignificance of first-person authority into the method rather than discovering it there, so adopting it is not a neutral methodological choice but a metaphysical ruling dressed as bookkeeping. Your memo needs to face this objection at full strength, say honestly whether it is fatal, and explain to the committee why what you are recommending is not just old-fashioned behaviorism with better manners. Where the method genuinely underdetermines the policy choices before us — for instance, whether a no-report design is heterophenomenological hygiene or an evasion of the method's whole point — say so and make the call anyway. The deliverable is a document the committee could vote on line by line, with a clear bottom line and reasoning strong enough that the losing investigator would recognize their position as having been understood rather than dismissed.
