Two factions in my game-theory seminar have spent the term arguing past each other about the centipede game and the backward induction paradox, and I want to force them into a structured collaboration. Call the first faction the orthodoxy: they hold that backward induction delivers the unique rational solution — with common knowledge of rationality, each player takes at the first opportunity, since each expects the other to take at the next one, and so on down the tree — and that observed cooperation in such games is either noise or evidence that the monetary payoffs are not the players' utilities. Call the second faction the revisionists: they hold that the paradox shows the solution concept misfires, because by the time a player finds herself moving at a late node, the common knowledge of rationality that backward induction requires has already been refuted by the very fact of arrival; on their view, passes that look irrational to the orthodoxy can be rational responses to what being reached teaches about one's opponent, and the standard orthodox reply — model the opponent as occasionally trembling — either concedes this or changes the subject.

Please draft the joint statement these two factions could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the following components:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can endorse, in wording neither side would find slanted. At minimum it should cover: what backward induction establishes as a matter of formal reasoning, namely that if common knowledge of rationality could be assumed at every node, play would end with a take at the first move; the point on which the paradox turns, namely that common knowledge of rationality cannot coherently be assumed at the later nodes, since the fact of reaching them would itself overthrow it; and the robust experimental finding that when humans play such games, play typically continues for several moves before someone takes, with the available payoff growing as it does.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, each stated so that its own side would accept the wording of its position. Among the candidates: what exactly a player should infer, at a late node, from the fact that she was reached at all; whether the dispute over the word "rational" is partly verbal, and if so which part — for instance, whether rationality is properly a property of complete strategies chosen in advance or of choices made node by node in light of what has been learned; whether the trembling-hand reply answers the revisionist objection, merely relabels it, or tacitly grants it; and whether experiments on human play bear on the rationality question at all, given the confound that a subject who passes may simply value the other player's payoff, in which case the game being played is not the game being modeled.

3. The cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one faction. For each, give a concrete case, argument, or experiment both factions would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Two cases worth including: what happens to play as the stakes are scaled up, where the orthodoxy and the revisionists may predict different things; and what happens when players are told their opponents are drawn from a pool who have demonstrably reasoned by backward induction in the past, where the factions may disagree about whether arrival at a late node remains informative or becomes impossible in a way that dissolves the revisionist's point.

Assume the reader knows the standard centipede setup — two players alternate, at each turn a player may take, receiving the larger share of a pot that has grown with each pass, or pass, which grows the pot further but offers the other player an even larger take on the next turn — so you need not re-derive it, though you may fix a small concrete instance if doing so sharpens any claim. Do not adjudicate who is right: the deliverable is a constructive text both factions could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than resolved. If you conclude that some portion of their dispute is purely verbal, say which portion and why, but only in the sections where the factions themselves would concede it.
