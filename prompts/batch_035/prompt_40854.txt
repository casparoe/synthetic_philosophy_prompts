The exam question (worth 25 points) was:

"Hobbes famously extended the Ship of Theseus puzzle: as the decayed planks are removed one at a time during repairs, someone saves them, and eventually reassembles them into a second ship. Explain how the 'best candidate' (or 'closest continuer') account of identity handles this two-ship case, and how Chisholm's mereological essentialism handles it. Which of the two views better preserves the intuition that identity is all-or-nothing, and why?"

And here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly:

"The best candidate theory, which comes from Parfit, says that a later thing is identical to an earlier thing iff it is the strongest continuer of it — the candidate that stands in the strongest relation of continuity. In Hobbes' case there are two candidates, the ship that stayed in the harbor and was gradually repaired, and the ship reassembled from the saved planks. The theory says the harbor ship wins because it has continuous space-time continuity and continued functioning as a ship, even though the reassembled ship has more of the original matter. Since continuity comes in degrees, the theory actually makes identity a matter of degree too — the harbor ship is maybe seventy percent identical to the original ship, and the reassembled one only fifty percent — which is honest about how these cases feel. If only one candidate existed, it would automatically be the best candidate and so identical. Chisholm's mereological essentialism says a thing cannot survive the loss of any of its parts. But in Hobbes' version nothing is really lost, since the old planks are all kept in storage and eventually reused, so on Chisholm's view the harbor ship survives the repairs just fine, because the matter was merely redistributed. So Chisholm preserves all-or-nothing identity better, but at the cost of having to say the ship was destroyed if any plank had been tossed out instead of saved, which seems too strict."
