I teach an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and every year the split-brain unit goes the same way: half the room insists it is obviously two minds, the other half that it is obviously one mind with a plumbing problem between its halves, and both sides point at the same data until the hour runs out. This term I want to replace the shouting with a diagnostic instrument, so that at least they discover what they actually think before they defend it. Write me a decision tree that takes a student from the agreed empirical baseline — the commissurotomy patient who, in the laboratory, verbally denies having seen a stimulus to which the left hand is responding, yet who, outside the laboratory, behaves as a single person, reports feeling like one, and is regarded by everyone including herself as one — to a position on the unity of consciousness in these cases.

I want the tree built from the logical space, not from a list of names: work out first which questions genuinely divide the territory, and only then locate the actual views in it. Every node must be a question about a case or a first-person datum that a student could answer without knowing which position it feeds — nothing like 'do you think there are two minds?'. The tree must include, somewhere among its nodes, the following pressure points: intermanual conflict, where one hand undoes what the other has just done; cross-cueing, where one hemisphere cues the other through behaviour the patient herself seems unaware of; the confabulatory explanation the speaking hemisphere gives of what the other hemisphere did; the question of whether there are two experiences in a split brain that is doing an ordinary, unstimulating task with both hemispheres receiving the same information; whether the pre-operative patient survives the surgery as one continuing person, as two, or as neither; and whether the answer for a split brain differs in kind or merely in degree from the answer for an intact one — that is, whether an intact brain already contains two quasi-subjects held together by the corpus callosum, which cuts against treating the intact case as the unproblematic baseline. The leaves should include at least the following: the view that the operation creates two subjects of experience; the view that there is one subject with two partly non-communicating streams of experience; the view that there is one subject whose unity is intact and whose disunity is merely of access and agency; the Nagel-style view that the number of subjects is indeterminate, that the case falls somewhere between one and two because 'how many' is a question our ordinary concept was never equipped to answer; and a deflationary view on which unity is a construction or performance rather than a fact to be counted at all.

For each leaf, give the position's name where one is in use, and one sentence on where it bites the bullet hardest — the consequence its defenders grimace at rather than celebrate. Then two further tasks. First, identify the combinations of answers that no one occupies, and say whether you think the empty leaves are empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it simply hasn't occurred to anyone yet. Second, be honest about the tree's limits: flag any pair of positions your nodes cannot actually separate — I suspect, for instance, that one-subject-with-two-streams and the indeterminacy view will receive identical answers on every node you can construct from the behavioural data, and if so, say what kind of question, if any, could separate them. I would rather hand my students an instrument with two acknowledged blind spots than one that papers over them. Keep it to something that fits on a two-page handout, and assume no prior acquaintance with the literature; the tree should be answerable by someone who has seen only the case descriptions.
