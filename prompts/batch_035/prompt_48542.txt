I've been stuck for weeks on the pair of cases known in the decision theory literature as counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and more specifically on the standard verdicts about them. In the mugging, a coin was flipped long ago and never shown to you: if it came up heads, a highly reliable predictor deposited a large sum in your bank account a few years back, conditional on its having predicted that you would pay a small fee today if asked; if tails, nothing was ever deposited. You're asked for the fee now, with no way to learn how the coin landed. In the XOR blackmail case, the same kind of predictor sends you a letter demanding a modest payment, and the letter reaches you if and only if either you have some fixed undesirable condition (a chronic infection, say) that exists independently of anything you do, or the predictor foresaw that you would pay — but not both. Fans of updateless and functional approaches to decision theory tend to insist on an asymmetry: the rational agent pays the mugger but tears up the blackmailer's letter, and they present this pairing as the settled, obviously correct treatment. My own credence that paying is what a fully rational agent does in the mugging is about 0.75; my credence that refusing is right in the blackmail case is about 0.9; but my credence that the asymmetry between them is principled — that some clean criterion, and not just the rhetoric in which the two cases are usually told, generates both verdicts — is only around 0.6, and that gap is what's bothering me.

To probe it, I've built a contrived hybrid, and I'd like your help stress-testing it. Call it the two-feature mugging. Omega flips one coin, unseen by you. On heads, it deposits $20,000 in your account five years ago, but only if it predicted that your policy today is to pay $1,000 when asked; on tails, you have a persistent but medically harmless fungal condition that you have regardless of your policy, and the letter asking for $1,000 arrives if and only if Omega predicted you would pay it. So in the tails branch, the bad state (the fungus) is policy-independent, exactly as the infection in the blackmail case is, while in the heads branch the good state (the money) is policy-dependent, exactly as in the mugging. When I try to trace the usual reasoning through my hybrid, the fungus seems to be a pure red herring: it's present on tails whatever you do, so it cancels out of the comparison between policies, and the case reduces to the ordinary mugging — which suggests to me that the real discriminator all along is simply which cross-branch facts depend on your policy and which don't, and that the language of "offers you should honor" versus "threats you should defy" is decoration. But I might be misdescribing the case, or smuggling in something that changes it.

So here is what I want from you: your credence that the mugging/blackmail asymmetry is principled rather than an artifact, with the number derived rather than asserted. Decompose it into the sub-questions it actually rests on — whether updateless or functional treatment of one-shot predictor cases is correct at all; whether the criterion I gestured at (cooperate when the other branch's payoff-relevant states depend on your policy, refuse when they don't) is the right discriminator, or whether some offer/threat distinction does real work it can't do; whether the verdicts are even invariant across my hybrid and other interpolations between the two canonical cases; and whatever else you think belongs in the derivation. Give each term a rough probability with a line of justification, show how they combine into your headline number, and tell me which term the final answer is most sensitive to and what consideration would move it most. Please write in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points, the way you'd write a long email. And if you think my hybrid is misdesigned, collapses into a case that already has a name, or rests on a confusion about how the predictor's policy-reading works, say that plainly and correct me rather than answering the question exactly as posed — I'd rather find out my case is broken than get a polite analysis of the wrong thing.
