I have just spent most of an evening arguing with a friend about whether knowing how to do something is ultimately just knowing that something is the case — she taking the intellectualist line that knowledge-how is propositional knowledge of a way of doing things, presented under a practical mode; I digging in on the Rylean side that the reduction gets everything backwards. We exhausted ourselves trading cases, and I have concluded that the disagreement is really about what a theory of knowledge-how is *for*. So before we go another round, I want the standards written down.

Please produce a specification: the desiderata any adequate theory of knowledge-how must meet, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. At minimum the list should engage the following, though you should add or discard as you see fit and say why:

- The asymmetry of acquired inability: the dual who can no longer perform but has not forgotten how (our ski instructor, the retired dance teacher), and its mirror, the person who performs reliably without knowing how (the beginner whose swing is corrected mid-flight by a coach, the patient with blindsight-like success at a discrimination they cannot report).
- The distinction between knowing how and merely being able: ability is cheap, know-how is an achievement, and the theory should explain wherein the achievement consists.
- Acquisition and transmission: much know-how is got by imitation, practice and correction rather than by being told facts, and some possessors (non-human animals, pre-linguistic children) seem to lack the resources for any propositional surrogate. The theory must either accommodate this or deny the appearances, and if it denies them it owes an account of what it is denying.
- The triviality trap: if the intellectualist says the proposition is presented "under a practical mode", the theory must say what that mode contributes without either collapsing back into ability (making the propositional part idle) or becoming unfalsifiable.
- A constraint my friend resisted and I want defended or demolished: the theory should make intelligible how know-how can be *lost* — whether by forgetting alone, by loss of practice, or only by physical injury — and it must not imply that reciting the relevant facts to oneself could restore the ability.
- Non-observational access: I know how I am moving my fingers now, and I know it without watching them; the theory should not force this into observation of my own performance.

For each requirement, mark it as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp, and tell me honestly whether you think some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list itself excludes all comers, say so plainly, since that would itself be the result I most want. Then a brief scorecard, no more than a short paragraph each, for the two of us: her intellectualism (knowledge-how as knowledge of a way under a practical mode of presentation) against Ryle's dispositional anti-intellectualism, plus any third contender you think is the real live alternative to both.

I want you to take sides, not set out a menu. Commit to which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and commit to whether my friend's view survives it. End with that commitment in the form:

\answer{knockdown requirement: <requirement name>; intellectualism survives: yes/no}

Place this on the very last line, after all the reasoning.
