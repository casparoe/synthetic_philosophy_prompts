I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and in three weeks we reach anthropic reasoning: the question of how, if at all, a rational agent should update on evidence of the form "this is me, here, now." The students arrive knowing the labels — self-sampling assumption, self-indication assumption — mostly as tribes to belong to, and they use them as substitutes for argument. I want a handout that sorts them by what they actually say about cases before they know which camp that commits them to.

Please write a decision tree for the handout. The entry point is fixed: the agent accepts ordinary Bayesian conditioning on non-indexical evidence, is uncertain between rival cosmological hypotheses, and knows she exists as one observer among the observers, if any, that the true hypothesis entails. From there, every branch node must be a concrete case or question the student can answer without knowing where the branch leads — for example, whether finding oneself in a universe of a trillion observers rather than a lonely one should shift credence toward the populous hypothesis, or what to say when the two candidate hypotheses each contain "a person just like you" at a different location. Nodes that ask "do you accept SIA?" are worthless for my purposes; the point of the instrument is diagnostic, not tautological.

The tree must get the student to at least these leaves: SIA, SSA with a narrow reference class, SSA with a broad or universal reference class, and a deflationary leaf for the view that self-locating evidence never requires updating beyond ordinary conditioning. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands it, but keep the total between six and eight. Each leaf should carry the position's name (or "unnamed" if you think no one defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — for the SIA branch, for instance, the presumptuous philosopher result should surface somewhere, and the student should be able to see it coming from the branch questions rather than having it announced at the end.

Three further requirements. First, at least two of the diagnostic cases should be drawn from thought experiments the students will have met — Sleeping Beauty and the Doomsday argument are the obvious candidates, and both should do real work at distinct nodes; if your tree cannot distinguish a halfer from a thirder using Beauty alone, say so rather than pretending otherwise. Second, note explicitly any pair of leaves your tree fails to separate — I have in mind the possibility that an SSA with a sufficiently inflated reference class mimics SIA in some setups, and I want that diagnosed rather than papered over. Third, walk the unoccupied branches of your own tree: for each answer combination that lands on no named position, spend a sentence on whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because it has not been explored.

Do not worry about scholarly apparatus; the students have the reading list already. I want an instrument they can take before class and argue with afterward — a tree whose branch questions are the kind of thing two reasonable people could answer differently on a Tuesday afternoon, with the positions as discovered destinations rather than chosen destinations.
