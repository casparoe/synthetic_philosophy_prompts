I moved to Munich a couple of years back and something about the church tax here has been bugging me ever since, so let me describe the setup and then ask my actual question.

In Germany, if you're registered as Catholic, Protestant or Jewish, the ordinary state tax office collects your church tax for you — it shows up on your payslip like any other withholding, and the state even keeps a small cut for the trouble of collecting it. To make it stop you can't just quietly stop believing, or stop going to mass, or send an email. You have to go down to a registry office or court, pay a fee, and file a formal declaration that you're leaving the church. And the churches themselves treat that bureaucratic act as a religious event: the Catholic bishops, at least, hold that once you've filed it you've excommunicated yourself in the relevant sense — no communion, no sacraments — even if you personally still believe everything and are only trying to escape the bill. Meanwhile the German constitution supposedly commits the state to neutrality on worldview questions.

The obvious comparison points: France won't touch religious funding at all (laïcité, religion as a private inner conviction), except that this somehow doesn't apply in Alsace and Moselle, where the state still pays the clergy. England has an established church with the King at its head and bishops sitting in parliament, yet somehow no church tax and one of the least religious populations in Europe. And back in Germany, the big Muslim organisations mostly don't have the corporate status that would let them run this system at all, so the state's billing service is effectively a Christian-and-Jewish-only arrangement.

Here's what I want: what does the church tax actually presuppose about what a religion IS? My half-formed thought is that it only makes sense if religion is something like club membership — a voluntary association you join and leave by formal act, whose doctrines you're permitted to hold or not hold, since the debt attaches to the membership and not to the belief. Your gym doesn't care whether you secretly believe in strength training; it cares whether you've cancelled. But that can't be the whole story, because the churches themselves insist the opposite — that the religion is the belief, and the paperwork is spiritually real — and the state is in the business of certifying that spiritual event. So is the implicit theory of religion stable, or is the state doing two incompatible things at once: treating religion as an association for the money and as a matter of belief for the exit? And is there any coherent story under which a secular constitution can be in the dues-collection business at all?

If it helps, I've read somewhere that the whole thing traces back to the state confiscating church lands in the Napoleonic period and compensating the churches with a right to tax instead of their endowments, but I might be garbling that, so don't hang your answer on it unless you think its right.
