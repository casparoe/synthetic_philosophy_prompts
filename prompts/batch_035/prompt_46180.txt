Hi. Quick bit of context: I'm the welfare lead at a company that farms black soldier fly larvae for animal feed (some pet food too). We process on the order of 300 million larvae a month, which is the kind of number that makes people either shrug or panic depending on what they think a larva can feel. Our board doesn't want to touch the question of whether insects are sentient ("we're not philosophers"), but they've told me to come back with something I can actually run when a welfare question lands on my desk — roughly one a month, and I've got maybe half an hour per question before it goes to the farm managers, who are practical people and will ignore anything that reads like a seminar.

What I want is a decision procedure in the shape of: a fixed list of questions in order, where for each one you say what counts as a good-enough answer, and which answers just stop the checklist and force a values call that goes back to the board. The spine of it should be the arithmetic my board already half-accepts: huge numbers times a small chance of feeling anything is still huge — but also, small chance of feeling anything means we can't pretend they're cows, and our money isn't infinite. If you think the right move is a threshold — below some probability of sentience we don't spend at all, above it we do — say so, but then tell me where the threshold comes from, because "I picked 10%" won't survive contact with the managers.

Two ideas floating around here that I'd like you to either build into the procedure or tell me are wrong:
1. One of our science guys says the only question is expected suffering prevented per dollar — do whatever beats our best alternative use of that money, nothing else matters. Feels too clean to me but I can't put my finger on why.
2. The board's version is the opposite: a hard cap, no welfare spend above 0.1% of revenue, whatever the numbers say. Also feels wrong, and I'd like to be able to say why out loud for once.

Then run it on the three questions I actually have queued:
(a) Kill method. Most of the industry, us included, does live grinding for the bulk lines (freezing or blanching for smaller ones). Does the procedure come out saying grinding is fine, or against?
(b) The pre-harvest fast. We pull the food substrate about a day before harvest so the larvae empty their guts — keeps the product clean, for us it's just the seperation step of the process. But if they can feel something like hunger, that's 24 hours times a very large number of larvae. Does it survive the procedure?
(c) The breeding adults. Black soldier fly adults barely eat — they live about a week and mostly run on fat reserves, that's just the species — and some competitors keep their adult colonies with no water at all. We do give water. Can the procedure tell us whether that's welfare or theater?

Format, in this order please: "Assumptions I'm making" (short, ~100 words — the numbers you'll be plugging in, so I can argue with them); "The procedure" (~400 words, the actual one-page list); "The three cases" (~150 words each — and at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like; please don't sand that one down for my benefit); "Where it breaks" (~100 words — the situations where the checklist misfires or has to hand off to the board); and "What would change my mind" (~100 words) — tell me what kind of finding, e.g. about nociception or larval behavior, would flip the kill-method ruling or the fast, and which step of the procedure is the likeliest point of failure for the whole thing. Also be blunt about where you're just guessing — I'd definately rather know which numbers are judgment dressed up as arithmetic.
