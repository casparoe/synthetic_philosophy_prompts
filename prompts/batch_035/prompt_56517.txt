My philosphy reading group has two members who've been arguing about peer disagreement for over a year now -- one's a conciliationist, one's a steadfasteer, and they keep talking past each other. Write the joint statement they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, the document they could both actually put their names to.

For context: the conciliationist (call her C) thinks that when you discover an epistemic peer disagrees with you -- someone you antecedently judged about as likely as yourself to get this kind of question right -- that's higher-order evidence you can't just dismiss, and you should move your credence at least partway toward theirs. The steadfast one (S) thinks you can rationally keep your credence where it was, on the strength of your own first-order reasoning: you already took your evidence into account, and the mere fact that someone else got a different answer doesn't defeat that.

I want the document to contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both accept. They should be able to agree on more than they think -- e.g., that some disagreements are evidence of error somewhere, that finding out the other party was drunk, or guessing, or had less training dissolves the peerhood and you can dismiss them, that two people can't both be right about a coin's bias, maybe that updating is required in some cases and forbidden in others. Find the real common ground.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept. I suspect one is whether rationality is permissive -- whether there's exactly one rational credence to have given the evidence, or several -- because if several are permitted, the steadfast view gets easy, and if not, it looks ad hoc. Another is the self-undermining problem: if C learns that epistemologists she counts as peers reject conciliationism, does she have to conciliate away from conciliationism, and is that fatal or just a feature? A third is probably what "peer" even means -- first-order competence, track record, shared evidence -- since S can win just by narrowing the category and C can win by broadening it. State each of these (and anything else that's genuinely left over) so its own side would sign the wording.

3. For each disagreement, the crux -- the subquestion that would move at least one of them if settled -- and a concrete case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Good candidate cases: pairs of equally trained statisticians who get different analyses of the same dataset and then see whose predictions calibrate better over time; weather forecasters with matched track records who disagree about tomorrow; the old worry about conciliating with the astrologer or the creationist, and whether it's a real counterexample or just a case of non-peers misdescribed. And if any part of their fight turns out to be verbal rather than real, say which part and why.

One rule: nobody gets to win by drafting. If S wouldn't sign C's position as stated, or vice versa, the wording is wrong and needs to be redone. Please take that seriously -- the whole value of the document is that both of them could actually endorse it.
