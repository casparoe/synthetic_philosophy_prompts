ok so I run a small podcast on top of that (philosophy prof by day, mediocre interviewer by night), and next week's guest is a philosopher who's built her career defending the knowledge norm of assertion -- the view that one should assert that p only if one knows that p, Williamson's line. My audience is mostly grad students, so they know the basics cold and I do NOT want her to spend 20 minutes re-explaining the view. Draft me around ten interview questions that press where the literature actually presses. Specifically:

1. One question on the lottery/Gettier asymmetry: after the drawing, "my ticket lost" seems improper to assert even though the speaker has a highly justified true belief; in a Gettier case the speaker also lacks knowledge, yet asserting feels pretty fine. Ask her what in her framework explains why one is bad and the other isn't. Include a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give (probably something about the lottery justification not being "safe" or not figuring in the explanation of truth), and make the follow-up push on whether that just smuggles in a different norm.

2. One on Moorean assertions: "p, but I don't believe p" sounds insane, while "p, but I don't know p" sounds merely odd. Get her to say whether the knowledge norm predicts this graded weirdness or only gives her all-or-nothing predictions.

3. One on the constitutive-rule story: the norm is supposed to be like the rules of chess, part of what assertion IS. But if some community happily asserts with just justified belief and nobody objects -- did they redefine assertion, or are they violating the norm, or is the norm maybe not constitutive after all? Make her pick.

4. One on the blameless-but-norm-violating speaker: someone whose evidence has been rigged by a demon (or is Gettiered to the gills) asserts something false-ish or unknower -- we don't blame them, but the norm says the assertion was improper. Ask her what work a norm is doing if it's never reasonably followable in exactly these cases.

The rest can be your choice but should be stuff she can't answer from a canned lecture -- I want at least a couple where she has to actually think on her feet. No "what drew you to philosophy" filler, and nothing about her personal life. Also if you could keep each question under like 60 words so I can actually read them off a screen mid-conversation, that'd help alot. its fine to number them.
