I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a paper on Bernard Williams, and I've run into a design problem I cannot solve to my own satisfaction: I need a thought experiment that runs Williams's complaint in reverse, and every case I construct for myself either cheats or collapses back into the familiar dialectic. I would like you to build the case for me.

Some background, to fix terms. Williams presses two complaints against impartial moral theory, and they tend to get run together. The first is the integrity objection proper: a moral theory that treats my ground projects as just one consideration among others, to be overridden whenever the impersonal balance of reasons demands it, alienates me from my life — the theory makes my deepest commitments into fungible inputs. The second, drawn from his discussion of the man who swims out to save his drowning wife, is the one-thought-too-many point: there is something wrong with an agent who needs a detour through impartial theory before rescuing his own wife, even when the theory licenses the rescue, because the deliberative detour itself is a failure of the relationship — a kind of bad faith or coldness that survives even a correct verdict. My thesis is that these two complaints come apart: I want to argue that the second does not follow from the first, because a person can be perfectly unalienated from a project and still have too few thoughts rather than too many — spontaneous, wholehearted project-loyalty can be a failure precisely as an expression of the project, and the corrective can come not from an impersonal standpoint but from the project's own internal demands.

The request: construct a case meeting all of the following specifications.

1. It must be realistic — a hospital, a family, a workplace, an artist's studio. No trolleys, no ticking bombs, no contrived certainty about outcomes.
2. The agent acts immediately and wholeheartedly on a genuine ground project — no hesitation, no calculation, no visible deliberation at all. The action must be intelligible as an expression of that project, and it must be criticizable — but not because the underlying project is pathological or the person is a monster. A sympathetic reader should feel the pull of the spontaneous response before seeing what is wrong with it. If the only way you can make the loyalty look bad is to make the project itself sinister (venality, obsession, cruelty dressed up as devotion), the case cheats.
3. There is a further thought the agent needed to have, and having it would have changed the action. But — and this is the hard constraint — that further thought must not be an impartial calculation, and it must not have the content that any ideally consistent utilitarian, Kantian, or contractarian would settle on. The correcting thought should draw on something partial: the standpoint of the person the project is about, or standards internal to the project itself. The case should make it plausible that the needed reflection is as partial as the response it corrects.
4. Time pressure cannot be doing the work. It must be true that even with unlimited time, the immediate response would still be a failure, and the reflective thought, once had, should not become obsolete after the fact — it should still be a thought the agent needed, not merely one it would have been nice to have.

After presenting the case in a paragraph or so, please do the following.

First, show that it meets each specification, taking the third most seriously. The obvious danger is that any correcting thought can be redescribed as covert impartiality — say, as respect for persons under a partial label — so you need to explain why the correcting thought in your case resists that redescription. If you cannot fully discharge this, say so honestly and diagnose which pair of specifications is in tension.

Second, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work in reversing Williams's verdict, and what happens to the case when you remove that feature. My own suspicion is that the crucial feature is that the project's object has a standpoint of its own — that the drowning wife, by contrast, is conveniently inert — but I want to know whether your case confirms or complicates that hunch.

Third, give me the strongest Williamsian countermove. Two candidates I anticipate: (a) that the correcting thought is not one thought too many only because it occurs before or outside the moment of action, so the case concedes Williams's point about deliberation in the moment; and (b) that a person of proper character would have internalized the correction as a disposition and produced the right action without the thought at all, so what the case shows is a defect of character formation, not an argument against the complaint. Assess which of these, or some other line, is the best response and how much trouble it makes for my thesis.

Finally, tell me plainly what you take the case to show about whether the wedge I am trying to drive — between the alienation complaint and the one-thought-too-many complaint — actually holds.

I should say what the case is for, so that you know what counts as cheating. I am using it as a premise check: if the two complaints really do come apart, there should be a describable case where wholehearted, unalienated project-loyalty is criticizable and where the criticism is not a proxy for impartial theory. A case that merely stipulates that the spontaneous response is bad has not shown anything; the badness has to be generated by details the reader can verify against ordinary understanding of the relationships involved, and the partial character of the correcting thought has to be visible in its content rather than asserted. A case whose correction turns out on inspection to be a welfare calculation or a universalizability test refutes me instead of supporting me — which, to be clear, is a legitimate finding, and if that is where the design problem bottoms out, I want to hear it argued rather than papered over.
