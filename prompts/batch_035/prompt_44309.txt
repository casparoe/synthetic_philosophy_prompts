so I'm a novelist, four years deep into a big historical novel about the first age of globalization — roughly 1580 to 1650, the stretch where Potosí silver, the Manila galleon and the East India companies started stitching the planet together — and somewhere along the way I accumulated a set of beliefs about how the past should be told that I can no longer tell apart form each other. Here's the set:

1. The usual popular telling of world history is Eurocentric in a way that is factually wrong, not merely perspectival — it makes Europe the protagonist from the Greeks onward and reduces everyone else to scenery the West eventually reaches, and that's bad history full stop, not just one vantage among many.
2. There is no view from nowhere. Every history, and every novel, is told from somewhere, and honesty means owning the standpoint rather than pretending to have escaped it — and yes, I include the omniscient narrator in that, which I take to be a convention readers agree to accept, not an actual eye.
3. Because of 1 and 2, my plan for this book is to tell the period from everywhere at once: braided protagonists — a Kongo ambassador's son in Lisbon, a Gujarati ship's pilot, a Genoese merchant's wife, a smelter's daughter in Potosí — equal interiority for all of them, nobody granted the center.
4. I also believe the world we actually live in has a fairly specific and fairly local causal origin: the divergence in Britain and northwest Europe — coal, colonies, fiscal-military states — and that it was late and contingent, not the culmination of some two-thousand-year western essence. If a book sets out to explain how we got here, I think it has to keep the causal weight on that, even when the story starts long before it.
5. Novels need a center of consciousness. Readers can hold maybe three protagonists; past that you have a mosaic, and a mosaic with equal weight everywhere is a chronicle, not a story. I don't want to write a chronicle.

I keep assuming these all fit together, and every time I sit down to outline I find I'm smuggling one of them out the back door thorugh my own choices — I'll give the pilot three chapters and the Genoese woman ten, or I'll quietly promote someone to being the reader's way in and then feel gross about it, and I can't tell whether that's hypocrisy, necessary craft, or my convictions quietly fighting each other. Can you audit the set for me: which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost? Rank the tensions by severity rather than handing me a list where everything's equally doomed. I honestly don't know which of these I'd drop if I had to — they feel like they come from different parts of me, the reader, the craftsman, and the citizen of the world the divergence built — so treat all five as genuinely mine.
