Quick context so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired geochemist — thirty-odd years of trace-metal measurements and propagating error bars, and now that the lab doesn't need me I've gotten into philosophy, currently via a decision-theory reading group. My instinct is that everything is expected value plus a bit of measurement noise, but one member, Dana, keeps making choices I can't fit to any single rule, and it's bugging me. All the amounts below are gains, money she'd notice but that wouldn't hurt her.

Her track record on gambles:

1. She takes $100 for sure over a coin flip between $0 and $300.
2. Between $100 for sure and a coin flip between $95 and $105, she says it's basically a wash, and if you push her she'll takes the sure thing but with zero conviction.
3. She takes $100 for sure over a gamble that pays $500 with 10% probability, $100 with 89%, and $0 with 1%.

5. She takes the 60/40 version of $200-or-$100 over the 50/50 version, so she's not ignoring probabilities wholesale.

My knee-jerk explanation was diminishing marginal utility, but #2 makes that look pretty weak — at these stakes a utility curve over money barely curves, at least by my back-of-envelope reasoning. Someone in the group keeps pushing risk-weighted expected utility, where instead of transforming the payoffs you transform the cumulative probabilities read up from the worst outcome. I can also imagine a cruder security-first rule (fixate on the floor unless it's clearly tolerable) and a rule that inflates small chances of big payoffs.

So, three new cases to predict. (a) 99% chance of $300, 1% chance of $0, versus $50 for sure. (b) 1% chance of $20,000 versus $150 for sure. (c) She's handed the coin-flip ticket from #1 ($0 or $300) for free and can sell it for $100 — same economics as #1, but does she sell?

What I want: predict her choices, tell me which rules actually fit the full track record (I suspect more than one does, and I'd like to see the fit checked case by case), what each of those rules predicts for (a) through (c), your best guess at the rule she's running and why, and the single further choice you'd put to her that would most cleanly split the top two candidate rules.
