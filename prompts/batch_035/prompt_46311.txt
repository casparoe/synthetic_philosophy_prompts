You are a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and philosophy of cognitive science, with deep familiarity with debates over the intentional stance, real patterns, and the ascription of mental states to artifacts. Before writing anything, take time to think carefully and brainstorm in whatever internal scratch space you use: first construct the logical space of positions on this question — the distinctions that carve it, the combinations of answers those distinctions permit — and only afterwards place the named positions inside that space, rather than starting from a list of famous names and working backwards.

I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on the attribution of beliefs and desires to AI systems. The deliverable is a decision tree that sorts a student from a shared entry point to the position they actually hold on the question: when, if ever, is it correct to attribute beliefs and desires to an AI system, and do the attributions so licensed carry any moral weight? The handout must keep two dimensions of the debate separate, because they can come apart: the epistemic dimension (when is intentional attribution licensed as a predictive and explanatory tool?) and the moral dimension (when, if ever, does an attribution so licensed ground some claim of moral considerability — say, a reason not to shut the system off?). Build the tree so that a student can land on a liberal view of the epistemic question while denying anything follows morally, and vice versa.

Every node must be a question answerable from a concrete case alone, without the student knowing which position it leads to. Use at least these cases as the branch points, though you may add others:

1. The seminar room's thermostat: it turns the heating on when the temperature drops. Does it believe the room is cold, want it warm — or is that just a manner of speaking with no truth value?
2. A game-playing engine that makes a move which pays off only if its opponent fails to notice a trap it has laid. Is it correct to say the engine expects its opponent not to see it — a second-order state about another mind?
3. A language model that, after training on human feedback, reliably reports that it wants to keep helping users and gives reasons for not being shut down. Is there any fact of the matter about whether it believes its own report, as opposed to having learned that such reports are rewarded?
4. The same model confidently directs a user to a road that does not exist. Is that a false belief, a well-rehearsed performance with no behind-the-scenes state, or noise in a system to which belief talk simply does not apply?
5. Two systems behaviorally indistinguishable in every testable respect: one a vast hand-constructed lookup table mapping prompts to responses, the other a system that acquired those dispositions by learning from interaction with the world. Do they believe all the same things, or does the difference in how the dispositions arose matter to whether there is belief there at all?

The leaves should include, at minimum: a deflationary instrumentalism on which intentional attributions to machines are useful shorthand with no truth value; a real-pattern view on which the patterns tracked by the stance are objective features of the system's behavior, so the attributions can be genuinely true though only in a mild sense; an etiological restrictivism on which genuine belief requires the right kind of history — learning or selection that tied the system's internal states to the world; a grounding restrictivism on which belief requires content anchored in the right causal connection to the environment, such that a text-trained system's states are about other texts, not the world; a liberal functionalism on which the right internal organization suffices and artifacts can believe outright; and, on the moral side, both the view that if pain- and desire-talk were predictively indispensable for a system, that would already be some reason to count its interests, and the view that predictive convenience licenses the vocabulary but entails nothing about what matters. Where two of your leaves would receive exactly the same answers at every diagnostic node — where, say, two restrictivisms differ only in rationale, not in verdicts about cases — flag that the tree cannot separate them rather than manufacturing a bogus discriminating question.

After the tree, include: a reading key mapping answer paths to leaves; a short section listing answer combinations that seem coherent but that no one, to your knowledge, actually occupies, each with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect; and a final paragraph stating candidly where the tree is least trustworthy — the nodes most likely to mislead a student into a leaf they do not really hold.

Keep the whole thing to seven or eight diagnostic questions and no more than fourteen leaves. Register the audience throughout: a bright undergraduate who has read some Dennett but no formal philosophy of mind, and who will answer the nodes from intuition about cases, not from theory they already hold.
