I've been going round in circles on the natural kinds thing and what I want is a diagnosis of the standoff, not another tour of the positions. Here's how it looks to me after a few years of reading: on one side there's the Putnam/Kripke line, where natural kind terms rigidly designate, water is H2O in every possible world, and kinds are out there waiting to be discovered. Then Boyd's homeostatic property cluster stuff loosens that up — no essence required, just property bundles held together by causal mechanisms. And then someone like Dupré goes the whole way and says 'real' more or less means 'useful to us', with many equally legitimate cross-cutting classifications. As far as I can tell nobody has actually changed sides in decades.

And here's the thing: all these people seem to agree on the plain facts — which classifications each science actually uses, which ones support predictions and generalizations. The fight is over whether there's a *further* fact about which carvings are at the joints. My hunch is it's a verbal dispute about the words 'natural' and 'real', sitting on top of total agreement about everything else. But I'm not confident — maybe the essentialists and the Dupré crowd are answering different questions entirely, one about the semantics of kind words and one about whether the world has similarity structure independent of our classificatory projects, and the shared vocabulary of 'joints' hides that.

So: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what the sides themselves say, and then tell me straight whether anything would count as progress — some distinction, some kind of case study, or genuinely nothing, and why.
