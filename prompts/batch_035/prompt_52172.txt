So here's the situation: my metaphysics reading group has, for going on three years now, been a running battle between two people who never actually engage each other's arguments. One is a hard-core mereological universalist — any two things whatever compose something, my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower included — and he leans constantly on the argument from vagueness (composition can't come in degrees, so there's no fact of the matter about where it stops, so it never stops). The other is a straight-up nihilist about composition: there are no tables or chairs, just atoms "arranged table-wise," and she thinks every extra object is ontological freeloading. Whenever one of them talks, the other just restates their own view louder. Our department has a graduate symposium coming up, and the two of them have been talked into submitting a joint statement instead of yet another pair of talks past each other. Somehow I got volunteered to draft it, and I want it to be the real thing, not a mushy "both sides have points" cop-out.

Specifically I need: a numbered section of claims both could actually sign their names to — about what the special composition question is even asking, about what the no-go-style considerations (like the problems that arise for vague composition) rule out, about where the burden of paraphrase lies, maybe also about the problem of the many and about whether some parts of the dispute are merely verbal (they suspect the "how many objects are in this room" part might be, and I'd like that addressed either way). Then a table laying out the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — the nihilist has to be ok with how her view is described and vice versa, since they're both going to sign it. Then the cruxes, and for each crux at least one concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I'm guessing one crux is the vagueness premise itself, another is how much evidential weight ordinary talk about tables deserves, and they keep circling something about gunk — a world with no atoms at all — so please work out what each side predicts about a gunky world and whether that prediction embarrasses them.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions. Keep it neutral enough that neither of them can complain I slanted it toward the other, and flag explicitly any disagreement you conclude is verbal. They're both going to read this over coffee tomorrow morning, so it has to survive both of them.
