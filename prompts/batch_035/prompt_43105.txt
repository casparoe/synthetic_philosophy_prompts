I've been following and occasionally participating in the same arguments about cancellation and deplatforming for over a decade now, and I've noticed that the arguments never actually engage with what the practice itself commits anyone to. So let me try to state the practice carefully and get your read on its implicit theory.

The practice, concretely: a private individual — sometimes a public figure, sometimes a nobody with a few hundred followers — says or is discovered to have said something that a large number of people judge beyond the pale. The judgment spreads faster than the context. Consequences follow at several distinct layers: a coordinated or uncoordinated pile-on of abuse and denunciation; withdrawal of association by acquaintances and employers, often formalized as firing or contract termination; and removal of platforms — account bans, dropped by a hosting provider, cut off by a payment processor, dropped by a publisher. Two features seem to me philosophically loaded. First, there is no endpoint: no sentence is ever pronounced complete, no mechanism marks the sanction as served, and the record stays permanently searchable. Second, there is no agent: the sanction is produced by millions of uncoordinated individual decisions, each of which taken alone looks like the innocent exercise of ordinary rights — to speak, to associate, to decline business.

Here is what puzzles me. The people who defend this practice oscillate between two descriptions that pull in opposite directions. On one description, it is not punishment at all: nobody has a right to an audience, employers may fire whom they like, platforms may host whom they like, and all that has happened is the aggregation of legitimate private choices. On the other description, the person deserved it — which is desert talk, and desert is the home territory of theories of punishment. And the same people who would never accept criminal punishment without due process, proportionality review, and a conception of when the debt is paid seem entirely comfortable administering a sanction with none of the three, administered at a scale and intensity that would make a DA blush, over an offense with no statute of limitations, where a decade-old remark counts the same as one made yesterday.

So: what theory of punishment, if any, does this practice presuppose? Work through the standard candidates — retributivist desert, general deterrence, incapacitation, expressive condemnation, communicative or restorative accounts — and for each say whether the practice merely coexists with it, positively presupposes it, or is in tension with it. I'd particularly like you to address: whether the permanence and lack of endpoint fits desert-based views badly and incapacitation-based views well, or whether the actual disappearance of most targets from public life within a few years functions as an informal sentence-served; whether the chill on others is a bug that deterrence theories would rationalize as a feature; whether a distributed practice with no coordinating agent can presuppose a theory of anything, or whether the presupposition talk only makes sense once you treat the mob as a single sanctioning institution; and whether the no-punishment description is a stable resting place or just an accounting trick that lets one enjoy the expressive benefits of punishment while disclaiming its constraints.

Then tell me what would actually have to change in the practice under each replacement theory: if we took a restorative view seriously, what would the apology-and-reintegration mechanism look like, and what would play the role of expungement? If we took pure incapacitation seriously, would most of the pile-on be revealed as pointless cruelty with no theoretical role? And is the practice as it stands underdetermined — several incompatible theories all licensing it — or does some feature of it, such as the permanence or the intensity asymmetry between famous and obscure offenders, quietly rule some views out?
