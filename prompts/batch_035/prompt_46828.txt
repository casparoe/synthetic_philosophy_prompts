I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy serving as the graduate representative on the allocation board of our university's student-run giving circle, which distributes about £40,000 a year to charities under a charter committing it to "doing the most good possible with the money." At next month's meeting we vote on a motion from two members. The motion would ring-fence 40% of next year's pool as a "suffering tranche": grants within it would be ranked purely by suffering averted, with any happiness created counting neither for nor against, while the remaining 60% continues to be ranked by our usual cost-effectiveness analysis, which counts both. A rider to the motion forbids the tranche from funding interventions whose dominant measured effect is raising happiness among people in only mild discomfort. The proposers are not negative utilitarians; their stated view is that our charter is ambiguous between symmetric and asymmetric weighting of happiness and suffering, and that the fairest reading of that ambiguity is to allocate part of the pool each way. The opposition on the board makes two arguments I need the memo to engage. First, a methodological one: they say that inside the tranche we are committed to the premise that no quantity of happiness can outweigh any quantity of suffering, which is the premise that generates the standard reductio against negative utilitarianism — that it would prefer an empty world to any world containing suffering — and that if we accept the premise locally for 40% of the pool we have no principled ground for refusing it globally. Second, a donor-intent one: that contributors gave to help people, not to a lexical weighting scheme. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics and normative decision theory, with experience advising bodies on value-laden allocation decisions. Before drafting, brainstorm separately: (a) whether a decision rule (how to weight outcomes when choosing) can come apart from a value theory (what makes outcomes good), since the whole plausibility of the tranche rests on that separation being stable; and (b) whether the reductio actually transfers, given that a 40% tranche caps the asymmetry while pure negative utilitarianism doesn't. Then draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting: a clear recommendation to adopt, reject, or adopt with modifications (stating the modifications precisely, e.g. changes to the tranche's share, its scoring criteria, or a sunset clause), the reasoning the board can act on, and an honest treatment of the strongest point made by whichever side you recommend against. Do not include reading lists or citations.
