After thirty years in condensed-matter physics I retired, and for the past two years I have been co-teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar called Symmetry in Physics and Metaphysics with a philosopher colleague. The students are a mix of physics majors who have never read a page of metaphysics and philosophy majors whose group theory is shaky. I am responsible for writing the take-home final, and I want the centrepiece essay question built around Max Black's universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres one mile apart. What I want to prevent is the standard recitation — Leibniz's Law, the spheres share every qualitative property, therefore the principle fails, done — because half the class could produce that without understanding anything.

The question should instead make the students confront what happens when the physicists' notion of a symmetry enters the debate. In seminar, the physicist students kept insisting that the 180-degree rotation swapping the two spheres is just gauge: it maps one description of the world onto another description of the same world, the way a global rotation of an isolated system is a redundancy of description rather than a change in the system. Their conclusion was that Black's world contains two spheres but no fact of the matter about which is which, and that the identity of indiscernibles fails only if you posit haecceitistic facts that no experiment, even in principle, could detect. The philosophy students retorted that this begs the question: treating the swap as a mere re-description already assumes there is nothing to the spheres over and above the total qualitative facts, which is precisely the anti-haecceitism the argument was supposed to establish. My colleague then sketched for the class what she called the weak discernibility manoeuvre: the spheres share every monadic property, yet each bears to the other the irreflexive relation of being one mile from a sphere, a relation nothing bears to itself, so the spheres are discernible after all and the principle survives in weakened form.

Please produce three things. First, the essay question itself, suitable for a 1500-word take-home answer, worded so that the student must adjudicate among the gauge-symmetry anti-haecceitist, the question-begging objection, and the weak-discernibility rescue rather than merely describe them in turn. Second, a model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite stance. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit moves, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the mistake students most commonly make at that point, together with a short statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer — where the B answer is one that accurately characterises all three positions but never notices, for example, that the weak-discernibility response itself owes us an account of why relations holding between the spheres rather than of each alone should be allowed to ground distinctness, or that the physicist's symmetry argument and the anti-haecceitist's conclusion may be less equivalent than both parties assume.
