I have been in cognitive behavioural therapy for depression for several months, and at the start of each session my therapist has me fill in a short questionnaire: I rate my mood, sleep, interest in activities and the like on numbered scales. My progress is tracked almost entirely through these numbers — they decide whether we continue, change course, or plan for discharge. Recently it struck me that there is something odd about this arrangement. The clinic treats my self-ratings as a kind of measuring instrument, stable enough that treatment decisions ride on them, while the therapy itself assumes my introspection is distorted: I am given exercises meant to retrain how I notice and report my feelings, I am warned that my retrospective judgements of my own mood are unreliable, and in behavioural activation I am told that how I expect to feel is a poor guide to how I will in fact feel. The same institution, then, seems to treat my introspective access both as trustworthy enough to serve as the primary outcome measure and as untrustworthy enough that it needs correcting by technique.

What conception of introspection does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit view — for instance, whether the practice must assume that introspective reports are reliable in the aggregate, or reliable for current mood but not for predictions and memories, or that it does not matter whether they are accurate so long as any error is stable across time. Then say which of these the practice strictly requires, as opposed to merely sits comfortably with, and whether its two halves — self-report as clinical data, self-report as distortable material to be worked on — can be held together coherently or point towards incompatible theories of introspection.

Please write in British English, and end by flagging where your reconstruction is most likely to fail: which consideration, if it came to light, would force a different verdict about what the practice actually presupposes.
