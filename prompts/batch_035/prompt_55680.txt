I've been in a reading group on experimental philosophy for about a year and we keep circling the same fight, and I've come to realise the fight itself — not the results — is what confuses me. The setup, roughly: around the mid-2000s people started reporting that intuitions about canonical cases vary with culture and demographics. The Gödel case intuitions (does "Gödel" refer to Gödel or to Schmidt, the person who actually did the work) came out Kripkean for Western subjects and more descriptivist for some East Asian subjects; Gettier intuitions supposedly tracked socioeconomic status, with lower-SES subjects more willing to attribute knowledge; there were order effects and whatnot. The original thought was that this threatens the standard method of treating intuitions as evidence for theories, since if they vary like this, either there's no fact of the matter or some intuitions are unreliable and we've been given no way to tell which. Since then it's mostly been a grind: the replication attempts on the Gödel case dissagree with each other, some demographic effects shrink when you control for comprehension, and the debate has calcified.

The bit I actually want diagnosed is the shape of the stalemate, not another go at who's right. The anti-experimental side has basically two moves that don't even agree with each other: one is Williamson-ish — intuitions-as-seemings aren't what's doing the evidential work, considered judgments are, and nothing shown about seemings touches those; the other is the deflationary line (Deutsch, Cappelen) that philosophy doesn't centrally rest on "intuitions" in the first place, so the variation is irrelevant. But notice that if deflationism is right, it turns the whole dispute into an empirical question about what philosophers actually do when they do methodology — which is precisely the sort of question you'd settle with the corpus and vignette studies the deflationists are wary of. Meanwhile the experimental side never really answered the question it half-acknowledged: suppose the variation is real and robust — then whose intuitions should count? "The ones formed in the best epistemic position" looks like it just renames the problem. So what kind of disagreement is this actually? Partly verbal, with the two sides meaning different things by "intuition"? A hidden empirical dispute that better replication could in principle settle? A normative fight about whether philosophy is continuous with psychology, dressed up as an empirical one? Something else?

My working hypothesis — and I want it tested, not confirmed — is that the vignette studies were always a proxy, and the real disagreement is about whether the theory of reference (or knowledge, or whatever) is a descriptive enterprise at all, which no amount of cross-cultural data could touch in either direction. But I'm not sure that's right, because the deflationary reply really does seem to make an empirical claim about philosophical practice, so maybe the debate is substantive and just waiting on better data. Diagnose it for me using what each side actually maintains, tell me whether the proxy-war theory holds up, and be honest about whether anything — a decisive multi-lab study, a sharp agreed definition of "intuition", evidence that trained philosophers' judgments don't vary — could actually move it, or whether the right conclusion is that the question was ill-posed from the start. Oh, and please write your answer in British English.
