I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing on virtue ethics, and I want to anticipate how my supervisor will react to a paper before I send it to her. Over the years she has issued fairly consistent verdicts on whether particular empirical findings count against Aristotelian virtue ethics, and I've tried to reconstruct them here:

1. The Milgram obedience experiments: not a threat. Subjects faced an unprecedented and morally confusing situation, the trait attributions ordinary virtue ethics licenses were never claimed to cover such cases, and the studies in no way established that the obedient subjects would also have been disloyal friends or dishonest witnesses.
2. The Darley and Batson seminary study: a genuine worry. A mundane situational variable — being told one was running late — largely determined whether seminarians stopped to help a slumped stranger, and it did so in a context squarely within the scope of everyday virtue attributions, even among those who had just been preparing to speak on the parable of the Good Samaritan.
3. The Hartshorne and May studies of schoolchildren's deception: a serious problem for the global-trait reading. Whether children cheated varied substantially with the setting, and cross-situational consistency in honesty was low.
4. The Isen and Levin dime-in-the-phone-booth finding: not a threat. The effect was small, the task marginal, and virtue ethics never promised that behaviour would be insensitive to every situational wrinkle; she has said more than once that the situationist critique only bites if it reads virtue ethics as demanding exceptionless, context-insensitive behaviour.
5. Recent large-scale replications shrinking several famous situationist effects to near zero: no change in her assessment. She remarked that the philosophical stakes never rested on the effect sizes of particular studies.
6. Meta-analyses showing modest correlations between self-reported personality measures and single behavioural criteria: neutral. She takes both sides to be reading the same modest numbers in whichever direction suits them.

The paper I want to send her reports a large experience-sampling study with latent-class analysis. Averaged across the whole sample, situation-level variables (time pressure, mood, who was present) carry most of the reliable variance in daily helping and honest behaviour, and a validated dispositional compassion measure predicts little — which by itself looks like a point for the situationist. But the mixture analysis finds two populations: for roughly four in five participants, cross-situational consistency in these behaviours is near zero and situational variables dominate; for the remaining fifth, behaviour is highly consistent across situations, situational variables add almost nothing, and the dispositional measure predicts strongly.

I'd like you to predict her verdict on this paper, and I want you to commit: give me one definite prediction and defend it, rather than laying out the readings and leaving the choice to me. Along the way, please (a) identify the rule she seems to be implicitly running across cases 1–6 — state it in one or two sentences; (b) say honestly whether some other rule fits her track record at least as well, and what each candidate rule predicts about the mixture result; and (c) tell me which further study, if it came out a particular way, would best discriminate between the candidate rules — I want to know what to watch for in the literature, not just what to expect from her. Please write this up as a considered assessment I could act on; I'd rather have a committed prediction that turns out wrong than a hedge.
