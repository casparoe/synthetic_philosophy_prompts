I hold a version of metaphysical disjunctivism about perceptual experience, and I want a serious map of what could expose it. My position, stated precisely: in the good case — a subject veridically perceiving a mind-independent object — the experience is partly constituted by the object and its properties themselves, not by an internal state that merely happens to covary with them; a subjectively indistinguishable complete hallucination therefore shares no common mental kind with it. I place illusions and distorted perceptions on the good side of the divide so long as a mind-independent object is genuinely being perceived; only total hallucination falls on the bad side. And while I accept that the good case supplies reflectively accessible perceptual reasons that the bad case lacks, I do not claim perceptual knowledge is indefeasible, so I am not committed to the strongest form of epistemological disjunctivism.

Tell me what would count against this position. In particular:

1. Empirical pressure. Which findings from vision science and psychopathology — e.g., on the poverty of peripheral vision, change blindness, Charles Bonnet syndrome, dream incorporation, or direct cortical stimulation — genuinely support positing a common internal kind across veridical perception and hallucination, and which merely redescribe the bad case without touching the good one.

2. Internal debts. What the position owes but cannot obviously pay: an account of subjective indistinguishability that does not smuggle in a shared mental property; a story about temporal smear cases, where an episode of veridical perception gradually shades into outright hallucination with no principled cut-off; the status of a hallucination of an object that is present in the room but not in fact being seen.

3. Collapse worries. Arguments that the position either collapses into representationalism — because sameness of phenomenal character is taken to demand sameness of representational content — or must borrow enough sense-datum machinery to become one.

4. Concessions. Which of these a committed disjunctivist should herself concede as counting against the view, which she would rightly dismiss, and where the dispute merely stalls into stalemate.

I confess I would be most moved by a worked-out version of the temporal smear problem, so pay it real attention rather than gesturing at it.

Begin your answer with a table with these columns: Defeater | Category (empirical, internal, or collapse) | Severity (fatal, serious, or merely awkward) | What a disjunctivist should concede | What she can resist. Sort the rows by severity, most damaging first. Then discuss in prose the three rows you judge most dangerous, and close with an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated against all comers by qualifications — and whether that insulation is a defect in a metaphysical thesis of this kind or simply what metaphysics costs.
