Been arguing with my labmate about what actually counts as alive (it started over whether the protocell paper we were reading was describing a living thing or a very elaborate chemical demonstration) and instead of another shouting match I figured I'd just lay out my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see if there's a principle behind them. So, my verdicts:

1. A mule: alive, obviously, even though it's sterile. Reproduction can't be required, fine.
2. A worker honeybee: alive, also sterile. But the hive as a whole — I waver. Some days the colony feels like the organism and the bees are more like cells, but I can't tell if that's a real verdict or just a metaphor I like.
3. A virus floating outside a cell: not alive. The same virus mid-takeover inside a cell: still not alive I think, though the infected cell as a whole seems alive, just very sick and making virus parts.
4. A bacterial spore frozen in permafrost for 30,000 years that revives when thawed: alive the whole time, even though during the freeze there was literally zero metabolism happening.
5. Fire: not alive, even though it "eats" fuel, grows, spreads to new fuel, and maintains itself against the surroundings. Everyone says this and I agree, but I want a reason that isn't just "fire isn't fancy enough."
6. A crystal growing in a saturated solution: not alive.
7. A self-sustaining autocatalytic reaction network in a flow reactor, the kind that regenerates its own catalysts: I genuinely don't know. This is the case I keep flip-flopping on.
8. A self-replicating RNA strand sitting in a test tube with free nucleotides: the RNA by itself doesn't feel alive to me, but the tube as a whole (RNA plus building blocks plus energy gradient) feels closer to alive than the RNA alone, which is a weird verdict to have.
9. A glider in Conway's Game of Life, or one of those big self-replicating patterns people have built in it: not alive, fairly confident, but can't say why when it satisfies reproduction and persists in a hostile environment better than most bacteria do.
10. A computer virus: not alive. But a designed self-replicating automaton with heritable variation and mutation, left to run in a simulation where lineages evolve new survival strategies, the artificial life stuff like Tierra — honestly if you showed me a run like that I'd feel a pull toward yes. Which bugs me, because it seems like I said no in case 9.
11. A freeze-dried tardigrade in its tun state: alive.
12. A mature red blood cell: alive-ish? It has no nucleus, can't reproduce, but it does have active metabolism. Maybe like a very commited worker bee. Except the spore in case 4 had no metabolism and I called it alive, so what does the red cell have that the spore doesn't... you see my problem.

I know the NASA working definition everybody cites, the self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution one — the mule kills the Darwinian part and the spore kills the self-sustaining part, so I don't think that's it either.

So: is there a principle (metabolism? individuality? being part of an evolving lineage? some autonomy or homeostasis thing?) that captures all or most of these? Tell me which verdicts it overturns, and specifically whether I can keep the virus one and the artificial-life one at the same time, because those are the two I'm most attached to and I suspect they're incompatible. Also tell me what it says about the autocatalytic reactor and the hive, since those are the two I can't decide on my own.
