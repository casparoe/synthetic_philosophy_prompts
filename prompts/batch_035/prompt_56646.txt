I teach a senior seminar in the philosophy of physics: twelve students who have completed one semester of quantum mechanics, so they can read a state vector and know that amplitudes are complex numbers whose squared moduli sum to one, but who have not studied decoherence or decision theory. I have a single 90-minute session on the measurement problem, and I want to spend it on the Born rule specifically — not the textbook statement, but why it is a live embarrassment for the Everettian picture: if every outcome of a measurement actually happens somewhere, what could it even mean to say that one outcome has probability 0.97 and another 0.03? Design an in-class simulation of repeated measurement in a branching universe with the following properties.

The students should be placed inside branches — sorted and resorted into groups by an opaque procedure that I run from a master ledger of amplitude weights that no branch is permitted to see — so that from within any branch, no student can tell whether they are numerous or rare, heavily weighted or nearly negligible. Before the first branching, students should have to commit to something whose fairness they will later have to defend: posted odds, token-currency bets, or recorded confidence ratings. The question of how to weight branches should arrive as their problem, not as my lecture. At some point the design should force a collision between two ways of counting — how many branches there are versus how much amplitude each one carries — and the students should notice that these two measures come apart without my announcing it. Ideally the moment of noticing is spread across several rounds, so that different groups arrive at different conflict points and have to argue with each other about whose bookkeeping reflects reality.

Then a debrief that names what happened: why naive branch counting gives the wrong statistics, why the move of saying I was always going to end up in a typical branch looks circular the moment someone asks who decides which branches count as typical, and how the decision-theoretic program tries to change the subject from counting branches to what a rational agent confined to one branch should prefer, and what it costs to make that move. Map specific moments in the activity onto each of these positions rather than presenting them as free-standing views.

Please specify the materials I need, the rules, a round-by-round timeline for the full session, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which features of the real problem no classroom-sized activity with finitely many students can reproduce — I have in mind that the number of branches is not even well-defined in the theory, but tell me what else gets lost, so I can decide what to state myself in the last five minutes.
