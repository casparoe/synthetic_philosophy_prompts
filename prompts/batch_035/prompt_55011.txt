I'm a PhD student in comparative political economy, currently on leave interning at the economics ministry of my home country, which is one of those mid-sized places where maybe a third of the economy still runs through state-owned enterprises and administered prices. Every few months we get a proposal to either liberalize some activity (let the price float, tender it out, let private firms in) or to keep it planned and centralized, and right now the office decides these things in a completely ad hoc way, basically whoever argues loudest in the Tuesday meeting. My director, half-joking but also kind of not, told me to turn all that Hayek stuff I keep going on about into something the unit can actually run on a proposal, and since I'm the one who did the reading, that's apparently my job now.

The reading in question is the old socialist calculation debate. Mises's argument from the 1920s was that without genuine markets for capital goods and inputs there's no rational way to calculate what anything's worth, Lange replied in the mid-30s that a planning board can find prices by trial and error, adjusting them in response to shortages and gluts the way markets supposedly do, and Hayek then moved the goalposts to the knowledge itself being dispersed, local, and often tacit, so prices matter less as solutions to an equation than as signals that let strangers coordinate without anyone having the full picture. What I want is a decision procedure my team can run on a sector or activity before we recommend liberalize vs. keep it planned: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "keep it" or a "price it," and where it honestly throws up its hands and hands the decision to the minister because the remaining question is equity or national security or some other values question that no amount of Hayek resolves. The ministry folks are economists but not theory people, and the whole thing has to fit in a memo they can read in fifteen minutes, so the translation matters more than the scholarship, and you should say plainly where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as a checklist.

Then run it on two live-ish cases from our docket. One is bulk irrigation water: no world reference price, the relevant knowledge is hyper-local (soil, aquifer levels, cropping patterns), but the good itself is homogeneous and the stakes are public health and food security, so half the office thinks markets here are obviously insane. The other is government-wide IT procurement: world prices exist for hardware and licenses, the specs look codifiable on paper, but the knowledge about what users actually need lives with people who can't articulate it and shows up only after the contract is signed. Fair warning, I definately have a dog in the fight on water, I want it kept out of markets, so if your procedure says otherwise, tell me straight instead of massaging the questions to get my preferred answer. And since I'll be putting my name on this memo, please keep what's genuinely settled in the debate separate from what people who know the literature still fight about -- I'm aware there's no consensus that the whole thing was ever "won," or about what the planned economies of the last century actually demonstrate -- and don't let contested interpretive calls appear as if they were consensus. One last thing: the minister reads prose, not decks, so please write the procedure itself as continuous prose like an email or letter, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, however tempting the checklist format is.
