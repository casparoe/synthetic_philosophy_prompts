The thing I came to defend is the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism (the Rawls/Nozick-ish point that utilitarianism treats society as one giant person with one giant pot of utility, and thereby misses that individual people live individual lives -- the losses of the man who loses don't somehow get compensated by the gains of the people who gain, because he never feels them). I still believe that. But here's what happened when I tried to formalize WHY aggregation is supposed to be the crime:

(1) Utilitarianism is objectionable because it aggregates: it lets one person's loss be canceled out by other people's gains, as though all utilities flowed into a single pot.
(2) What makes this a mistake is that distinct persons are distinct loci of experience: the one who sacrifices never experiences the payoffs going to the others, and no one experiences the net sum.
(3) My future self and my current self are also distinct loci of experience in exactly that sense. The version of me doing the painful rehab this month will never experience the payoff -- by the time the knee works again, that sufferer is gone and only the beneficiary is around; during the suffering, the benefit exists only as an expectation, which is not the thing itself.
(4) The separateness objection is supposed to be a deep structural claim about when aggregation is permissible, not a shallow census fact about how many bodies are in the room. If it's the distinctness of experiential perspectives that does the work, it works identically between my time slices as between persons.
(5) So the objection also condemns prudence: sacrificing your Tuesday self for your Friday self, or your 30-year-old self for your retirement, is the same crime, and saving for retirement is structurally identical to harvesting one person's organs to save five.
(6) But prudence is obviously permissible -- arguably paradigmatically rational.
(7) So either (2) is false and distinct perspectives aren't what's doing the work, or (4) is false and the objection secretly leans on there being one PERSON over time rather than one experiencer at a time -- in which case it needs a theory of diachronic identity robust enough to carry moral weight, and I have a bad feeling about what Parfit's reductionism does to that.

Where I keep getting stuck: my advisor floated a rescue along the lines of "single agency" -- aggregation is okay when the earlier self can act on behalf of the later self under a shared plan, and not otherwise. But I can act for the sake of future strangers too (I can donate to disaster relief for people who don't exist yet), and conversely the fission people claim prudential concern survives even when there's no determinate fact about whether it's the same person. And a friend in the econ department keeps saying the utilitarian just bites the bullet: prudence is prudent, not morally required, and maybe there's no duty to sacrifice across time either -- so much the worse, she says, for framing this as permission vs prohibition. Which honestly might be a real position and not just contrarianism, I can't tell.
