Some background so you know what you are working with: I spent my career in physiology and have only lately begun reading philosophy of mind in earnest, so treat me as informed about the experimental side and newly arrived on the conceptual side. I want a thought experiment built to order, and I want it assembled entirely from real, well-replicated psychophysical and neuropsychological phenomena — no artificial minds, no futuristic devices, no thought- experiment machinery that could not in principle sit in a lab today. The point of the case is to force a disagreement between global workspace theory, in its broad functional reading, and the distinction Ned Block draws between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness.

The specification is a matched pair of occasions for a single subject. On the first occasion, the subject should meet every functional criterion a workspace theory could reasonably demand — the information is globally available to report, reasoning, and memory — while sincerely and stably denying any visual experience. On the second, matched occasion, the subject should fail the broadcast criterion, in the workspace theorist's own terms, while insisting there was a definite, faint but real experience of the stimulus. Plausible ingredients include Sperling's partial-report paradigm and the fate of iconic memory, masked priming below the threshold of report, and residual vision in blindsight-type patients, though you may substitute other ordinary phenomena. The two occasions should be as alike as possible except for the workspace variable. If you find that one or both occasions cannot be built from real phenomena without smuggling in a stipulation, do not pretend otherwise: build the nearest stable version, and mark exactly what you changed and why. I would rather hear that the case is unstable at some joint than have the joint papered over.

After presenting the pair, I would like four things. First, what a workspace theorist should conclude about each occasion, and what someone taking the access/phenomenal distinction seriously should conclude. Second, an identification of which single feature of the case is doing the work in producing the divergence. Third, the most plausible line of resistance available to the workspace theorist against the unfavorable occasion — for instance, that the subject's report is an unreliable guide, or that some weaker notion of broadcast is present after all — together with what that resistance costs the theory. Fourth, and most important to me given how new this literature is: keep two ledgers strictly separate. One lists the ingredients that are settled empirical findings; the other lists what is genuinely contested among people who know the field — for example, whether subjects in the partial-report paradigm are conscious of more than they can report is itself disputed, as is whether workspace accounts are meant as theories of consciousness at all or only of its reportable aspect. Do not present any contested reading as the consensus, and where the field itself has not settled a question the case leans on, say so plainly rather than resolving it by fiat.
