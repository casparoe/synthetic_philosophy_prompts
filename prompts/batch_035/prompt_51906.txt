I'm in a little book club that mostly reads novels but last month we went off-script and read a pop psychology book, and at the discussion one member, Dan, gave the rest of us this confident rundown of moral foundations theory. He said liberals and conservatives don't just disagree about values — they literally perceive different moral dimensions. Everyone has gut intuitions about care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity/purity, but liberals build their morality almost entirely on the first two while conservatives draw on all five, so conservative moral perception is more complete, like a person who can see five colors versus someone who only sees two. He also said the research shows moral judgments are just post-hoc rationalizations of these gut feelings — the reasoning comes after, like a press secretary — so you can't really argue anyone across the aisle into seeing loyalty or sanctity as genuine moral goods; it would be like arguing a colorblind person into seeing red. He capped it off with the line that gets repeated a lot in this space, morality binds and blinds, and concluded that cross-partisan moral debate is basically pointless, you're not exchanging reasons, you're just running different software.

I found the whole thing pretty persuasive in the moment, especially the color analogy, but the "no point debating" conclusion felt like a cop-out and I couldn't articulate why. Can you audit this for me? Like, what parts would a psychologist who actually works on this stuff accept, which are stretches or conflations, and is the "conservatives perceive more of morality" claim actually a finding of the research or Dan adding a spin on top? I'm especially fuzzy on whether the descriptive part — assuming it's even right about which groups lean on which foundations — is supposed to tell us anything about who's *right* on the moral questions, or whether it only explains why the arguments keep stalling. If you could seperate what the theory claims about why we judge from what it could possibly say about whether our judgements are correct, that would help, cause that distinction feels like its doing all the work in Dan's conclusion and nobody at book club could nail it down.
