I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm currently drafting a review of a popular book about physics for a general readership. The chapter on what scientific revolutions leave behind includes this summary of structural realism, which I'd be implicitly endorsing in my review unless I have reason not to:

"Structural realism is the view that what science gets right, across all its revolutions, is not the things it talks about but the pattern of relations between them. When a theory is overturned, the objects it posited go with it — phlogiston, caloric, the ether — but the mathematics survives. Fresnel's equations for light were carried over essentially unchanged into Maxwell's theory, so what Fresnel had actually discovered was the structure of light rather than its substance. On this view the electron is not really a thing at all, just a node in a web of relations, and if physics someday discards electrons the way it discarded the ether, structural realism will have seen it coming. John Worrall, who revived the view in 1989, showed that this is the only version of realism compatible with the actual history of science: earlier realisms were all refuted by theory change, so a realist today must either become a structuralist or concede that science does not describe the world."

Before I put anything of the sort in print, I want to know how much of this I can rely on. Would you go through the passage sentence by sentence and sort it into what a philosopher of science working on this debate would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would say is simply wrong? My own suspicion is that the sentence about electrons is doing more work than the passage lets on — it seems to slide from a claim about what we can know about nature into a claim about what nature contains — but I don't know the literature well enough to tell whether the book is presenting one view or quietly merging two. I'd also like your judgment on whether the passage's conclusion follows from its accurate parts: my review will end up repeating whatever verdict on realism this summary supports, so I need to know whether that verdict is earned or whether it leans on the distorted bits. And please distinguish genuine errors from standard popular simplifications — I want to be fair to the author and not hold a book of this kind to standards the genre doesn't pretend to meet.
