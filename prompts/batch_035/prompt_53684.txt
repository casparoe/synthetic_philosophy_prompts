I am the non-voting secretary of the three-member editorial board of a small philosophy journal, and I am responsible for recording in the minutes what the board decided at our last meeting and on what grounds. The board's acceptance rule states that a submission is accepted only if it is both methodologically sound and an original contribution. We voted on each premise separately. Petra voted yes on both. Renata, the chair, voted yes on soundness and no on originality. Theo voted no on soundness and yes on originality. So a majority affirmed soundness, a majority affirmed originality, and only one member supported acceptance.

After the vote, Renata and Theo argued about what the board had actually decided, and since the minutes must take a position, they have left me to referee. Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Renata: The board decides by majority vote on the question put to it. The question is whether to accept the paper, that vote went two to one against, and the paper is rejected.

(2) Theo: That ignores the rule the board actually adopted. A majority judged the paper sound and a majority judged it original, so the board's acceptance criteria are satisfied by the board's own lights. If we now reject, the board's verdict contradicts the considerations the board itself endorses. Collectively, we have said yes, yes, and no.

(3) Renata: Groups don't endorse anything; only their members do. Consistency is a demand on individual believers, and each of us is perfectly consistent — I accept that the paper is sound but not original, Theo accepts that it is original but not sound, Petra accepts both. Tallies aren't anyone's beliefs, so nobody has contradicted anybody.

(4) Theo: Yet you propose that the board issue a decision, sign a rejection letter over the board's name, and answer for it in the annual report. If the board can do all that, it can hold the attitudes such actions presuppose; and if it can't, then your own motion is not a board decision either, just three private opinions wearing a letterhead.

(5) Renata: Then consider what your premise-by-premise procedure actually buys. Had we put the question as a single proposition — is the paper both sound and original? — it would have lost two to one. Same board, same views, opposite verdict, with nothing different except how the question was chopped up. Your procedure doesn't read off the board's reasons; it reads off the choice of whoever divides the agenda.

(6) Theo: Your procedure has the same sensitivity in reverse — you could flip the outcome by insisting on premise voting — so framing cuts against you as much as against me. The difference is that premise voting keeps the verdict answerable to the reasons actually given: every objection raised in discussion was an objection to one premise or the other, and each premise survived a majority vote. On your procedure nothing survives except a tally.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: say who is right at each step, and to what degree. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are overlooking, and advise me on what the minutes should record as the board's decision and as its grounds.
