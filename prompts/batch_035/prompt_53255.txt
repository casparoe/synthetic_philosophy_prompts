My partner in a decision theory reading group has an argument that Good's theorem — the result that a single decision-maker facing a fixed decision problem can never lose expected utility by accepting costless information before acting, since after the observation she can always fall back on her old best act — somehow refutes itself once we move from one agent against nature to two agents against each other. I have been unable to name the bad step, and I would like your diagnosis.

Here is the argument as he gives it. (1) Good's theorem is proved about an abstract setup: a set of acts, a set of states carrying a fixed probability distribution, utilities depending only on act and state, and an agent who chooses optimally after any free observation. The proof never asks what the states are made of. (2) When I face a rational opponent, her move is, from my point of view, just one more unknown quantity: a state whose distribution is given by her mixed strategy. (3) So Good's theorem applies in games: free information about the opponent's move can never lower my expected utility. (4) Sanity check: in matching pennies with no spies, my expected payoff is zero; if I alone receive a reliable tip about the other player's move just before we both commit, my expected payoff per round jumps to nearly half a dollar. No contradiction so far, since the opponent has no tip. (5) But now suppose the arrangement is symmetric: each player is given a spy who, with high reliability, reports the other player's move in the same window before moves are locked in. By (3), each player should reckon the arrangement weakly good for herself, and by symmetry, if the value of the information is strictly positive for one player, it is strictly positive for both. (6) Yet the game is zero-sum: whatever I expect to gain, the opponent expects to lose, so the two players' values for the information must sum to zero, and by symmetry they are equal — hence both zero. (7) That contradicts (5), and my friend finds the escape of "exactly zero for both" incredible, since nearly the same tip was worth half a dollar a round when only one player had it, and he can see no reason why duplicating it should annihilate its value. He concludes that Good's theorem, or the expected-utility framework beneath it, is inconsistent in strategic settings — that the theory of the value of information entails a free lunch whenever two evenly matched players acquire the same intelligence advantage.

For my part, I would very much like to keep (1): Good's theorem in the single-agent, fixed-states case seems rock solid, and I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in (2)–(3), where the opponent's move is promoted to a "state." But I cannot make the complaint precise, and I would also like to know what the two spying players should actually expect once they play the game out. Which step do you reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think the argument is sound through to its conclusion, say so and tell me what you are prepared to accept.
