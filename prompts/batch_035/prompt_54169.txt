I sit on the Investigations Committee of a small learned society, founded in the tradition of Henry Sidgwick's work with the Society for Psychical Research, whose remit is the scientific examination of alleged communications from the dead. A decade-long study has just concluded, and at next month's meeting the committee must decide what to do with the final report. Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate beforehand.

The study: for eleven years the society supervised sittings with an automatic-writing medium — a retired schoolteacher who charged no fee and gave sittings only in members' presence. Scripts were sealed and dated; sitters were instructed not to feed information. Of roughly four hundred scripts, a scoring committee rated twelve as containing specific, veridical details about deceased persons, details the medium could not plausibly have acquired by ordinary means. Against this: the criteria for counting something as a hit were fixed only after the scripts had been read, so the impressive dozen were selected post hoc from a much larger pool; the strongest scripts cluster around the sitters with the closest personal relationships to the medium; and an audit found that the medium had, months before one of the best scripts, spent a week in the town where one of the deceased persons had lived. No fraud was ever detected in a supervised sitting, and the statistician on the committee maintains that even after conservative correction the aggregate remains improbable on the hypothesis of chance alone.

The committee is split three ways. One faction wants the report published with the society's endorsement of survival-communication as at least a working hypothesis; one wants the raw data published with no endorsement, inviting outside scrutiny; one wants the whole thing archived while a prospective study is designed with scoring criteria fixed in advance of any sitting.

Draft the memo. It should recommend one course of action, with a concrete time frame the committee could adopt, and it should argue the recommendation from Sidgwick's own position on psychical research: his insistence that the survival question be treated as an empirical matter rather than settled a priori in either direction; his twin fears, of credulity on the one side and of a dogmatic refusal to examine on the other; and above all his account of how individually dismissible testimony can nonetheless accumulate — on his picture, no single sitting should convince anyone, yet dismissing all of them at once seems to require a general assumption of unreliability that would itself stand in need of evidence. The case forces two questions on that picture, and the memo should confront both rather than gesture at them. First, whether a learned society's standards for certifying a conclusion to the public should differ from the standards a rational individual would need before believing it — arguably the core Sidgwickian question, since he spent his career torn between what the evidence warranted for him and what the Society could responsibly say. Second, whether his aggregation principle has the resources to handle selection effects: whether the roughly four hundred unrated scripts must be counted against the hypothesis rather than set aside as merely inconclusive, and whether retrospective scoring corrupts the very cumulativity the Sidgwickian relies on — since a dozen hits chosen after reading a large pool may show nothing more than that something memorable always turns up in a large enough pool.

Give the strongest consideration on whichever side you end up rejecting its full weight; the committee will not forgive a memo that has not read its opponents. And do not pad the memo with references — argue from the position, not from the literature.
