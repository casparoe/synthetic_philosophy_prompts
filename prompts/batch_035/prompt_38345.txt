I've been convinced for a while now that updatelessness is basically right -- you choose policies rather than acts, pick the policy you'd have endorsed before you knew what you'd observe, and that's what makes you pay in counterfactual mugging, one-box in Newcomb, etc. Fine. But lately I've been worrying about a specific way this could all come apart, and I want you to tell me what would actually count against the view -- including whether my worry is one of the real defeaters or just me being confused.

Here's my gloss on the view so we're on the same page: I think the right version of updatelessness isn't only about observations. You should also be updateless with respect to logical/mathematical facts you haven't computed yet -- act as if you dont know the output of your own computation before you run it. And you should be updateless sequentially: in a game where you observe stuff between moves, you commit up front to a whole policy tree, not to a per-decision updateless choice at each node. That combination feels forced to me, since each piece seems to follow from the others.

So what would count against it? I want the real defeaters, ranked by how much they'd move someone who actually holds the view, not just bullets from critics. Things I half-know matter: does full logical updatelessness mean you just ignore true information forever, like a paranoid precommitment you can never renegotiate (the "why can't I make the best decision now using everything I know" intuition); is there even a coherent notion of the prior standpoint you're being updateless *from* once logical facts are in play, or does it get circular? Also whether the view is testable at all -- it keeps winning thought experiments it was partly designed to win, and I can't tell if that's cheap. And specifically about my sequential-commitment bit: if you've committed to a policy tree and then observe something the tree didn't anticipate, what does the view say to do? Is the standard answer (adopt the policy you would have wanted to have adopted about such situations) a real answer, or a hand-wave that re-imports ordinary conditional reasoning through the back door?

Be honest about which of these a committed updateless theorist would concede as counting against them versus absorbing with a qualification. And if you think the view's insulation from disconfirmation is itself the biggest problem, say so, and say whether that insulation disqualifies it as a normative theory of choice or is just what you'd expect from one.
