I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a large Introduction to Ethics course, and I've hit a grading dispute that I'd like you to dramatize for a piece I'm writing for our department's pedagogy workshop on how philosophical content leaks into routine grading decisions. The exam question asked students to put an argument into premise–conclusion form and assess its validity, where the argument ran: tormenting the cat for fun is wrong; if tormenting the cat for fun is wrong, then paying your little brother to torment the cat is wrong; therefore, paying your little brother to torment the cat is wrong. One student wrote that it is a straightforward instance of modus ponens and therefore valid. Another wrote that the premises "aren't the kind of thing that can be true or false — they just express disapproval of tormenting the cat — so the argument can't be valid, since validity requires premises that could be true." My co-TA, who is writing a dissertation defending moral realism, wants to give the first student full credit and the second student no credit for that answer. I've been reading about the Frege–Geach problem and suspect our grading session is about to turn into an ambush on expressivism: the student's bad answer is uncomfortably close to a real objection to my co-TA's preferred view of what the second student got wrong, and uncomfortably close to something a naive expressivist might actually have to say.

Write a dialogue between the two of us — call us Dana (the moral realist) and me, Priya (the expressivist, and the narrator whose sympathies are at stake) — set in the department common room late in the evening with a stack of blue books between us and a deadline for submitting grades. Dana should press the classic challenge hard: moral sentences seem to retain a stable contribution to validity and inconsistency when embedded in conditionals, negations, and disjunctions, and a view on which their unembedded uses merely express attitudes or prescriptions appears to have no account of what contribution that could be. Priya should not retreat into vagueness or concede the problem is fatal; she should develop at least two distinct expressivist repair strategies in real detail (for example, treating an embedded or conditionalized moral sentence as expressing a complex, higher-order state of mind — a disposition toward patterns of attitudes — rather than the simple unembedded one), and Dana should respond to each strategy on its merits with a specific difficulty rather than a wave of the hand. At some point Priya should also exploit the grading context itself: Dana must explain what credit the first student deserves, and whether the second student's answer deserves zero once we notice that it is a garbled version of a serious objection that Dana herself takes seriously in her own work.

Both characters must be equally competent and genuinely responsive to each other — no strawmen, no talking past each other, and no dramatic conversion at the end. The dialogue should be roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, should end unresolved but with the two of them identifying precisely what would count as progress (a specific claim one of them would have to defend or abandon), and should be written so that a reader who has never heard of the Frege–Geach problem could follow the whole exchange from the context and examples alone. You don't need citations or references to the literature; keep names of actual philosophers out of the dialogue itself, and let the philosophical substance carry the argument rather than footnotes.
