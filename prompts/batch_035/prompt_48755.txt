I'm a student in an upper-level seminar on Bayesian epistemology and information economics, and I need help with a slogan that has become our professor's all-purpose conversation-ender. Last week we hosted two guest forecasters — both thoughtful, both working from the same public data — and they gave us materially different probability estimates for the same event. Each had heard the other's number, and neither budged. One finally smiled and said they would just have to agree to disagree, at which point the professor declared that, by Aumann's agreement theorem, at least one of them must be irrational or ill-informed, and moved on. That inference felt far too quick to me, but I couldn't say precisely why — partly because I'm honestly not sure what the slogan is even asserting.

Please untangle it. Lay out, as a numbered menu ordered from weakest to strongest, the distinct precise theses that 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree' could be expressing. For each reading, I'd like a verdict on its status — tautology, genuine theorem, substantive but contestable philosophical claim, or outright false — and, importantly, a note on what each of the loaded words is doing in that reading. Specifically: whether 'rational' there means merely Bayesian updating from one's evidence, or additionally requires shared priors, or requires agents to know their own and each other's information partitions (since merely hearing your counterpart's number doesn't obviously make the posteriors common knowledge in the technical sense — and I'd like to know whether common knowledge of the posteriors was even achieved in our seminar scenario, or whether that's already a live escape route for the forecasters); whether 'agree' covers identical posteriors after full disclosure of evidence, identical posteriors from common knowledge alone, convergence after iteratively announcing estimates back and forth, or agreement extended to actions and willingness to bet; and whether 'cannot' rules out disagreement existing at a moment in time, or disagreement persisting after discussion, or disagreement being common knowledge — which are importantly different prohibitions.

Then close with two verdicts. First, which reading, if any, actually licenses my professor's move: does the theorem by itself entitle him to conclude that one forecaster is irrational, rather than the more modest conclusions that their priors differ, or that the posteriors were never common knowledge in the first place, or that neither is a perfectly Bayesian machine? Second, what would the forecasters actually have to do — stated concretely — for it to count as a violation of the theorem rather than a mere illustration of its fine print? Please keep it rigorous but self-contained, since I want to bring the menu to seminar and push back during office hours with confidence about which version of the claim I'm challenging.
