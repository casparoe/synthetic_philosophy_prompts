I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a long feature about the old fight over whether machine translation "understands" language. Ever since Hofstadter's Atlantic piece on the shallowness of Google Translate back in 2018 (and really since Le Ton beau de Marot, and before that the whole Chinese Room business), there's been this standoff between two camps: the shallowness camp, who think statistical/neural MT is sophisticated pattern-matching with no real grasp of meaning -- you can catch it out with poems, idioms, sentences needing world knowledge, the way you'd catch the old vodka-is-good-but-the-meat-is-rotten systems -- and the functionalist camp, who think understanding is a graded, capacity-based notion, that "understanding" isn't a magic on/off switch, and that a system that reliably handles context, disambiguation, register, and figurative language has nontrivial amounts of whatever that thing is. I've now interviewed people from both sides for the piece and honestly they barely engage each other: the shallowness people keep moving the goalposts to ever weirder sentences, and the functionalists keep pointing at benchmark scores that the other side considers irrelevant by construction. It's exactly the kind of stalled debate where nobody's predictions are ever written down in advance.

So here's what I want for the article: draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration -- maybe imagine it's the document produced at a workshop I'm fictionalizing where Hofstadter-style translators and working NLP researchers hashed it out over two days. Give me a numbered section of claims both sides can genuinely accept (about what translation systems actually do, about what "understanding" needs to mean for the question to even be coherent, about which older arguments they both think are dead), then a disagreements section where each disagreement is phrased in wording its own side would actually endorse -- no slanted framing. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, with for each crux a concrete test, case, or type of evidence BOTH sides would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down beforehand. I'd particularly like you to deal with whether any of this is purely verbal (e.g. if "understands" just gets defined differently by the two sides, say which part of the dispute dissolves and which part is substantive), and with the awkward fact that the debate has now outlived two architecture shifts -- statistical MT gave way to neural MT which gave way to LLMs, and each generation does better on the previous generation's gotcha sentences without the shallowness camp conceding anything. Also worth including: whether translation is even the right probe (maybe it's too constrained a task to seperate deep understanding from good mimicry), and whether a system could have understanding-of-what-the-sentence-means without understanding-what-it's-like-to-be-a-translator, if that distinction makes sense.

Format-wise: agreed claims as a numbered list, then a disagreements table (disagreement / shallowness-camp wording / functionalist wording), then cruxes with predictions. Two or three paragraphs of framing for the top of the document are fine, in the register of something both parties would put their name to, not a press release. Don't turn it into a history of the field -- the value for my article is that it makes the residual disagreement exact, so I can show readers exactly where the real fault line is.
