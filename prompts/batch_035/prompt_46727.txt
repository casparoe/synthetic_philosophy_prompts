My friend and I have just spent an entire evening arguing about scientific realism, and we ended at an impasse over a point neither of us could resolve: he was happy to grant that all our theories are strictly false — Newton's gravity no less than Einstein's — but insisted that calling Einstein's theory "closer to the truth" is empty rhetoric, since if both theories are false there is simply no fact of the matter about which is nearer to the truth, and the phrase is just a disguised way of saying we prefer one for pragmatic reasons. I would like to know whether he is right. Please explain the problem of verisimilitude: why "closeness to truth" is harder to define than it first appears, why the natural idea of comparing theories by their true and false consequences collapsed the way it did with Popper's original comparative definition (the Miller–Tichý result), and what any adequate account must deliver — for instance, whether it should satisfy something like the requirement that verisimilitude can increase only when truth content increases without falsity content increasing. I am less interested in the technical details of the proofs than in why the naive idea breaks down, and in what a workable analysis would have to achieve for my friend's deflationary hunch to be mistaken.
