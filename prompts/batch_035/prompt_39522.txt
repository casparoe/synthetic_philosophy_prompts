I lead the economic foresight unit inside a government ministry. Our job is to produce short assessment memos — never more than two pages — for a senior committee of officials, on questions ranging from the near-term (what a scheduled tax change will do to household borrowing) to the remote (how the structure of the economy might look in twenty years under some technological shift). None of the committee members has statistical training, and they read whatever we write as settled fact unless we signal otherwise; that is the recurring failure I want to fix. Twice this past year a memo attached a point estimate to something we had no real basis for a number on, and the committee mistook the spurious precision for competence.

I want a decision procedure my analysts can run in under an hour per memo, which tells them how to express the unit's confidence in each substantive claim. The grounding should come from Keynes. In his Treatise on Probability (1921) he argued that probability is a degree of rational belief rather than a frequency, that many probabilities cannot be given numbers even though they can still be compared with one another, and he distinguished the degree of a probability from what he called the weight of the argument behind it — the amount of relevant evidence one has, as opposed to how that evidence balances. Later, in his 1937 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper on the general theory of employment, he contrasted questions on which calculation is possible with those where, in his view, there is no basis for forming any calculable probability at all. I want the procedure to translate these ideas into an editorial rulebook, in a way that survives the simplification.

Concretely, the procedure should sort each substantive claim in a draft memo into one of four or so registers: a number or a range, where warranted; a bare comparison (more likely than not, somewhat less likely than its negation, and the like); an explicit statement that the question is open and the unit declines to quantify it; or a flag that the claim is not the unit's to make at all. For each register, it should give the tests to run in order: what to ask about the evidential base, what to ask about its weight, what counts as enough to move a claim up or down a register. One distinction that must survive from Keynes is that between having balanced evidence and having much evidence — a claim can sit at exactly fifty-fifty and be well supported, or sit at seventy-thirty and rest on almost nothing. The procedure must treat those differently. It should also mark, honestly, the points where an analyst cannot avoid a judgment call, rather than dressing the judgment up as a formula.

Then run the procedure on three worked cases. First: an estimate of how much a two-percentage-point change in a transaction tax will shift trading volume, drawing on several decades of studies of similar taxes in other jurisdictions. Second: a judgment on whether a newly offered retail investment product will behave benignly or dangerously in a severe downturn, based on a decade of data containing no severe downturn. Third: whether a permanent reorganization of global supply chains will, twenty years out, leave the domestic economy more or less resilient to shocks. For each case, give the register the procedure assigns, the reasons, and the sentence the memo should actually contain.

I expect one of these to produce an answer my analysts and the committee will dislike — most likely the third, where I suspect the honest register is the refusal to quantify on exactly the question the committee most wants a number for. Show that the procedure still yields that answer, and explain how I should defend it upward.

Finally, append a short self-assessment: how confident you are that the thresholds and tests you chose are faithful to what Keynes actually held — especially the notion of weight, which his interpreters argue over — and which single step of your procedure you are least sure of.
