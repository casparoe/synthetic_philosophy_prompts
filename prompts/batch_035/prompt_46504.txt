I'm currently TA-ing an introductory decision theory course, and this week's section covers the St. Petersburg paradox. When I laid out the standard resolution — that diminishing marginal utility tames the game, since a sufficiently concave utility function renders the expected utility of entry finite — one of my students pushed back in a way I found hard to answer on my feet. She said her previous instructor had claimed the logarithmic-utility "fix" was never really a fix at all, and muttered something about how a determined game designer can always outrun any concavity assumption you pick. I'd like to give her a concrete demonstration of that point, and I'd rather it be a properly engineered case than a hand-wave.

So here's what I want you to build: a variant of the St. Petersburg game that survives the diminishing-marginal-utility solution. Specifically, in your game an agent with logarithmic utility of wealth must still face infinite expected utility from playing — the concavity fix should simply not apply. Constraints: the randomizing device is just fair coin flips, the payoffs are in ordinary money, there are no demons, no perfect predictors, and no infinitely wealthy house; and the whole game should be explainable in one paragraph to someone with no decision theory background. This is for a section handout, so a case that cheats by stipulating its verdict rather than generating it from the math is useless to me.

After presenting the game, I need the demonstration that it meets spec. Show the expected-utility calculation for the logarithmic agent, term by term, so the students can follow it. Then run two sanity checks on the case. First, the bounded-utility check: what happens to the expected utility of your game if instead of unbounded concavity we assume utility is bounded above, and is retreating to boundedness an innocent refinement of the concavity solution or an admission that the original fix failed? Second, the truncation check: suppose the house can actually pay out at most one trillion dollars, so the payoff schedule gets capped somewhere; say what the game is then worth to both a linear agent and a logarithmic agent, and whether the paradoxical flavor survives. Finally, tell me which of these two moves — concavity or truncation — is doing real work in resolving the original paradox and which is closer to presupposing its conclusion, and sketch how a philosopher who thinks the St. Petersburg game shows nothing wrong with expected-utility theory at all would respond to your variant. I'd like the walkthrough to be rigorous enough to hand out, but pitched at students seeing the paradox for the second time, not the tenth.
