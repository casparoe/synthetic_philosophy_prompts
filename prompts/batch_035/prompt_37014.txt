So I think I hold a pretty modest view about suffering and happiness: some suffering is so bad that no amount of happiness, of any intensity or duration, can make up for it. Give someone an hour of the worst torture there is, and no amount of bliss for other people evens the scales — past some point the two things just stop sharing a currency. This is basically a lexical threshold version of negative utilitarianism, and I was feeling fine about it until my roommate hit me with this:

(1) Some suffering is uncompensatable: no amount of pleasure, however large, can outweigh it. (This is the thing I believe.)
(2) Whether a suffering is uncompensatable can't hinge on an imperceptible difference. If some suffering is uncompensatable, then one barely distinguishable from it is too — otherwise a difference you can't even notice is doing all the moral work.
(3) There's a chain of barely distinguishable sufferings running from torture all the way down to a papercut.
(4) So papercuts are uncompensatable too.
(5) So a world with one papercut in it and otherwise endless bliss for billions of people is worse than an empty world, since nothing can outweigh the papercut. And by the same token it would be better to painlessly blink humanity out of existance than to let one more person stub a toe.

He's thrilled, because he hates threshold views and thinks he's shown that mine refutes itself. I think (5) proves way too much, but every step looks okay to me when I stare at it individually. His latest move is to say this is just the sorites paradox in a funny hat — heaps, bald guys, whatever — so I should say about "uncompensatable" whatever I'd say about "heap." But I'm honestly not sure the cases are parallel (is "worse than" even vague the way predicates are?), and even if they are, I don't really know what I want to say about heaps. Which step should I give up, and what's the price? For the record I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (5) — (2) I could maybe live without, but I can't see how.
