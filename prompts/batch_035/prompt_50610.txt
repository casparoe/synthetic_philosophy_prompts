I'm a second-year student on the Judicial Council of our university's Student Assembly, and I'm currently taking a seminar on legislation and statutory interpretation, which has made me realize that our council interprets the assembly's constitution the way courts interpret statutes -- and that the two longest-serving members of the council disagree about methodology in almost exactly the way the seminar readings do.

The case we must decide by Thursday concerns Article IV, Section 3 of the constitution, which requires that a candidate for executive office have attended at least three general body meetings during the preceding academic year. The candidate, our current treasurer, spent last fall on exchange and attended five meetings by videoconference -- the minutes record her as present-remote, speaking and voting in each -- and she attended two further meetings in person in the spring. Article II, however, defines a meeting for quorum purposes as a gathering of members physically present on campus. If that definition carries over to Article IV, she has only two qualifying attendances and is ineligible; if Article IV's word is read on its own terms, she has seven and is plainly eligible.

Two complications make this harder than it looks. First, the section was adopted in 2013 after a slate of candidates who had never once appeared ran and won; the evident purpose was ensuring that candidates are engaged members rather than strangers to the assembly, and remote attendance seems to serve that purpose as well as physical presence does. Second, our constitution was largely copied from a model constitution distributed by a national student-governance association, and the drafting minutes show that the physical-presence language in Article II was inherited boilerplate rather than a deliberate choice -- which raises the question of whose purpose, if anyone's, a purposivist should even be consulting.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's hearing. It should recommend a concrete certification decision, give reasons that could actually persuade the textualist members rather than dismissing their approach, treat the template problem honestly, and say something about the downstream consequences of whichever reading we adopt, since several other clauses also use the word meeting without saying which sense they intend.
