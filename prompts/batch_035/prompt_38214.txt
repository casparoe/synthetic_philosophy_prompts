I moved to Singapore for work at the start of this year, and the question of performance legitimacy has stopped being a seminar topic for me and become something I argue about over dinner with my brother, who visited last month. My position, roughly, is that Singapore is a live demonstration that a state can trade procedural consent for delivered results. He thinks I am mistaking a survivors' sample for a theory. To break the deadlock, he proposed testing the idea against the Asian financial crisis of 1997-98 — the one episode where the region's regimes, most of them built on some version of the same growth-for-deference bargain, were hit by the same shock at roughly the same time. I would like you to conduct that test properly, because I am not equipped to.

Take as the case the following, which I believe is common knowledge and which I state so that nothing turns on contested detail: by the mid-1990s Indonesia under Suharto had delivered roughly three decades of strong growth; when the rupiah collapsed, he was out of office within about a year. Malaysia's Mahathir weathered the crisis with capital controls and a campaign blaming foreign speculators, and remained in power. South Korea, mid-way through democratization, elected a longtime dissident to the presidency in the very middle of the crisis and absorbed the resulting austerity without institutional collapse. China was largely insulated — its currency was not convertible — and responded with state-directed stimulus, leaving its own bargain untouched.

I do not want the op-ed version of performance legitimacy; I want the careful one. In particular, I would like you to work with Scharpf's distinction between input legitimacy and output legitimacy, and to keep in play Habermas's claim that the legitimation crises of late capitalism are ultimately motivational rather than economic — that the system fails not when output drops but when the culture can no longer be moved to defer. Map the episode onto the theory in a disciplined way: for each regime, identify what counted as the performance being legitimated (growth as such? insurance against catastrophe? the fairness of how gains and losses were distributed?), what the crisis did to it, and what the theory predicts should have followed. Then tell me honestly where the mapping breaks.

The specific questions I want worked through rather than waved at:

1. The natural-experiment framing assumes the shock was exogenous and common to all the regimes. Was it? If the crisis was partly endogenous — if politically connected lending was itself a product of the bargain, as in Indonesia, where the ruling family and its cronies were deeply entangled with the very banking and corporate sector the crisis exposed — then the episode is not a test of the theory but a demonstration of its internal contradiction, and I want to know which reading the evidence better supports.

2. The theory as usually stated is symmetrical: performance buys legitimacy, so the loss of performance should spend it. But the episode suggests an asymmetry, as if legitimacy is evaluated on the loss side in something like prospect-theory fashion — regimes lost more deference from the crash than they had ever gained from the equivalent boom. Can performance-legitimacy theory absorb that asymmetry, or does the episode show that what was really being legitimated was insurance rather than growth, so the theory has been sold under the wrong heading all along?

3. South Korea is the anomaly on every telling: the state performed badly, the population paid dearly, and the regime emerged more legitimate, not less — because the performance being judged was by then at least partly procedural. Does the Korean case show that output and input legitimacy are not rival sources of authority, as the standard framing implies, but stages of a transition in which one is quietly revalued into the other? And if so, does my dinner-table question dissolve — so that the issue is not whether results can justify the absence of consent, but which stage of that revaluation Singapore is actually in?

4. Finally, Habermas. If the Indonesian collapse is best read not as an economic failure but as a motivational one — the crisis converted an accounting problem into a fairness judgment, and the deference was already gone before the numbers were — then say so, and tell me what that does to my position. I would rather learn that my brother is right for reasons I can state precisely than keep winning an argument whose machinery I do not understand.

Where the theory is silent, say so plainly; do not force it to speak.
