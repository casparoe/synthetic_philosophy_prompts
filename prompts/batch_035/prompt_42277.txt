I'm a second-year PhD student in an ML lab that does safety-adjacent interpretability work, and I've somehow been assigned to write the "conceptual foundations" section of our grant report, which mostly means I'm the one who finally has to admit we've been using a word we never defined. The project is mechanistic anomaly detection: the pitch is that instead of flagging inputs where the *output* looks weird, we flag inputs where the model produced its output via a *different mechanism* than the ones it used on training data — so we'd catch cases where a model does something bad but the behavior itself looks totally benign, because the internals went sideways even though the output didn't. The problem that keeps derailing our weekly meetings is that whether two forward passes used "the same mechanism" seems to depend entirely on how coarsely you describe what's going on. If you individuate mechanisms at the level of individual attention heads, nothing is ever the same mechanism twice, even on repeated inputs. If you individuate at the level of "the model recognizing objects," almost everything is the same mechanism, and genuinely novel behavior becomes invisible in principle. Our postdoc thinks this is just an engineering tradeoff; I suspect it's hiding a real conceptual hole, but I can't quite put my finger on it.

Here's what I want from you: a worked example that makes the problem vivid enough to put in the report. Give me an example of some system — biological, mechanical, a piece of everyday software, whatever works — where a novel input gets handled by something that is arguably the same mechanism as before and arguably a different one, AND where the two verdicts actually matter: they should lead to different conclusions about whether the system failed on that input, or about whether a detector that did (or didn't) flag it as anomalous got things right. Please make it concrete enough to walk through step by step — describe what the system does on familiar inputs, what changed with the novel one, then describe the mechanism at two or three different granularities and show exactly at which granularity the verdict flips.

Ideally the example is one where the most natural granularity is genuinely contestable, not one where there's an obvious right answer if you just think about it for a second — because that contested middle ground is exactly the situation our detectors are in. And if you can end by saying something about what would make a granularity choice principled, rather than just picking whichever level of description gives the answer you already wanted, my postdoc and I would both be grateful. Don't worry about citing literature, we just need the example itself to be sharp.
