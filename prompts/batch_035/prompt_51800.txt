i've been reading about Émilie du Châtelet's little treatise on happiness (the one she wrote in the 1740s, usually called the Discourse on Happiness) and it struck me how close she is to Pascal on one point and then how far apart they end up. Both basically think human beings can't stand quiet, honest reflection on our own condition. Pascal calls our constant scrambling for distraction divertissement and treats it as proof of our misery; du Châtelet says something like the same thing about how illusions and passions are the only thing keeping us afloat -- but then draws the opposite conclusion: cultivate them, guard them, don't go poking at them. Pascal thinks the fleeing is the disease, she thinks it's basically the cure.

So what I'd like is a comparison of the two views centered on the role of false belief in a happy life. To keep it from being a book report, I want to stress-test a distinction I've been toying with: illusions about the value of what you're doing (that your physics translation matters, that your circle of friends is the point of it all) versus illusions about the facts (that you're not aging, that death is a problem for other people). My hunch is that du Châtelet's recipe mostly needs the first kind and doesn't care much about the second, whereas Pascal's diagnosis is mostly about the second kind -- reflection on death and the wretchedness of our condition is exactly what he thinks we're fleeing. Does that split survive contact with what either of them actually wrote? And which framework does a better job with a case like this: someone who throws themself into scholarship knowing full well it's a game they freely chose, no cosmic importance attached, and is happy anyway. Is that person living on du Châtelet's illusions, or have they found something her recipe and Pascal's diagnosis both miss?
