I audited a graduate course on Bayesian epistemology last term, and I came out able to run the machinery — Bayes, and Jeffrey conditioning for when your evidence is itself uncertain — without being at all sure which of my actual epistemic behavior the machinery describes. So I have kept a notebook: six situations in which I gained evidence without becoming certain of anything, each with my prior, the relevant reliability judgments, and the posterior I would actually endorse. What I want from you is this: tell me whether some one updating principle generates all six verdicts — Jeffrey conditioning, plain conditioning under a better description of the evidence, or something else entirely — or whether the verdicts fragment. Please (i) state the principle precisely enough to compute with, (ii) run every case through it and compare its output with my number, (iii) say candidly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot reproduce, rather than patching the principle ad hoc to save them, and (iv) tell me what the principle predicts in a fresh case I have not listed — hearsay passing through two links in a chain, each link of ordinary reliability, where the first link heard the source directly.

My tentative slogan, which you should test and repair or reject: all uncertain evidence is really certain evidence about my own experience, once the experience is described properly; so plain conditioning does everything, and Jeffrey conditioning is merely what conditioning looks like when I cannot articulate what I learned. I cannot make this slogan work for cases three and four, and I would like to know whether the failure is mine or the slogan's.

**Case 1 — the matchbox in the dark.** The matchbox is on the table or in the drawer; my prior is 50/50. I grope in the dark, and the grope gives me 0.80 credence that the feeling of cardboard under my hand is veridical. If the feeling is veridical, the box is on the table for sure; if it is not, I am back to 50/50. My verdict: 0.90 that it is on the table.

**Case 2 — the office rumor.** My prior is 0.25 that Nadia has resigned. A usually reliable colleague tells me flatly that she has. I am certain of exactly one thing — that the colleague asserted it. Given that Nadia resigned, the colleague would assert this with probability 0.90; given that she did not, with probability 0.20 (the colleague sometimes presents hunches as facts). My verdict: 0.60. A friend in the course tells me that in doing this I have performed Jeffrey conditioning; my own view is that I have just conditioned on a proposition. Settle this.

**Case 3 — the dermatologist's credence.** A mole has me at 0.50 that it is malignant. The dermatologist examines it and announces, not a finding, but a credence: that she is 0.75 confident it is benign. My verdict: 0.65 that it is benign. I cannot derive this number from anything; it is where my gut lands between my 0.50 and her 0.75.

**Case 4 — the tipster and the racing paper.** My prior is 0.10 that Fielder's Flame wins. A tipster tips the horse; I judge he would tip it with probability 0.80 if it will win and 0.20 if it will not. My verdict after the tip: 0.30. The next day the racing paper publishes his full record, and I learn I had him wrong: the true probability of his tipping a loser is 0.40, not 0.20. My verdict: I should throw out the 0.30 and recompute from my original prior with the corrected numbers, landing near 0.18 — even though the racing paper's report is itself just further evidence that I could, in principle, condition my 0.30 on. This feels backward-looking and non-commutative to me, and I have been told Jeffrey conditioning is neither.

**Case 5 — the colleague twice.** My prior is 0.30 that the takeover goes through. My colleague Mira, whose judgment on such matters I rate, says it will. Given that it goes through, she would say so with probability 0.60; given that it does not, with probability 0.20. My verdict: about 0.55. A week later she says the same thing again, having looked at nothing new; my verdict: I move not at all, or almost not at all. But if a second colleague of the same reliability, with independent information, had also said yes, my verdict is that I would move again, to somewhere in the high 0.70s.

**Case 6 — the second look at the same matchbox.** Back in the dark, a grope leaves me at 0.60 credence that the cardboard feeling is veridical (same structure as case 1, so 0.80 that the box is on the table). I then switch on a dim flashlight that only modestly improves matters, and the look it affords leaves me at 0.75 credence that what I am seeing is veridical. My verdict: my final credence should be what the better look alone would give — about 0.875 — as though the first grope had never happened, not the compounded result of Jeffrey-updating my 0.80 posterior with a fresh 0.75 reading. Yet in case 5 I insisted that two genuinely independent pieces of testimony should compound, and that their order should not matter. I cannot state what separates the second look at the same matchbox in nearly the same light from an independent second look, and my slogan about describing the evidence proposition properly does not help me here.

That last pair is the one I keep circling: replacement in cases five and six, compounding and commutativity elsewhere. If your principle draws that line cleanly, tell me where it is drawn and why it is not simply gerrymandered to fit; if it cannot, tell me which of the verdicts to surrender.
