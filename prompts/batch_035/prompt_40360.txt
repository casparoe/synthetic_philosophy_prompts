My closest friend from graduate school trained as an economist before switching to public health, and over the years she has accumulated a set of firm verdicts about when we can correctly say that one person's burden or benefit is greater than another's. She insists it is obvious that a deep burn is worse for the person who suffers it than a mild sunburn is for whoever suffers that — anyone who doubted it, she says, could not responsibly practice medicine. She is equally confident that a $600 fine is a bigger setback for a struggling graduate student than for her comfortably off uncle, and she leans on exactly this kind of judgment when she defends progressive taxation or need-based aid. But she flatly refuses to rank the griefs of two bereaved parents against each other, even when both describe their loss in the same words; there is, she says, no fact of the matter about whose grief is heavier, and treating such rankings as facts is a mistake. Likewise, she denies there is any fact about whether a wine connoisseur's pleasure in a great vintage exceeds a tired commuter's pleasure in a cold beer at the end of a shift — too different in kind to compare. Yet at the dentist once, with a botched anesthesia leaving a patient in genuine pain while her companion paced the corridor in anxiety, my friend said without hesitation that the patient was having the worse time of the two — no scale in sight, just the scene in front of her.

Last month a case came up that I think splits these commitments, and I want to predict what she will say before our next dinner, since I have a small wager with her husband about it. Two members of my running club have the same knee condition — identical findings on the MRI, the same surgeon's assessment — and both were asked by the physiotherapist to rate their pain on the standard 0-to-10 scale. One of them, a former athlete proud of never complaining, said 3 and has kept training through it; the other said 8 and has taken to avoiding stairs. The physiotherapist wants to prioritize the second for surgery, and at the club someone asked, in my friend's hearing, whether that was right — whether the second runner is simply in more pain, and the first just tougher. She hasn't answered yet.

So tell me: what will she say — that the second is in more pain, that there is no fact of the matter and the two numbers only measure willingness to report, or something in between? I care less about the answer than about the rule she seems to be running. Several candidate rules fit her past verdicts as far as I can tell — something about comparisons being legitimate only when the two states sit on a scale fixed independently of the persons doing the reporting, something about an impartial and well-informed sympathizer being able to rank the two situations, something about one state being so overwhelming that the difference in kind stops mattering — but I cannot tell which of these her record actually supports, or whether they even come apart on the knee case. Lay out the rules that fit her track record, say what each predicts here, give me your best guess about her verdict with your reasons, and then tell me which further case — one you invent, in the same style as her earlier verdicts — would best force the surviving rules apart.
