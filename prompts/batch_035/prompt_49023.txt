I help run a small online reading group that started on philosophy of mind and has since drifted into whatever arguments the members get into afterward. A pattern keeps recurring across those arguments, and I'd like to know whether it has a name. In one recent thread, someone insisted that chairs don't exist, on the grounds that a completed physics would describe arrangements of particles without ever needing the word "chair," and that tables and chairs belong in roughly the category of phlogiston. In another, two members spent the better part of a week arguing about whether a hole in a pipe is a thing in its own right or merely where the pipe isn't. In a third, someone claimed that corporations don't exist — that only people, contracts, and bank accounts exist — and got the exasperated reply that of course corporations exist, you can sue them, they can be fined, they can go bankrupt. Every one of these arguments eventually reaches a point where one side declares: we don't actually disagree about anything, we're just using the word differently, so the argument is empty. And then, at least as often as not, the other side refuses to accept that: they insist that there is a genuine question about what there is, that this question is not settled by tidying up our usage, and that declaring the argument empty is itself a substantive philosophical position masquerading as neutral bookkeeping.

What I want a name for is the second-order thing, not the first-order disputes about chairs and holes. The configuration is: a disagreement that looks substantive; an attempt to dissolve it by claiming the parties mean different things by some word; and then a genuine disagreement about whether that dissolution works — so an argument about objects turns into an argument about whether there was ever an argument at all. The nearest everyday phrase I know is "that's just semantics," but that's typically an insult rather than a concept, and I've noticed that in some of our threads the dissolution clearly succeeds (once the parties agree on what they each meant, everyone loses interest), while in others it clearly fails or is at least resisted (the person who denied chairs exists doesn't back down once the semantic mismatch is pointed out; they say that was never their point). So my questions are: does this situation — a dispute over whether a dispute is merely verbal — have an established name? Is there literature offering actual criteria for when diagnosing a dispute as a merely verbal one dissolves it, versus when the diagnosis is a way of dodging it? And is there a standard name for the position of those who think the dissolution never does the work it's supposed to — the people who think there's a fact of the matter about what there is that our word usage can't touch? If nothing in the literature matches this configuration exactly, I'd be grateful if you could coin terms for the two moves — the dissolution attempt and the refusal of the dissolution as a substantive metaphysical claim — and give a definition of each that marks off the clear cases and the borderline ones. I'm not looking for a bare label; I'd like to come away with a good sense of what exactly the phenomenon is and what its edges are.
