Longtime lurker here (lesswrong since ~2012, EA forum, ssc/astral codex comment sections, the works), and I've noticed that basically every fight about medical ethics, sex, research, and data privacy eventually bottoms out in what actually makes consent count. So I tried the classic move: catalog my gut verdicts on cases and see if there's a principle underneath. Problem is my verdicts seem to contradict each other, and I'd like someone smarter than me to either find the principle or tell me which verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases and my gut calls:

1. A surgeon tells a patient a procedure is "basically risk-free" because he honestly believes it (it has a 2% paralysis rate he doesn't personally know about). Patient consents, gets paralyzed. I think the consent was valid — bad luck plus bad doctor, but not invalid consent. But if the surgeon *knows* the 2% rate and says "basically risk-free" to get the patient to agree, the consent feels invalid. So falsity alone doesn't kill consent; it's something about knowing deception? That already seems weird to me.

2. A woman sleeps with a man because he told her he's single and he's actually married. Sleazy, but I find myself saying the consent was still valid. But a man who agrees to sex with a condom on and his partner secretly removes it mid-act — clearly invalid consent. Both are lies about the encounter, so what's the difference? Risk of physical harm? But suppose the married guy also has an STD he doesn't know about — now is it invalid? My gut says still kind of valid, which is not helping.

3. Two people at a party, both tipsy, both enthusiastic, hook up: fine, valid. One tipsy person agrees after the other says "if you don't, you can find your own ride home": invalid. Sober person agrees after two hours of "everyone else said yes, you're the only one ruining the weekend": feels invalid-ish but I'm much less sure than the threat case, and I honestly can't say why, since the threat case seems to make any pressure at all disqualifying and then the nagging case should be easy.

4. An elderly man with mild cognitive impairment wants to enroll in a risky experimental drug trial. He gets the gist — it's experimental, it could hurt him — but he can't retain the actual odds for more than a minute. Borderline, leaning valid, because he grasps the big picture? But a very smart, fully competent patient who signs a consent form where the risk was correctly disclosed but buried in paragraph 37 of dense legalese while the cover brochure was all sunshine — I feel like that consent is *weaker* than the old man's, even though the smart patient had all the info available and the old man didn't. These two verdicts together seem to rule out both a pure competence criterion and a pure information criterion, and I don't know what's left.

5. Someone consents to a fight (actual consensual violence, MMA-style) after being taunted into it in front of friends: I say valid. Someone consents to sex because their partner threatened to leave them: I want to say invalid, or at least compromised. But "I'll leave you" and "you're a coward if you don't fight" feel like the same kind of social pressure applied to different acts. Maybe consent to high-stakes acts is more fragile? That can't be right either, because people consent to way riskier things (organ donation) under way less scrutiny all the time and we're fine with it.

6. And the pair I really can't square: clicking "I agree" on a website's terms of service that nobody reads — valid, whatever. Signing a gym contract with an auto-renewal clause that was disclosed but visually buried — feels invalid to me. Same epistemic situation, opposite verdicts. I suspect I'm just being a snob about gyms, but maybe there's a real difference.

I did read at some point about the distinction between reasons that count for the *state* an option puts you in versus reasons that count as *evidence* — like consenting to surgery because it fixes you versus because you enjoy hospitals — and I have a hunch the coercion cases turn on something like that (the threat gives you a reason you wouldn't have wanted), but every time I try to spell it out the nagging case and the drunk case break it.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of clauses, that captures all or most of these? Please state it as precisely as you can, say which case motivates which clause, and tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't accommodate — I'd rather overturn a verdict than bolt on a sixth epicycle, but I want to know the price of each option. Also please apply it to two cases I deliberately left out: (a) a patient who tells her doctor "don't tell me the biopsy result unless it's bad news, just say I'm fine" and then relies on that 'fine' — has she pre-authorized the deception, and does that change anything? and (b) a guy who agrees to a private poker game knowing full well that some private games have card mechanics in them, and this one does — valid consent to lose money, or no? My intuitions on those are a total coin flip and I'm curious where the principle lands.
