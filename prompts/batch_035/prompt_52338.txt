I'm a novelist, three quarters of the way thru a book whose protagonist is a professional poker player who spends the whole thing sneering at her devout mother. Near the end, after a diagnosis she can't think about straight, she starts showing up at mass anyway. When her brother catches her at the back of the church and asks if she's converted, she says she's hedging — you've got nothing to lose and maybe infinite to gain, she shrugs, same as any other bet she'd ever make.

My editor read the draft and says the ending doesn't work because nobody that rational takes Pascal's wager seriously, it's a punchline argument, and my character would see thru it in one hand. And honestly I can't tell if the editor is right because I don't actually know what the wager amounts to once you press on it. I have a feeling the line "you've got nothing to lose" could be doing several very different jobs and I've been sliding between them without noticing.

So: what are the distinct precise claims that one-liner could actually be making? I want the whole menu — the reading where it's just trivially true decision theory, the reading where the many-gods problem kills it (bet on WHICH infinite payoff?), the reading where probabilities so tiny you can't even assign them mess up the arithmetic, the reading where the real problem is whether belief is even the kind of thing you can choose at all versus just showing up and hoping it rubs off, and anything I'm not thinking of. For each: is it defensible, is it false, and — this is the part I actually need — does that reading make my character's move a rational expected-value play in her own terms, or does it make her move something else entirely (fear, sentiment, hedging against the mother's death rather than God's judgment), which might honestly be the better novel.

Bottom line, tell me whether to keep the "hedging" line or cut it, based on which reading I'd be committing to if I keep it. I'm fine with her being wrong about her own motives — I'm not fine with the scene resting on an argument that only sounds good to someone who hasn't thought about it for ten seconds, because she thinks in odds for a living and readers will call me on it.
