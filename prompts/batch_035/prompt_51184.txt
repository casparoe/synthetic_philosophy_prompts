My sister's a palliative care nurse and we got into an argument over one of her patients. He has this thing called pain asymbolia — from brain damage in a particular region, she says — where he genuinely feels pain, can tell you exactly where it is and how intense, but simply doesn't care about it. He'll report "it hurts, quite badly" while smiling and letting the doctor carry on. He refuses painkillers, not out of stoicism, but because there's honestly nothing he wants relieved.

I tried to insist that pain is intrinsically bad and she flattened me with this:

(1) Nothing bad is happening to him during these episodes — no suffering, no wanting-it-gone, no harm he registers in any way.
(2) If pain were bad just in virtue of how it feels — intrinsically, in the sensation itself — something bad would be happening to him, because the feeling is fully intact.
(3) So the badness of pain isn't in the feeling at all; it's in the aversion. Pain is bad when, and because, it's disliked.
(4) And if that's right, the same goes for anything else we're tempted to call intrinsically bad: the humiliated man who feels no shame, the forgetful man who doesn't mind forgetting — if they genuinely don't care, nothing bad is happening to them either.
(5) So nothing is intrinsically bad for a person except what that person cares about. Badness collapses into caring.

That conclusion seems too strong to me but I can't find the seam. I'd most like to keep (2) — the thought that the felt quality itself matters — but I'd rather know where it actually goes wrong than win the argument. So: has she smuggled something in, or is the argument valid and the real choice is about whether to swallow (5)?
