My closest friend from graduate school now works on defense policy, and we spent most of last weekend in an argument that never really got resolved. The subject was nuclear declaratory policy — specifically the practice, common to several nuclear-armed states, of refusing to specify exactly what would trigger nuclear use. States decline to promise they will never strike first; they decline to enumerate the provocations that would cross the threshold; they deliberately cultivate uncertainty about how much risk their leadership would be willing to run. My friend kept insisting that this calculated ambiguity only makes sense against an adversary who models us as a rational actor weighing costs and benefits: the whole point is to shape the adversary's expected-utility calculation by leaving the worst outcomes in the distribution. But I kept noticing that the textbooks she leans on also celebrate brinkmanship as the deliberate creation of a shared risk of escalation that neither side fully controls — the idea, going back to Schelling, of a threat that leaves something to chance. That doesn't look like shaping anyone's calculation. It looks like getting value out of the possibility that no rational choice gets made at all, because events outrun the decision-makers.

Here is what bothers me, and what we ended up shouting about. The same governments that trade on strategic ambiguity also insist, in every other context, that nuclear decision-making is strictly centralized, procedurally rational, and under continuous positive control — that no weapon flies unless cool heads authorize it. Yet the deterrent value of brinkmanship seems to presuppose that the adversary believes escalation could happen without anyone choosing it, or that our leadership might do something a rational agent would not. So does the practice presuppose that we are rational, or that we might not be, or something else entirely — perhaps that rationality itself includes the deliberate acceptance of uncontrolled risk, so that the apparent contradiction dissolves? My friend waved me off by saying that ambiguity is just cheap insurance and presupposes nothing very deep, but I don't think a practice this consequential can be entirely philosophically innocent, and I also suspect it can't be entirely coherent either.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction. Take the practice as I've described it — declaratory ambiguity, brinkmanship as shared risk, combined with the public insistence on tight control and purely defensive intent — and lay out what it presupposes: what view of rational agency, of what a threat is, and of what makes a threat credible the practice only makes sense under. Then tell me which rival views it quietly rules out — for example, whether it's compatible with thinking of deterrence as a straightforward expected-damage calculation, or whether it strictly requires the adversary to credit us with some propensity toward the arational. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, since I suspect several different underlying theories could license parts of it, and say where you think the practice is genuinely underdetermined rather than contradictory. And if you think my friend is right that the tension I see isn't really there, I'd want to know that too, with the reasoning spelled out rather than assumed.
