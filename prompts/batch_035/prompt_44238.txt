i'm writing a problem set for my phil of biology course on the units of selection fight, and i'm sick of the standard survey that just rehearses the history and declares the gene's-eye view the winner by vibes. i want the students to actually stress-test the frameworks. so here's what i need from you.

Take these four frameworks — (1) the gene's-eye view (replicators as the units, organisms as mere vehicles/interactors), (2) classic individual/organism-level selection, (3) old-school group selection a la Wynne-Edwards, and (4) the later trait-group / multilevel revival, Wade and Sober-Wilson style — and score each of them on three dimensions, 0 to 1:

- "bookkeeping": when selection does its counting, is it clear which entity fitness is being assigned to, and does the framework keep its own books consistent? (this is basically the Williams/Dawkins line about what adaptations are *for*, but i want it actually evaluated, not just recited)
- "altruism": how cleanly can it explain one organism sacrificing for others, without smuggling in inclusive fitness where it wasn't invited
- "intragenomic": can it even talk about meiotic drive / segregation distorters, where pieces of DNA compete against other pieces of DNA inside the same body — this one's my acid test, i've almost never seen a textbook treat it honestly at the organism level

Give me a JSON array, one object per framework, with fields: "framework" (short name), "scores" (an object with the three keys above, each 0-1), "tier" (S/A/B/C), and "one_liner" (a sentence on why it lands where it does). Then, after the array, a seperate JSON object with two fields: "verdict" (which of the three dimensions ended up doing the most work separating the tiers, and why) and "surprise" (one place where your own scores bugged you — e.g. where you caught yourself wanting to score the same framework differently depending on the dimension).

two ground rules: don't derive the tier by averaging the three scores mechanically — noticing when one dimension should dominate is half the point. and don't rank by who won the rhetorical war in the 60s and 70s, rank by the actual bookkeeping.
