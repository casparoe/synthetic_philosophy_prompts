When a radiologist reads a scan and writes the report, the language is two-tiered. What was seen is asserted flat out: there is a 4 mm nodule in the right upper lobe; no pneumothorax; the remainder is unremarkable. What it means, though — the part the referring physician actually wants — is hedged by convention, using stock phrases of the form likely represents, cannot be excluded, recommend follow-up, correlate with clinical findings. A radiologist who is ninety-five percent confident that a nodule is an old healed granuloma will still not write that it is one; the report will say something like probably benign, with short-term follow-up recommended. Yet the same radiologist, telephoned by the referring physician, will often drop the hedge entirely: it's an old granuloma, tell the patient not to worry. And the profession asserts the perceptual tier flatly even though everyone in it knows that detection is itself fallible — nodules are missed at rates the specialty measures and publishes — so the descriptive claims rest on pattern recognition that is no more reliable than the diagnostic inference, yet only the inference is hedged.

Please reconstruct the norm of assertion this practice presupposes. Does the two-tier structure presuppose that perception yields knowledge while inference yields mere belief, so that a knowledge rule for assertion would license the flat findings and require the hedge on the conclusions — and can that line survive the known miss rates? Or is the written hedge not an assertion of a diagnostic proposition at all, but a different speech act: a rating of the state of the evidence, addressed to readers who were not on the phone — other physicians, insurers, and, in the worst case, a court — so that report and phone call are governed by different norms rather than inconsistently by one? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well: say whether the liability-driven reading, on which the hedge exists so that the sentence survives cross-examination, and the epistemic reading, on which the hedge marks the boundary of what the radiologist knows, can both be true; and identify where the practice is underdetermined enough that a truth rule or a reasonable-belief rule would license it just as well as a knowledge rule. Finally, tell me what would have to change if the implicit norm were replaced by a straightforward probability rule — the direction some specialties have already taken with structured reporting, where findings are sorted into numbered categories, each carrying a published probability range. Would the hedges disappear, would the flat answer on the phone have to change, and would the report cease to be an assertion at all and become a measurement?
