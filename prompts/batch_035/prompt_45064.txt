I'm a novelist, and after years of living inside other people's heads for a living I've drifted toward something like Hofstadter's strange-loop view of selves: an "I" is a self-referential pattern, and those patterns aren't sealed inside one skull — they leak. When my mother died I noticed I could partly reconstruct how she'd react to things, hear her voice weighing in on my decisions, and it occured to me that some version of her is literally running in my brain, the way a compressed copy of a novel lives in anyone who's read it closely. So I've come to believe selves are distributed: a person is instantiated, at various resolutions, across every brain they've patterned. Tell me what would count against this. Not just "that's sentimental nonsense" — actual defeaters: what we know about how memories of a person get distorted and simplified after they die, whether a low-res pattern can carry whatever it is that makes a self a *self*, and where the view overcommits (does my character Anna, whom I invented out of nothing, count as a distributed self too? did my mother live on in her cat, or dont cats have the right kind of brain for it?). Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones a committed Hofstadterian would actually concede versus wave away, and be honest about whether the view has been hedged until no observation could ever refute it — and whether that even matters for a claim of this kind. As you go, say how confident you are in each claim, and flag the single point you're least sure of.
