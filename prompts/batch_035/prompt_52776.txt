I'm taking an introductory ethics course, and we've just covered the is–ought gap. As I understand it, Hume demonstrated that it is a fallacy — the naturalistic fallacy — to move from purely factual premises to a moral conclusion. Yet my lecturer constantly appeals to empirical findings in her own arguments: she says that because psychological studies show extreme inequality causes measurable harm to people's wellbeing, we have moral reason to oppose extreme inequality. To me this looks like exactly the move Hume proved to be fallacious: she starts from facts about harm and wellbeing and finishes with an 'ought'. When I raised this in the seminar, she said only that my objection trades on conflating two different things, but she didn't elaborate and I felt too embarrassed to press her.

My friends and I have been arguing about it ever since. One thinks the lecturer is safe because the studies describe suffering, and suffering just is bad by definition, so no inference from 'is' to 'ought' ever occurs; another thinks my objection succeeds outright, and that the lecturer is at best smuggling in a moral premise while pretending to have derived one from the data. I'd also like to know whether the definitional move — treating a word that appears to have moral content as simply synonymous with a descriptive one — actually dissolves the is–ought problem, or whether it merely shows that moral vocabulary was hiding in the premises all along. And if the latter, was Hume's point really that such arguments are invalid, or only that they are incomplete?

Please write in British English. Explain precisely where the fallacy, if there is one, occurs in the lecturer's argument; assess my two friends' competing diagnoses; and clarify whether the naturalistic fallacy and the is–ought gap are in fact the same thing, as I have been assuming throughout. I'd also appreciate a worked example of an argument that looks like it derives an 'ought' from an 'is' but is perfectly respectable once its premises are laid bare, so I can see the contrast clearly.
