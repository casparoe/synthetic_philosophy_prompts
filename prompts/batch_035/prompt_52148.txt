i run a lunchtime reading group at my software company — bunch of engineers who like philosophy but none of us have any formal training. last week's topic was Dennett's four kinds of creatures (darwinian, skinnerian, popperian, gregorian) and i had everyone write a short answer to a question i made up. now i have to grade them and honestly im not confident i can spot a subtly wrong answer vs a right one, since im winging it as much as they are. here's the question and one student's answer. can you grade it out of 10 and give me specific point-by-point comments — what they got right, what they got wrong, and what exactly the mistakes are? i want enough detail that i could defend the grade to the student if they pushed back on it.

the question was: Dennett divides creatures into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian kinds. For each kind, explain what new ability it has that the previous kind lacks, give an example of a creature at that level, and explain why Dennett thinks this progression matters for understanding what minds are and what they're for.

the student's answer, copied as-is:

"Dennett's four kinds are basically an evolutionary ladder that species climb one rung at a time. Darwinian creatures are the simplest: all of their design work is done by natural selection, so the learning happens across generations, in the gene pool, not in the individual. A Darwinian creature can't learn anything during its own lifetime — even a tiny habit is out of reach — since selection has to do all the work. A bacterium is a good example.

Skinnerian creatures get an upgrade: they can be conditioned. Behaviors that get rewarded happen more often, behaviors that get punished happen less, so error correction moves into the lifetime of the individual. A rat in a Skinner box is the obvious example. They figure out which behavior worked by thinking it over after the reward arrives.

Popperian creatures go one better: they can test candidate behaviors offline, in an inner model, before acting, so the bad options die in the model instead of the creature dying in the real world. This is named after Popper's picture of science, where conjectures get refuted and thrown away cheaply. They're also the first kind that can learn by watching others — a monkey that sees another monkey get sick from eating a berry avoids that berry. So chimps are a good example.

Gregorian creatures (named after the psychologist Richard Gregory) use mind-tools: words, maps, diagrams, methods — tools for thinking that we pick up from our culture. The advantage is mainly that language makes thinking much faster, and humans are born with these tools already installed. Example: us — a human engineer sketching a flowchart before writing code.

Why it matters: for Dennett, the progression shows that mind isn't magic, it's a series of engineering upgrades to the problem of deciding what to do next, where each upgrade makes the search for good actions cheaper and safer."

some of it sounds right to me but a few things feel off and i can't quite put my finger on why. go through it carefully.
