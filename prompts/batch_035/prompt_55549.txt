I'm grading take-home finals for a third-year course on moral responsibility and I've read this one four times now without converging on anything. I'd like another set of eyes before I commit. The exam question was:

"Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' is sometimes described as changing the subject -- away from the metaphysics of free will and towards the interpersonal character of responsibility. Does the argument actually change the subject, or does it quietly presuppose an answer to the metaphysical question? In your answer, explain the role the objective attitude plays in the argument, and say whether the exemption cases (children, the severely mentally ill) support his thesis about determinism or threaten it."

And here's the student's answer, typed out as submitted (typos theirs):

"Strawson's central move is to side with the optimist against the pessimist: the pessimist insists moral responsibility requires contra-causal freedom, the optimist denies this, and Strawson shows the optimist is right because our practices rest on the reactive attitudes, which are just natural to us. The reactive attitudes are things like resentment, gratitude, indignation, and their self-directed analogues like guilt and shame, which we take up towards others (and ourselves) as participants in interpersonal relationships. His argument for their justification is basically that they're universal human responses -- nobody can help resenting an injury -- so they don't need any further warrant. That's why determinism doesn't undermine responsibility: even if determinism is true, we'd go right on feeling these attitudes.

The objective attitude is the stance of treating someone as an object of social policy rather than a participant -- the doctor's or case worker's stance, the one we take towards a small child. Strawson thinks we can always take this attitude towards anyone, and sometimes we should, as with the severely mentally ill. This is where the exemption cases matter, and I think they cut against him more than he admits. If we already exempt children and the mentally ill from the participant stance, then the same considerations extend to anyone whose actions are sufficiently explained by their causes, and determinism says that's everyone. So on Strawson's own framework the exemption generalizes and the pessimist wins after all. Strawson's reply would presumably be that a total suspension of the reactive attitudes is not psychologically possible for creatures like us, so in practice nothing changes.

Does he change the subject or presuppose metaphysics? He changes it. He replaces the question 'is responsibility compatible with determinism' with the question whether our practices are self-supporting, and since they plainly are, the metaphysical question never has to get answered. Though one might object that the psychological unavoidability of resentment doesn't show resentment is appropriate -- feeling something strongly is not a justification of it, and Strawson never really addresses this."
