I'm in a small philosophy-of-language reading group with a friend — I'll call her Dee — who has never read a word of the literature as far as I know, but who has firm and strikingly stable verdicts about what people's words refer to and what people believe. She never uses the labels philosophers use; she just says what she thinks is true of a case. I keep notes of her rulings, and I'd like to be able to predict what she'll say about cases she hasn't yet heard. Please build me a model of the rule (or rules) she seems to be running, and apply it to some new cases I'm considering.

Her verdicts so far, each on a case we've discussed: (1) Twin planet. An Earthling in 1750 has a molecule-for-molecule duplicate on a far-off planet where the clear, drinkable liquid in the rivers is some other compound — not water as we know it, though it looks, tastes and behaves the same. Dee says the duplicate's word 'water' refers to that other compound: when the duplicate says "water quenches thirst", she is talking about the local liquid, and once the twin planet's chemists identify that compound, the duplicate would be saying something false if she declared "water is H2O". (2) The patient with the odd medical belief. A man who sincerely believes arthritis can lodge in the thigh tells his doctor "the arthritis in my thigh is flaring up". Dee says his word 'arthritis' means what the medical profession means by it, so what he said is false — not that he said something true of a thigh ailment as he understands the term. (3) The stolen theorem. Suppose it turned out that the famous logician credited with proving a celebrated theorem had in fact stolen it from an obscure colleague. Dee says that, in that scenario, when people say "the famous logician proved the theorem", they are talking about the famous one, so the sentence comes out false — the name doesn't silently shift to the obscure colleague. (4) Elms. Dee can't tell elms from beeches and would happily point to a beech when asked to show us an elm. Yet she insists her own word 'elm' refers to elms: if she said "that elm is shedding early" while pointing at a beech, she'd have said something false about an elm. (5) The empty planet. About the planet astronomers once hypothesised to explain irregularities in Mercury's orbit, before the hypothesis was refuted: Dee says sentences like "that planet lies between Mercury and the Sun" are not true, and not made true by anything in anyone's head. (6) Cross-community variant. Dee adds that if the patient in (2) had lived in a community whose doctors and laypeople alike used 'arthritis' for the thigh ailment too, his sentence would have been true.

Here are the new cases I'm considering putting to her, and I'd like a prediction of her verdict on each, together with an account of what turns on it. (A) The self-styled coiner. A man lives alone on an island, cut off from any community. On a calm evening he writes in his diary, "the ocean is calm tonight". Is his sentence true (his word refers to the pond) or false (his word means what everyone else's 'ocean' means, and no ocean is in sight)? (B) Same belief? At that moment, did the two of them believe the same thing, or different things? (C) The astronomers' word. Imagine a community — this is a stipulation, not a real one — whose astronomers introduced the word 'tove' for a precise and rare celestial phenomenon. Over a century, the lay majority came to use 'tove' loosely for a broader, related spectacle; the astronomers and their textbooks still use it in the strict sense, the laypeople are unaware of any difference, and neither group thinks there is a dispute. One night a layman, watching a display that falls under the broad use but not the strict one, says "that was a tove". Is his sentence true or false?
