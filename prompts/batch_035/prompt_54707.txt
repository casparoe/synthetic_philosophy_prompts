So a few weeks ago I got sucked into Hofstadter's old Scientific American columns on superrationality (they're reprinted in Metamagical Themas) and I can't stop poking at them. The two classics: in the Platonia dilemma, twenty people are told that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by the deadline, that lone sender gets a billion dollars, and if zero or more than one telegram arrives, nobody gets anything -- and Hofstadter's claim is that a superrational thinker, knowing all twenty think alike, should send a telegram with probability exactly 1/20. Then the Luring Lottery, where the prize is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, a random entry wins, and readers were explicitly allowed to enter with any positive integer -- so superrational readers were supposed to each submit a single entry, while actual readers submitted googolplexes and torched the prize to a fraction of a cent. The core idea, as I understand it, is that a superrational thinker includes in her calculation the fact that she's in a group of superrational thinkers who will converge on the same answer she does -- so she can pick the strategy as if choosing for the whole group. Fine. Here's what's been nagging me: the whole apparatus seems to presuppose that the symmetry class is the whole relevant population. Nobody seems to have asked what happens when there's an *outside* party who understands all this and isn't playing along.

Here's the ask. I'm writing a blog post arguing that superrationality, whatever its merits in a sealed room of idealized twins, is brittle in the wild -- and I want a centerpiece thought experiment, built to spec. Construct a scenario in which a community of genuine superrationalists gets systematically exploited by an outside party who has modeled their superrationality, and ends up strictly worse off than an otherwise identical community of boring old causal-decision-theory agents facing the same setup would. Constraints: keep it realistic (a fundraiser, a subscription drive, a platform's onboarding scheme, a community raffle -- nothing sci-fi, no demons, no perfect predictors, and the outside party can be no cleverer than a shrewd fundraiser or product designer who's read the same Hofstadter columns), and the exploitation must fall out of the structural features of superrationality itself -- the symmetry assumption, the common knowledge that everyone in the group reasons alike, the treating of one's own choice as informative about the group's -- and not from the group being dumb or having adopted a badly calibrated convention. To be clear what counts as cheating, since this is for the blog post: you can't just stipulate that the superrationalists lose; the walkthrough has to *generate* the loss from the theory.

After the scenario, do the demonstration part: walk through what the superrationalists do and why, what plain causal-decision agents would do in the same position and what they'd walk away with, and identify exactly which structural feature of superrationality is doing the work. Then stress-test it -- what happens if the symmetry is diluted even slightly (one closet defector in the group, or members merely suspecting one exists), and does the exploitation survive that? Finally give Hofstadter's best defense his due: he'd presumably say superrationality was never meant to apply to interactions with non-superrational outsiders, the fundraiser isn't in the symmetry class, etc. -- and then tell me honestly whether that defense actually blocks the case you built, or just reclassifies the damage. If you can, also say whether the case generalizes: is this a one-off trick or does it suggest that any tightly-knit community of likeminded deliberators is a resource someone can farm?
