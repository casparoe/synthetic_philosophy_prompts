so I'm a novelist, four books in, all fairly domestic realist stuff, and the narrator of the one I'm drafting now is basically me with the dials turned up a bit. she's the sort of person who has opinions about how you behave in queues and whether that counts as morality, and the plot partly turns on her being a bit insufferable about it. my editor read the first hundred pages and says the narrator's views on everyday morality are incoherent, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's true or whether the editor just dislikes her (which, honestly, is a lot of the point of her). so here is the bundle, as close to how she'd state it as I can get, and I want to know if it hangs together:

1. tipping in america is a genuine moral obligation, not mere etiquette — waitstaff are paid on the assumption of it, so if you don't tip you're making someone else subsidise your meal. but tipping in tokyo is just an odd western habit, and leaving a tip there isn't wrong, merely strange.

2. cutting a queue is a real wrong — not enormous, but not just rude either. it's unfair, and it treats everyone behind you as furniture.

3. jaywalking across a completely empty street at 3am is not even slightly wrong, wherever you are, even where it's illegal — no one is put at risk, no one is deceived, nothing.

4. when you travel, you're obliged to follow local conventions you personally think are silly — covering your shoulders in a cathedral, not handing money with the wrong hand, queueing customs, whatever — even though you had no say in making them and they're obviously arbitrary.

5. locals get latitude visitors don't. she'll jaywalk in her own city because she's assessed the risks herself, but she was horrified when a visiting friend did the same, and she doesn't think that's inconsistent of her.

6. this is the one I suspect the editor means: she holds that conventions as such never create moral obligations — a queue is just where people happen to stand, a tipping norm is just how the payments happened to get arranged — so any real wrong involved must come from something underneath (fairness, harm, someone's livelihood depending on it), and if you could secure that underlying thing without the convention, there'd be nothing left to object to.

7. visitors who violate a norm they didn't know existed are still blameworthy, because you chose to travel and could have found out. ignorance of the local is no excuse.

what I want from you: go through these and tell me which ones are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest fix would be for each real tension — ranked by severity, please, not a flat list of worries. I'm fairly sure 1 and 4 are pulling against 6 somehow but I can't see whether 2 can be rescued by grounding it in the fairness rather than the queue itself, or whether that rescue just concedes everything to the editor. and if there's one commitment I could soften or drop that would clean up most of the mess, tell me which, because I'd rather not rewrite her wholesale — she's mine and I'm fond of her. also, briefly: what sort of person ends up holding this bundle? I want to write her as coherent from the inside even if she's wrong, and it'd help to know what view of the world would make all seven of these feel like common sense to someone.
