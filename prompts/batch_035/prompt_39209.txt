In a methods chapter my supervisor passed along, the author summarizes the foundations of choice theory roughly like this: "Before Samuelson, economists treated utility as a quantity of felt pleasure, measurable by introspection. Samuelson's revealed preference program replaced this with observable behavior: when a consumer chooses one bundle over another that was affordable, they are thereby revealed to prefer it. He then proved that a few consistency conditions on observed choices suffice to construct a utility function the consumer can be represented as maximizing. Utility is therefore nothing but a bookkeeping device for recording choices, economics needs no psychology, and it follows that preferences cannot be mistaken — whatever you choose is, by definition, what you prefer — and that interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless."

I'm a first-year PhD student in economics, and something about this feels too tidy, though I can't locate exactly where it goes wrong. Please audit it for me: which parts would a careful historian of choice theory accept, which are simplifications of modest claims into strong ones, and does the final sentence follow from anything accurate earlier in the passage? I'm particularly unsure about two moves: the slide from "can be represented as maximizing a utility function" to "utility is nothing but a bookkeeping device," and the claim that the theory itself establishes that preferences cannot be mistaken. Are these results of the theory, or extra philosophical steps being smuggled in on its behalf? I'd also like to know whether the closing point about interpersonal comparisons is a consequence of revealed preference specifically or whether it's being imported from an older ordinalist argument and credited to the wrong place.
