I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about the modern Stoicism revival — the apps, the corporate resilience trainings, the therapists who trace cognitive behavioral therapy's lineage back to Epictetus and his line about our being disturbed by our opinions rather than by things. In interview after interview I keep hitting the same analogy, usually from the more scientistic side of the movement: that Stoic practice, and the dichotomy of control in particular, trains your "psychological immune system." The pitch runs roughly as follows. External events are pathogens; first impressions are the point of infection; the discipline of assent is the antibody response; you cannot sterilize the world, so you strengthen the organism. The work the analogy is doing is clear and considerable: it licenses the claim that Stoicism is a mental-health technology, dosable and prescribable, rather than a complete ethical system — virtue as the only good, the cosmopolis, the old image of the dog and the cart — that the apps would rather not advertise.

Before I put the analogy in the piece, I want it audited properly. Please spell out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the pathogen, the antigen, the antibody, the fever, the memory of past infections — and then lay out where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that don't. Two candidate breaks seem worth pressing. First, immune responses are automatic and pre-rational, whereas the entire point of the discipline of assent is that nothing gets my assent without me; if the psychological immune system fires as involuntarily as the biological one, the practice collapses into temperament, and the courses have nothing to sell. Second, immune systems misfire — autoimmunity, cytokine storms — and it is not obvious whether that has an illuminating counterpart or an embarrassing one: perhaps the armored Stoic, defended against everything including the people he owes something to. Then tell me the thing my editor will ask anyway: does the therapeutic conclusion — Stoicism as a portable, secular, evidence-shaped resilience technology — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy quietly carrying it the whole time? And if there is a better analogy for what the dichotomy of control actually does in the modern revival, one that neither oversells nor apologizes, I would like to hear it, together with the same honesty about where it too breaks down.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. As you go, flag how confident you are in the central claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
