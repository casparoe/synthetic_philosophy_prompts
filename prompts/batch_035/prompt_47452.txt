You are a professional philosopher of mind with working knowledge of reinforcement learning, both the formalism and the engineering practice. I want you to think carefully and methodically: before settling on an answer, brainstorm two or three candidate principles, then evaluate them against the verdicts below.

Some background: in my lab we keep getting into an argument about whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. One faction holds that a negative reward signal simply is negative valence -- that "pain" in an RL agent and pain in an animal are the same functional thing, a signal that lowers the probability of the preceding behavior. Another faction thinks that's a category error, that reward is a number in a training loop and valence is something that happens to a subject. I've tried to sort out my own position by listing my gut verdicts on concrete cases, but I can't find the principle behind them, and I suspect at least one verdict has to go.

My verdicts:

1. A chess engine whose evaluation function returns a strongly negative score for a position: obviously no suffering.
2. A robot vacuum trained by RL, given negative reward for cliff-adjacent tumbles, which now executes what looks like frantic avoidance behavior near stair edges: no suffering, even though the behavior is superficially panic-like.
3. A virtual creature in a physics sandbox, trained by a genetic algorithm with a penalty term for tissue damage, which writhes and recoils when damaged in simulation: no -- though I'm slightly less confident here, which already bothers me.
4. A mouse caught in a trap: yes, obviously.
5. An octopus with an injured arm, which guards it, stops foraging, and behaves differently for weeks: yes.
6. A patient with pain asymbolia, who feels the pain but reports it doesn't bother her: I say no suffering -- something can be nociceptively detected without being suffered.
7. A patient on morphine who says the pain is still there but no longer matters to her: no suffering -- and I think that's exactly what makes morphine palliation rather than mere dampening.
8. A whole brain emulation of a mouse running on silicon: yes, no less than the biological mouse.
9. A hypothetical future agent deliberately designed with a distress subsystem -- a persistent negative-reward state that gates all planning, reshapes memory, drives relief-seeking, and that its designers describe as the architectural analog of pain: I lean yes, and I notice I'm uncomfortable saying it out loud.

The trouble: verdicts 2 and 3 say "negative reward driving learning and avoidance is not enough," while 9 says a negative-reward construct can be enough. Verdict 8 blocks the easy rescue of saying suffering requires biology. My tentative principle is something like: suffering requires negative valence that is globally integrated -- that structures the whole agent's mental life rather than modulating one policy channel -- but I can't tell whether that distinguishes 9 from 3, or whether it accidentally makes 6 into a suffering case.

So, please: state one or at most two candidate principles precisely, with each clause keyed to the case that motivates it. Say what each principle predicts for cases I haven't listed -- specifically, an adversary who forces persistent negative reward on an agent built like case 9 (can it be tortured?); a swarm of simple agents whose aggregate state looks distressed though no single agent integrates anything; and an agent like case 9 but with episodic memory wipes after every training episode, so no distress ever accumulates. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and say which one you'd overturn and why.
