I'm preparing a seminar unit on where meaning resides — in the message itself, in the receiver, or in the relation between them — loosely organized around Hofstadter's distinction in Gödel, Escher, Bach between the frame message (the signal that there is a message here at all and that it needs decoding), the outer message (the information needed to identify the decoding scheme), and the inner message (the content once decoded). Before I ask my students to take sides, I want the specification that any theory of the location of meaning would have to meet. Write the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it: how the theory must locate all three layers without simply relocating the problem; how it must break the regress of decoding, since recognizing and exploiting an outer message already presupposes decoding capacity — a book of Chinese instructions for reading Chinese is useless to someone who cannot read Chinese; what it must say about messages that are never decoded (a letter read by no one, a language with no living speakers) — whether the meaning is present, absent, or in some sense waiting; how it must handle the same artifact yielding different contents in different receivers, without either collapsing meaning into the receiver's response, so that anything at all could mean anything, or making the receiver's contribution nugatory; what principled line it must draw between a message and mere noise — what makes the sequence of bases in DNA, or a hypothetical signal from another civilization, a message while a crystal lattice is not, given that none of these arrives labeled; whether it may allow that a message can carry its own decoder, as the Rosetta Stone partly did, or whether that is strictly impossible and decoding capacity must always come from outside; and whether it must remain consistent with the plain fact that receivers learn to exploit new outer messages from scratch, as infants and as cryptanalysts do, and so cannot make meaning wholly hostage to a fixed, pre-given codebook. Mark which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of one camp — for instance, whether "meaning exists undecoded" is a genuine requirement or a substantive claim that begs the question against receiver-constituted views — and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied: in particular whether "the same message can carry the same meaning to receivers with no shared history" and "the outer message is itself a message that requires decoding" can both be true. Then, briefly, score three contenders against the list — the triad picture itself, on which meaning is shared between message and decoder with the outer message as the hinge; a thoroughgoing reader-constitution view, on which the reader's activity determines the content entirely; and a purely information-theoretic view, on which meaning is just reliable correlation — and tell me which single requirement does the most work in separating them. A table is acceptable, but the criteria are what I care about; keep the scoring short.
