Some years back I read a psychiatrist named Thomas Szasz who argued that mental illness is a myth: real diseases, he said, are things wrong with the body — a tumor, an infection, damaged tissue — while what psychiatry calls mental illness is really just problems in living, which are nobody's medical business. I never bought it whole, but it has stayed with me, and lately I've noticed I carry around a pile of gut verdicts about what counts as an illness, and I can't find one rule that fits them all. Maybe you can find the rule, or tell me which verdicts have to go.

Here are the verdicts. A man is sluggish and low for years, nothing in his life explains it, and a blood test shows his thyroid gland is underactive; pills fix him. That was an illness — clearly. A different man has the same sluggishness and low mood, no test shows anything, but he hates his job and his marriage is falling apart, and his misery seems like a normal response to a bad life: I don't think that's an illness, and it feels wrong to hand him to doctors instead of to a better life. A woman whose husband died two years ago and still can't get out of bed in the morning: I say not ill, just terribly bereaved — grief, even at that length, is not a disease. A man who hears voices and is convinced his neighbors are poisoning the water supply: no test shows anything there either, but I say that one is an illness; something in him has broken, and it would be broken whatever his life looked like. Epilepsy a hundred and fifty years ago, before anything could be seen in the brain and while seizures were put down to possession: I think it was an illness then too, just undiscovered. A fever: no damaged tissue to point to, but obviously an illness. And one that bothers me: a veteran who decades later still gets nightmares and flashbacks, whose life is otherwise stable. That state is fully explained by what happened to him, which by my reasoning about grief and the unhappy husband should make it not an illness — but I want to call it an illness, and I want him to have a doctor.

So: is there a single principle that delivers all of these? If not, which verdicts should I give up, and which is the cheapest to lose? And what does the winning principle, or the best candidate, say about the case I keep going back and forth on — a man who gambles away his family's savings, says he cannot stop, and has no drug in him: is that an illness, or a weak character? Please be willing to overturn my verdicts rather than rescue them all with something baroque.
