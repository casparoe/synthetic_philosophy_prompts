I work in ML interpretability, and over the past few years I've become a realist about what we're finding: that neural networks genuinely contain features — directionally encoded, individually causally efficacious components that the network itself computes over, in roughly the way a compiled program operates over its variables — and that the business of interpretability is discovery, not convenient description. I take the work on superposition and dictionary learning to have strengthened this picture: even where features aren't aligned with the activation basis, the claim is that they're still there, spread across directions, and recoverable. Increasingly, though, I worry that my realism is built on a definitional trick. "Feature" gets glossed flexibly enough — sometimes as a linear direction, sometimes as a multi-dimensional subspace, sometimes as whatever a sparse autoencoder's decoder column happens to pick out, sometimes as an abstract property only instantiated in a circuit's input-output behavior — that almost any network we probe can be declared to have them, and almost any failed decomposition can be redescribed as a case of messier-but-still-real features.

What would genuinely count against my view? I don't want the standard deflationary sermon about how all description of complex systems involves some projection, and I don't want to be reassured that the realism is just a pragmatic attitude. I want the actual defeaters: empirical findings that would show that the causal structure of trained networks is not organized around discrete, recombinable features at all; formal results; and cases where my view buys me nothing its rivals don't — for instance, whether the success of feature-based descriptions could be explained entirely by the fact that we, the describers, only ever test the network on the distribution we care about, so that our "features" are artifacts of our choice of probes rather than of the network's organization. I'd also like to know which of these defeaters a committed realist would concede as counting against them versus explain away, and whether the explanations-away are legitimate repairs or degenerate problem-shifts — whether, that is, the view has been insulated into something that can't lose, and whether that would make it scientifically empty or just appropriately humble about where the current concepts break.

Where you can, be concrete about what a network would have to be like for the anti-realist to win: continuous, non-compositional causal structure? Arbitrarily fine-grained functional decomposition with no fact of the matter about which carving is the network's own? Interpretability results that replicate mechanistically on one distribution and dissolve on the next? I'd also like an honest statement of what the realist view predicts that a deflationist, instrumentalist, or merely "weakly realist" alternative does not — what puts my view at risk of being wrong, rather than just vague.

Finally, please keep two registers separate throughout: what is genuinely settled among people who know this literature (and by that I mean settled by argument or by experiments that have actually been run, not just widely repeated), and what remains contested — including whether my framing of the position itself, with features as discrete causal units and superposition as their non-aligned mode of existence, is the live version of realism or just one contingent way of spelling it out. If parts of my own characterization of the debate are tendentious or out of date, say so directly rather than building on them.
