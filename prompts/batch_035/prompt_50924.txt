I'm a retired physical chemist — thirty-some years in a spectroscopy lab, mostly measuring things like dipole moments and worrying about error bars — and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy of science rabbit hole. The question I've gotten stuck on is verisimilitude, i.e. whether one false theory can be closer to the truth than another false theory. People keep telling me the intuitive idea is fine and the formalization is the hard part, but honestly I'm not even sure my intuitions are consistent, so I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. Newton's gravitation is closer to the truth than Kepler's laws of planetary motion, even though both are strictly false — Newton kind of contains Kepler plus a lot more, and the extra is roughly right. The Bohr atom is closer than Thomson's plum pudding model — discrete levels, roughly right spectra, versus basically nothing right. Now here's where it gets embarrassing: I have a soft spot for phlogiston theory. My chemist friends treat it as the poster child for dead wrong, but it had substances exchanging stuff during reactions, it got calcination backwards-ish rather than not at all, and I genuinely feel it was closer to the truth than, say, a theory that said combustion was just "fire particles leaving." They laugh at me for this one and I'd like to know if I have to give it up.

The pair I can't reconcile: I feel that a theory which says a lot, with everything slightly off, is closer to the truth than one that says almost nothing but gets its one claim exactly right — the ambitious sloppy theory beats the timid exact one. But I also feel that padding a theory with true-but-irrelevant claims (say, appending a correct fact about beetle mating behavior to your dynamics) shouldn't make it one whit closer to the truth, and neither should tacking on trivialities like "2+2=4" — that's gaming the system, truth-content for free. And one more from my lab days: we always said a measurement of "5.02 plus or minus 0.01" was better than "7" when the true value was 5.0, fine. But whether a theory is "2% off" in mass versus "2% off" in the log of the mass seems to depend on which variable I happen to write my results in, and that choice is conventional — surely how close a theory is to the truth can't depend on my choice of units or parametrization, yet I can't see how to avoid that. So: is there a principle that captures all these verdicts, or most of them? I want the principle stated precisely enough to tell me things like whether a theory that gets nine things exactly right and the tenth catastrophically wrong beats a theory that gets all ten slightly wrong. And please be honest about which of my verdicts has to be sacrificed — I suspect the phlogiston one is on the chopping block, but I'd like to know for sure before I concede it to my friends.
