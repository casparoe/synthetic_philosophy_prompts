In discussions of the Kosovo war and the 2011 Libya intervention, a colleague of mine defends a picture she thinks of as surgical: a sovereign state is like a patient, its borders like skin, and the Security Council like the hospital board that alone can authorize cutting. On her view, humanitarian intervention without that authorization resembles operating without consent — it may occasionally save the patient, but it is battery all the same. So far, so arguable. What interests me is that she then draws two further, more specific conclusions from the same picture. First, she says, humanitarian intervention is like surgery in that half-measures are the worst option: a no-fly zone is like excising part of a tumor and sewing the patient back up — having crossed the skin, you are committed to removing the whole growth, which means regime change; so the real menu in each crisis is all or nothing. Second, the intervening coalition is like the surgical team: once it has operated, it owes the patient postoperative care and owns the complications (civil war, state collapse), so something like the Iraq occupation was not an optional afterthought but an entailment of the original authorization. My own suspicion is that the first conclusion is the one she actually wants and the second is baggage the analogy smuggles in, but I cannot cleanly separate the parts of the picture that carry weight from the parts that merely comfort.

Could you set out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the patient, the skin, the consent, the tumor, the surgeon, and the postoperative complication — then mark where each mapping breaks down? Please distinguish the disanalogies that matter for her two conclusions from the ones that don't (a table may lump everything together, but not every failed mapping sinks every conclusion). Then assess which, if either, of her conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table: is there any way to keep the duty of postwar care while rejecting the all-or-nothing claim, or do they stand and fall together? And if some nearby analogy does the work better — mechanic, gardener, bystander, firefighter — say what it would change about each conclusion. End with a final line in the form "Verdict: all-or-nothing [survives / survives with repairs / fails]; duty of care [survives / survives with repairs / fails]".
