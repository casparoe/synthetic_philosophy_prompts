I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — explainers, mostly — and a while back I did a piece about how categories in biology and medicine refuse to stay inside their definitions ("species," "alive," "healthy" — the virus question, the mental illness question, all that). While reporting it I kept getting sent back to Wittgenstein, specifically the stretch of the Philosophical Investigations where he works through games one by one — board games, card games, ball games, children's ring games — looking for the common thread, and instead of finding one, just ends up gesturing at it: the way members of a family resemble each other, some in build, some in gait, some in temperament, without any single feature showing up in all of them. The piece is done now but the idea stuck with me, and our magazine runs a poem on the back page, and the editor owes me a favor.

What I want is a sestina about family resemblance, with the form carrying the argument rather than just name-checking it. Concretely: the six end-words should be drawn from the games themselves — something like ball, board, card, rule, fun, win (feel free to swap one or two, that's just my stab at it) — so that across the six stanzas the same six words keep coming back in different combinations: sometimes nouns, sometimes verbs, sometimes literal, sometimes stretched into metaphor. Each stanza can dwell on a different kind of game, the way he walks down the list, and somewhere along the way the reader should start to notice the trick — the poem coheres, it feels like one thing, even though no two stanzas share a defining feature, because the end-words keep trading places and never quite settle. That crisscross of the same material in shifting arrangements IS the point: the family resemblance of the words doing the work that a definition was supposed to do. Then in the envoi, get all six words in at last, close together, and let the final line or two land on the thought that we don't use a word like this because of some hidden essence we've discovered, we use it because of this — this overlapping, this resemblance, this same-six-words-different-order. Someone who knows the Investigations should recognize exactly what's being dramatized, and someone who doesn't should still come away with a genuinely good sestina about how the things we call by one name hang together.

dont make it sound like philosophy homework — no name-dropping Wittgenstein in the verse itself, that always reads cheap on a back page. And please actually follow the sestina rules, the 123456 rotation and everything — my editor checks, she's picky.
