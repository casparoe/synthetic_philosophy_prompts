I'm a final-year undergraduate taking my first philosophy of mathematics course, and we just finished a unit on Benacerraf. My grasp of the material from the lectures is decent, but outside of class I mostly get my philosophy secondhand, and I've realized I can't always tell the good secondhand versions from the bad ones. The version I've absorbed comes from a friend of mine, a physics PhD student who reads a lot of philosophy of science and delivers these confident capsule summaries at dinner. Here is what he told me, more or less verbatim: Benacerraf proved back in the seventies that mathematical Platonism can't be right. He had already shown a few years earlier that numbers can't be sets, because set theory gives you several different but equally good ways of building the natural numbers out of sets, and there is no fact of the matter about which construction is the real one. So the only options left are that numbers are ghostly abstract objects outside space and time, or that mathematics is just a game we play with symbols. If numbers are abstract objects, then they are causally cut off from us — no number has ever pushed a neuron in anyone's brain — so by the causal theory of knowledge we couldn't know anything about them; and if mathematics is a symbol game, then arithmetic isn't true in the first place. Either way, mathematical truths turn out to be unknowable or unreal, and Benacerraf's own conclusion was that we should all be nominalists. It's a nice argument, my friend adds, but nobody accepts the causal theory of knowledge anymore, so the dilemma died along with it.

My professor has hinted that this kind of gloss runs together things Benacerraf kept separate, but she moved on quickly to discussing Hartry Field's response and never came back to say exactly where the gloss goes wrong, and I don't feel equipped to audit it myself. So I'd like your help with a careful evaluation. Please take the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a working Platonist philosopher of mathematics would accept as fair, which are common simplifications that hide something important, and which are just wrong. I'm particularly unsure about three things: whether the argument about numbers not being sets and the later dilemma about knowledge of abstract objects are actually two premises of one argument, as my friend presents them, or two distinct arguments with different targets; whether Benacerraf drew any conclusion of his own from the dilemma, nominalist or otherwise; and whether the final verdict — that the dilemma is dead because causal theories of knowledge are out of fashion — actually follows, or whether it depends on the assumption that the only way to make the epistemological horn work is through a specifically causal theory of knowledge rather than some other constraint on how our belief-forming methods must relate to what our mathematical claims are about. Finally, tell me whether my friend's bottom line, that mathematical truths are unknowable or unreal, follows from the parts of his summary that survive your audit.
