I'm in a philosophy of mind course this semester and we just finished the unity of consciousness unit, which means we spent a while on the split-brain material. My TA suggested that instead of summarizing the various unity positions like everyone does, I should try writing up the original studies as an actual case study that the theories get tested against, and that's what I want your help thinking through.

The episode: the commissurotomy patients from the early 60s — epilepsy patients of Bogen and Vogel whose corpus callosum was sectioned (and I think sometimes the anterior commissure too? not sure), then tested in Sperry's lab with lateralized tachistoscope presentations. The salient facts as I understand them: images in the left visual field project to the right hemisphere, only the left hemisphere can really speak, so when a picture is flashed to the left field the patient verbally reports seeing nothing, yet their left hand can pick the object out by touch. And there's the famous confabulation case where the speaking hemisphere is shown a chicken claw, the other hemisphere a snow scene, the two hands point to a chicken picture and a shovel picture respectively, and when asked to explain, the patient says something like the shovel is for cleaning out the chicken coop — the talking hemisphere invents a story that papers over the fact that it doesn't know why its hand did that. Meanwhile, in ordinary daily life these patients mostly came across as a single ordinary person, not two.

Here's the analysis I want. Map the episode onto three candidate readings of it: (a) two subjects of experience in one body, the fragmentation view in the neighborhood of Nagel's; (b) one subject with disunified access — the information is all in one mind but can't be integrated; and (c) something like the view we skimmed from Bayne, where unity is a matter of states standing in the right togetherness relation, so the question becomes which states are together and which aren't. For each reading: what in the episode is evidence for it, what's evidence against, and what should it say about the features that pull in both directions — the fact that the subcortical arousal systems were left intact and shared, the fact that the disconnection is basically invisible outside the lab (you need fixation control and lateralized presentation to see it at all), and the confabulation itself, which looks like one part actively manufacturing narrative unity rather than there being none. Then run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theories? Does the daily-life behavior refute (a), or just show that (a) is a claim about persons in normal circumstances rather than all circumstances? And I'd like you to be honest about where you think each theory is genuinely silent on this stuff and where people writing on it force the theory to speak anyway — that last part is what I keep suspecting the literature of doing and can't quite pin down.
