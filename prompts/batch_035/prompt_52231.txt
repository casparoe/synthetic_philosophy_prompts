I'm a retired seismologist -- thirty-five years staring at seismograms -- and only in the last few years have I gotten into philosophy of science and collective judgment. A neighbor in my complex, a retired high school statistics teacher, walked me through the Condorcet jury theorem and then turned it on me, and I can't find where the argument goes wrong, though I'm sure it proves far too much. Here it is in his numbering:

(1) The jury theorem: if each voter has a better-than-even chance of answering a yes/no question correctly, and the voters' judgments are independent, then as you add voters the probability that the majority answer is the right one climbs toward certainty. With enough voters, the majority is more reliable than any single voter, however smart that voter is.

(2) Every functioning adult is right more often than wrong about matters of fact. Someone who was wrong more often than right about the everyday world -- is the stove hot, is the bus coming, will the paycheck clear -- couldn't get through a week. So every functioning adult is better than a coin flip at getting at the truth.

(3) Being good at getting at the truth is a general capacity, like balance: it doesn't switch off when a question turns technical. So on a contested factual question -- say, whether a given chemical at a given exposure level caused a cluster of illnesses -- the average voter still has a slightly better than even chance of landing on the right answer.

(4) The voters' judgments are independant: no one person's mistake forces another's, each voter makes up their own mind.

(5) The electorate is enormous. So by the theorem, on any contested factual question, the public's majority view is right with near-certainty.

(6) There are vastly more laypeople than specialists, and the theorem's reliability grows with numbers, so whenever the public's majority and the expert consensus disagree, the majority is the more likely to be correct.

(7) Therefore expert consensus ought to defer to majority opinion wherever the two conflict -- and the bigger and more confident the public's disagreement, the surer we should be that the experts have it wrong.

He knows I can't swallow that. I spent a career watching majorities be confidently, durably wrong about things I could check with my own instruments, and I'm not going to start believing a referendum outperforms a metrology lab. But I also don't want to just stamp my foot at the conclusion -- I want to know which step is the bad one, why that step looked so reasonable to both of us, and what it costs to give it up. For what it's worth, I trust (1) as pure mathematics, and my gut says the trouble is somewhere in (3) or (4), but I can't put my finger on the exact slip, and he's got a comeback for each of my stabs at it. Name the step, explain the sleight of hand, and tell me what rejecting it forces me to concede -- including whether the repaired version of his argument still proves something uncomfortable.
