I moderate a mid-sized discussion forum, and part of my job is enforcing our rule against slurs. Drafting and applying that rule has surfaced a pattern in how these words behave, and I want to know whether linguists and philosophers already have a name for it — or whether I need to invent one.

Here is the pattern, in three cases. First, embedding under negation: if a user posts "No one in this thread is a [slur], and I'd ban anyone who was," nobody is called anything — the sentence denies that the word applies — yet it still lands as an attack, and our users report it. Second, embedding in a conditional: "If anyone here is a [slur], they should leave." Conditionals do not normally commit you to their antecedent — "if he's guilty, he should resign" is not an accusation — but this one still reads as an insult. Contrast both with ordinary pejoratives like "stingy" or "coward": "he is not stingy" really does wash the smear out of the sentence. Third, quotation: when I post the rule itself — "the word [slur] is banned effective today" — the word is being mentioned rather than used, not applied to anyone at all, and yet some users still feel it should not appear even once, while others see no problem. So whatever the badness is, it seems to live somewhere that ordinary grammar cannot reach, somewhere "stingy" does not live. Fill in the blanks with any of the slurs you have in mind; I am deliberately not spelling any out.

I know the use/mention distinction is somewhere in the neighborhood, but it only addresses the third case, and awkwardly. I know connotation is the standard tool for words that carry an attitude, but connotation seems to fade when the surrounding sentence pushes back, and this does not — or perhaps I am wrong about that, and I would like to know. What I do not want is a bare label: I want to know what exactly survives the embeddings — something the sentence says, something the act of uttering it does, or something the word carries regardless of the sentence — and which way of carving it best fits all three cases, including the differences among them.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with nothing before point one and nothing after point five. Points one through four: one established concept, distinction, or type of account apiece that could explain the pattern, ordered from closest fit to furthest, each stating what it gets right about my three cases and where it breaks down. Point five: your verdict — which carving handles the cases best, what (if anything) it still leaves unexplained, and, if no established concept fits the whole pattern, a name and definition for the residue.
