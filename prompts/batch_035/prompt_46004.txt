My friend Dana from my game theory course has an argument that iterated dominance either dictates two different outcomes or dictates nothing at all, and I've spent a week on it and still can't find where it breaks. Help me out, because the conclusion feels way too strong.

Her argument:

(1) A rational player never plays a dominated strategy. She means dominated in the wide sense: weakly dominated counts, and the thing doing the dominating can be a mixed strategy. Her reasoning: if some plan -- possibly a random one -- is at least as good as yours against every option the opponent has, and strictly better against at least one of them, then sticking with yours is refusing free insurance. Even granting yourself the right to randomize, you'd never keep the dominated plan.

(2) Both players are rational, and that's common knowledge: each knows it, each knows the other knows it, and so on up.

(3) So dominated strategies can be crossed off, everyone can cross them off, and then the same logic applies to the smaller game that remains. Iterate until nothing dominated is left.

(4) Whatever survives that process is what rational players with common knowledge of rationality must play; if the process whittles things down to a single cell, that cell is the prediction. Rationality dictates it.

(5) Now look at this game (row's payoff first):

```
        L       M       R
A    (0,1)   (1,2)   (0,3)
B    (1,0)   (4,2)   (2,1)
C    (3,0)   (4,1)   (1,0)
```

Chain one: cross off A (B is at least as good against everything and strictly better at L and M), then cross off R (once A is gone, M is at least as good as R against both B and C, and strictly better against both), then cross off B (with R gone, C is at least as good as B against L and M, strictly better at L), then cross off L (against C alone, M beats L). What's left: (C, M), payoffs (4,1).

Chain two: cross off L first (M weakly dominates it), then cross off C (with L gone, B is at least as good as C against M and R, strictly better at R), then cross off A, then cross off R (against B alone, M beats R). What's left: (B, M), payoffs (4,2).

Both chains follow the rules from (3), both keep going until a single cell is left, and they land in different places. Column's payoff comes out 1 or 2 depending on which of her dominated strategies got crossed out first -- a choice that neither player ever makes or even observes. Nobody is "deleting" anything in real life; it's bookkeeping.

(6) So rationality dictates (C, M) and also dictates (B, M). If you flinch and say the procedure just has no determinate output when orders disagree, then (4) collapses and rationality dictates nothing. Either way (4) is broken, and since (4) was supposed to follow from (1) through (3), one of those is false. Her conclusion: either rational players do sometimes play dominated strategies, or common knowledge of rationality is an unusable concept.

Things that bug me about my own attempted replies. I checked, and both (B, M) and (C, M) are Nash equilibria of the full game, so I can't comfort myself with "one chain lands somewhere no rational player would ever go." Also, the first move of each chain is something even a strict-dominance purist allows -- A is strictly dominated by B, and L is strictly dominated by M -- so the trouble doesn't even start with a weak deletion. And running strict dominance to exhaustion just stalls with {B, C} against M, which is extra annoying because the one live question in this game -- whether row plays B or C, worth 2 vs 1 to column, with row indifferent -- is precisely the thing weak dominance claims to settle, and it settles it by coin flip of deletion order.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what its worth, I'd really like to keep (1); her insurance argument seems flatly correct to me. And please don't just say "weak dominance is invalid, use strict" unless you can tell me why they differ in principle and not merely in results. Dana also claims this deletion style of reasoning is what powers forward-induction arguments -- the burning-money stuff, where you read sophistication into someone's costly choice -- so if I have to give up iterated weak dominance generally, is that a cost I'm actually paying, or was that reasoning never on solid ground to begin with?
