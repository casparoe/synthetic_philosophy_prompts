I'm putting together a position statement for my graduate epistemology seminar — the instructor wants each of us to write down where we stand on the value-of-knowledge debate, derived from the Meno, so the rest of the group can poke at it next week. This is what I've drafted as my profile, and I'd like a consistency check before I submit it. (a) I think the reason knowledge is better than mere true belief is that knowledge is safe — a belief that could not easily have been false — so its superiority lies in being reliable rather than lucky. (b) I take whatever extra value knowledge has over true belief to be entirely practical: it concerns success in future action and nothing else; knowledge has no final value beyond that of the true belief it contains. (c) I'm nonetheless drawn to the swamping problem: once a belief is true, its truth swamps the value of the justification or reliability that produced it, so for any single action, a lucky true belief and a piece of knowledge are equally good guides. (d) I think someone who acts on a Gettiered true belief — the person who glances at a stopped clock at the right moment, the visitor in fake-barn county who looks at the one real barn — acts exactly as well as someone who knows; nothing practical is missing. (e) At the same time, I hold that knowledge is the norm of assertion and of practical reasoning: one ought to assert, and perhaps to act on, only what one knows. (f) And I think Plato's own resolution in the Meno is basically right: true beliefs are like Daedalus's statues, which run away unless tethered, and being tied down by an account of why the thing is so is what makes them stay put — knowledge is stable, settled belief.

So: does this package hang together? Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve once you get the scope of the claims right, and if something has to give, what is the cheapest revision and what would it cost me elsewhere in the list? Please rank any tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning — I'd rather know which one a sharp interlocutor would press first.
