I'm a hospital chaplain. Couple times a week, sometimes at 3am right after someone dies, I get the question every chaplain dreads: why did God let this happen. I have maybe ten minutes, the family is in the hallway, and whatever I say next their going to remember for years. I also train volunteer chaplains with basically zero theology background, and they freeze exactly here — either they say nothing, or worse, they reach for some line about everything being part of a plan.

Theologians have whole literatures on this — theodicies, the free will defense, soul-making, the Book of Job, distinctions I half remember from seminary. But none of it is in a shape I can actually use at a bedside, and I need something the volunteers can run too. Can you build me a procedure?

What I want: a set of questions to ask myself (or the family) in order, to figure out what kind of "why" question I'm actually facing — is it a demand for a metaphysical explanation, or is it really "where were you / do you still love me" dressed up as theology — and then, based on that classification, which resources are on the table and which are flat off-limits. It needs to say where the procedure stops with "just be present, drop the theory," and where it hands off to me because it's a values call that a checklist can't make. Then run it on three cases that feel real: (1) a mother asking why God let her 8-year-old die of cancer, who grew up believing suffering is punishment, and who has now concluded her daughter died because of something she did — I need to know if the procedure tells me to engage that frame or just sit with her; (2) a family who wants me to tell their dying father it's God's will so he'll stop fighting the ventilator — they're basically asking me to deploy a theodicy as a tool, and I suspect the answer is no but I want the procedure to say so; (3) an atheist patient, no belief at all, who asks me mockingly whether I've got an answer for him too.

And be honest about where this thing will misfire — I'd rather find out now than in the hallway.
