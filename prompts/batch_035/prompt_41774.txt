the question on the exam was:

"Explain the difference between a theodicy and a defense. Explain why Plantinga's free will response is standardly classified as a defense rather than a theodicy, and what it needs to succeed. Then explain one objection to the free will response that appeals to natural evil, and how a defender of it might reply."

and the student wrote:

"A theodicy and a defense both try to answer the problem of evil, but they aim at different targets. The problem of evil itself comes in two forms: the logical problem, which says God's existence is flat-out incompatible with any evil existing, and the evidential problem, which says the amount and distribution of evil we observe makes God's existence improbable — that's the version Mackie pressed in his famous paper. A theodicy tries to give God's actual reason for permitting evil, whereas a defense doesn't claim to know the real reason; it only needs to exhibit a possible reason, some story on which God and evil coexist. Plantinga's free will response is classified as a defense because he never claims to know why God permits any particular instance of evil. The core idea is that genuinely free creatures can't be causally determined to always do what's right, so it's possible that God could not have created significantly free creatures who never go wrong — every world with significantly free creatures in it contains at least some evil. And since the logical problem needs God and evil to be strictly incompatible, exhibiting even one possible scenario where both exist is enough to defeat it. For the defense to work, it has to show that we really do have libertarian free will, since compatibilist freedom would be no help: if our choices were determined by prior states, God could have arranged things so that we always freely chose right.

The main objection from natural evil is that the free will story only covers moral evil, the stuff agents do to each other. Natural evil — diseases, earthquakes, the fawn that lies suffering for days after a forest fire that Rowe talks about — doesn't result from anyone's choice at all, so free will can't explain it, and a lot of it looks pointless in a way that's hard to square with an all-good God. Plantinga later answered this with his soul-making theodicy, on which natural evil gives creatures opportunities to develop virtues like courage and compassion that they couldnt develop any other way. So moral evil gets explained by freedom and natural evil gets a seperate story, and between the two the objection is supposed to be blunted."
