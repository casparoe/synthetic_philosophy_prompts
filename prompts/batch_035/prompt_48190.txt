I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I've been going down the quantum entanglement rabbit hole after watching some youtube videos on Bell's theorem, and now my intuitions about "locality" are kind of a mess. Quick background for the scenarios: there's this game (I think it's called CHSH) where two players in separate rooms each recieve a random bit, and they each have to output a bit, and they win if the XOR of their outputs equals the AND of the input bits. They can't communicate during the game. If they pre-agree on any deterministic strategy, or share any pre-generated random string, they can win at most 75% of the time. Entangled quantum particles measured at separated detectors let you win about 85%.

My verdicts on the cases:

Case A: Two instances of the same service launched in different datacenters with the same PRNG seed. Ask them the same question, get the same answer. Verdict: obviously fine. The correlation was baked in at startup, nobody is talking to anybody.

Case B: Same two services, but now they're allowed to call each other over the network. Verdict: fine, communication is boring. If they coordinate their answers, no mystery, though the latency is bounded by the speed of light.

Case C: Two labs, far apart, each receives one particle of an entangled pair. Each lab picks a measurement setting at literally the last second, spacelike separated from the other's choice (I looked it up: no light signal could connect the two choices). The outcomes beat the 75% bound from shared randomness. Verdict: something fishy is happening. It feels like one side's setting choice is somehow influencing the other side's outcome, which is influence without a channel, which sounds like cheating.

Case D: But now suppose I try to actually exploit Case C to send a message: I force my measurement setting to encode a bit and my partner just looks at their outcomes to decode it. Turns out this is impossible -- their raw outcomes look like pure noise no matter what I do. No channel, no bandwidth, nothing to intercept. Verdict (and here's where I contradict myself): if nothing can be signaled, then in some sense nothing nonlocal is happening, so Case C is exonerated? But then how the hell did they win the game at 85%? I genuinely go back and forth on this one.

Case E: My buddy says the resolution is superdeterminism: the labs' "random" setting choices were never independent of the particle source, everything was correlated from the big bang, so there's no influence anywhere, just a fixed script. Verdict: I hate this so much, it feels like it explains everything and therefore nothing. But I can't articulate why it's worse than the shared PRNG seed in Case A, which I was totally fine with! Both are "the correlation was baked in earlier." Why does one feel like clean engineering and the other feel like a conspiracy theory?

Case F: Retrocausality -- the future measurement settings send influences backward in time to the source, which then sends out appropriately correlated particles. Verdict: sounds insane, but I notice I can't explain why backwards-in-time influence is worse than the faster-than-light sideways influence I was suspicious of in Case C. They both violate my sense that causes precede... no wait, only one of them does that. I don't know, it just feels icky.

So what I want from you: is there a single principle here? Something like "no information travels outside the light cone" versus "no causation outside the light cone" versus whatever the philosophers actually mean by locality -- which one am I committed to, and does it rate Case A as fine, Case C as suspicious, Case D as exculpatory, and Case E as terrible? Or do I need to pick a stronger/weaker principle and swallow overturning one of my verdicts? Does my principle call that a locality violation or not? And honestly tell me which of my verdicts is the weak link that has to go.
