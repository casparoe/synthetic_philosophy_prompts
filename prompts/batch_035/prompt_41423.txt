Here's the question exactly as it appeared on the exam:

Question 4 (20 points). Lena and Ravi are both home-care aides. Lena visits her elderly clients because she genuinely likes them; she finds the work satisfying and says she'd want to do something like it even without pay. Ravi has no special warmth for the clients — he finds the work draining, and he does it because he believes he has a duty to help people who need help, and that it would be wrong not to. Both do the work reliably and well. (a) On Kant's account of moral worth, does Ravi's doing of this work have greater moral worth than Lena's? Set out the distinction(s) that decide the answer. (b) Suppose someone insists that moral worth depends on the motive from which an action is done. Can such a view assign any moral worth to Lena's actions, given that her motive appears to be inclination rather than duty? Discuss, making use of at least one objection to the Kantian view.

And here's the student's answer, copied word for word:

"For Kant, the moral worth of an action depends on the motive from which it is done, not on its consequences. He distinguishes between actions that are merely in accordance with duty and actions done from duty. His example is the shopkeeper who is honest because honesty is good for business: if he could overcharge a child without anyone finding out, and refrains only because it might hurt his reputation, his action is in accordance with duty but has no moral worth. Kant is quite explicit that when inclination and duty point the same way, the action loses its moral worth, because we can never tell which motive is actually doing the work. Lena visits her clients out of warm feeling, so her actions are like the shopkeeper's — in accordance with duty but not from duty. Ravi acts from duty even though it goes against what he feels like doing, so on Kant's account Ravi's work has moral worth and Lena's does not.

Mill would assess the two differently. In Utilitarianism he agrees with Kant that the moral worth of an action is fixed by its motive, but for Mill the right motive is the greatest happiness rather than duty. Since Lena and Ravi produce the same welfare, utilitarianism gives them equal moral worth at the level of the act, and would judge Lena's character better overall, since she is the kinder person.

For part (b): since inclination is by definition a form of self-interest, Lena's warmth for her clients is just self-interest in a nicer costume — she visits them because it makes her feel good. So the motive-based view cannot give her actions any moral worth. An objection to Kant here is Schiller's, that his view makes it a moral defect to enjoy doing your duty, which seems wrong. But Kant can accept that and just say we should try to act against our inclinations so it is clearer the action is from duty, which is what he in effect recommends."
