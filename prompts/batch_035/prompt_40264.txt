I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and I am writing the take-home final. Please draft a single essay question on Wigner's "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" that cannot be answered by summarizing the lecture or reciting its standard glosses.

The question's difficulty should come from forcing students to engage with a selection-effect reply to Wigner: the claim that mathematics' effectiveness is only apparently unreasonable because we keep score selectively. Textbooks canonize the formalisms that fit phenomena, while the vast graveyard of mathematical structures that describe nothing, and the phenomena that resisted mathematization for centuries (turbulence, heredity before population genetics, most of economics), drop out of the record. On this view, asking why mathematics is so effective is like asking why fortune-tellers' hits are so impressive. The question should make students assess whether this reply actually dissolves Wigner's puzzle or merely relocates it — for instance, by leaving unexplained why there are enough hits to select in the first place, or why the selection happens at the level of laws rather than ad hoc curve-fitting.

The question should additionally require students to distinguish Wigner's puzzle from the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument, which students in this course routinely conflate with it, and it should be anchored in at least one concrete case from Wigner's own lecture (the Newtonian extension from falling bodies to planetary orbits, or the matrix mechanics of Born, Jordan, and Heisenberg, would both work) rather than inviting a general survey.

Please provide three things:

1. The question itself, worded so that the selection-effect reply and its strongest counterargument are laid out in the prompt's set-up, in the student's own terms, rather than left for the student to recall from lecture.

2. A model answer of approximately 700 words that argues for a definite verdict about whether the selection-effect reply succeeds, rather than cataloguing positions without committing to any.

3. A marking scheme worth 25 marks in total, organized around five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how students typically fumble it. Also list two or three specific mistakes that should cost marks across the board — for example, treating Wigner's puzzle as identical to the indispensability question about mathematical ontology, or padding the answer with a tour of the history of mathematics.
