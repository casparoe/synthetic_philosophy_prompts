For most of my career I was a systems neuroscientist, and I confess that I came to philosophy of mind only in retirement, when I finally had time to read beyond the journals. A question I keep circling back to, partly because my old colleagues still debate it over email, is whether brain size has any direct moral significance — not as a correlate of something else, but in itself. I've tried to settle what I actually believe by walking through cases, and I'd like help extracting a principle from my verdicts, because I can feel them pulling in different directions. The cases: a child born with severe microcephaly, whose brain is a fraction of the typical size, has exactly the same moral status as any other child — no discount whatsoever. A sperm whale, with a brain roughly five times the size of a human's, does not thereby outrank a human in moral status, though I do think it matters a great deal. A corvid, with a brain smaller than a walnut, seems to me to matter more than some mammals with substantially larger brains, because of what the bird demonstrably does with it. A patient in an irreversible coma whose brain is of entirely normal size has, on my view, lost most of what made their life matter, so sheer anatomical bulk clearly does nothing there either.

So far my verdicts point toward "function, not mass." But here is where I get stuck. I also hold — and my scientific training makes this hard to give up — that a being with the same functional organization as a human but at greatly enlarged scale, say a hundred billion cortical neurons at ordinary density rather than our twenty-odd, would plausibly matter more rather than merely differently, if only because there is more of whatever it is that scales. And I judge that a 15% difference in brain volume between two people (I had colleagues who studied exactly such natural variation) carries no moral significance at all, while the comparable proportional difference between species seems to me to track something real. So within a kind, size seems inert; across kinds and at large enough magnitudes, it seems to matter. I also keep coming back to the border cases: an octopus whose neurons are distributed between brain and arms, and a hypothetical engineered animal with a huge but simplistic cortex, like an elephant's brain grown onto a mouse's behavioral repertoire.

What I would like, then, is a principle — or a small set of candidates — stated precisely enough to make predictions. What work is brain size actually doing in my verdicts: is it a mere proxy for some capacity that saturates, is it constitutive of something like the magnitude of experience, or does it do no work at all anywhere, so that some of my verdicts simply have to go? I'd want the principle to tell me what to say about the cases I haven't listed — the distributed octopus, the overgrown-simple cortex, and an artificial system with no brain but human-scale function — and I want an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot save, and which of those I should revise. Please don't be gentle with the enlarged-scale verdict; it may be the one my scientific priors are merely protecting.
