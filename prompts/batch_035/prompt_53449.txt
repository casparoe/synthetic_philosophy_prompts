Last month my cousin lent me an enormous, strange book — the title is just a mathematician's, a painter's, and a composer's names strung together — and one chapter of it has been living in my head ever since. It's a dialogue in which an anteater sits down for a friendly chat with an intelligent ant colony. The colony converses fluently and even has a name, while the individual ants are described as far too mindless to understand a word of what's being said; the anteater happily snacks on a few of the colony's ants during the conversation, and the colony doesn't mind, since what the colony as a whole thinks is what matters, and a few dozen ants here or there are nothing to it. I found the idea delightful and then disturbing in roughly equal measure, and I made the mistake of bringing it up at my brother-in-law's barbecue, where his neighbour Marcus and my sister Dana went at it for the better part of an hour while I mostly watched.

Marcus took the colony's side. He pointed out that colonies genuinely solve problems: ants laying down scent trails end up, between them, selecting the shortest route to a food source that no single ant ever saw whole; colonies shift workers between foraging, nursing, and defence as conditions change; some colonies are bold and some are timid; and they get more set in their ways as they age. No ant knows anything, he said, so the knowing has to live at the level of the colony — the ants are like the brain's cells, and the colony is the one doing the thinking. Dana thought this was just a pretty metaphor. A river also finds the shortest path downhill, she said, and nobody claims the river has a mind; markets and traffic jams behave in ways that look smart from outside, but the smartness is in our description, not in them. Besides, she added, brain cells are wired to each other in stable circuits, and that's where a person's memories live, whereas ants just wander around following smells — a gust of wind rearranges the whole "circuit," and a month later not one of the original ants is even alive.

Marcus answered that the same is true of him: his brain cells die and are replaced throughout his life, and what persists is the pattern, not the particular cells, so the colony's ants turning over proves nothing. Dana's real objection, as she put it, was that when Marcus decides something, there is a Marcus it happens to — one thing having the experience — and she couldn't see where that is in a colony. The "decisions" are just the total of thousands of ants each doing their own thing; slice a colony in two with a shovel and you get two colonies, neither of which misses being one, and you can't do that to a person. And, she said, look at the anteater in my book: it eats the colony's ants while the colony carries on chatting. Anything you can graze off of, mouthful by mouthful, without its minding, can't really be a mind. Marcus shot back that people survive strokes that destroy whole chunks of the brain and are still themselves, so the shovel case just shows the colony is more resilient than we are, not that it thinks less.

They eventually drifted off toward the potato salad, and I was left holding the whole argument without any idea how to settle it. Could you go through their exchange and tell me who is right about what at each step — not just who wins overall, but which of the specific points each of them makes actually holds up and which don't? And if there is something important that both of them kept missing, I'd like to know that too.
