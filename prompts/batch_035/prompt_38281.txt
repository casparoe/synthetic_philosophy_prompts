My lab keeps leaning on the courtroom picture when pitching debate-style oversight: two models argue opposite sides about some artifact (a safety report, a piece of code, a claim about what a system actually did), and a weaker human judge just follows the cross-examination. The pitch goes: like in an adversarial trial, whatever is false gets exposed by the opposing side, so the judge can reliably pick the winner without ever being able to check the substance themselves. Someone skeptical in the group keeps saying the analogy is doing all the actual work — real courts convict inocent people all the time, cross-examination rewards confident performance over accuracy, and the judge in a real court can always go look at the physical evidence, which is precisely the thing ours can't do. There's also a worry floating around here that the two debaters could tacitly collude — each avoiding the thread that would expose both of them, like two defense attorneys who quietly keep their clients' stories out of conflict — which the courtroom picture arguably encourages us to forget, since prosecutors and defense attorneys have different clients.

Audit the analogy for me: what maps onto what (judge, lawyers, witnesses, perjury rules, the appeals process), where the mapping breaks, and — this is the important part — which of the disanalogies actually matter for the oversight use-case versus which ones a courtroom defender could wave off. Real courts are messy for lots of reasons that don't transfer; I want the load-bearing differences isolated, not a laundry list. Then commit to a verdict: does the conclusion "the judge doesn't need to understand the content, only the exchange" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it being carried by the courtroom picture the whole time? And if you think there's a better analogy for what debate-training is really doing (sports with umpires? peer review? something weirder?), pick one and defend it — don't hand me a menu of options to choose from.
