So my reading group has been arguing about preference idealization lately -- like, the idea that your "real" preferences are what you'd want if you were fully informed and thinking clearly, not the ones you actually act on -- and I've become convinced that the whole debate deserves a parody that people who know the literature would actually find fair. Something like how a good terms-of-service parody makes a contracts lawyer laugh. I could try writing it myself but I keep being too nice to the position.

Write me a customer-facing FAQ, around 700 words, for "Volition+," a fictional subscription app whose whole pitch is that it acts on your *coherent extrapolated volition* instead of your stated preferences. The tagline can be something like "You, but finished." The humor has to come from the actual content of preference-idealization views, not from strawmanning them: e.g., the app dismisses your 2am taco order because your extrapolated self isn't hungry; it quietly cancels your lottery tickets; it interprets "snooze" as "no"; its "Coherence Correction" feature resolves your inconsistent preferences into a single tidy ordering without asking which parts to keep. Give it at least one genuinely pointed Q&A about the identity question -- who is the customer, present-tense you or the idealized version, and can they disagree without one of them being wrong -- and one about the "extrapolation dial" (settings like: Better Informed, Smarter, More Patient, The Version Of You That Got More Sleep). Somewhere it should cheerfully admit that if you and your extrapolated self ever have a dispute, the app sides with the extrapolated self every time, and frame this as a feature. A quote from an invented "Chief Extrapolation Officer" would be good.

Keep it deadpan and legible: no winking, no sledgehammer asides about how creepy this is, and crucially, make it fair -- the app should occasionally be *right* in ways the reader grudgingly has to concede (you really do regret staying up scrolling, you really do want the alarm to work). The reader should come away unsure whether the target is the app or themselves. Formatting should look like a real FAQ: bolded questions, chipper corporate answers, maybe a closing "Still have questions?" that doesn't quite answer the obvious one. And if you can work in something about how the app handles households or friend groups -- like, whose volition gets extrapolated when the preferences are pooled -- that's the cherry on top, since thats the part of my reading group's argument that got genuinely heated.
