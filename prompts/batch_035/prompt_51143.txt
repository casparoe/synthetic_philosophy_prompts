I'm a third-year philosophy undergraduate taking a course on perception and empiricism, and my instructor has asked me to run next week's tutorial for the first-years — fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and students who have read a little Descartes but no Locke. The topic is Molyneux's question: whether someone blind from birth, newly granted sight and shown a cube and a sphere, could tell which was which before touching either, given that they already knew both objects perfectly well by touch. Don't explain the problem to me; plan how I should teach it. Work out the order the pieces must go in — I suspect the distinction between the empirical and the conceptual versions of the question has to come early, but I don't want to presuppose that if there is something else that has to be in place first. Decide which single case should carry the whole session and what it must include and avoid: the obvious candidates are Locke's blind man who had never understood colour and the famous eighteenth-century cataract-recovery report, and part of what I need from you is a judgment about which of these, if either, is the right vehicle rather than a trap. Map the misconceptions the students will bring or form — my guesses are that they will treat any single recovery story as settling the question, that they will run 'could see at all' together with 'could tell which was which', and that they will think the whole thing is really about whether concepts are innate — and say exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off rather than left to fester. My own hunch from the course, which I would like built in or explicitly rejected, is that the real action is a three-way split between mere visual discrimination, identification by any visual means whatever, and recognition by visual shape alone, and that much of the historical debate talks past itself by sliding between these. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level and say so to them, and give me one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would show whether they have grasped why the question stayed open for over three centuries and was still being put to empirical test within the last two decades, rather than just memorized the history.
