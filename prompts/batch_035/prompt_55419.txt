I teach an upper-level seminar on free will and philosophy of action, and I'm writing the take-home final. The last third of the course dealt with the digger wasp: Fabre's observations, retold by Wooldridge and put to philosophical work by Dennett and Hofstadter, whose coinage sphexishness the class has been using as shorthand for behavior that looks intelligent up close but turns out to run in a rigid, restartable loop. The trouble is that a final question on this material almost writes itself — recount the wasp story, contrast the wasp with reasons-responsive agency, gesture at Frankfurt and Fischer, done -- and I want to make that recitation impossible.

Here is the framing I want the question built around, which I've been developing in conversation with a colleague and haven't seen in the literature: the class noticed that sphexishness is almost never stated as anything other than a binary, and we began to suspect this is a mistake. A thermostat is rigid about everything; the wasp is rigid about nest provisioning but arguably flexible elsewhere; a chess engine is fantastically flexible within one domain and utterly sphexish a millimeter outside it; and, uncomfortable as it is to say, humans seem to have their own reset loops -- someone who walks into a room and forgets why, or who reaches for their phone in every pause in a conversation, is at least superficially doing a small version of the wasp dragging the cricket back to the threshold and starting the inspection over. Suppose we invented a term for the graded version, which we've been calling loop-liability: the extent to which an agent's behavior, in some domain, is hostage to a routine it cannot notice itself executing. The question I want must force students to take a stand on whether sphexishness is binary, graded, or domain-relative, and then draw the consequence: if human freedom differs from the wasp only in degree of loop-liability, the compatibilist has to say what exactly about low loop-liability is worth calling freedom; if instead there's a qualitative jump, the student has to say where the jump could possibly come from without smuggling in an unmoved mover.

Please draft the exam question itself (suitable for a 1,500-word answer by a strong student over a weekend), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. At least one must-hit point should concern a student who tries to have it both ways by treating loop-liability as a mere metaphor rather than a property that can be made precise, and the marking scheme should explain what that student owes us but never delivers. You may use the wasp, the thermostat, and the chess engine as examples, but the model answer should not lean on them as crutch; it should do its real work on the structure of the freedom-at-stake question.
