I work in operations at a distribution center, and last month we had an incident where a palletizer reassigned itself to a backup routine after a sensor got dirty (a cleaning that was supposed to happen weekly but had quietly slipped to monthly), and someone got a badly pinched hand. The internal review basically ended with a shrug: the line operator did what the UI told her, the maintenance guy followed the (outdated) schedule he'd been given, the vendor's manual had a footnote about the backup behavior that nobody had flagged to us, and the engineer who wrote the routine three years and two employers ago is long gone. Since then I've gotten kind of obsessed with the philosophy literature on this, the stuff about responsibility gaps for autonomous systems — the original idea being that a machine can do something that nobody could foresee or control, so nobody is responsible, which seems bad because it means harm without a responsible party. Fine. But when I read around, a lot of the responses seem to talk past each other, and I can't tell whether the gap in our incident is a real philosophical thing or just a fancy name for the boring old problem that big organizations diffuse blame across many hands, which happened long before robots.

So here's what I want: build me a thought experiment that settles it, at least for my own head. The scenario must be realistic — a warehouse, a hospital, a port, whatever, no killer robots and no magically emergent machine intelligence — and one paragraph. It must come as a matched pair: the same accident told twice, once where the harm flows through the autonomous system, and once where the exact same harm is produced by the same chain of humans doing the same steps by hand (the humans making the same calls the software would have made, for the same reasons). The autonomous version has to generate a genuine responsibility gap — a real failure of whatever conditions you think responsibility needs (control, foreseeability, ability to do otherwise) — while the human version comes out as something less than that: diffusion of responsibility, sure, but not a gap. And here's the hard constraint: you can't get the gap by stipulation, no just declaring that the system's behavior was unforeseeable or that nobody could control it. Also you can't use the standard easy route of the machine having learned something unpredictable in deployment — no self-modifying learner. The gap should fall out of the structure of the case even though every individual component was understood by somebody at some point.

Then, after the pair, walk me through the audit: which condition of responsibility exactly fails in the machine version and not the human version, which single feature of the case is doing the work (is it the transfer of decision-making across time? the way the system combines decisions from people who never interact? something about no human being able to see the whole chain at once?), and what happens to the verdict if you remove or weaken that feature. Finally, give the strongest attack on the case from someone who thinks responsibility gaps don't exist — someone who'll say that whatever failed in the machine version fails equally in the human version, or that tracing responsibility back to the designers or deployers always closes the gap — and tell me whether that attack actually works against your pair or only against the sloppier versions of the thought experiment (like ours, maybe, which is the part that keeps me up at night).
