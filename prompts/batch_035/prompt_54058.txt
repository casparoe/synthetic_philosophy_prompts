Retired chemist here, spent 35 years on polymers, and now that the lab is behind me I've fallen down a philosophy of mind rabbit hole, so bear with me. My question is about my friend Diane. We met decades ago when our labs shared a floor; she later moved into neuropsych and spent most of her career around split-brain patients, and now that we're both retired we argue about conciousness over coffee. Over the years she's handed out verdicts on whether a given patient is "one subject or two," and next month she's staying with me for a week, during which I plan to spring some new cases on her. I'd like to walk in with a prediction, but first I need help working out what rule she's actually running, because I honestly can't tell.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A patient shortly after full callosotomy (severing the main cable between the hemispheres). In the lab the two halfs act like they don't know about each other — one hand picks something up, the other puts it right back, and if you flash a picture to just one visual field only the opposite hand can act on it. Diane: "Two subjects in one skull, at least for now."

2. The same patient a year later at home. She cooks, gossips, drives (barely), and insists she feels exactly like one person, no different from before the surgery. In the lab, if you test carefully, the information still doesn't cross — the two halves still can't be made aware of the same thing at the same time. Diane, watching her chat over coffee: "One person, look at her." Diane, watching the same woman in the lab the next week: "Two streams, no question." She says both without blinking.

3. A man who had an entire hemisphere removed as a child, for severe epilepsy. Diane: "One, obviously, that one's easy."

4. A patient with alien hand syndrome after a stroke — the left hand does things he says he didn't intend and doesn't feel like his. Diane: "One subject, he just has a badly behaved body part. Sad, but one."

The new cases I want to spring on her:

A. A woman born with no corpus callosum at all — it just never developed. Fully functional adult, a schoolteacher I believe, never noticed anything odd about herself, says she's obviously one person. Careful testing shows only some kinds of information get across (there are smaller backup cables, apparently), and she's learned tricks to compensate without knowing it. One or two?

B. Conjoined twins joined at the head, with some shared brain tissue at the junction. Reportedly, if you show something to just one of them, sometimes the other laughs at it before anyone says a word. Two girls, two heads, mostly separate bodies below. One subject, two, or something in between?

C. A split-brain patient twenty years out, who has unconsciously learned to cross-cue — the one hand nudges, the other points, the head turns just so — so that out in the world he runs like a perfectly ordinary single person and insists he is one. Under strict lab conditions there's still no real information sharing, just signaling. In his kitchen, is he one? In the lab, two? Same man either way?

What I want from you: a guess at her verdict on A, B, and C; then the candidate rules that fit her track record — I can see a "count them by information flow" rule and a "count them by how they live when nobody's testing them" rule, but I suspect there are others — and which one fits best, especially given that she'll happily say both "one" and "two" about the very same patient depending on which room she's standing in. And then tell me what case I should invent to force her to choose, because if I get another "it depends on the room" I'm going to scream.
