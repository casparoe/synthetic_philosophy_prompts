The exam question was:

"In his 1950 paper, Turing proposed what he called the imitation game. First, describe the setup as Turing originally presented it. Then assess whether passing the test gives us evidence that a machine can think, and discuss how Searle's Chinese room argument bears on this."

Here's one student's answer, typed out as submitted:

"Turing's imitation game comes from his 1950 paper in Mind. It's played with three people: a man (A), a woman (B), and an interrogator (C). The interrogator stays in a separate room and communicates with the other two only in writing, through typed answers. The interrogator's object is to determine which of the two is the man and which is the woman. The man's object is to try to cause the interrogator to make the wrong identification, and the woman's object is to help the interrogator. Turing then asks what happens when the machine takes the part of the man: if the machine does as well as the man did, the test is passed. In practice this becomes the familiar setup where a judge chats with a hidden human and a hidden machine, and has to say which is which. Turing was pretty confident about how soon this would happen — he predicted that by the year 2000, machines would be able to pass, fooling interrogators more often than not.

Turing also thought the question of whether machines can think is too vague to be worth arguing about directly, so he replaced it with the more precise question of whether machines can succeed in the imitation game. And he dealt with several objections, including the Lady Lovelace objection, that machines can only ever do what we program them to do.

Searle's Chinese room argument, published about thirty years later, is aimed directly at Turing's test. Imagine a person locked in a room who follows a huge rulebook to answer questions written in Chinese, and does it so well that people outside think the room is a fluent Chinese speaker. But the person inside doesn't understand a word of Chinese, and by extension the room as a whole doesn't either — it's just the same kind of symbol shuffling a computer does. Since the room would pass any Chinese-language version of the Turing test, Searle shows that passing the test can't be proof of understanding. So the Turing test fails as a criterion for mind: purely behavioral evidence can never show that a machine thinks, because behavior always leaves open what's really going on. What we'd need to do instead is look inside the system and check whether it has genuine semantic understanding, and by that standard the test is useless as a test of thinking."
