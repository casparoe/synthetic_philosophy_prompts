I write about science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, which means my whole job is gently extracting honesty from people who'd rather be polite and bluntness from people who'd rather be managing me. My closest colleague through all of it is Sanne, a Dutch researcher-turned-fact-checker who has never once softened a sentence, and over the years i've collected her verdicts on every little act of politeness-deception we run into:

- She thinks it was wrong of me to tell our friend her cake was really good when it was dry as chalk (her description of the cake, not mine).
- She did NOT think it was wrong to let her dying uncle keep beleiving the chemo might still work.
- She bluff-quoted a rate she'd never have accepted in her freelance negotiation with our editor and considers that completely fine, even funny.
- She told a mutual friend his pitch was strong when she thought it was weak; he pitched it everywhere, got rejected everywhere, and she now says the softening was the real mistake, not the pitch.
- She happily ran a week of misdirection to get a friend to a surprise party.
- She tolerates, barely, my Tokyo habit of answering party invites with "maybe next time" when I mean no -- finds it exasperating but not wrong.

Here's the new one: our colleague Emma has a friend finishing a first novel who has begged her, repeatedly and explicitly, for brutal honesty on the draft. Emma thinks it's bad. But she also thinks the friend is one harsh critique away from quitting writing forever, so she gave warm, general praise -- genuinely, I'm fairly sure, for the friend's sake and not to dodge an awkward conversation.

What will Sanne say about Emma? Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up: (1) your prediction for Sanne's verdict, (2) the rule that best explains her track record, (3) the strongest rival rule that explains it just as well, (4) which of those two you'd bet on and why, and (5) a new case that would actually split the two rules. Don't be polite about it.
