At my group meeting last month a visiting postdoc tried to open a discussion about whether our lab's work on decoherence bore on any particular interpretation of quantum mechanics, and our PI shut it down with Hawking: philosophy is dead. He was quoting the opening chapter of The Grand Design, where Hawking and Mlodinow announce that the big questions — why there is something rather than nothing, why the laws are what they are — were traditionally questions for philosophy, "but philosophy is dead," since "Philosophy has not kept up with modern developments in science, particularly physics," and scientists must now carry the torch. Our PI invoked it as a license to skip the discussion and get back to the measurements. The postdoc fumed afterward that the same book then spends a whole chapter building a position it calls model-dependent realism — which is to say, the book declares philosophy dead and then immediately does some — but he couldn't get any traction on why that mattered, and neither could I. I want to be better armed than he was.

What I'd like is a dissection of the slogan itself. "Philosophy is dead" gets used in my department the way "that's just philosophy" gets used everywhere: to end a conversation. But I can't tell whether it's a substantive claim or a grunt. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — the sociological one about professional academic philosophy having no institutional effect on physics as practiced; the methodological one that philosophical reflection on science has produced nothing useful to working scientists; the epistemological one that science has subsumed philosophy, so that conceptual questions are either empirical in disguise or empty; maybe the normative one that scientists should simply decline to spend attention on them; and any others you think are genuinely in the neighborhood, since I don't trust my taxonomy. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, defensible but contested, or flatly false, and then — this is the part I actually need — say which readings, if any, would support the use our PI put it to: dismissing, mid-meeting, the question of whether interpretations of quantum mechanics matter to our work. My suspicion is that most of the defensible readings don't license that move at all (the question we were discussing was arguably internal to physics), and that the readings that would license it are the indefensible ones, but I haven't thought it through and could easily be wrong.

Two requests about the answer itself. First, I don't want a literature survey or a list of who responded to whom — I have no way to check references right now and would rather have your own analysis. Second, if you think my framing is off — say, because the slogan in the book is functioning as a rhetorical pivot rather than a thesis, or because "philosophy" in it means something narrower than I'm assuming, or because the postdoc's tu quoque about model-dependent realism doesn't actually land — please say so directly rather than working within my terms. I'd rather be corrected than flattered into thinking I've found the one question to bring back to the meeting.
