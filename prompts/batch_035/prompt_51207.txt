As a philosopher of language who has taught the vagueness literature for the better part of thirty years, I have grown steadily less confident that the dispute between epistemicists and supervaluationists about vagueness is a single disagreement at all. Papers continue to appear; the arguments on each side are, by now, well known to everyone; and nobody moves. What I should like from you is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than another rehearsal of the standard arguments.

The setting, briefly, so that your diagnosis can engage with what the parties actually say. Both sides accept the same data: there are borderline cases of 'heap', 'tall' and 'bald'; each predicate is sorites-susceptible; and competent speakers feel that there is no determinate boundary between heap and non-heap. Beyond that they part company. The epistemicists, in the tradition of Timothy Williamson's Vagueness (1994) and Roy Sorensen's work on the topic, hold that every vague predicate in fact has a sharp boundary, fixed by our usage, but one that our cognitive limitations make unknowable: vagueness is, on this view, a kind of ignorance. The supervaluationists, in the tradition of Kit Fine's 'Vagueness, Truth and Logic' (1975) and as developed more recently by Rosanna Keefe, hold instead that vagueness is semantic indeterminacy: a vague expression admits a range of equally admissible precisifications; a sentence is true only if it comes out true on all of them; and a borderline statement is therefore neither true nor false.

My working hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is this: the two parties can agree on every observable fact about speakers — their dispositions to assent, their tolerance of small changes, their reluctance, in the border zone, to assert either that p or that not-p — and the residual disagreement is not about vagueness at all but about what it takes for the use of a word to determine truth conditions. The epistemicist holds that anything short of a determinate extension fails to make sense of truth ascriptions; the supervaluationist holds that a semantic machinery of precisifications suffices, so that there is no further fact for the epistemicist to be gesturing at. But I can also see the shape of a rival diagnosis: that the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading — one a question in the philosophy of language about what our word 'heap' means, the other a question in the philosophy of truth about whether truth must be bivalent — and that the appearance of direct contradiction, over whether a borderline statement lacks a truth value or instead has one unknowably, is generated by an equivocation on 'determinately'.

So please tell me: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two questions under one name? State precisely what the epistemicist asserts that the supervaluationist denies, in a formulation that is not simply a restatement of one of the theories; say which kind of disagreement the impasse betrays, and whether it is best explained by divergent starting intuitions, by divergent standards for what a semantics must achieve, or by something else; and then tell me what, on your diagnosis, would count as progress — a general theory of how linguistic conventions fix semantic values, further work on the 'definitely' operator and the pressure of higher-order vagueness, empirical work on categorisation, a change of question — or whether we should expect no progress at all, and why.
