I am grading finals for a third-year course in philosophy of physics and would like a second opinion on one script. The question, worth 20 marks, read as follows:

"Bell's theorem is often summarised as showing that 'local realism' is false. This summary hides several subtleties. (a) Explain what Bell's locality assumption amounts to: state the factorisation condition and give its physical motivation. (b) Explain the status of determinism in Bell's derivation — is it a premise, or is it derived, and from what? (c) Explain why violations of Bell inequalities do not permit superluminal signalling, and say whether this means locality is preserved. (d) Assess the claim that an interpretation can preserve locality by denying realism. Note that treating realism as an independent premise of Bell's derivation will be penalised: say why."

The student's answer reads, in full:

"Bell's theorem concerns measurements on pairs of particles prepared in the singlet state. Suppose each particle is sent to a distant observer, who independently chooses which spin direction to measure. Quantum mechanics predicts correlations between the outcomes that violate the CHSH inequality: the relevant combination of correlations can reach 2√2, whereas any theory satisfying Bell's premises gives at most 2. Experiments from Aspect onwards, up to the loophole-free tests of 2015, confirm the quantum prediction. So Bell's premises cannot all be true.

Bell's derivation requires two independent premises: locality and realism. Locality means that the outcome at one wing cannot depend on the measurement setting chosen at the other wing. Realism means that outcomes are determined in advance by pre-existing properties of the particles — hidden variables. Since the experiments, most have concluded that realism is the premise to abandon, and this is what Copenhagen-style interpretations do: the particles have no definite spin values until measured.

However, the nonlocality this introduces is benign. The no-signalling theorem shows that the marginal statistics at each wing are independent of the setting at the other wing — in the singlet set-up each outcome is 50/50 whatever the distant observer chooses. So no observer can use the correlations to transmit a signal faster than light, and special relativity is not threatened.

Finally, one might worry that denying realism is a cheap escape, but it is not clear what more is required. As Jarrett showed, Bell's locality condition decomposes into two conditions: parameter independence (no dependence of one outcome on the distant setting) and outcome independence (no dependence between the two outcomes beyond the shared state). Quantum mechanics violates only outcome independence, which is just the realist assumption that the outcomes were already fixed. Since parameter independence — genuine locality — is preserved, quantum mechanics is a local theory in the only sense that matters physically."
