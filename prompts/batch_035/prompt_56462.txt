A chapter of a popular book on the foundations of mathematics that I have just finished reading claims, at the end, to have settled the century-old quarrel between classical and constructivist mathematics, and I would like to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it to others. The chapter's argument runs roughly as follows.

The author presents L. E. J. Brouwer as the first mathematician to insist that mathematics must be constructive, and places his break with Hilbert's formalist school in the 1920s. On the author's telling, the constructivist's central thesis is that a mathematical statement is true only when somebody actually holds a proof of it, and that mathematical objects do not exist until a mathematician constructs them. It is this ontological doctrine, the author says, that explains the constructivist's rejection of the law of excluded middle: for an object that has not yet been built, there is simply no fact of the matter about whether it has a given property, so a disjunction asserting that it either has the property or does not can be neither affirmed nor denied.

The author then reports that Heyting tidied Brouwer's rather mystical views into a clean formal logic, which behaves much like a three-valued logic: each proposition is either proved, refuted, or as yet undecided. And finally the author brings in two heavyweights to close the case. First, Gödel and Gentzen showed that every theorem of classical arithmetic can be translated into a theorem of intuitionistic arithmetic, from which the author concludes that the two systems prove exactly the same theorems and so disagree about nothing. Second, Bishop's constructive analysis reconstructed the whole of classical analysis without the law of excluded middle, showing that the intuitionists sacrificed no actual mathematics by their austerity. Hence, the author concludes, the entire quarrel between Brouwer and Hilbert was ultimately verbal, and a relic of temperament rather than substance.

I am broadly inclined to believe the author, since I know that Bishop's work exists and that the Gödel and Gentzen results are real. But I confess that the three-valued description struck me as odd, because I dimly recall that intuitionistic logic cannot be captured by truth tables at all, and the inference from the translation result to 'they prove exactly the same theorems' felt suspiciously quick. Would you please audit the chapter for me? Go through it part by part and tell me which claims a working constructivist or logician would accept, which they would regard as a tolerable simplification, which they would wince at, and which are simply mistaken. In particular, I should like to know whether the author has conflated anything the constructivist tradition keeps distinct, and whether the closing conclusion — that the classical–constructivist dispute is purely verbal — follows from whatever accurate parts of the chapter survive your corrections.
