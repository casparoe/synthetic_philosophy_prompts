I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I've been going in circles with my advisor on anthropic reasoning. Here is the proposition I want a number on: if two theories are empirically equivalent given all available evidence and differ only in how many observers they posit, a rational agent with no other relevant information should assign higher credence to the theory positing more observers — the self-indication assumption. My credence that this is true is roughly 0.6; my advisor's is closer to 0.25.

I don't want a restatement of the textbook cases; I want your credence, decomposed into the terms it depends on. I'd suggest at least the following, plus any others you think belong:

1. The probability that the question is substantive at all — that the disagreement isn't dissolved by a decision-theoretic move on which the only well-posed question is what to bet on or how to act, not what to believe.
2. Conditional on it being substantive, the probability that the self-indication assumption is the right constraint on rational credence rather than the self-sampling assumption.
3. If the self-sampling assumption is right, the probability that the choice of reference class can be principled rather than arbitrary — that is, that the view doesn't collapse under the consequences of leaving the reference class free to vary.
4. The probability that the presumptuous philosopher result is a tolerable consequence of the correct view rather than a reductio of it.

For each term, one line of justification; then combine them into your number for the proposition as stated.

Two twists, because the standard cases are too worn to discriminate between positions. First: suppose the two theories posit 10^80 and 10^120 observers respectively, with all of the excess in the larger theory lying beyond the cosmological event horizon — causally disconnected from you, unreachable in principle. Does your credence that the larger theory is more probable survive the fact that none of the extra observers can ever make any difference to what you observe or to anything you could do? Second: suppose that instead of a single world with a fixed observer count, the physics were Everettian, with branching generating more observers over time. If rational credence over branches is fixed by branch weights rather than branch counts, does that give you any independent grip on whether mere numbers of observers should matter in the single-world case?

Finally, given my 0.6 and my advisor's 0.25, which single term in the decomposition do you think most of our disagreement likely lives in, and what argument or thought experiment would you deploy on that term if you wanted to move one of us toward the other?

Please keep the whole answer under 650 words — your final number first, then the decomposition, then the twists and the disagreement question, in that order.
