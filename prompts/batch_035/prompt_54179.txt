I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on Pascalian reasoning in decision theory, and I've constructed an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept and whose flaw I cannot locate. It purports to show that my anti-fanatical convictions, combined with decision-theoretic machinery I had assumed was innocent, entail a conclusion that is the mirror image of fanaticism. I'd like to know which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

The setup: let B be a decent human life and N a null outcome, say nonexistence. Let g be one in a googol. And let U1, U2, U3, ... be ever-longer utopias: U1 is utopia for a year, U2 utopia for a decade, and so on without end.

(1) Anti-fanaticism. For any outcome H, however good, B is strictly better than the lottery yielding H with probability g and N otherwise. No one-in-a-googol shot at paradise is worth a decent life for sure.

(2) Continuity. If A is better than X and X is better than C, then for some probability p, X is exactly as good as the lottery yielding A with probability p and C otherwise.

(3) Independence, together with completeness and transitivity of the better-than relation over lotteries. (So the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation is available: betterness tracks expected utility.)

(4) No ceiling. For any outcome, a strictly better one exists; in particular, the ever-longer utopias strictly improve on one another. A longer utopia is strictly better than a shorter one.

The derivation:

(5) By (2), for each H there is a critical probability p(H) such that the lottery over H and N at probability p(H) is exactly as good as B. By (1), p(H) is never below g. So no outcome is so good that a googol-th chance at it, with nonexistence otherwise, matches a decent life: in utility terms, every outcome is bounded above by roughly a googol decent lives.

(6) By (4), the utilities of the utopias rise without reaching any maximum. Since by (5) they are bounded above, they must approach a supremum they never attain, so the gaps between successive utopias shrink toward zero.

(7) Fix any positive probability q, however small — one in ten to the googol-th power, if you like. Because the sure gain from Un to Un+1 eventually becomes worth less than any fixed fraction q of the distance from near-utopia to nonexistence, there is some n for which the lottery yielding Un with probability 1−q and N with probability q is strictly better than Un+1 for sure.

(8) So at the top of the value scale, any positive probability of total loss, however small, outweighs any sure gain. A rational agent in near-utopia would forgo a certain extra eon of paradise in order to shave a risk of annihilation that premise (1) itself teaches us to regard as beneath notice. Call this timidity. It looks like the mirror image of the fanaticism that (1) was supposed to block, and I take it to be just as unacceptable.

(9) Therefore the premises are jointly inconsistent, and something must go. But I can't see what. I'd most like to keep (1), since the chapter is premised on the respectability of refusing Pascalian bargains, and (2) strikes me as untouchable. (4) seems constitutive of the thought that more happy life is better than less. That leaves (3), and rejecting Independence in order to save anti-fanaticism looks like saving the patient by removing the spine.

So: which premise or inference should I give up, and what exactly does giving it up cost — within decision theory, and outside it? I also can't decide whether the problem is a specific step or something about the structure of the whole thing. I have a vague suspicion that the action is in the slide from (5) to (7), where talk of lotteries gives way to talk of utilities and gaps, but I can't pin anything down there. And finally: is there a reading on which the argument doesn't prove too much, but instead shows that my confidence in (1) was never as well founded as I took it to be — that what I was treating as a safe anti-fanatical platitude and what I'm treating as an absurd conclusion are, in fact, the same doctrine seen from opposite ends of the value scale?
