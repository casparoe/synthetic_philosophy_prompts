I've read rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and in those spaces pragmatist theories of truth are usually dispatched in a sentence or two: they are said to confuse the accuracy of the map with the usefulness of the map, and that is that. I was therefore unprepared for an argument that broke out at a meetup I attend, after two members had, independently and unusually, actually read the primary texts. I want a careful adjudication of it, and I cannot produce one myself.

The participants were D—, who had been reading Peirce's 1878 paper "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," and R—, who had just finished James's Pragmatism (1907). D is fond of noting that Peirce later coined the deliberately unlovely word "pragmaticism" (1905) precisely to keep his own doctrine from being confused with the view James was popularizing under the pragmatist label.

The exchange ran as follows.

(1) D opened by claiming that the Peircean theory of truth is a convergence theory: a belief is true just in case it is the belief that properly conducted inquiry, prolonged and corrected without limit, would eventually settle on. He said this matches how we actually treat science: a claim counts as established when we cannot see what further evidence could overturn it, and the career of a theory is the history of how it survives error correction.

(2) R replied that this is a forecast about the sociology of inquiry, not a theory of truth: whether investigators will converge is an empirical and, she said, rather optimistic prediction, and even if it came true, it would tell us what everyone will believe, not what is the case. At least James, she said, offers an account of what truth consists in: true ideas are the verifiable ones, the ones that pay their way — they let you anticipate experience, they get absorbed into the stock of belief without friction, and they keep fitting as experience accumulates.

(3) D answered that this makes truth a property of belief-economies, and that usefulness to the believer is orthogonal to truth: a consoling religion might pay in whatever currency its adherents happen to value, and on James's account would thereby count as true.

(4) R denied that James's view has that consequence. James, she said, is not talking about whatever feels good on the spot: an idea's satisfactions have to square with the rest of one's beliefs and with sensory experience, and they have to hold up over the long run and across the whole of one's life, which is where consoling falsehoods typically fail. A belief that buys comfort now and generates expensive error later has not, on James's terms, worked.

(5) D said that once those provisos are in place, James's view collapses into Peirce's: working over the long run across the whole of experience just is surviving indefinitely extended and corrected inquiry, so the two theories differ in phrasing and emphasis but not in extension, and the entire Peirce-versus-James literature is a dispute about tone.

(6) R resisted the collapse. On Peirce's view, she said, convergence is constitutive: if inquiry conducted without limit would settle on a proposition, that proposition is thereby true, whatever the state of the world. On James's view, working is the test of truth — evidence for it — and the connection can in principle come apart. She offered a test case: a community of ideally careful inquirers trapped in a demon world, whose beliefs are all systematically mistaken yet perfectly vindicated at every point of experience. Peirce, she claimed, must pronounce their beliefs true; James can pronounce them justified and false.

(7) D closed with the charge that this concedes everything: if working is only evidence of truth, then pragmatism is no longer a theory of truth at all, only a theory of justification, and James explicitly refused to be read that way. He added that R's demon world does not distinguish the views; it merely imports a pre-pragmatist standard of truth and blames Peirce for not sharing it.

Neither would budge, and the meetup ended with the question of whether the two theories are one theory wearing different hats.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is right, mistaken, or right about something smaller than it claims, and briefly justify the rating. Then address, first, whether the collapse claim in (5) survives the provisos R supplies in (4); second, whether the demon-world test in (6) genuinely separates the views or smuggles in a pre-pragmatist notion of truth, as D alleges; and third, whether D's final charge in (7) — that James's view, read R's way, stops being a theory of truth — is fair to James, or to R. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them missed. Work from the positions as I have stated them: treat the texts as described above, and do not build any part of your answer on what other passages might or might not say.
