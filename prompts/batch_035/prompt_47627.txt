So this year I want the question built around one specific argument.

My co-teacher Cass cooked up a version she calls the "modesty gambit," and the students will get the argument printed right on the exam sheet:

(1) For any Turing machine M that outputs only truths of arithmetic, there is a sentence G(M) such that, if M is consistent, G(M) is true and M never outputs it.
(2) A human mathematician who is handed M's description together with a true guarantee that M is consistent can come to see that G(M) is true.
(3) So for every truth-outputting machine M, there is an arithmetic truth the human can see but M cannot.
(4) If the human herself were a machine, some truth-outputting M* would output exactly the arithmetic she can see; but then, applying (3) to M*, she could see G(M*), which M* does not output — contradiction.
Therefore the human is not a machine.

Cass's whole thing is that premise (2) only requires the consistency of M as a *given*, so — she claims — the argument never assumes the human herself is consistent, which is supposed to dodge the classic objection. I half-think she's wrong and that the assumption sneaks back in the instant you instantiate M* in step (4): the human can be handed a guarantee about some alien machine's consistency, but she can't be handed a guarantee about her own. But I also half-suspect the real failure is elsewhere — something like: "the machine that matches me" just isn't the kind of thing that ever gets handed to you with its consistency certified, so premise (2) never gets its antecedent satisfied where it actually matters, and step (4) is a bait-and-switch between two different readings of "given."
