So my day job is backend/systems engineering, about ten years in, mostly maintenance of other people's code, and lately I've been reading Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the second Untimely Meditation, because someone told me it's basically about burnout. I can follow it fine but I keep thinking "this is just legacy code stuff," and I want to test that properly instead of hand-waving.

Translate the three modes of history he talks about into my vocabulary. Monumental, antiquarian, critical -- give me each one as a thing that actually happens in software teams, using the terms I use at work: git blame and archaeology, tech debt, deprecation RFCs, backwards compatibility, greenfield rewrites, blameless postmortems, "we've always done it this way," hero stories about how some famous company solved it, the cynics who say every idea is a rerun of something from the 70s. Like, I'd guess monumental is the "read the stories of the great engineers" thing that inspires juniors but also lets weak teams say "that was done once by better people than us, we're not them," and antiquarian is the team that won't delete a deprecated codepath because someone somewhere might depend on it, sentimental about the old system. And critical is forcing a migration, judging the legacy code and condemning it, except he has this caveat that you can only condemn it because you grew out of it -- you're its descendant, scrapping it is a bit of an injustice you have to be able to live with, and you'd better do it from a place of strength or you'll just get bitter. I want to know if that caveat survives at all in postmortem culture, because "blameless" seems like it's doing something similar but I suspect not the same thing.

Then also do his two extra claims: that too much history is literally a disease (the team that spends every sprint on archaeology and process writeups and never ships anything -- analysis paralysis, the past swamping the ability to act), and the plastic power thing, the capacity to forget, where someone with a big horizon can absorb a lot of past and someone without it gets crushed by much less. Is there anything in my world for "the right amount of history depends on the organism's capacity to digest it"? Closest I know is "don't let perfect be the enemy of shipped," which doesn't feel like the same claim.

Finally give me the residue report, which is honestly what I care about most. Which of his points translate cleanly, which only fit after I distorted them, and which can't be said at all in engineering vocabulary. My guess about the biggest residue: his standard for everything is "life," the health of the people doing the living, and my vocabulary almost always talks about the artifact -- the codebase, the system, tech debt as a property of the code. Even when we talk about humans it's velocity, burnout as reduced output. If "the point of history is that a person or a team can act" doesn't translate, tell me whether that's because my vocabulary is missing a concept (it has no word for the team as an organism whose mood and courage are what's actually being spent or saved) or whether his claim is less clear than it looks. Don't be charitable to either side by default, I want the honest carving.
