I coordinate a working group at an interdisciplinary institute on long-term social change, and we have just concluded a two-year adversarial collaboration between two scholars who have spent their careers talking past each other. One is a committed cliodynamicist in Peter Turchin's tradition: she takes structural-demographic theory — the interaction of popular immiseration, elite overproduction (more elite aspirants than the society has elite positions to absorb them into), and state fiscal distress — to be a genuinely predictive scientific theory of history, generating secular cycles of roughly two to three centuries with disintegrative and integrative phases, a shorter roughly fifty-year cycle of internal violence, and, crucially, out-of-sample forecasts. The other is a narrative political historian who regards the whole enterprise as curve-fitting on incommensurable societies: he grants that demographic and fiscal stress matter but insists that the models' apparent successes are retrodictions fit to already-known data, that the coding of "instability" and other variables in databases like Seshat is too contestable to support strong inference, and that contingency — the careless match, not the accumulated deadwood — does the real explanatory work. They have now spent enough structured time together to have located their actual points of contact, and my job is to get from them a document they can both sign.

Please draft that joint statement. It should contain: a numbered section of claims both accept — for instance about what structural-demographic theory actually asserts, about the genealogy running from Ibn Khaldun through demographic-structural work on early modern rebellions to the current models, about the historical episodes both agree any theory must accommodate, and about the evidential status of the widely discussed 2010 letter to Nature, which stated that the next decade would likely be a period of growing instability in the United States and western Europe (they disagree about what that prediction's apparent success shows, but not about what was predicted); then a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording as a fair statement of its own view — including, at minimum, whether prediction from already-known data can confirm a theory of history, whether cross-societal comparison is possible without smuggled in commensurability assumptions, and whether the 2010 forecast was a genuine out-of-sample success or something a cautious trend-extrapolator could have produced without any structural theory.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, each paired with a case, test, or analysis both sides would in advance accept as bearing on it — a retrodiction protocol, a comparison of structural forecasts against naive base rates, an audit of coding disputes in the databank, or whatever else you find that both could sign — with each side's prediction about the outcome written down where applicable. Since they suspect part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, end with a short section adjudicating which disagreements are real and which dissolve under better wording, and why — for example, whether "contingency" and "structural constraint" are actually competing explanations or descriptions of events at different timescales.
