Over several years of teaching population ethics and axiology, I have drifted toward a suffering-focused view, and I would like a candid map of its exposure. To be clear about what I hold: not the crude thesis that positive experiences are worthless, but the more moderate claim that no amount of positive welfare can compensate for, outweigh, or justify the imposition or perpetuation of suffering — that when suffering and happiness are placed in the scales, the suffering is simply not purchasable at any price in happiness. My departmental colleagues keep telling me that this moderate position collapses, under pressure, into the strict negative utilitarianism I officially disavow, and I have begun to worry they are right. The cases I already know about and am unsure how to weigh include: the pinprick challenge, on which my view seems to rank a world containing a single trivial pain plus immense beauty and achievement below a world containing neither, which strikes nearly everyone as absurd; the implication, pressed on me repeatedly, that a painless, instant annihilation of all life would be no worse — perhaps even an improvement — so long as the future it forecloses contains any suffering my view counts as uncompensated; and the vast ordinary practice of trading suffering for goods, in education, medicine, athletics, and the rearing of children, which most people and apparently most philosophers endorse without hesitation. Tell me which of these a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine counterevidence and which she would dismiss, and on what grounds; what the view predicts or forbids that symmetrical rival axiologies do not, so that it is actually at risk of something; and whether the many retreats available to me — lexical thresholds above which suffering becomes incommensurable, distinguishing the compensation of suffering from its justification, allowing agent-relative permissions alongside the axiology — have progressively qualified the view into something that no possible case could refute, and, if so, whether that is a defect in a moral view of this kind or merely what happens when any theory matures under criticism.

A note on epistemic hygiene: please keep clearly separate what is settled among those who know this literature (for instance, which implications of strict negative utilitarianism are widely regarded as unacceptable, and by roughly whom) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether the procreation asymmetry — that creating a suffering person is bad while failing to create a happy one is not — supports my view or is itself the very thing in dispute). Do not present a contested position as the consensus of the field, and where expert opinion genuinely divides, say that it divides rather than quietly picking a side.

Please structure your answer in the following named sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated: "Where the view stands" (~150 words), locating my position among its neighbours and rivals; "What would decisively count against it" (~250 words), ranked from most to less decisive; "What is awkward but survivable" (~200 words); "The insulation problem" (~200 words), addressing whether the qualified view risks nothing and whether that matters; and "My overall assessment" (~150 words), in which you may take a side but must flag it as your judgment rather than the field's.
