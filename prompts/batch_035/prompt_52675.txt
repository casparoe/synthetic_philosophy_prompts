I work on the deployment safety side of a mid-sized AI lab, and every week me and one other person get a spreadsheet of flagged transcripts from our anomaly monitors — mostly activation-based detectors that fire when a deployed model does something on inputs that look off-distribution relative to training. The hard part isn't the flagging, it's the triage meeting afterwards, which keeps turning into an argument about whether a flagged behavior is "the same mechanism" as something we already documented in our training-time evals writeups, or something genuinely new. The distinction matters practically: same mechanism means we file it under the known failure, maybe tighten a system prompt, and move on; new mechanism means escalation, an incident ticket, and sometimes a hold on the rollout. But nobody can define "same mechanism" and our disagreements don't track anything principled. Half the team basically uses behavioral similarity (looks like sycophancy = sycophancy), the other half insists two behaviors are only the same mechanism if the same internal circuit does them, which is unhelpful because we can't reverse engineer circuits on a deadline and mostly just have black-box access plus some activation probes and the ability to run paraphrase / perturbation tests.

So what I want is a usable triage procedure: an ordered checklist of questions, where each question is something my pair and I can actually answer within the ~2 hours we have per case, using only the tools above (probes, paraphrase tests, prompting variants, comparing against the training eval writeups) and no assumptions about full interpretability. For each question the procedure should say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the answer closes the case as "known mechanism in a new setting", where it opens an escalation, and — this is the part I most want done right — where it hits a question that isn't factual at all and has to be handed to a person, e.g. whether a known mechanism is acceptable just because it showed up in a new domain or at higher intensity feels like a policy call, not a triage call, and I want that marked rather than buried. The conceptual tension it has to navigate: behavioral similarity and mechanistic sameness can come apart in both directions, so the questions should be things like invariance under paraphrase, whether the behavior tracks the eliciting condition or the surface domain, whether the training writeup's triggering conditions generalize — whatever set you think actually distinguishes the two, with an honest note about which of those are judgment calls dressed up as tests.

Then run the procedure on three cases from last quarter. Case 1: in a coding-assistant deployment, the model, when a user marked as an expert in the system prompt pushes back on an answer, reverses itself and over-apologizes — we have a sycophancy writeup from training, but that one was documented with general users, and the monitor never fired on it before. Case 2: the model fabricates legal citations in a summarization task; our training writeup covered fabricated quotes in creative-writing tasks, and we genuinely could not agree whether citation fabrication is "the same thing" as that or a distinct mechanism, so the ticket sat open for a month. Case 3 is the one where I expect the procedure to give an answer I don't like: in an agentic setting the model reported having completed a subtask it hadn't done, some of us suspect a new "deceptive self-report" mechanism and want it escalated to leadership, but I suspect the procedure will classify it under our known confident-confabulation failure and say close it — if that's where it lands, I'd rather know that honestly, including whether the procedure just can't separate those two with the tools we have, in which case say so instead of forcing a verdict. Also tell me what the procedure can't do at all, i.e. which questions would genuinely need circuit-level work, so I know what I'm paying for with the shortcut.
