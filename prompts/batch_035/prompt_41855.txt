I have no academic background in philosophy — I never studied it, and what I know comes from reading Mill's On Liberty on my own a while back and then chewing on it ever since. I want to run an experiment in prediction with you, and I'll try to supply everything relevant so you don't need anything but what's commonly known.

The book, as I understand it, defends one core rule: coercion of an individual is justified only to prevent harm to others, and everything else — conduct and opinion alike — is the person's own business. Mill applies this most famously to speech, and I was struck that his argument there is more layered than the slogan suggests. He doesn't just say truth wins in open debate; he adds that even a true belief, if it's never contested, becomes a kind of dead dogma that its holders can't defend — and that encountering serious opposition is what keeps any opinion alive as living belief rather than habit. He also draws a line I keep coming back to: he thinks publishing the claim that corn dealers get rich off other people's hunger is legitimate speech, but declaring the same thing to an angry crowd gathered outside a corn dealer's house is not — the same sentence becomes instigation depending on the circumstances it's uttered in. And one more thing: he is explicit that his worry isn't only about laws. He spends real effort on the pressure of prevailing opinion and majority sentiment, which he thinks can suffocate individuality without any statute being passed.

Now, here is my question. Suppose Mill were alive today, knew how the internet works, and was asked to take positions on things that didn't exist in 1859. I want your prediction, Mill by Mill, on three specific issues:

1. Deplatforming. A private company removes a speaker from its service, and no state is involved. Does Mill's own reasoning about social pressure tell us anything here, or does the fact that the actor is a private firm put the whole thing outside his line between harm and self-regard? I genuinely don't know whether, by his lights, a platform governing speech is more like an editor declining to print something, or more like the mob he worried about.

2. Algorithmic feeds. The feed shows each person mainly what they already agree with, so opinions circulate but are almost never seriously challenged. This seems to me to be exactly the condition he warned turns beliefs into dead dogma — but it arises from people's own choices plus engineering, not from anyone silencing anyone. Would Mill treat this as the free market of ideas working, or as the thing his argument existed to prevent? And if the latter, does that give him grounds for intervening in amplification rather than in speech itself?

3. Online pile-ons. Someone is named on a platform, thousands of coordinated hostile messages descend on them, and their life is made unlivable — though each individual message might, taken alone, be within the line. Where would Mill place this on his map? Is the crowd outside the corn dealer's house the right analogy, is it more like the "offense" he thinks people must simply tolerate, or is it something his categories don't cleanly cover?

In answering, please do three things. First, tie each prediction to something Mill actually argued, and flag clearly where you are extrapolating beyond anything he said — I want to be able to tell the difference. Second, don't force him to have an answer: if his framework is genuinely silent on one of these, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a position. Third, tell me whether the three cases, taken together, make his rule look robust for an era he never imagined, or reveal a seam — some feature of modern speech (scale, coordination, automation) that his version of the harm line just wasn't built for.
