After twenty years of writing novels, I've come around to Tolstoy's late theory of art: a genuine work is the transmission of feeling from artist to audience — infection, as he puts it — and its quality varies with the individuality, clarity, and above all the sincerity of the feeling transmitted; whatever fails to infect, or transmits only the feelings of a small leisured class, is counterfeit. I even accept his harder applications. He pronounced his own earlier novels bad art by these standards, and I am prepared to find some of mine counterfeit. What I want to know is what would count against the theory. My worry is that its key term is epistemically unreachable: sincerity leaves no trace in the finished text, so in practice I infer it from whether the work moves me — which makes the test look circular, since whatever infects me gets called sincere and whatever bores me gets called counterfeit. There is also the propagandist problem: a demagogue who genuinely feels contempt can produce a work that transmits that feeling with full clarity and individuality, so Tolstoy has to bolt on an independent test of subject matter, and I cannot tell whether that test is load-bearing or merely there to save the theory from counterexamples. And there is my own trade: the sincerity he prizes is exactly what a professional novelist learns to simulate — every late draft I write is engineered to feel unengineered — so the criterion seems unable to distinguish art from skilled manipulation. Tell me what would genuinely count against the infection theory: which actual works, which facts about how art gets made, which consequences a reasonable person could refuse to accept. Which of these would a committed Tolstoyan concede as counting against him, and which would he explain away? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the circularity I have noticed is a real vulnerability or just what the theory was always going to look like from inside.
