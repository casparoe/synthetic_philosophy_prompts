I'm a software engineer, and my instinct with any "random quantity" is to ask how I would code the sampler — what I'd actually type to produce one draw. Bertrand's paradox, the random-chord problem, has made me realize my instincts about when such a question is well-posed are not consistent, and I'd like help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. The setup: take a circle, inscribe an equilateral triangle, and ask for the probability that a random chord of the circle is longer than the triangle's side. My verdicts on a spread of cases:

1. Procedure: sample two independent points uniformly along the circumference and join them. Answer 1/3. Perfectly well-posed; I've simulated it and it converges.
2. Procedure: sample a point uniformly along a radius, draw the chord perpendicular to that radius through the point. Answer 1/2. Also well-posed, also easy to code.
3. Procedure: sample the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk's area. Answer 1/4. Same: well-posed as stated.
4. The bare question — "a chord is drawn at random, no procedure given" — I'm fairly confident is ill-posed, and the three answers above are the proof.
5. But now: "a point is chosen at random in the disk," no procedure given. I would have sworn this is perfectly well-posed — uniform over area, obviously; it's how everyone Monte-Carlo-estimates π. Yet the rotation invariance that fails to pin down a measure over chords also leaves a whole family of candidates here (any radial density is rotation-invariant), so by the reasoning behind verdict 4 it should be ill-posed too. I don't want to give this verdict up, but I can't say why.
6. Buffon's needle: "a needle is dropped at random on a floor ruled with parallel lines." I've never doubted this was well-posed, and the standard answer (for a needle shorter than the line spacing, 2l/πd) feels forced on me in a way the chord answers don't. But it too assumes a uniform position and a uniform angle without naming a procedure, so by verdict 4's logic it should be ill-posed.
7. "A positive integer is chosen at random." No uniform distribution exists over the integers, and I'm confident the question is ill-posed. This feels like it belongs in the same bucket as the chord, but I can't articulate the shared feature, since there the problem is that too many measures exist and here it's that none does.
8. A practical one: at work we model defects on a circular wafer as random chords and estimate what fraction of dies a crack will cross. We pick whichever of the three measures best matches observed crack data. This feels like the obviously right engineering move, but it seems to concede that even when the world generates real frequencies, "the" probability isn't fixed by the problem — it's fixed by the physical process, which we can only learn empirically.

My tentative principle is this: a question about a "random X" is well-posed if and only if either (a) the sampling procedure is fully specified, or (b) there is exactly one probability measure invariant under the natural symmetry group of the problem. Clause (a) handles verdicts 1–3 and 4 cleanly. But clause (b) appears to fail for the disk point and Buffon cases — in each, many symmetric measures exist, so (b) declares them ill-posed, contradicting verdicts 5 and 6. And I'm honestly unsure whether clause (b) saves "a random point on the circumference" (arc-length measure feels forced on me, but again many rotation-invariant measures exist).

Please repair or replace my principle, state the result precisely, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why those are the ones to drop. I'd also like its predictions on cases I haven't listed: a random triangle formed by three points sampled independently and uniformly on the circumference (what's the probability it contains the center, and is that question well-posed under the repaired principle?); a random point on the surface of a sphere; and the wafer case — whether the principle says anything about what probability statements mean when the generating process exists but is unknown to us.
