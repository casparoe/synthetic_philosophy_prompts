A friend of mine took exactly one philosophy of religion course years ago and has been wielding the Euthyphro dilemma against everything ever since. In its original home, as I understand it, Socrates asks Euthyphro whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved by the gods; the modern monotheistic rendering asks whether actions are right because God commands them, or God commands them because they are right. Fine in that setting, perhaps. But last week he used it to settle an argument about recommender systems: either the algorithm promotes a video because the video is good, or the video is good because the algorithm promotes it, and since both horns are supposed to be unacceptable, the idea that promoted content is good simply collapses. I suspect this move doesn't work, but I can't say exactly why — partly because I'm no longer sure what precise thesis the two-horn question is expressing even in its original context.

Please disassemble the slogan for me. Lay out the distinct precise claims the dilemma could be making — logical, metaphysical, semantic, epistemic — and classify each as trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or confused. Two things I want addressed explicitly along the way:

(1) The dilemma's bite in the original case is often said to depend on features of God that a recommender system lacks: omniscience, perfect goodness, and legitimate moral authority in the first place. If that is right, the dilemma is not a universal scalpel but a targeted argument. Which of your readings survive being aimed at a recommender system, a court, or a peer-review process, and which quietly presuppose that the target is divine?

(2) The standard escape in the philosophy of religion literature grounds rightness not in God's commands but in God's necessarily good nature. Does that escape generalize? For each of my friend's targets, is there an analogous constitutive move — say, that being surfaced by the system partly constitutes being good content — and is that a genuine escape or just one of the horns wearing a disguise?

Present the disambiguation as a table with the following columns:

- reading: a one-sentence statement of the precise thesis;
- kind: one of platitudinous / substantive and plausible / substantive and implausible / confused or question-begging, with a brief justification;
- needs a divine target: yes or no, with one sentence explaining why;
- verdict on my friend: whether this reading, if correct, actually undermines his recommender-system argument.

Below the table, in at most three paragraphs, tell me which reading my friend most likely has in mind when he deploys the slogan against algorithms, and whether his argument stands or falls under that reading.
