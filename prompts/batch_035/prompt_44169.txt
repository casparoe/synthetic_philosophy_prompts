My ethics course spent two weeks on the Singer stuff — the drowning child in the pond, the claim that distance and how many other able bystanders there are don't make a moral difference, and how he has a hard version of the preventing-bad principle (don't let bad stuff happen unless doing so would cost you something of comparable moral importance) and then a watered down version in the later book, the one that only asks you not to give up anything morally significant, supposedly to dodge the too-demandingness objections. I feel like I actually understand that debate now, or thought I did until Thanksgiving. My uncle listened to my summary of the pond argument, nodded along, and then shut the whole thing down with "charity begins at home," and when I asked what exactly that means he kind of waved at "you know, your own people first," and I realized I would have had nothing better to say.

It's a proverb old enough that it surely isn't making one single claim, and different people probably mean wildly different things by it. Please put on your professional ethicist hat and think carefully before answering — maybe brainstorm the possible readings first — then lay out a menu of the precise theses "charity begins at home" could stand for. I'd want things like: proximity itself making a moral difference (the pond is special because the child is in front of you), special obligations to family/nation over strangers, a mere permission to favor your own rather than a duty, a psychological prediction about what motivation people can actually sustain, a prudial/effectiveness claim about where your money does the most good, or even just a conservative "don't let do-gooding wreck your life" warning. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivially true, defensible but contested, or just false, and — this is the part I actually need — whether that reading would do anything against the strong version of Singer's principle, or only against the weak one, or neither.

I'd like the menu ordered from weakest to strongest as an objection to Singer, since I suspect most of the ways people use the line against him don't even engage with the argument. And please flag explicitly if any reading is so obviously true that no one would bother asserting it, or so strong that nobody sane believes it. I want to bring this to the next family dinner so I'd rather it be honest than on my side.
