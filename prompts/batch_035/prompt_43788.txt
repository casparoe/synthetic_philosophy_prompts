Each spring I teach a unit, in an otherwise ordinary philosophy-of-science survey, on the logic of heritability estimates, and each spring I find myself issuing judgments about which inferences from those estimates are legitimate and which are sophistries. This year, rather than presenting students with another catalogue of misuses, I would like to hand them a principle. So I have written down my verdicts on a range of claims, and I want to know whether some one principle — or a small, explicit set of principles — captures them all, and what it commits me to. Here are the verdicts:

1. Height is roughly 80% heritable in contemporary affluent populations; therefore 80% of any particular person's height is attributable to their genes. — I judge this false: heritability is a claim about the sources of variance in a population, not a decomposition of an individual, and there is no coherent sense in which 80% of my centimeters came from my genome.

2. Height is roughly 80% heritable in the Netherlands; therefore no environmental or public-health intervention could appreciably raise the average height of the Dutch. — Also false, and dramatically so given that average Dutch height rose by leaps over the past century and a half with a genome that changed not at all.

3. Suppose we discover a population in which height is 0% heritable — everyone equally stunted by the same deprivation, say, so that all the variance has vanished. Some colleagues tell me this would show that genes are irrelevant to height in that population, and that a program relieving the deprivation would not raise anyone. — I judge both conjuncts false, indeed incoherent: a trait can be heritable in one environment and not another, and uniform environmental suppression tells us nothing about genetic irrelevance. Though I admit I am somewhat unsure what the zero estimate is even measuring once variance is destroyed.

4. A trait is 50% heritable within population A and 50% heritable within population B; therefore the mean difference between the groups is, in some proportion, genetic in origin. — I hold this to be flatly invalid: within-group heritability constrains nothing about the causes of between-group differences.

5. And yet, I confess: if a trait turns out to be substantially heritable in every population in which it has been measured, across wildly different environments, I find myself treating that as weak, defeasible evidence that a newly observed mean difference between two further populations has some genetic component. My students will notice this, because it appears to contradict verdict 4, and I do not currently have an answer for them.

6. I tell students that heritability is so thoroughly relative to population and environment that a bare figure — the heritability of intelligence, of religiosity, of criminality — is close to meaningless. Yet in the same lecture I will say, without hesitation, that because schizophrenia is substantially heritable, funding the search for susceptibility genes is a rational use of resources — apparently treating the estimate as evidence about something that generalizes beyond its originating sample.

7. A working dog breeder uses heritability estimates of a temperament trait to predict how fast the trait will respond to selection, and adjusts her breeding program accordingly. — I judge this an entirely proper use of heritability, apparently the one context in which the number does exactly what people naively take it to do.

My question, then: is there a single principled account of what heritability measures and what may be inferred from it that delivers all seven verdicts? I suspect the key is something about the range of environments over which the estimate holds being held fixed in some uses and not others — the breeder's inference works because the environment she extrapolates across is the one the estimate was computed in, and my embarrassment in verdict 5 is that I cannot say why the cross-population generalization in 5 differs in kind from the cross-population generalization I condemn in 4 — but every formulation I attempt either lets the inference in 4 slip through or forbids the one in 7. Please state the principle or principles precisely enough to test, show which verdict each clause earns, tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed (for instance: whether a heritability estimate from twin data transfers to an IVF-based design, or whether it licenses predictions about a population whose environment is deliberately being changed by an intervention targeting the genetically most vulnerable), and be blunt about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot save — particularly whether 5 or 6 has to go, since I have come to suspect I cannot keep both.
