I'm three years into a novel about the June of 1914, and my historian friend has planted an argument in my notes that I cannot dig out. I keep drafting scenes in which my protagonist — a young man with a pistol and a grievance — makes a choice, and I keep hearing my friend's voice telling me the choice is set dressing. His case, as he laid it over dinner: (1) An act is a cause of an outcome only if, had the act not occurred, the outcome would not have occurred either. (2) Take the war: even if the archduke's motorcade had turned down the wrong street and gone unmolested, the alliance commitments, the naval rivalry, and the general staffs' mobilization timetables were each, on their own, sufficient to bring about a general European war within a few years. (3) And what holds of the war holds of every major historical outcome: whatever one individual does, there are backups standing behind them that would have produced an outcome of the same kind. (4) So no individual act is ever a cause of any major historical outcome. (5) If an act did not cause the outcome, then the person who performed it bears no responsibility for it. (6) Therefore no individual is responsible for any major historical outcome — not the assassin, not the ministers who signed the mobilization orders, no one. Then he turned to me and added the sting: (7) if this holds even of history, it holds doubly of fiction — if structure suffices to produce the outcome in the real world, then in a novel the plot runs on rails and my protagonist's deliberations are decoration. I can live without a good deal, but I cannot live with (6), and I would rather surrender almost anything than (5). My suspicion keeps settling somewhere between (3) and (4) — something about how loosely "an outcome of the same kind" is doing its work — but I can't name it, and I notice I may just be protecting my book. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked unassailable when my friend said it, and what I pay to give it up, weighed against the cheapest alternative. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: the step you reject; why it seemed fine; what rejecting it costs; the least painful alternative rejection and its price; and, finally, what this means for whether a protagonist's choice can carry a plot at all. Please answer in American English.
