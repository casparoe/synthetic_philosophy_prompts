The idea in brief: ever since Chalmers's paper on verbal disputes, the standard tool is some version of the elimination test — if both sides can say everything they actually care about while dropping the contested term, the fight over the term is merely verbal. I want to argue this test has a blind spot. Some disputes are verbal even though neither side can paraphrase away the contested vocabulary, because what's really going on is a disagreement about which inferential role a term should play, not about what the world contains. Call these role-verbal disputes (not wedded to the name). My lead example: two people arguing about whether there are tables. The mereological nihilist and the common-sense ontologist both use table-talk in a way they can't eliminate without losing something they insist on — the nihilist because their claims get cashed out in terms of simples arranged table-wise, the realist because their practical reasoning is saturated with commitments to tables. Yet if you look at what each of them would predict, bet on, or do, there's nothing to choose between them: the dispute is verbal in every sense that matters, and the elimination test just fails to detect it.

Section sketch:

1. Setup: the standard tests — Chalmers's elimination and collapse tests, Hirsch's charitability — and why they work fine for the disputes they were built for.
2. The blind spot: cases where the contested vocabulary is unrevisable for both parties. Numbers vs. tables as the contrasting pair — I claim the numbers dispute is NOT role-verbal in my sense, because the two sides genuinely differ in explanatory commitments, whereas the tables dispute is.
3. The payoff: a practical equivalence criterion. Two disputants are in a role-verbal dispute iff they agree on all first-order matters and differ only over which linguistic bookkeeping they prefer. I try to make this precise with a Ramsey-sentence style move — strip the theoretical terms, compare what's left.
4. Objections I plan to handle: that the criterion just restates Sider's distinction between quantifier disputes and existence disputes; that it collapses into Thomasson-style easy ontology (if it's all bookkeeping, the realist wins by default); and that prediction and explanation can't be cleanly separated in the numbers case, which would wreck my contrast.
