I'm taking a seminar on social norms and convention, and I've become puzzled by the entire genre of travel-etiquette advice — guidebooks, State Department briefings for exchange students, the cross-cultural training modules that corporations put expatriates through before a posting. All of it issues the same instruction in the imperative mood: tip close to 20% in the United States, do not tip at all in Japan, stand in the queue in Britain, do not cross against the light in Germany even on an empty street at dawn. The advice is presented not as prudence ("you'll get better service") but as something closer to duty, and the visitor who ignores it is treated as having done something wrong, not merely something awkward. My question for the paper I have to write: what theory of the moral status of conventions does this practice presuppose? Does the genre commit itself to conventions being morally binding in themselves, or only to something narrower — say, that violating a local convention wrongs the locals by signalling contempt for their way of doing things? I notice the advice contains an odd asymmetry: newcomers seem to be excused in proportion to how recently they arrived, which fits a "conventions are like local laws" picture, yet the same advisors would never tell the visitor to adopt the locals' moral or religious convictions — only their conventions. So there must be some presupposed line between the two. Could you reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me which parts of the practice strictly require it and which are compatible with several rival views (for instance, a Lewis-style account of conventions as coordination solutions versus a respect-based account), and say what the advice would have to look like if we instead assumed conventions bind no one morally and only self-interest recommends following them?
