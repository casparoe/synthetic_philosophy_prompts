I'm teaching a bioethics elective this term that pairs a film with a concept each week, and for the genetics unit I've picked Gattaca, which the students mostly know and love. My plan is to use it against the concept of heritability — specifically what heritability does and doesn't mean, since that distinction is where my students reliably get lost. In the film's world, Vincent is told at birth that he has something like a 99% chance of a fatal heart condition and a life expectancy of about thirty years, and the whole society treats genotype as destiny. His arc — beating his engineered brother in the swimming contests, making it to Saturn's moon despite his predicted heart — reads as a refutation of genetic determinism. But I keep noticing that the film, and basically everyone who talks about it, slides between two different claims: that individual lives can't be predicted from genes, and that heritability estimates tell you nothing. Those aren't the same, and I want a reading that keeps them apart.

Here's the crux I want the reading to handle honestly. Heritability is a population-level, within-group statistic — it's about the proportion of variance in a trait attributable to genetic differences in a particular environment, and it says nothing about any one person, nothing about differences between groups in different environments, and nothing about malleability (a trait can be highly heritable and still respond enormously to environmental change). So when Vincent beats the odds, he's an n-of-1 anecdote, and heritability estimates never denied that such people exist; a high heritability of heart-disease risk is entirely compatible with Vincent outliving his prediction. Meanwhile the film arguably cheats in the other direction too: Jerome, the "valid" with the perfect genome, is depressed and suicidal, which the film presents as evidence that genes don't guarantee flourishing — but a sophisticated hereditarian never claimed they did. So the question I want to put to my students, and to you: is Gattaca refuting genetic determinism (a claim almost nobody in the actual behavioral genetics literature actually makes), or is it dramatizing something subtler about actuarial discrimination — the fact that insurers and employers act on population statistics even though those statistics are silent about individuals? Is the villain of the film really heritability, or is it the practice of collapsing a population-level probability into an individual verdict — which is a philosophical mistake about statistics, not a discovery about genes?

I'd like a proper critical reading, not just a mapping of concept onto plot: which scenes to actually point my students to (the birth-scene probability readout, the swimming flashbacks, Jerome's medals-as-luggage, the doctor Lamar moment at the end), where the film genuinely illuminates the heritability literature and where it distorts it, and where the honest critic has to admit the reading strains — e.g., whether the film even needs the concept of heritability at all, since in-world they seem to be doing direct genotyping rather than twin studies, and maybe the heritability framing is my imposition rather than the film's concern. Also, since I'll be grading essays on this, tell me where your own reading is most likely to fail or where a reasonable person would push back — what would make you abandon the claim that the film is (or isn't) trading on the individual/population confusion — so I can anticipate the strongest student objections rather than just the easy ones. No need for citations or a reading list; the students have the film and my lecture notes, that's it.
