Construct a decision problem, suitable for a graduate seminar on rational choice under ambiguity, that drives a wedge between two admissibility rules for imprecise probabilities: maximality, which admits an act unless some alternative beats it in expectation under every probability in the agent's credal set, and E-admissibility, which admits an act only if some single probability in that set makes it an expected-utility maximizer. It is a standard result that every E-admissible act is maximal but not conversely; your case should make that gap bite in action, not merely in the roster of admissible acts.

Setting and constraints. The scenario must be realistic — a hospital committee weighing treatment protocols, an emergency manager planning for a storm season whose severity experts dispute, an operations manager choosing among suppliers, anything an ordinary professional could actually face — and it must not be an Ellsberg-style urn, a casino, or anything involving demons or idealized predictors. The credal set should arise from ordinary expert or model disagreement and be small enough to check by hand (two or three probabilities suffice). You must state the payoffs explicitly, in concrete units, so that every verdict in your walkthrough can be recomputed from your own numbers. The scenario itself should be one paragraph; the analysis follows it.

The specification. Your case must contain at least three acts, one of which — call it the pivotal act — is maximal without being E-admissible: no single member of the credal set ranks it first, yet no available alternative beats it under every member at once. The divergence must then matter for what is actually done. Model each camp as following its full two-level procedure: the E-admissibility agent filters first by E-admissibility and then breaks ties among the survivors by worst-case reasoning, randomizing among any survivors that tie; the maximality agent filters by maximality and then applies the same worst-case reasoning to whatever survives. Construct your numbers so that the two agents end up doing different things — specifically, so that the worst-case reasoning lands the maximality agent on the pivotal act, while the E-admissibility agent is led to one of the extreme acts or to a randomization between them. Finally, say whether randomizing between the two extreme acts would let the E-admissibility agent capture the benefits of the pivotal act, and be honest about what the answer does to the interest of the case.

After the scenario, provide the demonstration. Walk through each rule on your numbers: list the pairwise dominance checks that establish that nothing beats the pivotal act under every prior at once, and show that no single prior in the set ranks it first. Then state what each agent, following the two-level procedure above, actually does, and show the worst-case computation that gets each of them there.

Then the analysis. Identify the structural feature of the case that does the work — I would expect it to be something like the pivotal act's expectation profile sitting in the interior of the convex hull of the others, so that it is never an extreme point, but say what it is in your construction. Say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed, if the credal set is thinned to a single precise prior, or if the set is convexified by closing it under mixtures of its members; report whether convexification changes any verdict, and explain why it does or does not. Then give the exchange of objections: the best argument from the E-admissibility side that an act which no coherent probability ranks first ought not to be chosen even if nothing dominates it — that rational choice requires vindication from some single, fully opinionated standpoint — and the best reply from the maximality side, which should turn on the observation that choosing the pivotal act never exposes the agent to an expectation-based regret that some available alternative would have avoided.

A final note on what counts as cheating. The verdicts are not to be stipulated; they are to be derived from the numbers you provide. The case counts as a success only if a reader who recomputes your dominance checks and your expected-utility rankings under each member of the credal set arrives at exactly the verdicts you claim.
