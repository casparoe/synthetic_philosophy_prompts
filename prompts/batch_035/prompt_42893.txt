For my school's extended essay program I chose official state apologies for historical wrongs — the 1988 United States apology to Japanese American internees, the 2008 Canadian parliamentary apology for residential schools, university statements about founders' ties to slavery — and I have run into the same wall in every article and every discussion: the argument stops moving at exactly the same point, and I would like help figuring out what kind of standoff it actually is.

One side holds that a state is a continuing agent: the entity that governs Canada today is in a meaningful sense the same political community that administered the schools, so it can genuinely apologize, and the apology does real work — it reclassifies the event from a regrettable chapter of policy into an injustice, which changes what claims and expectations follow from it. The other side holds that apology is inherently second-personal: only a wrongdoer can apologize to a victim, both parties are dead, and so the ceremony rests on a fiction — the state can express regret or take a political position, but it cannot apologize in the strict sense. Then a third group tries to rescue the practice by saying the apology is addressed to the victims' descendants, which immediately raises the question of whether anyone can accept an apology on behalf of a wrong done to someone else. What strikes me is that nobody disagrees about the facts, and yet the moves simply bounce off each other: when the first side explains how corporate agency persists, the second side says persistence is not the issue, standing is; when the second side presses the standing objection, the first side says it mistakes a ritual's function for its truth conditions.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not really about apology at all — it is a hidden disagreement in social ontology about whether political communities are the kind of continuants that can inherit obligations, with the two sides also possibly answering different questions under one name (one asking what makes the words in an official apology true, the other asking what the ritual accomplishes for the living). But I am not certain, and I do not want a survey of positions. I want a diagnosis: is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or two questions conflated; is it ultimately about facts, about what counts as a moral reason, or about bedrock intuitions — for instance, about whether institutions are real agents or only fictions we maintain — that neither side can argue for without begging the question? And given whichever diagnosis you defend, what would count as progress here: a sharper distinction between apologizing for and apologizing that, a criterion for when a collective's identity over time matters, a reframing of what the apology is for, or nothing at all, because the debate should simply be dissolved or accepted as permanently open?
