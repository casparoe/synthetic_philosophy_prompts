I teach a fortnightly evening seminar at a continuing-education center: twelve adults, no philosophical training but strong opinions and long memories of reading the news. In two sessions' time I lead a discussion on Popper's case that there can be no laws of history of the kind that would license long-range social prophecy. What I want from you is not the content but the plan: the sessions are ninety minutes each, and the risk is that the whole thing collapses into a genial exchange of views about whether history repeats itself.

Please address four things in order.

First, the dependency structure. Several ideas are in play: what makes a prediction scientific rather than prophetic; the distinction Popper insists on between laws and mere trends; the Oedipus effect, by which a prediction of a social event can alter the event it predicts; and the contrast between unconditional long-range forecasts and conditional, piecemeal ones. I am genuinely unsure what must be established before what. Does his argument actually rest on the falsifiability criterion, or could I bracket demarcation entirely and run the case purely on prediction-induced feedback? Determine the order in which the pieces must be in place for the conclusion to feel earned rather than asserted, flag any circularity risk in that ordering, and tell me which piece you would sacrifice if time runs short and what the argument loses without it.

Second, the carrying example. I want one concrete case to build both sessions around, returning to it repeatedly rather than parading illustrations. It must exhibit the feedback loop between forecast and system, be intelligible to people who read newspapers, and not be so politically charged — no predicted revolutions — that discussion curdles into topical argument. Propose the case, state what it must have and what it must lack, and explain why the seemingly obvious candidates (astronomical prediction, the weather) are worse than they look for this purpose.

Third, the misconception map. My audience will arrive nodding at the thought that history doesn't repeat but rhymes, and I anticipate at least these errors: that Popper showed history to be unpredictable, full stop, so that all forecasting from the past is fraudulent; that the whole argument is a Cold War-era polemic against Marx and can be dismissed as such; that the trends-versus-laws distinction is a verbal dodge; and that if there are no laws of history, long-horizon planning — pension systems, climate policy — is somehow refuted. For each misconception you judge likely, identify the point in the sequence at which it should be preempted rather than corrected after it takes root, and say which one is better left to be made and repaired in open discussion, on the grounds that the repair teaches more than the preempting would.

Finally, the check and the refusals. Give me one question I can put to the room at the end of the second session that would reveal whether they have grasped the structure of the argument rather than a slogan about it — and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level, saying so plainly to the group, and why. I suspect the reception of Popper among historians and the question of whether Marxist theory really was his intended target belong on that list, but I would like your judgment rather than my hunches.
