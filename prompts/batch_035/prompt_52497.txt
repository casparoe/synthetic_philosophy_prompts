I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days and lurked LessWrong through the whole Sequences era, and I've noticed a reflex I want to interrogate rather than keep indulging: the trained-in response that deontological side constraints are a bias to be discarded — an artifact of scope insensitivity or the absurdity heuristic, from agents who never internalized that five deaths are worse than one. The usual statement of the paradox of deontology runs something like this: if killing one innocent is worse than letting five innocents die at others' hands, then refusing to kill the one seems to trade one thing you consider bad for five of the very same bad thing, which looks flatly irrational; so deontologists must either deny the arithmetic or give up the constraint. I want you to argue for a specific resolution of this paradox, one that a rationalist should find familiar from decision theory rather than from moral intuitionism.

Please argue the following position: the paradox is generated by a causal-decision-theoretic framing of the individual act — of the agent facing a unique, one-off, causally isolated choice — and it dissolves once the choice is modeled the way an updateless agent models it: as the selection of a policy, by an agent who knows she is one of many correlated reasoners running structurally similar decision procedures on structurally similar choices. On this view, the deontologist's refusal to kill the one is not a stubborn refusal to save five; it is the local execution of a policy whose value comes from what it does across the whole population of agents facing transplant-surgeon-type cases, in the same way one-boxing in Newcomb's problem is valuable because of what the policy does, not what the particular act causes. Develop the analogy explicitly: the deontologist stands to the consequentialist accuser roughly as the one-boxer stands to the two-boxer who protests that the boxes are already sealed, or as the agent who cooperates in a prisoner's dilemma against a near-copy of herself stands to the defector who correctly notes that her counterpart's move is causally independent of hers. The claim to press is that "my act's causal consequences, holding all else fixed" is the wrong criterion to evaluate constraint-following under, and that the deontologist was implicitly doing policy evaluation all along.

Two things I want you to handle carefully. First, engage the strongest consequentialist rejoinder: that the correlation between my constraint-following and other agents' constraint-following is far too weak and speculative to carry the argument — other people are not near-copies of me, my refusal to kill one does not functionally determine anyone else's choices, and updatefulness on the actual state of the world (in which the five die unless I act) seems exactly what a rational agent should do here. Say something about what the analogy genuinely requires, not just what it gestures at. Second, keep this a deontological argument and not a covert consequentialist rescue: the policy is meant to be non-negotiable even when you know with certainty that no one else's behavior depends on yours, and you should say how the defender of constraints can live with the least convenient possible world in which the correlation is stipulated away entirely — whether by conceding that the decision-theoretic analogy is only part of the story, or by finding something in the analogy that survives.

Write it as a continuous argument, at most 700 words, with no section headings and no bullet points. Keep it rigorous enough that I could forward it to the decision-theory people without embarrassment.
