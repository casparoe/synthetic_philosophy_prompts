I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and a colleague with a logic background keeps pressing on me an argument — his distillation of Tarski, he says — whose conclusion seems to prove far too much. It runs: (1) The equivalence between a sentence s and the claim that s is true is constitutive of what "true" means, so it holds for every English sentence without exception. (2) "True" in English applies to English sentences, so English carries its own truth predicate. (3) English can build self-referential sentences: "This sentence is not true" is a grammatical sentence of English about itself. (4) Applying (1) to that sentence yields a biconditional — it is true exactly when it is not true — from which classical logic derives that it is true and that it is not true, and then, by explosion, every sentence whatsoever. (5) So English, taken together with classical logic, is trivial: everything is assertible in it. (6) That is absurd; speakers of English assert some things, not everything. (7) Therefore one of (1) through (3) fails for English, or classical logic fails somewhere within English, or English is an inconsistent language whose triviality is staved off only by our habits of assertion.

He adds that this is Tarski's own moral from the 1930s — that a consistent language cannot contain its own truth predicate, and that truth for any language must be defined in a stronger metalanguage — and that English, being nobody's object language, has no metalanguage to retreat to. I confess the conclusion looks to me like it proves too much: it would make every ordinary speaker the user of a contradictory language, or force a hierarchy on which a newspaper could not contain the sentence that every sentence in it is true. I would most like to keep (1) and (6). Tell me which step to give up, explain why that step looked innocuous, and price the standard escape routes — the Tarskian hierarchy of object- and metalanguages, Kripke's grounded truth with its paracomplete gap, Priest's dialetheism with a paraconsistent logic, the revision theory of Gupta and Belnap — saying for each what it must deny that we ordinarily accept, and whether it merely relocates the paradox in strengthened form. Be candid, too, about where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail: which argument or consideration would move you to reject a different step instead.
