A podcast I listen to (pop-econ, usually pretty good) did an episode on common knowledge, and the host summarized it roughly like this: 'Common knowledge just means everyone knows it. The "and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on" part is a technicality that logicians bolted on -- in practice, once information is public, the infinite nesting comes along for free. The famous payoff of the idea is the no-trade theorem: if rational traders share a prior and have common knowledge of rationality, they can never agree to trade at all, because any offer to trade is itself information, and it's always bad news about what the other side knows. And Rubinstein's email game shows this is robust: even if you only have *almost* common knowledge, you get almost the same outcomes, so the distinction between common and mutual knowledge is mostly academic.' I have the sense some of this is subtly off -- like the claim that rational agents can never trade at all seems too strong -- but I don't know enough game theory to pin down where. Go through it claim by claim: what would a game theorist accept, what's a simplification, and what has the host actually gotten backwards?
