I'm 74, retired physical chemist, spent most of my career in spectroscopy, and since lockdown I've been slowly working through set theory and foundations, which I dodged my whole working life. My question is about the axiom of choice, or rather about my own reactions to it, which I don't think add up.

Here are my gut verdicts on choice-flavored statements, in no particular order:

1. Picking one shoe from each of infinitely many pairs of shoes: obviously fine, pick the left one. Picking one sock from each of infinitely many pairs of socks: something feels off, there's no rule for it, but honestly I can't get very worked up about it.
2. A countable union of countable sets is countable: fine. I needed facts like this in analysis forever.
3. Given a relation on a set, you can keep choosing elements to build an infinite sequence, where each choice depends on the previous ones (dependent choice, I think it's called): fine, that's just how you'd run any actual procedure step by step.
4. Every vector space has a basis: fine, even necessary. I used bases my whole career.
5. The reals can be well-ordered: no. I have no idea what such an ordering would even look like and no reason to believe one exists.
6. Banach-Tarski: too crazy, I treat it as a reductio of whatever went into it.
7. There is a non-principal ultrafilter on the natural numbers: fishy, though I admit I couldn't say why this one bothers me more than the socks.
8. Every surjection has a right inverse: fine? You're just labeling preimages. Though I notice I feel worse about this when the domain is the reals than when it's the integers.
9. An infinite product of compact intervals (arbitrarily many factors) is compact: fine, it's just topology, and compactness feels like it shouldn't depend on choice.

A guy on a math forum told me that number 4 and number 5 are equivalent, which I refuse to believe, because one of them is fine and the other is insane. But that got me worried that my verdicts don't form a consistent set at all.

My tentative principle was something like: accept choice when the family of sets you're choosing from is countable, or when there's an explicit rule that picks the element. But that principle kills 4, since nobody can write down a rule for a basis of the reals over the rationals, and I want to keep 4. Also my physicist instinct says nothing in physics ever needed more than the countable stuff, since Hilbert spaces in QM are separable and you get orthonormal bases by Gram-Schmidt, which is a rule.

So: is there a principle that captures all or most of my verdicts? State it precisely, clause by clause, and say which case motivates each clause. Then tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't thought about -- whether every connected graph has a spanning tree, whether every partial order extends to a linear order, whether non-measurable sets of reals exist, whether every field has an algebraic closure. And if the principle can't save all nine verdicts, please be straight with me about which one has to go and why that one rather than another. I'd especially like to know if my stubbornness about 4 vs 5 is just ignorance.
