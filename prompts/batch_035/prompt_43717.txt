The question, set by the module convenor, read as follows:

> Clark Glymour argued that Bayesian confirmation theory faces a problem of old evidence. Explain the problem, say why it is supposed to be embarrassing for the Bayesian (illustrating with a historical case), and critically assess one prominent Bayesian response.

And here is the student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

> The Bayesian holds that evidence e confirms hypothesis h to the extent that it raises h's probability, and on the standard ratio measure the degree of confirmation is P(e|h)/P(e). The problem of old evidence, first pressed by Clark Glymour, is that things go wrong for evidence already in the agent's possession: a rational agent assigns known evidence probability 1, so P(e) = 1; and conditionalising on h cannot shift a probability of 1, so P(e|h) = 1 as well. The ratio is therefore 1, and on the difference measure P(h|e) − P(h) we get zero, since when e is certain, learning it cannot change anything. So h receives no confirmation from e at all, however well h explains e.
>
> This is embarrassing because science repeatedly confirms theories with evidence that was old when the theory arrived. The stock example is general relativity and the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion: the theory accounted precisely for an observation that had puzzled astronomers for half a century, and this counts as one of the celebrated novel predictions of 1915, alongside the bending of starlight later confirmed by the 1919 eclipse expedition. A Bayesian who says that the perihelion evidence did not confirm general relativity is saying something intolerable.
>
> Note, though, that the problem bites only where the hypothesis entails the old evidence, as general relativity entails the exact size of the perihelion advance. Where the hypothesis merely raises the probability of the old evidence, Bayesian confirmation proceeds in the ordinary way.
>
> The best-known response, due to Howson and Urbach, is to evaluate the confirmation counterfactually: instead of the actual probability of e, use the probability the agent would have assigned e had she not known it. An astronomer who did not know the size of the perihelion advance — holding the rest of her background beliefs fixed — would have assigned it a fairly low probability, and a high one conditional on general relativity; the old evidence therefore confirms. The standard objection is that these counterfactual probabilities are under-determined: there are several ways to unknow e, and they can give different numbers, so the response buys confirmation at the price of arbitrariness. Still, it shows the problem is not fatal. The deeper lesson is that the old evidence problem exposes the Bayesian's inability to distinguish prediction from accommodation: by the time a theory is evaluated, the evidence is old in both cases, so Bayesianism must treat them alike.
