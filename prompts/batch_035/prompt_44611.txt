He cites Lucas's 1961 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and Penrose's later books, and he dismisses the objections he has heard of as evasion.

I want to be careful not to attack a strawman, so please evaluate the argument in its strongest defensible form, whatever you take that to be. Please give me your own number, but more importantly decompose it into the sub-claims the conclusion depends on, with a rough probability and a line of justification for each, and then show how they combine. At minimum I would expect terms for the following, though please add, merge, or restructure as you see fit: whether the mathematical output of a human being, or of the human mathematical community over time, can even be modeled by a single consistent formal system in the way the argument requires; whether the argument illicitly assumes human mathematical belief is consistent, given that the theorem's hypothesis of consistency is what generates the unprovable sentence in the first place; whether there is a real difference between seeing that a sentence is true conditional on the system being consistent and seeing that it is true outright, and whether the human reasoner only has the former; and whether the conclusion, if it worked, would prove too much — for instance by also showing that no human mathematician, or no finite community of them, could be modeled by any human mathematician, which seems like a reductio rather than a result about machines.
