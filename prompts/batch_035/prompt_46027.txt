Background if it helps: I'm a retired geophysicist. Forty years of writing papers where "X caused Y" had a pretty strict meaning -- you could in principle intervene on X and watch Y respond. Since retiring I've been reading my way into social theory, and Weber's Protestant Ethic came up in my book club (yes, we have a book club, it's exactly as pretentious as it sounds). Most of it I can follow, but a friend of mine, also retired but from chemistry, laid out an argument after our second glass of wine and it's been bugging me for weeks:

(1) To explain something is to cite its causes. (2) Weber explains the spirit of capitalism by the Protestant ethic: certain religious values caused people to work, save, and invest in a distinctive way. (3) But a value is just something in people's heads, and everything in people's heads has a prior cause -- Calvinist doctrine, the printing press, material conditions in northern Europe. (4) Trace the causal chain back far enough and you always land on non-cultural, physical facts. (5) So culture is never really a cause, just a relay station -- the true explanation sits at the physical end of the chain. He likes to put it as: explaining capitalism with the Protestant ethic is like explaining thunder with Zeus's temper. (6) And by exactly the same logic, an individual's values, beliefs, and decisions can't be causes either, since they also have prior physical causes. (7) Therefore Weber isn't merely wrong about capitalism -- no historian can ever explain anything by what anyone wanted or believed, and my own decision to retire early didn't cause my move to Arizona.

Now, my friend cheerfully accepts (7), which is what spooks me. I want to say I moved to Arizona because I wanted sunshine, and I also want to keep something like my working scientist's notion of causal explanation. My instinct is that something sneaks in around step (5) or (6) -- maybe something about how in geophysics I never had to cite quarks to explain a weather system, and that wasn't just a shortcut, it seemed like the right level to explain at -- but I can't turn that instinct into an actual diagnosis, and my friend says practical convenience isn't an argument about what's *really* doing the causing. So: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine to two retired scientists, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd honestly rather give up (5) than (7) if forced, but I want to know what I'm paying either way. Oh, and could you write your answer in British English? My wife is from Leeds and she reads over my shoulder.
