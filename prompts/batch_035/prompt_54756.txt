At a dinner I hosted last month, two friends of mine — Dana, an economics postdoc, and Marcus, who is finishing a philosophy PhD — spent three hours arguing about a pair of choices I had made in a paid study years ago, and I could not follow the endgame. In the study's first choice I picked a sure $1,000,000 over a gamble paying $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, and nothing with probability 0.01. In its second choice I picked a gamble paying $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90 over a gamble paying $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89. Dana called this pattern irrational, Marcus defended it, and the argument ran as follows.

(1) Dana said my two choices jointly violate the independence axiom: since the first and second choices share the same 0.89-probability component and differ only in what happens in the remaining 0.11, consistent valuations of that remaining part must order the pairs the same way, and mine do not. She added that no single utility function over final outcomes can represent both preferences, and that agents with incoherent preferences are exploitable: a bookie aware of my pattern could construct a chain of trades, each of which I would accept given that pattern, that leaves me with certainty of ending up worse off than I started, with the differences pocketed by the bookie.

(2) Marcus answered that this assumes rationality just is expected-utility maximization, and that Lara Buchak's 2013 book Risk and Rationality develops a rival: agents maximize risk-weighted expected utility, computed by ranking a gamble's outcomes from best to worst and replacing each cumulative probability — the probability of doing at least that well — with its image under an increasing function r with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. A risk-averse agent's r discounts the probabilities of doing well, which loads extra weight onto the bad tail, and with a suitably risk-averse r my choices are exactly what the theory recommends: in the first choice the gamble carries a 1% chance of nothing that the sure million eliminates, while in the second choice both options leave me with roughly 90% chance of nothing, so the downside no longer distinguishes them and the larger prize wins. Buchak proves a representation theorem for agents satisfying her axioms, so the preferences are representable by a coherent theory, and coherence arguments cannot simply rule them out.

(3) Dana replied that a representation theorem is cheap: if you are free to fix both a utility function and a risk function after seeing the behavior, some representation or other will bless any consistent pattern, so the theory has no normative bite. She also pressed the exploitation point again: the REU agent, offered the trades one at a time and evaluating each in isolation, still accepts each step of the pump.

(4) Marcus said r is not chosen per decision but fixed across all choices, just like utility in EU theory, so the theory genuinely constrains: it entails stochastic dominance, rules out cyclic preferences, and is falsifiable by stable patterns across many choices in exactly the way EU is. On the pump: it works only on an agent who evaluates each offer in isolation, whereas a sophisticated agent who foresees the sequence refuses the first trade — and EU theorists already appeal to that kind of foresight when defending their own axioms, so they cannot withhold it from the REU agent.

(5) Dana's last substantive move was that this concedes her deeper point: the real dispute is over the independence axiom itself, so appealing to a theorem that builds in weaker axioms assumes what is in question, and the only non-question-begging argument for independence — immunity to exploitation — still stands, since a patient bookie staggering offers and fees can bleed an REU agent who keeps paying to replace small downside risks with certainties.

(6) Marcus closed by noting that every real insurance policy has negative expected monetary value or the insurer would not sell it, so on Dana's reasoning all risk-averse purchasing is irrational; the pump merely sells the REU agent insurance against the 1% chance of ruin, which is profitable for the seller and perfectly reasonable for the buyer, and if that counts as exploitation then the entire insurance industry exploits its customers.

They then argued past each other about sure-thing reasoning until the table broke up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; do not open with preamble or restate my description.
