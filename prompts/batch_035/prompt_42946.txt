I'm teaching a small seminar on postwar analytic philosophy -- 13 students, meets once a week for 80 minutes -- and we're coming up on the Quine unit. The students have all seen the bachelor example a million times and can recite "analytic true by meaning, synthetic true by fact" in their sleep, and then they nod along to the idea that Quine "attacked" it without ever really feeling the attack. What I want them to actually feel is how the two dogmas are connected: first the definitional circularity -- every explanation of analyticity (cognitive synonymy, interchangeability salva veritate, semantical rules for an artificial language) ends up helping itself to some notion that presupposes analyticity or necessity, so the whole family of definitions spins without anything anchoring it -- and then the flip side, which is that if the analytic/synthetic wall falls, verificationism per-statement falls with it, and you're left with a web where experience hits the edges and revisions can propagate anywhere, including into logic, even if in practice we'd sooner revise almost anything else.

So here's what I'm asking for: design an in-class activity, no lecture up front, where they don't hear the word "analytic" until the debrief. My half-formed idea is some kind of game where small groups hold a set of statements -- a mix of stipulated definitions, simple math claims, and observation-level claims about a fictional scenario -- and then get hit with a series of experiment reports that are inconsistent with their current stock. They must keep the web consistent by revising *something*, and the scoring should reward minimal disruption rather than protecting any particular kind of statement. If it works, a group should discover two things on their own: (1) that no statement is flat-out immune, but there's a real gradient -- revising the observation claim is cheap, revising the definition is possible but expensive, revising the arithmetic feels almost unthinkable, and "almost" is doing real philosophical work there; and (2) that when they try to fall back on "but that one's true by definition," and another group asks what that means, their attempt to say it sends them around a circle of synonyms and stipulations that never touches ground. Feel free to restructure this completely if you have a better mechanism -- I tried running a debate last year (Team Definition vs. Team Experience) and it collapsed into two people arguing about whether math exists, so I'd rather have something where the structure does the arguing. One wrinkle I want you to address: at least one student will play the "come what may" holdout who refuses to revise anything ever, and I need to know what the facilitator does with that, since Quine himself leaves room for holding some statements come what may as a matter of pragmatic choice.

Please give me the full write-up -- materials, rules, the statement sets and experiment reports (make the fictional scenario weird enough that they can't pattern-match it to anything), a run-of-show with rough timings, and the debrief script that finally names the distinction and maps what happened onto the two dogmas, including the "tribe revises logic to keep a law" moment if you can stage something analogous. Then, after the write-up, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "activity_name" (string), "best_moment" (string, the part that should land the core idea hardest), "mapping" (array of objects, each with "what_happened" describing a moment in the activity and "quinean_point" naming the idea from the essay it instantiates), "predicted_failures" (array of objects with "problem" and "fix"), "what_activity_cannot_reproduce" (string -- be honest here, e.g. whether any 80-minute game can really show the pragmatic grading of revisability versus just asserting it), and "debrief_edges" (array of strings, moments where a clever student could derail the debrief and how the facilitator should handle it). Thanks, this seminar is the one class I actually get to teach for myself and I want this unit to not be the usual death-by-handout.
