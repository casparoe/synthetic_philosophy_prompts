The exam question was: "Augustine holds that it is never permissible to lie, not even to an attacker at the door who asks, gun in hand, where his intended victim is hiding. (a) Explain how he distinguishes lying from other kinds of false or misleading speech. (b) Explain the grounds on which he holds lying to be always wrong. (c) What does his position require of the person at the door — does it require telling the whole truth, or is there anything else they may do?"

The student wrote: "Augustine defines a lie as a statement made with the intention to deceive, so both the falsity of what is said and the intention have to be present. This means a true statement made in order to deceive also counts as a lie: if I say something literally true but phrase it so that my hearer draws the wrong conclusion, I've lied just as much as if I had said something false. On the other hand, a mistaken claim is not a lie, since I believe what I'm saying, and joking and fiction are not lies either since no one intends to deceive and the hearer doesn't expect literal truth. As for why lying is always wrong, Augustine's view is that it corrupts the speaker and destroys trust in language, which is the medium of society and of our relation to God; and since God forbids bearing false witness, no good consequence can ever make a lie permissible — he even considers the hypothetical where a single lie could convert the entire world and still says it may not be told. In the murderer case, his answer is that you must not lie, and if you tell the truth and the person hiding is harmed, the harm is the murderer's doing, not yours, whereas the lie would be your own sin. Since lying is forbidden, the only remaining option is to tell the truth: on his view the householder's duty is to answer the question honestly when asked, and silence, half-truths, and evasion amount to the same sin of deception anyway, so if you can't say something true you must say what is true even at the door. Augustine also sorts lies by their purposes — lies in matters of religion, lies that harm no one and benefit someone, and so on, down to lies told just for the pleasure of lying — and concludes that lies told to save a life are the least culpable kind. This is where he comes closest to allowing an exception, and in the end he concedes that in the extreme case, where a life is at stake, a lie is permissible because the alternative is a much greater evil. So the absolute prohibition is really aimed at gratuitous and self-serving lies."
