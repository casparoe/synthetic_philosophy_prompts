I'm teaching a seminar on Bauman's Modernity and the Holocaust next term and I want to pair it with a screening of Schindler's List, but I keep going back and forth on whether the film is the best illustration of his thesis or quietly its worst enemy. Help me think it through.

Bauman's line, roughly: the Holocaust wasn't a relapse into barbarism that modernity failed to prevent -- it was modernity working as designed. Bureaucracy, division of labor, technical rationality, authority substituting itself for moral judgment. The people who ran the machinery mostly weren't monsters; they were ordinary men at a comfortable distance from consequences, following the logic of their roles. Moral sleeping-in, he calls something like that -- the capacity to respond to another person's face gets suspended when routine and hierarchy take over.

So here's my problem. Spielberg's film kind of needs two things Bauman says modernity makes nearly impossible. It needs Schindler himself -- an individual whose conscience wakes up and who then operates *inside* the system, bribes and connections and playing the game, to save his workers. Is that Bauman vindicated (the only person who could do anything was one who gamed the machine from within, and even he could only save people by out-capitalisting it) or Bauman refuted (look, moral agency survived just fine)? And it needs Göth, the commandant, who is basically a sadist -- cruelty for pleasure, whim, spite, the most legible personal pathology imaginable. Doesn't the Hollywood evil-as-monster psychology just undo Bauman's entire point about the ordinariness of the perpetrators? Or is Göth actually an honest concession -- the camps contained some genuine sadists among the sleepwalkers, and a film can only show so many characters?

And the girl in the red coat. The film's signature move is individualizing and sentimentalizing one victim against a horror Bauman describes as precisely making victims faceless, statistical, processed. Is that a necessary artistic corrective to the dehumanizing machinery -- the camera doing what the bureaucracy couldn't, making one person vivid again -- or does the sentimentality undo something, and does the film's need for a redemptive arc (the shower scene, the ending, the ring) end up telling the exact moral story Bauman warned against, where the Holocaust becomes survivable and meaningful through individual virtue?

What I want from you: a reading that takes both the film and the book seriously. Which scenes should I build the seminar discussion around -- the Stern paperwork negotiations, the list-making itself, Göth's balcony shootings, Schindler's meltdown at the end? Where is the film genuininely doing Baumanian work, where is it evading him, and where does it show something his argument alone can't? Then an honest verdict: does Bauman survive contact with the film, or the film with Bauman? Don't go easy on either side, and if the answer is that the pairing only works if I teach the film *against* the book rather than with it, say so plainly.
