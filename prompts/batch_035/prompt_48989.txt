I've been in the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and it struck me recently that our community performed its own small eliminativism about belief long ago: we replaced binary belief with credence, sorted professed beliefs into belief-in-belief versus belief as attire versus anticipations that pay rent, and generally treated the folk category as something to be cashed out rather than trusted. Meanwhile the academic debate this all gestures at — whether propositional attitudes like belief and desire will go the way of phlogiston and caloric, as Paul Churchland argued, or whether they name the causally efficacious, law-supporting generalizations that any cognitive science must traffic in, as Fodor insisted — has been essentially frozen since the late eighties. The eliminativists said mature neuroscience would supply the replacement vocabulary; it never did, but it didn't vindicate the old one either, it mostly talked past the taxonomy entirely. And now the predictive-processing people cheerfully say the brain has beliefs, by which they mean priors, which looks like it should settle something and somehow settles nothing: both camps point to the same body of work as vindication.

My working hypothesis is that the standoff isn't empirical at all. It's a disagreement about what a taxonomy owes its users: the eliminativist demands that mental-state kinds earn their keep in the vocabulary of a completed neuroscience, while the realist demands only that they support robust generalizations at some usable level of description, and no experimental result could refute either standard. Is that diagnosis right? Or is this a verbal dispute about the word belief, or two distinct questions — one about the ontology of a future science, one about the everyday explanatory practice of psychology — traveling under a single name? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a completed neuroscience, a formalism, a distinction, or just everyone getting bored?
