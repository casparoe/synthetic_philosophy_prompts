I'm a philosophy student working on future contingents — the old sea-battle problem about whether statements about tomorrow are already true or false now — and rather than another pass through Aristotle I'd like to understand what our forecasting institutions implicitly commit themselves to. Consider the actual practice: national weather services issue probabilistic forecasts (a 70 percent chance of rain at this location tomorrow), bookmakers and prediction markets quote odds on elections and matches, insurers price policies against hurricanes and deaths, and all of these get used to make real decisions — the city salts the roads, the campaign reallocates its spending. And crucially the practice has a grading component: forecasters are evaluated on calibration — across all the days on which a 70 percent forecast was issued, rain should have occurred about seven times in ten — and there is an understood rule that a single 70 percent forecast that fails is not counted as an error, whereas a flat "it will rain tomorrow" that fails straightforwardly is.

My puzzle is what this whole practice presupposes about tomorrow's rain, if anything. It looks as though issuing a 70 percent forecast rather than a prediction only makes sense if the future is somehow open — as if the practice needs tomorrow's rain sentence to lack a truth value today. But the grading half of the practice seems to presuppose the opposite: calibration is computed by counting determinate outcomes, the number "70" is treated as trackable fact, and forecasters are told they're getting the world's frequencies right or wrong. So does the practice presuppose the failure of bivalence for future contingents, or does it presuppose nothing of the sort — merely epistemic humility that a fatalist could happily endorse — or does it presuppose something in between, like objective single-case chances over a future that becomes determinate as it arrives? And is the presupposition consistent across the parts: can the same institution sensibly issue a 70 percent forecast while acting as though it will either rain or not?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, tell me which parts of the practice strictly need it and which merely fit well with it, and say what the practice would have to look like if we replaced the implicit view with a straightforwardly bivalent or fatalist one — for instance, whether a forecaster under such a view would be reduced to reporting her credence, and whether calibration grading would still mean anything. If you think the practice is philosophically underdetermined to the point that no single reconstruction is forced — or that I've misdescribed something about how forecasting and its evaluation actually work — please say so plainly rather than building a theory on my framing.
