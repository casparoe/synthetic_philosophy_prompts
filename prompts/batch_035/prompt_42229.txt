In a philosophy of biology reading group I've been attending, one member repeatedly appealed to the phrase "free-floating rationale" — a term I understand traces back to Daniel Dennett — in defense of the idea that natural selection can explain the emergence of reasons and meaning from mindless beginnings. The crux of his argument was this: the rationale for a spider's web, the moth's camouflage, or the eye's lens existed and did its work billions of years before any mind could grasp it, so reasons do not presuppose reasoners; therefore, when we come along and represent the world as full of reasons, we are not conjuring normativity out of nothing but articulating something that was, in some sense, already there. He used this to rebut the objection that naturalistic accounts of content are circular — that selection can only confer meaning if something already understands what traits are "for."

I left the session unsure what the phrase actually commits us to, and I suspect it was doing more work than its defenders acknowledged. Please lay out, as a structured list I can work through, the distinct precise claims "free-floating rationale" could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest — e.g., a purely heuristic reading on which reason-talk is a convenient shorthand with no ontological import; a predictive-instrumentalist reading on which the reason-patterns are real enough to support prediction without being "in" the organism; a selection-history reading on which there are objective relational facts about what traits are for, grounded in etiology but still non-normative; and a robust realist reading on which there are genuine proto-normative facts about reasons obtaining in the world independently of any representer at all. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and note any readings I have not considered. Then address the question that matters most to me: which reading does my colleague's anti-circularity argument actually require? Could the deflationary readings carry the argument on their own, or does he need the strong realist one — and if so, can that reading be maintained without smuggling in, under another name, precisely the normativity it was supposed to explain? I'd like your verdicts to be explicit enough that I can bring the list back to the group and have something to argue about.
