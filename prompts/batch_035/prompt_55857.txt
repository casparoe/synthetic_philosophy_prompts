I work on safety evaluations at a small AI lab, and over the past year I've settled into a position about how honesty and deception should be defined for language models, and I'd like to stress-test it. My view is that the concept of deception we should use for AI systems is entirely non-intentional in the "intent to mislead" sense: a model's assertion is honest if and only if it tracks the model's internal epistemic state — the system asserts p only when the system's own representations support p — and deceptive whenever there is a systematic mismatch between what the system asserts and what its internal state supports, provided the assertion tends to induce belief in the user. The motivation is that "intent" does no useful work for current systems: we can't reliably attribute intentions to them, the notion we need for evals and policy has to be checkable from behavior plus (eventually) interpretability evidence, and a tracking definition makes honesty continuous with calibration rather than with sincerity. On this picture, an AI system that confidently asserts things its internal state doesn't support is deceptive in exactly the same sense whether or not there is anything in it that "wants" to mislead, and an ironic model, or one that says something false as part of a joke, needs an exemption clause I find awkward but necessary.

A colleague of mine thinks this is a mess for roughly Gricean reasons: deception, she says, is essentially a communicative act that exploits a model of the listener, so my definition will flag as "deceptive" systems that are merely miscalibrated, sloppy, or hallucinating in the ordinary way — thereby diluting a grave accusation into a technical property, which matters if regulators or the public adopt the vocabulary. She also argues that once I add my exemption clauses for fiction, jokes, role-play, and benign contexts, the definition becomes a patchwork held together by intuitions rather than principle, and that the same patchwork would have to be built for the intent-based view anyway, so I've bought nothing. I think she's wrong about the dilution point — I'd say "deceptive" functions like "toxic" in chemistry, a property defined by effect rather than by the compound's "goals" — but I'm not confident, and I'd rather find out now whether the view is actually load-bearing or just convenient.

So: what would count against my position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically, I'd like to know (1) which conceivable findings about how models actually represent their own uncertainty would undermine the tracking definition — e.g., if internal epistemic states turn out to be too fragmented, context-dependent, or unstable for "what the system's representations support" to be a coherent notion; (2) whether the view makes wrong predictions about edge cases — the confident hallucination, the model that says what it thinks the user wants to hear, the sycophantic model, the model that asserts true things for manipulative reasons — and which of these a committed defender of my view would concede as counting against it versus explain away; (3) whether the exemption clauses for fiction, role-play, and benign falsehoods can be given a principled basis, or whether their ad hocness is a genuine structural flaw; and (4) whether the view could be qualified into unfalsifiability — if every recalcitrant case can be absorbed by re-describing the model's "internal epistemic state," is that a sign the definition isn't doing any real work, or is insulation from counterexamples acceptable in a stipulative engineering definition the way it isn't in a descriptive ethical one? I'd also welcome a verdict on whether my colleague's "dilution" objection is best met, or whether the right response is to concede the word "deception" to her and keep my tracking notion under a different name. Please write your answer in American English.
