For two years I've been in a long-running Nomic game, and one player — a legal positivist with a logician's temperament — has produced an argument that most of our second year of play was legally void. I think the argument proves too much, but every time I point at a step he has a comeback, and I've stopped trusting my own diagnosis. Here is the argument as he states it:

1. (Pedigree) A rule is valid only if it was created by an act authorized by a rule that was itself valid at the time of the act.
2. In our game, as in any legal order, amendments are acts authorized by the amendment rules — the rules that say how rules may be changed.
3. In two years of play we have repeatedly amended the amendment rules themselves, including the rules governing how the amendment rules may be amended. In such an act, the rule that authorizes the act and the rule the act replaces are one and the same.
4. No act can be authorized by a rule whose validity that very act terminates. If the authorizing rule dies, the authorization dies with it, and nothing remains from which the act's validity could derive. A rule cannot be a party to its own repeal and at the same time serve as the warrant for it.
5. Therefore every act in which we amended the amendment rules was invalid, and every later rule whose pedigree runs through such an act is invalid as well — which, given how many times the amendment rules have been through the mill, voids nearly everything we now play by.
6. The point is general. Any exercise of a delegated power that alters the very rule conferring the power — a constitutional convention amending the constitution's amendment clause, a university senate rewriting its own quorum and voting rules, an agency revising the regulation that defines its procedures — is void by the same reasoning.
7. Ordinary amendments are implicated too. Every amendment alters the body of rules of which the amendment rules are a part, so every amendment partially overwrites its own source of authority; if partial destruction of the authorizing source is fatal, it is fatal here as well.
8. So no amendment was ever validly made, in our game or anywhere else. What we call rule changes are sociological events that participants have conventionally agreed to treat as law.

I cannot accept (8). It entails that no constitution with a self-referential amendment clause has ever been validly amended, that the difference between lawful amendment and revolution is a difference in manners rather than in validity, and that our game has consisted of people performing impressive-looking legal theater with no legal content. My friend is happy to bite all of these bullets.

My own suspicion is that (4) hides a temporal slide: the amendment is an act performed at a moment when the old rule is unquestionably in force, and its validity is fixed at that moment rather than continuously re-derived from whatever rules happen to be current later. He replies that this makes validity a fact about the past rather than about the rules now in force, and that a rule whose validity is merely historical cannot bind anyone today. He also leans on unamendable provisions: the German Basic Law famously declares certain principles immune to amendment, and he asks what work such clauses could possibly do if self-amendment were harmless — aren't they supposed to mark exactly the line his argument draws, the point at which authority runs out?

Tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. I would rather keep (1); I think pedigree is the best theory of validity we have. But I want to know what I am paying if I give up (4) instead, and I want an honest verdict on whether my temporal story is a real diagnosis or just a smoother version of what he is accusing me of. Finally, tell me whether his step (7) is a genuine extension or a bluff: does the self-amendment problem really generalize to every amendment, or is there a principled line between amending a rule and amending the system that contains the rule?
