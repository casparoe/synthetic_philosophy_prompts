I'm in a reading group that's been going through Dennett's stuff on free-floating rationales — reasons out there in nature that no reasoner has ever formulated, like the rationale for a flower's coloring that was sitting there for millions of years before anyone articulated it — and one member, a committed naturalist, walked me into an argument whose conclusion he himself hates but which none of us can cleanly refute. It goes: (1) There are reasons that no reasoner ever entertained: before there were minds, there was already a reason petals are brightly colored, namely that bright coloring attracts pollinators, and that reason was there doing its work, with selection "discovering" it rather than anyone inventing it. (2) A reason, whatever else it is, is the kind of thing that could justify — if an agent were to pick it up and weigh it, it would count in favor of something. (3) The petal case shows that a reason's obtaining is independent of any actual act of considering it; nobody considered it for eons and it was still a reason. (4) If reasons can obtain unconsidered, then what counts in favor of what doesn't depend on anyone's attitudes, since in the petal case the reason outruns all attitudes. (5) Facts about what counts in favor of what, independent of attitudes, just are normative facts in the realist's sense. (6) So accepting free-floating rationales buys you attitude-independent normative facts, which is non-naturalism about reasons — the one thing my friend, as a good naturalist, refuses to sign up for. So either there are no free-floating rationales and Dennett's picture of reasons stretching back into pre-history is a picturesque way of saying nothing, or naturalism about reasons is false.

He wants to keep both the free-floating rationales and his naturalism, obviously, and he suspects something slipped between (2) and (4) — something about "could justify" doing double duty — but he can't say what without just stipulating that only considered reasons justify, which he admits would beg the question against (3). When I stare at it myself I can't tell whether it's a genuine proof or a trick with the word "reason": in the petal case "the reason petals are colored" sounds explanatory (it answers why the petals are colored, a causal selection story), while in (5) "counts in favor of" sounds normative (it answers what ought to be believed or done), and nothing in the chain marks where the crossing happens, if it happens. So: which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1) — the thought that nature is full of reasons that predate any reasoner is the whole reason I found this stuff excitng in the first place — but I want to know what I have to give up to keep it, and whether my friend is right that the only available fix works by begging the qustion against his own premise.
