ok so background on me: I'm a software engineer, been working in fintech for a while, and i've fallen down a rabbit hole lately about how rational agents should handle money across time. no formal philosophy or econ training, just a lot of blog reading and messing around with simulations in python, so go easy on the jargon.

Here's the controversy I want an essay on, and I want you to actually take a side at the end, not just survey stuff:

The old textbook answer to "how should a rational agent choose between gambles" is expected value maximization (or expected utility, whatever). But there's a crowd — the "ergodicity economics" people, plus Kelly criterion folks, plus some decision theorists — who say this is quietly assuming that the agent gets to average over many parallel copies of itself, one per possible outcome. A single agent doesn't get to do that. It lives through one path, sequentially, and multiplication compounds. If each bet can multiply your bankroll by 1.5 or by 0.6, the arithmetic expectation looks great, but if you repeatedly stake everything, the multiplicative path wrecks you: (1.5 × 0.6) < 1, so equal numbers of wins and losses still shrink you. Their fix is to maximize the geometric growth rate — which is what the Kelly criterion does, betting only a fraction of your bankroll — and they claim this isn't just a niche finance trick but arguably the more fundamental notion of rationality for anything that evolves over time. Some of them go further and basically say expected utility theory survives in economics only because it secretly helps itself to the ensemble average, and that's a bug.

The other side says: no, this is all much ado about nothing. An expected utility theorist who happens to have log utility reproduces Kelly exactly, so there's no deep disagreement, just a preference. And more than that: someone who genuinely doesn't care about wealth later, or expects to die soon, or gets to make a one-off bet with outside financing, or has diminishing needs, has perfectly good reasons to overbet or underbet relative to Kelly. On this view Kelly isn't a theory of rationality at all, it's just the answer for one particular utility function under one particular situation (repeated bets, bankroll-linked stakes, long horizon), and dressing it up as a rival to expected utility is marketing.

So, the essay should:

2. Grapple with the strongest point on each side. For the Kelly side I'd include the point that real agents making real sequential decisions are always on a single trajectory, and things like gambler's ruin are facts about trajectories, not about averages across hypothetical agents. For the EU side I'd include the log-utility-is-just-a-preference point and maybe that Kelly-style rules get weird when the agent can bet on credit, or when the "bankroll" isn't the thing the agent actually cares about.
3. Reach a real verdict. Some acceptable verdicts, just so you know what I mean: "geometric growth is the right default for agents whose stakes track their resources over long horizons, but it's not a replacement for expected utility, it's a special case" or "the ergodicity people are right that EU theory as practiced smuggled in ensemble averaging, even if the formal theory can be patched."
