I'm writing an essay on authorial intention and I keep coming back to A Clockwork Orange. The American edition famously cut Burgess's final chapter — the one where Alex, a few years older, runs into his old droog Pete, now settled down, and starts imagining himself eventually having a son and quietly outgrowing the violence — and Kubrick's film follows the American version, so most people know a book that ends with Alex restored to his old self. Burgess spent decades complaining about this, saying the truncated book misses the point entirely, that the novel is about maturity and moral choice, not about a boy who is broken by the state. Read the two endings through the actual debate: Wimsatt and Beardsley's intentional fallacy, Hirsch's defense of authorial meaning, maybe Barthes for contrast. Is the American version a different work, a worse one, or the same work with a botched ending? And here's the part I can't work out on my own — I think the bleak ending arguably fits the title better (a man who is organic turned clockwork, and stays that way), which suggests the author can just be wrong about which parts of his own book carry the meaning, or even about what his book is about. Does believing that make me an anti-intentionalist about this novel specifically, and is that a stable position to hold in an essay, or does it collapse back into intentionalism the moment I invoke what Burgess "meant" in order to say he failed? Don't just lay out both sides for me — tell me whether the novel does something the theory can't account for.
