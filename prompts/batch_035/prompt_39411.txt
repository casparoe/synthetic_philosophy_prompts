I'm a philosopher by trade — ethics, mostly, with a bit of ancient stuff — so confirmation theory is adjacent to my lane rather than in it, and something from a faculty dinner last week has been nagging at me ever since. Our new colleague in the physics department was holding forth about scientific method, and when a grad student brought up Bayesianism he dismissed it in one breath, roughly like this: Bayesianism says evidence confirms a theory exactly when it raises the theory's probability, and you learn by conditioning on whatever you observe. But Glymour showed back in 1980 that this collapses the moment you look at real science: the anomaly in Mercury's perihelion was known for decades before Einstein, and everyone agrees general relativity was confirmed by explaining it — yet for a Bayesian in 1915 the perihelion data is old news, an agent who already knows something assigns it probability one, and conditioning on a proposition you already give probability one to leaves everything unchanged. So Bayesians can't even represent one of the most celebrated confirmations in the history of physics, and Glymour thereby refuted the whole Bayesian confirmation program.

He delivered it very confidently and everyone nodded along, and it smelled off to me — it had the shape of a punchline rather than a problem — but I honestly could not have said where it goes wrong, if it does.
