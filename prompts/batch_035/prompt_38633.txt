The debate over Newcomb's problem is now more than half a century old. Nozick's original 1969 presentation already conceded that the arguments on both sides seemed compelling to their adherents while the opposing arguments seemed clearly wrong to them, and the field has essentially lived with that stalemate ever since: surveys of philosophers continue to show a persistent, near-even split between one-boxers and two-boxers. Both sides know the full stock of moves by heart — the dominance argument and its reliance on a particular partition of states of the world; the evidential rejoinder that the predictor's track record makes two-boxing bad news; the challenge that two-boxers owe an explanation of why the one-boxers keep leaving rich; the reply that rationality evaluates acts, not the lifetime wealth of agents; medical and smoking-lesion variants constructed to peel intuitions apart; appeals to precommitment and to what you would rationally wish your earlier self had done. Every argument is on the table, and nobody budges.

I want a diagnosis of this impasse, not a verdict on the case. My working hypothesis — offered only as a hypothesis — is that the dispute is not really about decision theory but about the individuation of decision problems: whether Newcomb's problem names a single problem on which two rational analyses genuinely conflict, or two problems that merely share a payoff matrix — one in which the predictor's accuracy is a brute correlation between your act and the box contents, and one in which it reflects a deeper common structure connecting your deliberation to the prediction. On the second reading, one-boxers and two-boxers are answering different questions under one name, and the appearance of direct contradiction is an artifact of treating the payoff matrix as the whole of the problem.

But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I can see alternatives: that the disagreement is a substantive one about the semantics of counterfactuals; that it is a clash of bedrock intuitions about what rationality is for, neither of which can be argued for non-circularly; that it is a verbal dispute about the word 'rational'; or that it is an empirical question in disguise about what agents can and cannot predictably decide.

Diagnose the disagreement for me, grounded in what each side actually says rather than a survey of positions. Tell me what kind of disagreement it is — verbal, substantive, or two-different-questions-under-one-name — and support your diagnosis with the considerations each side actually invokes. Then tell me what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress: a new case, a formal distinction, a reformulation of the question, something else. Or argue that the stalemate shows the question is ill-posed as posed, or that no progress should be expected and why. If you think my individuation hypothesis is on the right track but needs repair, say precisely where it breaks; if you think it is hopeless, tell me what it gets wrong and what a better diagnosis looks like.
