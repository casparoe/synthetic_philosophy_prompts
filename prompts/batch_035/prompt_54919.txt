I'm taking a graduate seminar on confirmation theory, and we spent three weeks on Goodman's new riddle of induction from Fact, Fiction, and Forecast before the discussion collapsed into everyone restating their starting positions. Rather than keep arguing in class, I've been keeping a private list of my own verdicts about which inductive inferences are legitimate, and I've realized the list doesn't obviously add up to anything coherent. I'd like help extracting a principle from it.

Here are the verdicts. Every emerald anyone has examined has been green, and I think this confirms that all emeralds are green. The very same observations do not confirm, even slightly, that all emeralds are grue — where an emerald counts as grue if it was first examined before some fixed future date and is green, or was not examined before that date and is blue — even though the two hypotheses agree on every observation anyone has ever made. Goodman's own example of the men in a room all being third sons strikes me the same way: having checked the thirty men present and found that each is a third son, I still wouldn't expect the thirty-first man to walk in to be one. Yet I think a European naturalist in 1650 who had seen a few hundred black ravens and projected that all ravens are black was reasoning perfectly properly — he turned out to be wrong, but he wasn't irrational — and I think a marine biologist who collects five specimens of a newly described deep-sea amphipod, finds them all scavenging, and tentatively expects the sixth to scavenge too is doing legitimate science, with a far smaller sample and a predicate that has essentially no history of use. And one final pair I can't reconcile: I would happily project that all crows in Australia are black from Australian observations, but I would refuse to project that all emeralds are green if found north of the equator and red if found south of it from northern observations, even though both hypotheses build a spatial restriction into the predicate. My discomfort with the emerald case is presumably that I don't believe geography affects mineral color — but that's background knowledge, and if I'm allowed to lean on background beliefs about which regularities are real, I'm no longer sure what work the riddle is doing.

So: is there a principle here? The obvious candidates — entrenchment of the predicate, natural-kind structure, dependence on background knowledge — each seem to save some of my verdicts and lose others, and I'd like you to be explicit about which ones. Please give me either a single principle or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough to make predictions; tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle overturns, and why those are the right ones to give up; and tell me what it implies for the two cases I keep going back and forth on. First, a hypothesis using a brand-new predicate introduced by a well-confirmed theory, which makes a surprising prediction and receives two confirming observations — projectible or not? Second, the version of grue where the crucial date has already passed, so that the hypothesis has in fact been tested on both sides of the divide and stands already falsified — was it not projectible all along, and does the answer to that threaten to make projectibility depend on when we happen to be standing in history? If my verdicts turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, I want to know that too, and to know which intuition about grue itself is doing the most damage.
