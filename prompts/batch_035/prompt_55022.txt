Two members of my reading group, one a committed Everettian and one broadly sympathetic to the Copenhagen tradition, spent an evening arguing about the measurement problem, and I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange rather than a general survey of the interpretations. The exchange went as follows. (1) The Everettian opened by charging that Copenhagen is not a theory but an incomplete recipe: it assigns two different dynamics to the wavefunction, unitary evolution and collapse, and offers no principled physical criterion for when each applies, since a measurement is just an ordinary interaction between a system and a macroscopic device. (2) The Copenhagenist replied that this is a demand for a purity physics never provided anywhere else: classical mechanics also took the observer's perspective for granted, and it is legitimate to treat collapse as an effective, coarse-grained description of interactions involving macroscopic degrees of freedom, provided the predictions come out right. He added that Everett buys exact unitarity only at the price of a multiverse that no observation from inside one branch could ever confirm or refute. (3) The Everettian answered that the branches are not an extra posit: they are what the universal wavefunction already contains once one refuses to truncate it arbitrarily, and decoherence explains, from within the unitary dynamics, why measurement-like interactions suppress interference, which converts Copenhagen's postulate that outcomes appear definite into something more like a derivable effect. (4) The Copenhagenist countered that decoherence at best explains the appearance of classical behavior, not definite outcomes: the total state ends up containing every term, so singling one out and calling it the actual world is labeling, not physics. He then pressed on probability: if Everettians count branches, the Born weights look pasted on, and if they refuse to count branches, they have removed the very thing probability was supposed to attach to. (5) The Everettian responded that branch counting is a non-starter because the theory nowhere supplies a fact of the matter about how many branches there are, decoherence yields a continuous spread of components with no natural integer attached, and that the serious Everettian route to the Born rule is decision-theoretic: an agent living through branching who cares only about how well things go for the future copies can be shown, under constraints of rational preference, to weight each branch by squared amplitude, so the probabilities come from the structure of rational decision rather than from counting worlds. (6) The Copenhagenist retorted that this concedes the point: an objective statistical pattern within one world is the sort of thing physics has always meant by probability, whereas weights whose grip on reality is only that a branch-dweller would be irrational to disregard them is something else entirely; he also charged that even setting probability aside, Everett is extravagant in a way that a genuinely parsimonious theory, such as one keeping a single world with particle trajectories guided by the wavefunction, is not. (7) The Everettian closed with parsimony being about the number and complexity of laws and basic entities, not about the size of the state a simple law generates, so a theory with fewer laws but a vast state space beats one with extra dynamical structure bolted on, and he noted that the rival single-world theory pays for its definite trajectories with a preferred foliation of spacetime that relativity gives no license for. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the seven moves, say whether the point as made is correct, partly correct, or confused, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who comes out more right overall, and identify anything both of them overlooked that a referee should have raised. Do not adjudicate the interpretations themselves beyond what the moves require.
