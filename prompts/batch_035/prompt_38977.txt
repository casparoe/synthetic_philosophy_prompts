As a retired physical chemist who has lately turned to philosophy of science, I find myself puzzled by a pattern I once took for granted in my own field's history: the calculus arrived independently with Newton and Leibniz, and natural selection was announced almost simultaneously by Darwin and Wallace. Two interpretations of such coincidences compete. On the first — call it the "ripe fruit" view — the accumulation of prior work makes each discovery nearly inevitable, so who happens to make it is of minor consequence, and priority disputes are largely about conventions for distributing credit. On the second — the "indispensable individual" view — a simultaneous invention shows only that several people could have done it, not that anyone would have soon; on this view the identity of the discoverer matters, since the timing and even the shape of a theory can depend on who formulates it (Newton's and Leibniz's calculi, for instance, looked rather unlike each other). Please compare these two views, and tell me which better explains simultaneous invention and which better captures what matters about who made a discovery. Structure your answer in four parts, in this order: "The two views in brief" (one short paragraph); "The case for the ripe fruit view" (about 150 words); "The case for the indispensable individual" (about 150 words); and "My verdict" (3–5 sentences), ending with a judgement on whether the inevitability or contingency of a discovery should affect the credit we award its discoverer.
