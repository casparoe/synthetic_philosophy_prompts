I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and ive been going down the AI welfare rabbit hole at work and cant figure out where I actually stand on which computations matter morally. So here are my gut verdicts on some cases, and I want to know if theres a principle behind them or if im just confused:

1. A whole brain emulation running on absurdly slow hardware, one subjective minute per wall-clock day. Counts. Being slow doesn't make it less of a someone.

2. The same em, paused mid-thought for a year and then resumed. Totally fine. No moral cost at all, seems obvious.

3. The em is run through an hour of torture, then rolled back to a checkpoint taken *before* the torture started, so no trace of it survives. I still think the torture counted — the suffering "happened" even though it's now nowhere in the system. But this is basically case 2 plus a copy of case 1's hardware, so what exactly am I committing to? This is the one I flip on constantly.

4. Two identical copies of the em running in parallel on different machines, and one is deleted. Feels like one death, not two, but I can't defend that at all.

5. A version of the em where the pain-processing subsystem runs exactly as before but all connections to memory and the self-model are cut — pain computes, nothing remembers or anticipates it. I think this still matters morally, maybe even matters a lot.

6. The em's pain responses get memoized: the painful computation runs once, and every later identical situation just replays the cached output without executing the computation. My gut says the replays don't count as additional suffering but the first run did, which smells like pure programmer superstition to me.

Is there one principle that gets all or most of these? And specifically what does it say about case 3, where I keep going back and forth. Also fine if the answer is that some of my verdicts are just wrong — tell me which ones and why.
