I'm in a small reading group on reasons and rationality — we just did the whole internal vs external reasons debate, Williams' stuff and the pushback against it — and I've started keeping track of my friend Maya's verdicts whenever cases come up, because I'm trying to reverse-engineer what rule she's actually running and it's genuinly hard. Her verdicts so far. A guy who keeps smoking but knows all the facts and would keep wanting to smoke under any amount of clear reflection: he has a pro tanto reason to keep smoking (he enjoys it), just more reason overall to quit. A man in a blind rage who wants to smash his noisy neighbour's window: no reason to do it at all, not even a token one, because the urge would evaporate if he cooled down and thought it through. A woman totally indifferent to custard apples who would adore them on first taste: she has a reason to try one. And a committed egoist who has no false beliefs, has thought everything through, would never regret a thing, and cannot be moved by any amount of reflection to care about anyone else: he still has a reason to return the wallet full of cash he watched a stranger drop, even though keeping it would cost him nothing and he'd feel fine about it forever.

Here's what's bugging me. A Williams-style internalist rule — reasons exist when sound deliberation from your actual motivations could get you there — handles the smoker and the custard apple woman cleanly, and can be stretched to cover the egoist if you make sound deliberation elastic enough (if he really understood what he was doing, he'd come to care...). An externalist rule — reasons can exist with no motivational hook at all — handles the egoist and the custard apple easily, and covers the window-smasher by saying the reasons against just swamp whatever reason the rage generates... except Maya said there's not even a pro tanto reason there, so the externalist has to say the rage-based desire is somehow defective rather than merely outweighed. So maybe neither rule fits all four verdicts as stated, or maybe I'm mis-describing the rules. There's also a third option people in the group keep pushing: a hybrid where most reasons need a motivational hook but there's a moral floor that doesn't.

Next week's case, which I suspect matters more than it looks: a man with an irreversible neurological condition that completely fixes his motivational set — reflection cannot create, strengthen, weaken, or remove any motivation he has; no therapy, no drug, nothing changes it; deliberation only helps him pick means to ends that are already locked in. All his goals are self-regarding, he's perfectly rational, and there's a charity he could donate to at a small cost to those goals. Predict Maya's verdict on whether he has a reason to donate. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record (including ones I haven't thought of — e.g. rules where any psychological route, not just rational deliberation, suffices, or where it's what he'd endorse after the fact that matters), say what each one predicts here and where exactly they come apart, give me your best guess with your actual reasons, and then tell me what further case would genuinely split the surviving rules apart. I have a feeling the whole internal/external debate turns on cases like this more than the papers admit, so if you think my framing smuggles something in, say so.
