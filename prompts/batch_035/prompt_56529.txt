At a reception after a departmental colloquium, two of my fellow graduate students got into an argument about Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and it ended without resolution. I'm not taking sides myself; I want someone to referee it move by move.

The exchange ran as follows. Dana opened by claiming Weber's thesis is the paradigm case for culture functioning as an independent historical cause: anxious Calvinists, unable to earn their salvation, looked for signs of election in disciplined work and reinvestment rather than consumption, and capitalism first took root where that psychology was widespread — a pattern no purely materialist story predicts. Marcus replied that Weber established only a correlation: values are downstream of material conditions, since populations adopt and keep the norms that pay off, so wherever early capitalist activity was profitable, ascetic norms would spread regardless of what the theology said; Weber offered regional co-occurrence and some remarks about Benjamin Franklin's advice on industry, but no comparison that could rule out the arrow running from the economy to the ethic. Dana countered that a reflection story cannot explain the fine grain: predestination doctrine produced a distinctive salvation anxiety, and an economy optimizing for thrift would get by just as well on confident, worldly norms — the compulsive, guilt-driven quality of ascetic capitalism is exactly what interest alone does not supply, and that came from the specific content of the belief. Marcus answered that Weber himself concedes the religious foundation eventually fell away, leaving habits that read as pure utility once the theology is gone; if the "spirit" can detach and float free, the doctrine looks like scaffolding — causally involved at one stage and then discarded, which is what you'd expect if competitive pressure were the real driver — and even at the start, nobody needs to actually believe in predestination, since conformity to a strict community's expectations yields the same conduct, so "values as causes" collapses into "institutions channel incentives," with Weber adding a mysterious extra layer. Dana closed by saying that calling the doctrine scaffolding concedes the causal point, since something whose removal changes the outcome is a cause; and if culture operates by shaping expectations within a community, that is not a rival to Weber's thesis but a mechanism for it — Weber never claimed beliefs push matter around by some extra-physical route, only that what counts as "paying off" is itself fixed by what people value, so material incentives are never self-selecting.

Please adjudicate the dispute as a numbered list of exactly five items, one for each step of the exchange, in order. Each item should be a sentence or two stating who is right at that step and why, flagging any place where a participant argued well for a weaker conclusion than she or he claimed. Do not include any introduction or conclusion. Please answer in American English.
