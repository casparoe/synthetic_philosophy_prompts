I'm a PhD student in HPS and my supervisor keeps pushing me toward Lakatos, but I've got an argument (built out of bits of Feyerabend plus my own reading of the 1970 paper) that seems to show the methodology of research programmes issues no norms to anyone, and I can't locate the flaw. A friend in my cohort says it's sound and that Lakatos is just Feyerabend in a wig. Help me out.

(1) The whole point of the methodology is to say when theory change is rational: prefer a progressive research programme to a degenerating rival -- degenerating meaning ad hoc patches, no confirmed novel predictions, the theory just absorbing whatever data arrives.

(2) But whether a programme is degenerating is only decidable over a long stretch of its history, because a degenerating phase can be followed by a progressive one. This isn't hypothetical: Prout's hypothesis (elements as compounds of hydrogen) looked degenerating for basically a century, since atomic weights stubbornly refused to come out integral (chlorine at 35.5 was the killer), before isotopes revived the programme. Copernicus's programme generated, as far as anyone could tell in 1600, no confirmed novel predictions at all, and it was still worth keeping alive until Galileo and Kepler. Newton's programme carried the Mercury perihelion anomaly for over half a century, patched with the never-observed planet Vulcan, and no one thinks physicists should have walked away in 1870.

(3) So at any moment t, a scientist sitting in the middle of one of these troughs cannot know whether their programme is degenerating or just quiet. The track record up to t underdetermines the verdict.

(4) And Lakatos seems to concede this: he allows that it can be rational to keep working on a degenerating programme, that one may rationally gamble on a revival, and he denies there is ever a point at which abandoning a programme is compulsory. That was the whole point of distinguishing himself from naive falsificationism.

(5) Put (3) and (4) together: at no time does the methodology give anyone a reason, as opposed to a mere permission, to prefer one programme over another. And since every progressive/degenerating verdict is provisional in the same way, the rationality of any actual switch -- Lavoisier over phlogiston, Einstein over the ether -- was invisible at the time it happened. The methodology ratifies it only in retrospect, and could equally have ratified staying put, had history gone the other way.

So the conclusion is that the methodology of research programmes gives working scientists no action-guiding norms at all, only after-the-fact descriptions -- which is just Feyerabend's anything-goes slogan with a fifty-year delay built in.

I have the feeling the slide is somewhere between (3) and (5), maybe from can't know for certain to has no reason, or else inside (4), from Lakatos's permission to keep working to flat indifference between programmes. But I can't nail it. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? For what it's worth, the historical cases in (2) feel rock solid to me and I'd much rather keep (2) and (4) and sacrifice (3), but I can't see how to deny (3) without smuggling in a hard abandonment rule Lakatos never wrote.
