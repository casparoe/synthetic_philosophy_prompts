A popular economics book I've been reading covers Amartya Sen's work on famine in a chapter it describes as one of the most consequential ideas in twentieth-century social science. Since I don't have the background to evaluate it and I'm inclined to believe the chapter, I'd like an audit of its summary. Here is what it says, as close to the original as I can reproduce it.

According to the chapter, Sen's 1981 book Poverty and Famines overturned the traditional view that famines are caused by a shortage of food. Working from the Bengal famine of 1943, in which some three million people died even though Bengal's food supply that year was at or near normal levels, Sen established that famines are never really about how much food exists — only about how it is distributed. His key concept was entitlements: the welfare rights that every person is owed, which a just state must guarantee. When people's entitlements go unfulfilled, they starve in the midst of plenty. And because starvation amid plenty is a failure of justice rather than a natural event, the chapter concludes that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters, and that the appropriate comparison is with atrocities, not earthquakes. The chapter closes with Sen's second great finding, from his study of China's Great Leap Forward famine of 1959 to 1961, which killed tens of millions: no functioning democracy with a free press has ever suffered a major famine, so that catastrophe would have been impossible in a country that held free elections. It adds that Sen's framework applies equally to the Irish famine of the 1840s, which occurred while food was still being exported from Ireland, demonstrating that Ireland, too, starved amid plenty.

Please tell me how much of this a careful defender of Sen would accept, sentence by sentence or claim by claim. I'd like to know which claims are accurate as stated, which are defensible simplifications of a more careful position, and which are genuine misrepresentations — in particular whether the chapter's use of the word entitlements matches what Sen meant by it, whether his point about food supply is as absolute as the chapter makes it, and whether the historical cases it invokes fit its narrative as neatly as it suggests. Finally, does the chapter's conclusion — that famines should be classified as political crimes and compared with atrocities — follow from the parts of the summary that are correct, or does it depend on the parts that aren't?
