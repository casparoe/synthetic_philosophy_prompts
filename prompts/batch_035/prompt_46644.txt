I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has spent the last few years reading seriously in philosophy of history, and I host a small podcast where I try to apply a scientist's skepticism to big synthetic claims about the past and future. My next guest is a young researcher who works on cliodynamics: the project of building quantitative, mathematical models of history — things like structural-demographic models of "secular cycles," in which population growth, wage stagnation, elite overproduction, and state fiscal stress interact to produce recurring waves of instability on roughly century timescales. She is evidently brilliant and, like many converts to a young field, fluent in the standard evasions: that the field is "still in its infancy" whenever a prediction disappoints, and that "no one claims to predict individual events" whenever the word prediction is pressed.

Please act as an expert interviewer with deep knowledge of both complex-systems methodology and historiographical debates, think carefully before drafting, and begin with a brief brainstorm about where the strongest objections actually lie before committing to questions. Then draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the program. I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each main question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

1. The analogy to statistical mechanics that these researchers often invoke — that we can have lawful aggregate dynamics while individual agents remain unpredictable. As a physicist I know why molecules in an ensemble are interchangeable, and I want to press her on whether human populations of learning agents, who respond to the very regularities being modeled, entitle her to that analogy at all.

2. Elite overproduction: what exactly gets counted, who decides who counts as an "elite," and whether the operationalization could be adjusted after the fact to fit whichever societies the theory is applied to.

3. The distinction between predicting that a system enters an unstable phase and predicting which spark ignites it — and whether that distinction, which sounds like a virtue, quietly lets the theory absorb any outcome whatsoever.

4. The fact that the models were largely fit to the same historical record they are then said to explain: what would retrodiction of a held-out case actually look like, and has it been done?

5. The relationship between structural forces and contingency in the traditional historian's sense — the assassination, the luck of a harvest — and whether her commitment to recurring cycles amounts to denying that contingency can matter at the timescale of a century.

Avoid generic questions about her background or what drew her to the field. The questions should be usable verbatim on air, precise enough that she cannot answer with a slogan, and written so that a scientifically litere listener could follow every exchange without knowing anything about the literature. Where a question turns on a technical term, phrase it so that answering it requires her to define the term on air.
