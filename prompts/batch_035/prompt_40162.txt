I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and for the last few the thing I keep circling back to is the value-learning problem: how you'd infer what a human wants from how they behave — the inverse reinforcement learning picture, or something near it. I've decided my own views on it are stable enough to write up, so before I do, I want a consistency check. Here is the full set of my commitments:

(1) Deeds over words. Talk is cheap; behavior is the primary evidence of what someone wants. When a person's stated values and their actual choices come apart, I weight the choices, and I'd want any preference-learning system to take the same default.

(2) Humans are systematically buggy. Hyperbolic discounting, status quo bias, scope insensitivity of the willingness-to-pay kind, where people say they'd pay roughly as much to save a few thousand birds as to save hundreds of thousands. So a straight revealed-preference read of raw behavior would often infer "values" that are really defects in the generating process rather than anything the person is for.

(3) There is nevertheless a determinate fact of the matter about what a person really wants — roughly, what they would endorse given full information, unhurried reflection, and no passing craving in the driver's seat.

(4) That fact is recoverable, in principle, from behavioral data alone, at the limit of a lot of observation. The person's testimony is not an independent input; it is just more behavior, to be weighted like any other data.

(5) Two verdicts on cases. My uncle has smoked for forty years, has made several genuine attempts to quit, complains about the habit constantly, and says he wants to be a non-smoker; when stressed, he lights up anyway. I say he wants to quit, and the lighting-up is akrasia, not evidence about what he wants. My friend says she values serious reading and a considered life, but every evening, without exception, the hours go to algorithmic short video; I say she prefers the feeds, whatever her self-description.

(6) A person can hold two real, conflicting preferences at once. My uncle really does want a cigarette in the moment and really does want to be a non-smoker; both are genuine preferences, neither is an error to be explained away, and a good theory of what he wants simply contains both.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I want an audit, not a survey: trace what my commitments commit me to, and price it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.
