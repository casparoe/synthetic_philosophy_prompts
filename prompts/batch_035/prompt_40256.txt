A former classmate of mine, now writing a dissertation on the structure of epistemic justification, refuses to attach any label to her position and will not tell me where she lands on the regress problem. She has, however, given firm verdicts on a series of cases over the years, and I want to use her track record to predict her verdict on a new case we argued about last week. Help me reason it out.

Her verdicts so far:

1. The narrow circle. A woman believes her newspaper is reliable because its editor says so, and believes the editor is trustworthy because the newspaper says so. Verdict: not justified, and not even marginally — the circularity is fatal however confident she is.

2. The ordinary perceptual chain. A driver believes the road is icy because it looks glossy to him; the look is not supported by any further belief of his, it is simply how things appear. Verdict: justified.

3. The dangling base. Someone believes P because Q, and Q because R, and believes R for no reason at all — not because it appears so, not because it seems self-evident; he simply believes it. Verdict: not justified; the chain transmits nothing downward from nothing.

4. The dense web. A working scientist's beliefs: hundreds of them, heavily cross-linked, with observational anchors in some regions, circular paths throughout, and no single master chain. Verdict: justified — and she did not seem at all troubled by the circular paths.

5. The long single loop. Thirty beliefs, each supported only by the next, the thirtieth supporting the first; no cross-links, no anchors, and the believer cannot tell he is circling. Verdict: not justified — and, she insists, not one bit better than the two-step circle; the loop's length is irrelevant.

6. The infinite chain. Asked to consider as a matter of principle, bracketing psychological possibility, a believer with an endless series of reasons, each supported by the next, no circles and no terminus. Verdict: not justified — in her words, that view mistakes the regress's failure to terminate for its resolution.

The new case, the enclosed web: a woman raised from childhood in a closed religious community holds a vast doctrinal system — history, cosmology, morals — whose parts support one another with real internal rigor. Her perceptual beliefs about daily life cohere with the doctrine, which predicts and accommodates everything she observes, but nothing she observes evidentially supports the doctrine itself; anything that looks like counter-evidence is absorbed by other parts of the web. Stipulate that the system is genuinely coherent by any structural measure — no internal contradiction, no narrow single loop — and that nothing within her epistemic reach has ever given her reason to doubt it. The question we were arguing about is whether her doctrinal beliefs are justified.

Predict her verdict on the enclosed web. Then identify the candidate rules her six verdicts are jointly consistent with — I can see at least two, and I suspect you will see the same two — and say what each predicts here. Tell me which prediction you would bet on and why, which of her earlier verdicts comes under the most strain depending on which way she goes (case 5 strikes me as the pressure point if she says yes, but I may be missing others), and, if her track record genuinely underdetermines the prediction, describe the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules she might be running.
