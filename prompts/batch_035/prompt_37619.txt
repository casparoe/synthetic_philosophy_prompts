I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level course in philosophy of science, and I'm grading take-home midterms from a unit on the philosophy of psychiatry and the human sciences built around Ian Hacking's work on looping effects and his making-up-people thesis.

The exam question was:

"Hacking holds that classifications of people are interactive kinds in a way that classifications of quarks or beetles are not. Explain what this contrast amounts to, what a looping effect is, and what does and does not follow about the status of psychiatric classifications. Illustrate with at least one psychiatric example that Hacking himself has discussed."

One student answered as follows:

"Hacking's point starts from a simple observation. When we classify rocks or beetles, the objects of classification do not change their behavior in response to being classified, whereas when we classify people, they can. A beetle labeled as a new species goes on being the same beetle; a person labeled as having multiple personality disorder may come to act more like the diagnosis. That is what Hacking means by an interactive kind: the kind itself is modified by our knowledge of it. He calls the result a looping effect: the classification changes the people, the changed people change the classificatory practices, and the loop keeps running.

This has a striking consequence. Since the classification alters the very people it classifies, human kinds cannot be stable objects of knowledge the way chemical kinds are, and Hacking concludes that psychiatric classifications are not in the business of discovering facts at all — in this respect his position ends up close to Thomas Szasz's, though it is reached through a study of classification rather than of the medical profession's authority. A human kind is, in the end, whatever a given society's classificatory practices make of it; this is what Hacking calls dynamic nominalism, his name for the view that the category and the people classified come into being together.

The loop must also eventually destroy every human classification it touches: because the people classified keep responding, the category can never remain true for long. Hacking's own example of multiple personality disorder in the 1980s shows this — once the diagnosis became widely known, the number of diagnosed multiples exploded and the diagnosis lost credibility, which is exactly what the looping thesis predicts for any psychiatric label. The lesson is that psychiatric classifications are temporary instruments, destined to be falsified by their own subjects."

Grade this answer. Give it a letter grade and a point-by-point assessment: identify specifically what it gets right, evaluate each of its substantive claims about Hacking's position and about what follows from the looping thesis, and explain how any errors bear on the grade. I care about whether the student has understood the position and its consequences, not about style or polish. Take a definite stance on the grade — do not give me a range, and do not tell me it depends on the rubric.
