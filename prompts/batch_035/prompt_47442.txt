I'm drafting a position statement for a seminar on obligations to future generations and, before I circulate it, want a rigorous consistency check. My commitments are these:

(1) I hold a person-affecting view: nothing is bad, and nothing is wrong, unless it is bad for or wrongs some particular person. Adding extra happy lives to the world is not in itself an improvement, and there is no impersonal moral remainder once effects on people have been fully accounted for.

(2) I analyze harm counterfactually: you harm someone only if you make them worse off than they would otherwise have been, and I take nonexistence never to be a baseline of well-being that could have been better for the person in question.

(3) I am confident that Parfit-style depletion is impermissible. A society that burns through its resources so that everyone born over the next several centuries has a life only barely worth living — where conservation would have produced flourishing lives, though by different people — acts wrongly, and I want to say it acts wrongly toward those future people specifically.

(4) In the wrongful-disability case, where a couple could wait a month and conceive a healthy child but instead conceive now, foreseeing that this child will have a lifelong condition, I judge the couple's choice wrong; I want to say they wrong the child even though they do not harm it, since harm and wronging can come apart. I also insist that this verdict is insensitive to the identity fact: had their timing made no difference to which child existed and they somehow imposed the same condition on whatever child they were going to have, the choice would be wrong in exactly the same way and to the same degree.

(5) I deny any duty to procreate, however happy the resulting children would be, and I deny that parents must select the best possible child among the children they could have: choosing among permissible procreative options for non-maximizing reasons is not a wrong to anyone.

A classmate claims the set is a shambles: that (3) is unmotivated given (1) and (2), since the depletion policy makes no one worse off than they would otherwise have been; that (4) collapses under (2) or else drags in an impersonal standard through the back door via the wronging notion; and that (5) cannot coexist with my insistence in (4) that it is wrong to bring a worse child into being when a better alternative was available, since by parity it should be wrong to bring a merely acceptable one. Audit the set rather than defending or indicting it wholesale: tell me which of these alleged inconsistencies are real, which dissolve on inspection, and what each repair would cost me. Respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, ordered from most to least serious, with no introduction or conclusion; the final item should name the single cheapest revision and state what I would have to give up in accepting it.
