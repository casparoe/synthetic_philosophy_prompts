I've been trying to get clear on what makes moral trade — the idea, associated with Nick Bostrom and with Toby Ord's work on moral uncertainty, that parties with different moral views can each get more of what they morally want by trading concessions — genuinely attractive rather than merely clever. To focus my thinking, I've assembled six candidate instances, and I'd like you to rank them from best to worst as exemplars of moral trade, where "best" means the case in which the idea does the most distinctive work: the outcome could not have been reached by ordinary self-interested bargaining or a mere coincidence of interests, and each side can honestly say it traded away something it cared about morally.

The six:

1. Vote pairing in a US presidential election: a third-party supporter in a swing state swaps votes with a major-party supporter in a safe state, so the third-party candidate's popular total is unchanged while the preferred major-party candidate gains a swing-state vote.
2. A meat-eater pays a monthly sum to an animal-welfare charity effective enough that, on the eater's own moral accounting, the suffering prevented offsets the suffering caused by their consumption.
3. A grand legislative bargain: opponents of abortion accept public funding of contraception (which they regard as merely tolerable) in exchange for supporters of abortion rights accepting a ban after viability, with both sides predicting the compromise will reduce late abortions, which each side counts as a moral win.
4. Intrapersonal trade under moral uncertainty: someone who assigns roughly 60% credence to a person-affecting view of population ethics and 40% to totalism resolves hard cases by letting each view "bid" for her decision, in analogy to how a parliament of moral views would logroll.
5. A trade agreement in which a wealthy country offers tariff reductions to a developing country conditional on humane slaughter standards, framed by its sponsors as trading prosperity for animal welfare across moral constituencies rather than within any single one.
6. My own proposal, which I'd like pressure on: a moral offsets exchange in which citizens who are about to violate a norm they themselves endorse — flying despite believing it wrong — buy offsets from citizens who were about to fly anyway but agree not to for a fee, with the exchange certifying the counterfactual.

Please give the ranking, a short justification for each placement, and — separately — a flag on which of the six you think fail to be moral trade at all, as opposed to moral licensing, an additionality failure, or side payments laundered through moral vocabulary. I'd also like your honest view of whether my criterion (distinctive work beyond self-interested bargaining, plus honest moral sacrifice on both sides) is the right test or whether it stacks the deck; if you think the ranking is underdetermined by the concept — if moral trade is too thin an idea to order these cases — say so plainly rather than forcing an order. And if you think one of my descriptions rests on a confusion about how the scheme would actually work, tell me that directly rather than accommodating it.
