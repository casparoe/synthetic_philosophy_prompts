I work for a mid-sized state government's office of continuity and resilience, and my least favorite part of the job has become the word "collapse." Roughly twice a month somebody — a consultant with a slick deck, a worried planner from the ag department, someone forwarding a think tank report — sends us a claim that some system we nominally steward is "collapsing": the groundwater in the eastern agricultural basin, the rural hospital network, the sardine fishery, one time even the DMV. The director wants to know which of these to treat as emergencies and which belong in the ordinary planning queue, and right now we have no principled way to answer; we sort of vibe based on how scared the writeup sounds. From what I've read, the actual collapse literature is basically a long argument about whether "collapse" even names one kind of event: the Maya case where the southern lowland cities emptied out while cities in the north carried on, Tainter's story about diminishing returns on complexity, the people who argue Easter Island wasn't so much a collapse as a transformation people survived, and the skeptics who say "collapse" is mostly a label outsiders stick on a change they happen to dislike. I want the usable parts of that debate turned into a screening procedure my program officers can run in under an hour per claim, with no field data, producing a one-paragraph verdict for the director.

So what I'd like is a set of questions in order — things like: what exactly is the unit claimed to be collapsing (a population, an institution, a way of life?), over what timescale, is the system ending or reorganizing into something cheaper, is the claimed loss one of complexity or one of wellbeing, who benefits from the word "collapse" being used here — each with a note on what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates early with "this isn't a collapse framing, recast it as something else," and where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because it's a values question no checklist can settle. I expect the Maya/Easter Island-style cases, where the answer hinges on what you think the society even is, will land in that last bucket, and I want the procedure to be honest about that about itself. It should also distinguish between claims that are wrong but urgent, and claims that are right but slow, since those need different routing, and it should flag that a "no" on the collapse framing doesn't mean nothing bad is happening.

Then run it on three claims I actually recieved last year: (1) a consultant says the eastern basin aquifer is being drawn down and regional agriculture will collapse within a decade; (2) a local op-ed calls the closure of four rural hospitals in two years "the collapse of rural healthcare"; (3) the sardine fishery, where the stock is genuinely way down but the fleet just switches target species every few years anyway. People in my office tell me the procedure will probably refuse the collapse framing on claim 2 even though the op-ed author is obviously right that something bad is happening — good, I want to see how the procedure handles handing back an answer I politically won't like. And please tell me flat out what the procedure cannot do: my guess is it can classify a claim but can't forecast whether a real collapse is likely, and if that's right, say so instead of smuggling a prediction in.
