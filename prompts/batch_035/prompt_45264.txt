I teach a 90-minute seminar on rational choice for fourteen upper-level philosophy students who have already covered extensive-form games and common knowledge. Design an in-class centipede game, run in three conditions, that makes them feel the backward induction paradox as a live problem about reasoning rather than a theorem to recite.

The conditions should be: (1) a known-length chain of alternating "take/pass" decisions with a growing pot, where each pair first completes a written strategy audit — stating, node by node from the last node backwards, what they would do there and why — before playing for real; (2) the same chain but with a publicly announced 5% chance at each node that an "accident" ends the game and destroys the pot; (3) a round against a facilitator-run bot that plays the backward-induction strategy without exception. Payoffs in points, no real money, with a small neutral prize so the stakes are non-trivial.

During play, participants should record, after every move, a prediction of the opponent's next move and their confidence in it; these prediction sheets are the raw material for the debrief.

The debrief must map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the paradox: how the strategy audit made the induction feel airtight; what an observed pass should do to a player's beliefs about their opponent, given that the theory said that node would never be reached; whether the accident condition rescues passing by giving rational players a reason to continue at every node except near the end; and why playing the bot felt different from playing a person whose reasoning one might influence. Include rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff table for each condition.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — participants passing out of politeness or confusion rather than strategy, students who already know the game defecting immediately and contaminating the data, pairs colluding quietly instead of competing — and be frank about which feature of the paradox no classroom version can reproduce: presumably the idealized common knowledge of rationality that the backward induction argument actually presupposes, which real, visibly hesitant opponents never give you. Say how the facilitator should supply that missing feature without simply lecturing.
