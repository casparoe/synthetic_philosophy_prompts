my research group has been stuck in the same fight for a year now and our PI suggested we do one of those adversarial collaboration writeups before we keep talking past each other. I want you to draft the joint statement we'd theoretically produce at the end of it.

The two camps: on one side, the realists about interpretability -- the people who think that when sparse autoencoder methods find a "deception feature" or a steering vector that makes a model refuse to answer questions about code, they've found something that is really in the network, prior to and independent of our probing tools. On the other side, the deflationists -- people who think "features" and "circuits" are interest-relative descriptions of our own making: the network has activation vectors and weights, full stop, and everything above that level is a clustering we impose because it helps us predict and steer, not a joint in nature. (Nobody in the second camp doubts the engineering value of the work, to be clear -- they just deny it's discovery.)

Here's what I want in the document:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can actually sign. Include the empirical stuff they agree on -- steering works better than you'd expect if features were pure artifacts, probes often generalize out of distribution, the same kinds of features show up across models trained on different data, etc. -- but also the conceptual agreements, e.g. about what would NOT count as evidence (like a human finding a nice story to tell about some weights).

2. The disagreements, each phrased so the camp it's about would accept the wording without wincing. I'm specifically interested in where they fall on the crux of what I've been calling retrain stability: if you retrain the same architecture on the same data with a different random seed or data order and get behaviorally near-identical models whose internals line up poorly with each other, what does that show? The realists in my group think the jury is genuinely out on that one and I want it recorded honestly rather than smoothed over.

3. For each crux, a concrete experiment or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. This is the part I care most about -- I've watched too many interpretability arguments where both sides claim the same result vindicates them after the fact.

4. Flag whether any part of the dispute is purely verbal, and if so which part. I have a suspicion that "the model has representations" means at least three different things in our meetings and we've never once untangled them, but I could be wrong.

Format: numbered agreed claims first, then a table (or table-like structure) for the disagreements with columns for the crux, the realist position, the deflationist position, and the agreed-upon test. Keep the language neutral enough that neither side would say the framing rigs it for the other guy. And please don't have them converge at the end -- the whole point of this document is to make the residual disagreement exact, not to paper over it.
