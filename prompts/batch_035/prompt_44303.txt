I'm a science journalist putting together a longread about the species problem, specifically about how it bites in conservation reporting: when a population gets delisted as endangered because geneticists decide it's "just" a hybrid swarm or not "really" a distinct species, that has legal and funding consequences, so the philosophers' usual move of shrugging and saying it's all convention doesn't really cut it. Before I start drafting I want to get my own head straight on which species concept I should actually be leaning on. Could you rate these on a scale of 1-10 for how much you'd trust them to settle a genuinely hard case — something like a wolf–coyote hybrid zone, or a ring species where the ends of the ring don't interbreed even though everything adjacent does: (1) the biological species concept (interbreeding plus reproductive isolation), (2) the phylogenetic species concept (smallest diagnosable cluster, or the smallest clade it makes sense to name), (3) the morphological one, and (4) the ecological one, where a species is a lineage holding down its own adaptive zone?

For context on where the people around me land: my editor's instinct is that the BSC is the real thing and everything else is bookkeeping, whereas a palaeontologist I interviewed thinks the BSC is useless the moment you deal with fossils or asexual lineages, and says species are more like a lineage with a beginning, middle and end — a thing you can only really individuate retrospectively, a bit like you can only say where a wave broke after it's already broken. And a programmer friend of mine reckons species are basically just clades with a popularity cutoff, which sounds like a joke but I suspect he's half-serious. Where would you slot those views into your rating too, and — the bit that matters for me — which concept would you actually use if you had to file by deadline with a yes-or-no on whether the population in question counts as a species?
