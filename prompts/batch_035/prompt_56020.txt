I'm a novelist, and I've found myself stuck on a problem that I suspect is actually a philosophical one. In my own drafts I keep noticing that every character I write is, in a sense, a Frankfurt case: I have guaranteed, before the reader ever meets her, exactly what she will do at the turning point of the book; there is no possible world of my novel in which she does otherwise. Yet I write her, and readers respond to her, as though her choices are hers -- as though at the climax she could have held back, and is to be credited or blamed for not doing so. The counterfactual intervener was never offstage; he was the author all along, and he never had to intervene, because he wrote the person who would not need intervening on.

I've been told this connects to the old debate about the principle of alternative possibilities, and to Harry Frankfurt's counterexamples in which a person who would have been manipulated, had they been about to choose otherwise, chooses on their own and never learns the intervener was there at all. I want to understand whether my craft problem is a version of that philosophical problem, and the text I keep circling is Sophocles' Oedipus Rex. Read the play through this lens for me. The prophecy functions, it seems, like Frankfurt's guarantee: given the oracle, there is no branch of Oedipus' life on which he does not kill his father and marry his mother, and yet at the crossroads and in Jocasta's bed he acts from his own temper, his own desire, his own decisions. The audience, meanwhile, knows the ending from the opening scene -- we occupy the intervener's seat, watching a man deliberate inside a story whose outcome is already fixed for us, and we still feel the horror as his. Where does that dramatic irony track the structure of a Frankfurt case, and where does it trade on something the philosophy would distinguish -- say, between an outcome being guaranteed and an agent being causally determined? And is Oedipus responsible, on the Frankfurt-inspired view, in a way he wouldn't be under simple fatalism, or does the play refuse to let that distinction do any work -- the plague punishes him as a polluter regardless, the world of the play apparently indifferent to the structure of his agency?

Some requests about how you handle this. I'm not a philosopher and I want to come away with an accurate map of the territory, so please keep separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested: in particular, whether Frankfurt's cases actually refute the principle of alternative possibilities or merely relocate the disagreement, and what the strongest surviving objection to them is -- I believe there is a family of objections charging that such cases presuppose the very alternative they claim to eliminate, and I'd like to understand it rather than just have it cited. Please don't present any one resolution of that dispute as if it were the consensus. And since my interest is ultimately practical -- how to plot a character whose freedom the reader can feel inside a story that forecloses it -- tell me frankly where the play resists this reading, and what tragedy does for the question that the journal articles, in your judgment, do not.
