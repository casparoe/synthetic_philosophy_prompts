I teach a seminar on the moral foundations of promissory obligation, and every year the discussion collapses into the same tired ping-pong between "it's a social practice, end of story" and "it's all about the promisee's expectations and reliance." I've become partial to a third kind of view and I want you to argue for it, properly: that the binding force of promises comes from the promisor handing the promisee a kind of standing authority over the promisor's future conduct. Not just creating an expectation, and not just inviting reliance — the promisee now gets to demand that the promisor perform, gets to rebuke her for breach, and gets to waive the requirement at her discretion. On this view a promise is a transfer of normative control, in the same family as handing someone the keys to your car, even though nothing remotely like property in your actions changes hands.

To keep you honest, the argument has to earn its keep on four fronts:

1. It should explain the release asymmetry — the promisee, and apparently only the promisee, can cancel the obligation by releasing the promisor. You can't release someone from their duty not to lie to you about a third party, and you can't forgive a debt of gratitude into nonexistence, so why is promising different? Your view should make this difference look principled rather than stipulated.

2. It has to handle coerced promises. If someone puts a gun to my head and extracts a promise, I can't reasonably be expected to rely on it, and yet we're still uneasy saying flat-out that nothing was promised at all. Say what your view commits you to here, and don't be sly about it.

3. It has to say why the authority, once given, can't just be treated like a liability you price into the deal — why can't the promisor renege, pay damages, and call it a day, like an ordinary contract breach? What exactly stops the moral residue from being fully dischargeable by compensation?

4. It should anticipate the obvious objection that this makes promising into a weird voluntary self-enslavement, and either show why that objection misses something or bite a bullet with a straight face.

One last thing, and this is the part my students always fumble: don't quietly collapse your view back into Scanlon's expectation/reliance story. If the only bad thing about breaking a promise were frustrated expectations, the release asymmetry would make no sense — you can't "release" someone from making you disappointed. The transfer of authority has to do real explanatory work in the argument, not just sit there as a label. No bibliography, no citations, just the argument, worked through.
