I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy trying to settle on a dissertation topic, and I've narrowed it to the problem of induction — Hume's original challenge and Goodman's new riddle in Fact, Fiction, and Forecast. My background is in formal epistemology (confirmation theory, Bayesian modelling, some learning theory and computability) rather than in metaphysics or the history of empiricism, and my department leans the same way, so I'd want a dissertation I can actually make progress on with formal tools. The trouble is that the literature is enormous and I can't tell which strands are genuinely live as opposed to settled, politely abandoned, or being kept on life support by exegesis. One senior colleague tells me the grue problem was effectively dissolved decades ago: fix a prior, let Bayesian conditioning do the work, and the residual discomfort about which prior to fix is just the ordinary pre-formal stuff of theory choice, no more troubling in the grue case than anywhere else. Another says precisely the opposite: Bayesianism merely relocates the riddle into the prior, where it becomes invisible rather than solved. I don't yet trust myself to adjudicate between these verdicts, and some of the classic lines of response — entrenchment, natural kinds, the reading on which Goodman's riddle is only Hume's in fancy dress — look unpromising for someone with my toolkit even if they are philosophically substantive.

What I want from you is a defended agenda rather than a survey. Which open questions in this area most deserve three years of a formally minded student's effort, and in what order? Candidates I can see include: whether there is a non-circular criterion of projectibility that discriminates green-like from grue-like hypotheses without presupposing a privileged vocabulary; whether the formal learning-theoretic programme, on which simplicity is measured by efficiency of convergence rather than description length, vindicates the Bayesian answer, revises it, or exposes a hidden assumption they share; whether encoding the green hypothesis's advantage in a prior is viciously circular or benignly pragmatic; and whether the new riddle is genuinely independent of the old one or a special case of it — but please add candidates I haven't listed if you think they matter more. For each question, tell me what makes it worth the years: how much downstream work turns on it, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and how tractable it is using probability and learning theory rather than metaphysics or Quine scholarship. Tell me which of the others depend on it, which are downstream and can wait, and which are tempting but would change little even if solved. Finally, say what developments over the next few years — a decisive impossibility result, a new formal framework, the field's usual drift — would move items up or down your list. Please do not give me a reading list; name the arguments and their load-bearing assumptions and assume I can find the papers.
