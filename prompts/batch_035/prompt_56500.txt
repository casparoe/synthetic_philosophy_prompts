In March 2008, New York Governor Eliot Spitzer — who as Attorney General had built a national reputation on morally charged prosecution, breaking Wall Street corruption and at least two prostitution rings — was revealed as a client of the Emperors Club escort service and resigned within days. Please analyze this episode through the literature on standing to blame and hypocrisy, as a careful mapping rather than a punchline. First, who are the blamers, and does standing matter for them: the diffuse public, the press, political rivals, prosecutors who had been his colleagues and targets? Second, what exactly is the hypocrisy supposed to add to the wrongdoing over and above the underlying conduct — is it a distinct wrong (moral fakery, trading on a reputation for integrity), or merely a discount on his earlier blame of others? Third, does the standard machinery apply at all: if blame is a two-person practice in which the blamer stakes her own character, and if a hypocrite's criticism can remain sound even once his standing to press it lapses, what happens when the blame is collective, anonymous, and laced with schadenfreude? Conclude honestly about what the case teaches the theory, including the possibility that it teaches nothing — that standing to blame was built for face-to-face reproach and simply misdescribes mass public condemnation.
