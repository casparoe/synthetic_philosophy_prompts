For a population-ethics workshop I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my considered positions before we meet, and I'd like a consistency check before I send it out, since I suspect I've assembled these from different teachers over the years and they may not form a stable package. Here is what I hold. (1) Where two possible worlds contain exactly the same people, the one with the greater total of wellbeing is better, and the distribution of wellbeing between those people is irrelevant. (2) Mere addition: adding lives that are worth living, while leaving everyone else exactly as well off as before, never makes a world worse. (3) The repugnant conclusion is false, and not merely unproven: an enormous population whose members each have a life barely worth living is genuinely worse than a small population of excellent lives. (4) The all-things-considered betterness relation over worlds is complete and transitive, and I refuse to escape through vagueness, parity, or claims of borderline incomparability between worlds. (5) The sadistic conclusion is equally false: it can never be better to add a few lives of negative welfare than a larger number of lives of positive welfare, which is why I reject critical-level views. (6) A sufficiently larger population at somewhat lower but still high quality can be better than a smaller, higher-quality one — quality and quantity trade off at the margin — so I also reject lexical thresholds of any kind. (7) The procreation asymmetry: adding a life of negative welfare to an otherwise unchanged world makes that world worse, while adding a life of positive welfare does not make it better. My ground for (7) is Narveson's old line that we should be in favour of making people happy but neutral about making happy people, and I admit I find that ground attractive in its own right.

Please go through these and tell me whether the set is jointly tenable. Where you find tensions, rank them by severity rather than treating everything as equally fatal, separate the tensions that are merely apparent from the ones that are structural, and for each real one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list. I know the standard escape routes from the repugnant conclusion exist and I want to know which of my commitments, if any, actually forecloses them and which escape I have left myself. Be specific about which pairs of theses are doing the work against each other, and if there is a single revision that would clean up the most, say which one and why, rather than suggesting I loosen everything a little.
