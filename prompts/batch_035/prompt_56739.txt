Every year the epistemology comp has a question on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, and every year it's basically "state the argument and raise one objection," which the students can now answer half-asleep since old exams float around. I want to replace it with something that actually tests whether they understand the moving parts. The argument I'm referring to: no condition is luminous, where a condition C is luminous for a subject iff, in every case in which C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains; Williamson's case is feeling cold, and the trick is that feeling cold fades off gradually, that one's confidence can't discriminate between cases only slightly different, and that in cases relevantly similar to one where you know you're cold you may be cold without being able to know it — so the chain unravels until even clear cases of feeling cold stop being cases of knowing you feel cold.

Here's the hook I want to build the question around, taken from an actual exchange in my seminar. One student, call her the repairer, said: fine, in borderline cases I don't know that I'm cold, but luminosity never needed to cover borderline cases — whenever I'm clearly cold, I can tell I'm cold, and if "in a position to know" is read as covering clear cases, the argument leaves luminosity basically intact. Another student, the presser, replied that this concedes everything: if "clearly" is doing any real work, then either there's a sharp, knowable boundary between clearly cold and unclearly cold cases — which the very same margin-for-error reasoning says we can't have — or the boundary is unknowable, in which case the repairer can't herself tell, in any given clear-seeming case, that she's on the clear side of it, so what has been repaired is a condition that does none of the anti-skeptical work luminosity was supposed to do. The repairer's last word was that this just shows the anti-skeptical work was over-advertised in the first place.

So: write me one essay question for a 90-minute exam in which candidates adjudicate exactly this dispute — is the retreat to clear cases a legitimate repair of luminosity, or a relabeling that either reintroduces the sharp-boundary problem or delivers something trivially weak — and, crucially, make them say how their verdict bears on the KK principle, i.e. on whether knowing that you feel cold puts you in a position to know that you know it. The question itself should restate the anti-luminosity argument informally in a paragraph, so it's self-contained and candidates are tested on the adjudication rather than on recall of the book. Then give a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, 20 marks total, with the marks each point is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically mess each one up. One thing I've been burned by before with model answers from colleagues: they present contested stuff as if it's consensus. So in both the model answer and the marking scheme, please keep a clean separation between what's basically settled among people who work on this (e.g. the structure of the argument, what the safety/reliability premise is claiming) and what is genuinely up for grabs (e.g. whether that premise is actually true of knowledge, whether there are "cognitive home" exceptions, whether some restricted form of KK survives the argument) — and have the marking scheme award something for a candidate who flags that a point they're making is contested rather than presenting it as orthodoxy. Please don't put anything in quotation marks that you can't vouch for as verbatim — describe positions in your own words.
