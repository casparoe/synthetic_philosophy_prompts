I'm a second-year PhD student applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship, and I need a two-page research proposal (roughly 1,200 words). My half-formed idea concerns the relationship between Keynes's Treatise on Probability and his later economics. The standard narrative is that the young Keynes held a logical theory of probability — probabilities as rational degrees of belief fixed by the evidence — and that the Keynes of the General Theory quietly abandoned the whole framework when confronted with fundamental uncertainty about the long-term future, for which no probabilities could be formed. I suspect this continuity-versus-repudiation debate has been framed badly, because the Treatise already contains two doctrines the standard narrative forgets: that some probabilities are non-numerical, not even comparable with each other or with any number, and the notion of the weight of an argument — how much of the relevant evidence one actually has, as opposed to how that evidence balances. My hunch is that the later Keynesian uncertainty about the long term is best read as low weight combined with non-comparability, not as the total absence of probability, and that this reading both vindicates continuity and yields a live philosophical payoff: a principled account of how agents should act under low-weight evidence that neither collapses into modern imprecise-probability frameworks nor licenses mere guesswork.

Turn this into a proposal. It should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain what the familiar positions (the repudiation reading, and the continuity reading as it is usually defended) leave open; and set out the method — what I would actually do in each quarter, and why the right method is textual work combined with philosophical reconstruction rather than pure exegesis or pure formal modelling. It should also address one wrinkle that worries me: if the subjectivists were right that the logical interpretation of probability failed on its own terms, does my reading leave Keynes's later economics resting on a broken foundation, or does the weight idea survive the collapse of the framework around it? Say what a positive result would look like — what the account of action under low weight would actually deliver, for instance for the rationality of relying on conventions and rules of thumb — and be honest about the failure modes, including the possibilities that weight is too loosely characterised in the Treatise to do the work I want from it, and that the project sprawls into an unmanageable history of twentieth-century probability theory.
