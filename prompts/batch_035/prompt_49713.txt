Each winter I run the tab room for a regional circuit of student parliamentary debate, and each winter the same fight breaks out about how we should aggregate judge ballots into tournament standings. This year I finally read up on why the fight can never fully be settled, and I've been asked to give a forty-minute session on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem at the coaches' off-season meeting — because I once said in an email that the theorem proves "no voting rule is safe from tactical voting," and the phrase has been circulating among the coaches ever since, mostly as a club to end arguments ("it's all manipulable anyway, so let's just keep the points rule we have"). The audience is about thirty coaches and tab-room staff: sharp, practically minded, allergic to math for math's sake, and several of them have opinions about Borda count that they formed before knowing it had a name. Chalkboard only, no handouts, no theorem-proofing.

I understand the theorem well enough myself; what I need is a plan for the explanation. Please work out the following:

First, the order of ideas. What has to be established before the theorem's statement can even be heard correctly? I suspect the definition of strategy-proofness is where it lives or dies — my coaches will hear "manipulable" as "the outcome is always determined by tactics" rather than "there exists some configuration of ballots in which some judge could gain by submitting a ranking that isn't her sincere one." Where in the sequence do I fix that, and what comes before and after it? Should the theorem be stated early and then re-stated correctly at the end, or only stated once, at a precise moment?

Second, the carrying example. Should I use the generic voters-and-candidates setup, or build the whole session on our own tab room — say, five judges ranking four debaters under our actual points rule, walking through a concrete ballot set where one judge gains by ranking insincerely? What does the carrying example need to have, and what must it avoid? One complication: our rule breaks ties using the chair's ballot, and I can't tell whether that's a gift for the explanation (a living, breathing partial dictatorship in the room) or a distraction the audience will seize on to avoid the main point.

Third, the misconception map. Besides the "everything is tactics" reading, I anticipate: that "dictatorship" in the theorem means something like a strongman rather than the precise formal condition; that the theorem is just Arrow's theorem wearing a different hat (two of the coaches conflate them already); and the reaction that the whole thing is obvious — "of course people can lie about their preferences." Which of these do I pre-empt in sequence, and which do I let surface on their own so the room can watch them get dismantled? Is the "it's obvious" reaction actually a misconception, or is there a sense in which the theorem's content really is that a trivial-looking observation has an ironclad universal form?

Fourth, the escape route. I'm torn about whether to close with domain restrictions — single-peaked preferences and the median-voter result — as a constructive "here's where the theorem's grip fails." Does that give the coaches a genuine insight, or does it hand them a rationalization ("debater preferences are probably single-peaked enough") for ignoring the theorem entirely?

Fifth, what I refuse to explain: I've decided the proof is out, and I assume I should also stay away from what happens when rules are allowed to select sets of winners rather than a single winner, and from anything about how hard it is to compute a worthwhile lie — but tell me if I'm cutting something the audience actually needs.

Finally, one check: a question I can put to the room at the end — ideally one that involves them trying to manipulate, or prove unmanipulable, some simple rule on the chalkboard — such that their answer tells me whether they understood the theorem or merely found it quotable.
