I'm a backend engineer who somehow got roped into running our team's little "ideas over lunch" thing at work — about 12 people, mostly devs, one hour, zero philosophy background in the room including me. I want an activity that makes them feel a problem I keep running into with committee decisions: whether you get a different group verdict depending on whether everyone votes directly on the conclusion, or everyone votes on seperate premises and the conclusion gets mechanically derived from the majority-premise answers. My idea: stage a "release committee" that has to decide whether to ship a feature to production. The verdict follows from three yes/no premises (e.g. tests pass in staging, no unresolved privacy concerns, rollback plan exists) plus a decision rule the committee adopts itself, like "ship only if all three hold." Run it twice: once with a straight up/down vote on shipping, once with premise-by-premise majority voting and then apply the rule. I'm betting the two procedures can disagree on the verdict and I want the group to be genuinely surprised by that. Design the whole thing — materials, ballots, some scoring or stakes that make people care but no real money, timeline, and a debrief where they figure out for themselves why the disagreement happened and argue about which procedure is actually the "right" one. Two twists I want built in: (1) let the committee first argue about and vote on the decision rule itself (unanimous vs majority on premises), since that changes everything; (2) partway through, quietly hand a couple of participants cards instructing them to vote strategically on a premise they privately think is fine, purely to force or block the final outcome — and tell me what to do if the rest of the room never notices the manipulation. Also be straight with me about what this game can't show: I gather there's an actual literature on this (something called the discursive dilemma / judgment aggregation) but none of us have read it, so tell me which part of the real problem the activity just can't reproduce and I'd have to just explain at the end.
