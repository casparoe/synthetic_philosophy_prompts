I'm a postdoc presenting next month to our department's philosophy of physics reading group, which is dominated by two committed Bohmians — one of whom knows Bell's collected papers practically by heart and tends to open every discussion by asking which version of the theorem we're even talking about — plus an Everettian who thinks the entire Bell dialectic presupposes definite single outcomes and therefore never gets off the ground, and a handful of agnostic graduate students who mostly want to know what they're licensed to tell their intro students. My thesis: Bell's theorem does not establish nonlocality, if locality means anything like the absence of superluminal dependence between physically separated events. What the theorem establishes is the failure of the factorizability condition, and the standard decomposition of that condition (the one everyone attributes to Jarrett, with the terminology refined by Shimony) splits it into two logically independent parts: a genuine locality condition — no dependence of the local outcome probabilities on the distant setting — and an independence condition on distant *outcomes* given a complete specification of the past, which I want to argue is not a locality demand at all but a demand about the completeness of the state description. Orthodox quantum mechanics violates only the second, which is why it preserves no-signalling; the kind of nonlocality that would actually threaten relativistic spacetime structure requires violating the first, and that is purchased only by deterministic hidden-variable programs like Bohm's. So the punchline I want to defend is that the popular slogan — Bell's theorem proves the world is nonlocal — is at best a renaming of the incompleteness the EPR argument was already gesturing at, and that Bohmians who celebrate the theorem as vindicating their ontology's nonlocality have the moral backwards: their theory pays the locality price precisely because it insists on the extra completeness that would make the outcome-independence condition hold.
