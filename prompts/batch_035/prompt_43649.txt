I'm a philosophy of science PhD student who's been invited to co-lead a ninety-minute session with an interpretability research group at a lab. The audience is a dozen ML engineers who spend their days finding "features" and "circuits" in models, and who uniformly hold what I'd call naive realism: of course the network has representations, that's what we're discovering, the philosophy question is empty. My co-host disagrees and thinks the whole discourse of "representations" is a projection of ours onto matrix multiplications — she'll push that line hard. What I want from you is not the content but the plan: how to structure the explanation so that the question becomes live for this audience, rather than either dismissed in the first ten minutes or turning into a turf war.

Here's the specific arc I need you to design. Decide what has to come first — my instinct is that the audience can't even hear the question until they've been forced to distinguish between "our tools reliably detect structure" and "the structure we describe is really there in the model rather than partly a fact about our descriptive scheme," but you should make your own call about the opening move and defend it. Then pick the one carrying example for the whole session: I'm inclined to use something like a steering vector that flips the sentiment of generated text, since everyone in the room has used one, but the example needs to be chosen so that it cuts both ways — showing the realist something real and the instrumentalist something projected — so tell me what the example must contain and what it must lack, and whether you'd pick a different one. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll actually arise: I predict "the direction either exists or it doesn't, this is just physics," then "so you're saying our tools don't work," then a slide into whether the model really understands meaning, which is a different and worse conversation. For each misconception, place it in the sequence — the point at which I should have already defused it, or, if it can only be addressed later, why the earlier material had to come first. Finally: one question I can pose at the end of the session whose answer would show they've grasped the distinction between detecting structure and settling ontology, and a short list of what I should openly decline to cover at this level (I assume consciousness is on it; tell me what else).

One constraint on your answer: commit. Don't hand me a menu of possible openings, examples, or framings with the pros and cons of each — make the definite choices, give the reasons, and build the session around them. If you think my instinct about the opening move or my preferred example is wrong, say so plainly and put your alternative in the plan, not alongside it. And where the philosophy genuinely underdetermines the pedagogy — if some choice really is a coin flip — make the call anyway and flag it in one sentence, rather than routing the decision back to me.
