Retired chemist here — 35 years in a spectroscopy lab, now I run a little adult-ed philosophy of science group at the library. 12 people, mostly fellow retirees, 90 minutes, comfortable with arithmetic but no formal training. We've just hit decision theory and I want them to feel why Ellsberg-type choices are supposed to be a problem, not just nod along to the reading. Can you design an in-class game for that? I can bring jars and colored balls and do actual draws, but no real money — some substitute payoff like candy or a points tally with a silly prize at the end. The sequence matters: they should place their bets first, then we work out on the whiteboard that their pattern of choices cant be squared with any single probability assignment for the unknown urn, and only then do I name the thing. So dont give away the name up front, and nothing that looks like a lecture before the choices are locked in. I'd also want a debrief mapping what happened onto the formal point — which principle exactly got violated and why that stings for expected utility theory — plus a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (contrarians, the one guy who's definately seen it before, people treating candy stakes as a joke), and an honest bit about what the activity can't reproduce: one run is an anecdote, not the systematic effect, and I suspect small stakes change behavior, though I'd take your word on that. If you can build in a moment where the group ends up arguing about whether their behavior was even irrational in the first place, that'd be gold — I want a fight, not agreement.
