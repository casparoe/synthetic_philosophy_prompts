My sister and her husband went through IVF last year — they had nine embryos, implanted the second one, and my niece Mia is now seven months old. At Thanksgving dinner my sister and my cousin (a philosophy PhD student) got into it about the remaining embryos, which are still frozen. My sister said something offhand like part of her feels bad that Mia's siblings are sitting in a freezer, and my cousin pounced on that. He said it's the wrong way to see it: on the standard view (he attributed it to a philosopher named Kripke) none of us could have come from a different sperm and egg — if a different embryo had been implanted, Mia would simply never have existed, and some other child would be sitting in that high chair. So the frozen embryos aren't Mia's siblings who lost out, and choosing embryo #2 wasn't choosing one child over another; there was never a possible world where one of those other embryos gets born and it's still her.

My sister pushed back hard. She said the cousin's view makes genetics do all the work of identity, but she's quite sure Mia could have been adopted at birth by a family in another country, raised speaking Portuguese, with completely different values and memories, and still be Mia — same origin, radically different life. So origin clearly isn't essential to everything about her; it's just essential to her body's recipe, and why should the recipe be the essence rather than the psychology? The cousin answered that origins are what individuate things: a table built from a different tree just is a different table, and a person grown from a different zygote is a different person — upbringing differs in degree, origin differs in kind. Then my sister, who'd been reading around on this, landed what she thought was a killer point: identical twins come from the *same* zygote, which splits early on — so if a person just is the zygote they came from, one zygote would somehow be two people. The zygote must be more like an acorn that could have forked, and whatever Mia is, she isn't the zygote. The cousin waved this off as a technicality about very early embryos, but he didn't really have an answer, and things got a little heated before dessert.

They both texted me afterwards asking for a verdict and honestly I can't give one. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who's right about the modal claim that Mia couldn't have existed without that particular sperm and egg, about the adoption/Portuguese-speaking counterexample, about the table analogy, and about the twinning point? And does any of it actually settle the question my sister started with — whether the frozen embryos should be thought of as possible children in some sense, or as not-possible-Mias that could never have been her? Also tell me if both of them are missing some option neither considered. And since this seems like exactly the kind of question where intuitions run ahead of arguments, be upfront about where your own reasoning is shakiest: what would someone have to show you, or what argument would someone have to produce, for you to flip your verdict on the central question?
