I teach an introductory aesthetics course, and in a few weeks we take up the debate over aesthetic testimony: Hume's reliance on the verdicts of qualified critics, Kant's insistence that a judgment of taste be grounded in one's own immediate response rather than someone else's report, and the contemporary dispute between pessimists, who deny that one can come to know a work is good on another's word, and optimists, who think testimony can at least initiate a process that ends in genuine first-hand appreciation. As a test case, I want to use a film my students all know well: Pixar's Ratatouille. Please read the film through the aesthetic testimony literature, with the mapping kept disciplined rather than loose.

Consider at least the following strands. First, Anton Ego: for the dining public his reviews are aesthetic testimony with teeth — his word closes restaurants — and the film presents his authority as taste ossified into power. The plot's hinge is that his verdict changes only when he eats the ratatouille himself and is carried back to a childhood kitchen by the taste, a response no critic's sentence could have transmitted; his climactic review then becomes a meditation on the critic's position rather than a transmission of his knowledge. Second, Gusteau's motto and Ego's eventual retraction of his own reading of it: he writes, "Not everyone can become a great artist, but a great artist can come from anywhere." Does the film thereby concede that testimony — the cookbook, the motto, the reputation — cannot identify genius in advance, only certify it after the fact, and what does that concession do to Hume's picture of critics as a reliable standard of taste? Third, the case the film raises and never resolves: the diners love the food while ignorant that the cook is a rat, and the film plainly treats their delight and their horror at the provenance as equally sincere. Can testimony about non-aesthetic facts legitimately bear on aesthetic judgment, and does the film take a stand? And fourth, the scene in which Skinner tastes the same dish before Ego does and rejects it: what does the film think separates the competent palate from the discerning one, and is that difference something a critic's word could ever convey?

I would like a reading faithful in both directions. Where does the film dramatize something the philosophical literature misses — for instance, the way a critic's testimony can manufacture the very standards by which it is later exposed as unreliable? Where does the philosophy catch the film cheating, as when it equates the critic's reformation with a single unmediated, memory-laden bite? Please close by saying what the film adds to the debate over aesthetic testimony that argument alone does not, and indicate which scenes you would have my students rewatch before the seminar.
