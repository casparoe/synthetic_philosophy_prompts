I lead the appraisal unit at a regional transport ministry. Before any major project goes to the minister for approval, my analysts must produce a probabilistic estimate of its final cost, and where relevant its demand. We have been shifting from bottom-up engineering estimates toward what the planning literature calls reference class forecasting: rather than building the project up from its parts, you place it in a class of completed comparable projects and use that class's distribution of outcomes. The trouble is that nothing tells us how to pick the class, and the choice moves our answers by billions. For a proposed metro line, one analyst wants all urban rail projects worldwide; another wants only rail projects in countries at our income level; a third only projects completed in the last fifteen years, on the ground that construction practice has changed. Each class yields a different distribution, and each analyst can tell a plausible story for theirs.

Please turn what philosophy of statistics actually knows about the reference class problem into a procedure my analysts can run in a working day, using a spreadsheet and no statistical training. It should specify: what makes a candidate class legitimate (enough members, genuine exchangeability with our project, outcomes measured the same way across the class); an ordered routine for generating, pruning, and where necessary blending candidate classes; what to do when every defensible class is small; and what to do when the class's overruns were driven by a feature our project itself has — so that the bad news in the class is not noise to be averaged away but precisely the thing we should heed. Mark clearly the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person, because the question is one of values rather than statistics. Then say how to present the result to the minister, including what to say when the reference class estimate and the bottom-up estimate disagree badly.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases: the metro line above; a fixed bridge-and-tunnel link replacing a ferry crossing, for which the only roughly comparable projects are a handful of famous ones; and a rural road upgrade, a project type so common that the class is enormous but consists mostly of projects far smaller than ours. In at least one case the procedure should give an answer my ministry will not like — say so plainly. And be honest at the end about what the procedure cannot do, in particular whether any procedure of this kind can dissolve the choice of reference class rather than merely discipline it.
