I'm a high school senior taking AP Biology, and while reading ahead for fun I came across a popular science book my uncle lent me. In one chapter the author gives a capsule history of what he calls the "levels of selection" fight, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I go around repeating it at the dinner table. He writes it roughly like this: For most of the twentieth century, biologists naively assumed that natural selection acts for the good of the species, and they happily explained everything from animal altruism to limited egg-laying as adaptations that keep the population healthy. In the 1960s this cozy view collapsed when Dawkins proved that the gene is the only real unit of selection, since genes are the only things that pass intact from generation to generation while bodies are throwaway vehicles built to carry them. On this view group selection was decisively refuted and no serious biologist accepts it anymore. What looks like animal altruism is really genetic selfishness: a worker bee sacrifices herself because her genes are literally using her to help copies of themselves in her sisters, and the same trick explains every apparent act of self-sacrifice in nature. He concludes that once you see organisms as lumbering robots built by immortal genes, the whole debate about levels of selection simply dissolves, because there is nothing left for selection to act on except genes.

Something about this bothers me. My textbook still talks about selection acting on phenotypes and populations, not just genes, and I've gathered that Dawkins was responding to people and ideas that came before him rather than single-handedly demolishing a monolith. But I don't know enough history to say where the book is cutting corners. Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a working evolutionary biologist would sign off on, which parts are defensible simplifications, and which parts are genuine distortions? In particular, I'd like to know whether "gene" is doing the same job in all the sentences where it appears, whether "no serious biologist accepts group selection" is even true today, and whether the author's grand conclusion really follows from the parts of his story that are accurate. I'd also appreciate it if you could explain the difference between a gene being the thing that gets copied and a gene being the thing selection acts on, if there is one, since I suspect that distinction is where the book is playing fast and loose.
