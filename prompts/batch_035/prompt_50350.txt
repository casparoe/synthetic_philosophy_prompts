I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has spent the past few years working through the philosophy of science in earnest, and I have become preoccupied with the debate over reduction and emergence — in part because my former colleagues deployed "nothing but" rhetoric with such confidence for so many decades. I would like you to write a satirical piece for a collection I am assembling.

Please write an Onion-style satirical news article — headline plus roughly 550 words — reporting that the administration of a mid-tier research university, having concluded that chemistry is "nothing but" physics, has formally dissolved its department of chemistry and reclassified its faculty as implementation staff reporting to the physics department. The university in question should be invented, and all quoted persons should be invented administrators and academics; no real institutions or living individuals, please.

The humor must come from getting the philosophy of science right, so that a philosopher of science laughs rather than winces. Specifically, the piece should work in several of the following, executed accurately: the provost promising to deliver Nagel-style bridge laws between thermodynamic quantities and statistical mechanics by the end of the fiscal year, and quietly reclassifying the wetness of water as "a figure of speech pending reduction"; an administrator wondering aloud why, if multiple realizability is real, the university cannot simply outsource molecular bonding to whichever department realizes it most cheaply; a dean citing causal exclusion as grounds for removing "redundant" higher-level explanations from the university's explanatory portfolio, on the grounds that the physical level is already doing all the causal work; a budget analyst treating supervenience as a staffing model; and a chemist's objection that the physical microstates and boundary conditions are individually vast and unspecified, so that the reduction, even where it succeeds in principle, is not usable for anything the department actually does. Somewhere in the piece, an emeritus faculty member should point out that the whole episode is an administrative misreading of a famous 1972 essay by a physicist arguing that successively higher levels of science are not merely applied versions of the levels beneath them; the administration should respond by applauding him for his commitment to interdisciplinarity.

Please satirize the practice of reductionist rhetoric — the habit of treating "nothing but" as if it were an argument — and the administrative opportunism that exploits it, rather than satirizing reductionism itself in strawman form. The administrators' stated position should be recognizably the respectable version of reduction (the old unity-of-science picture in which higher-level theories are, at least in principle, derivable from lower-level ones), so that a card-carrying reductionist reads the piece as an attack on crudeness, not on their view, and can still laugh. The emergentist rejoinders should likewise be the good ones — the appeal to unpredictability-in-principle, to weak emergence in complex systems, to the autonomy of special-science explanations — deployed naturally, not lectured at the reader.

Include at least one quote from a "concerned" philosopher of science on the faculty, and end the piece without the administrators suffering any ironic comeuppance that resolves the underlying question. A reader who knows nothing of this literature should finish the article having felt, without being told, the genuine issue: what, if anything, is actually lost when one science is absorbed by another — and whether the answer depends on anything more than how far away the promised derivations turn out to be.
