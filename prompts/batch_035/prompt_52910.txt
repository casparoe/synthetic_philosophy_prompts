I'm writing a response paper for a graduate seminar on Benacerraf's dilemma, and my TA has proposed a reconstruction of the epistemological prong that I now suspect proves far too much. Here is the argument as I have it:

(1) A true belief counts as knowledge only if its truth is non-accidental: there must be some explanation of why the belief-forming method that produced it tends to get questions of that kind right, rather than landing on the truth by luck. (This is meant to be the charitable, causation-free version of Benacerraf's epistemological constraint, along the lines Field suggested when he argued that the plain causal theory of knowledge is refuted by our knowledge of the future.)

(2) On platonism, mathematical objects are abstract: necessarily existing, outside space and time, and causally inert.

(3) The complete causal explanation of any mathematician's mathematical beliefs adverts only to neural development, training, blackboards, and social practice — never to the numbers or sets the beliefs are about.

(4) If nothing about the fact that p contributes to the explanation of why S believes p, then S's believing p while p is true is a coincidence, so condition (1) is not met.

(5) Therefore, if platonism is true, no one knows any mathematics.

(6) But now run (4) on other cases. The fact that there are no unicorns contributes nothing to the causal origin of my belief that there are no unicorns, which was produced entirely by picture books and testimony. The fact that the sun will rise tomorrow cannot contribute anything to today's belief, since future facts do not cause present ones. By (4), neither belief is knowledge — which is absurd.

(7) So (4) is false, the challenge to the platonist collapses, and the epistemological prong of the dilemma never gets off the ground.

I have three problems. First, I cannot locate the flaw: (4) looks like a harmless reading of (1), yet (6) shows it ruins the argument. Second, my TA insists there is a principled disanalogy — for the sunrise and unicorn cases there is a causal story about why our methods are reliable, and for mathematics there is not — but every attempt I make to state the disanalogy seems to smuggle causation back into (1), which just reinstates the original dilemma. Third, I am not sure whether the right move is to reject (4) outright or to accept it and abandon (2), taking the argument as a proof of some nominalism or structuralism instead.

Tell me which step I should reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs the platonist; then tell me whether the disanalogy my TA wants can be made precise without reintroducing a causal constraint, and whether it is principled or merely an ad hoc rescue. If it helps to know my priors: if forced, I would rather give up platonism than give up (1) or my knowledge of the sunrise, but I want a full price list before deciding.
