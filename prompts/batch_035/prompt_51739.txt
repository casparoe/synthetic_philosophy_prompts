My philosophy of science reading group has this recurring problem: two of our members have been going at each other for over a year about scientific realism, and it's gotten to the point where everyone else just tunes out. One of them is a pretty straightforward scientific realist -- he leans on the no-miracles argument, says the predictive success of mature theories would be an unexplained coincidence unless those theories were at least approximately true. The other one thinks the pessimistic meta-induction is close to a knockdown: physics has cycled through phlogiston, caloric, the ether, and plenty of other positings that were successful for a while and then got dumped, so induction over the history of science itself tells us our current theories are probably headed for the same fate, and "approximately true" is a phrase doing a lot of unearned work. The realist has retreated to selective positions at times -- saying only the load-bearing or "working" posits need to survive, or that what gets retained is structure, like Fresnel's equations carrying over into Maxwell's theory -- but the pessimist says that's just gerrymandering after the fact, drawing the line where the history happened to be kind to you.

They've finally agreed to try the adversarial-collaboration thing: one document they'd both actually sign. Draft it for us. I want (1) the claims both can accept, including about the historical record itself -- that past theories were genuinely successful by any reasonable success criterion, and that some mathematical structure did survive theory change -- since even the pessimist shouldn't deny those; (2) the precise formulations of their disagreement, with each side's own position stated in wording that side would endorse, so the realist gets the no-miracles point put the way he'd put it and the pessimist gets the meta-induction put the way she'd put it; (3) the cruxes, and I suspect at least one is whether the selection-effect story (that we only remember the successful theories, so no further explanation of success is needed) is enough to deflate the no-miracles argument, and another is whether the working-posit / idle-posit distinction can be drawn prospectively or only retrospectively; and (4) for each crux, some case, argument, or audit they'd both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance -- e.g., if they agree to treat the history of science as data, using a success criterion fixed before looking at what survived, the realist should predict a high retention rate for load-bearing posits and the pessimist a low one, and I want those predictions pinned. Also flag whether any part of the residual dispute strikes you as merely verbal (my bet is the "approximately true" vs. "empirically adequate" thing at least partly is), and if so, say which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and keep the tone like something two people who've argued for a year could actually co-sign without feeling sandbagged -- no strawmen, no hedged mush either.
