I'm taking a second-year course on political philosophy, and this week we discussed whether the world has become less violent. After the seminar, two students, Marcus and Yuki, fell into an argument I couldn't referee, and I'd like a careful adjudication of it.

The dispute started when Marcus claimed the best-supported answer is yes: measured per capita, deaths from war and homicide have declined substantially over the long run, and per capita is the right measure because what matters to each person is the risk that person faces. Yuki objected that per capita rates let a growing population hide a rising absolute toll: a world of ten billion with a hundred thousand violent deaths a year is not more peaceful than a world of one billion with the same number, just because the risk is spread thinner — the dead are no less dead, and total victims seem like what morality should track. She added that the standard datasets also exclude structural violence — deaths from poverty, famine, and denied healthcare, which plausibly exceed direct killing — so the optimistic answer is partly an artifact of what gets counted.

Marcus replied that once violence includes every bad outcome traceable to social arrangements, the term loses its meaning: on that definition, the decline thesis becomes unfalsifiable in either direction, and "violence" collapses into "injustice" or simply "bad outcomes," which is not what the question was asking. Yuki's answer was that this shows there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent: the answer is a function of the metric chosen, and the choice of metric is a political act that decides the question in advance. Marcus conceded that metrics are chosen, but insisted that there are facts of the matter relative to each specified metric, and that the remaining disagreement about which metric is morally most relevant is a substantive normative question one can still reason about — which is different from there being no answer at all.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, in the order the moves were made: say who is right about which specific point, flag where either of them overstates, identify anything both missed, and give an overall verdict on who came out more nearly right.
