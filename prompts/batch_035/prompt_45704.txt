So I'm a novelist, and I've gotten stuck on something that turned out to be more philosophy than craft, and I'm hoping you can tell me whether the thing I've noticed has actual names attached to it — right now my notes are a mess of phrases like "the collapse kind," "the ugly kind," and "the parasite kind."

Here's the situation. Several characters in the book I'm writing lean on the move where somebody justifies a moral rule by asking what would happen if everyone did the thing. You know the move. "What if everybody made promises they never intended to keep?" But while drafting argument scenes I realized the move doesn't come in one flavor, it comes in at least three, and I can't find the seams between them.

Flavor one: the reply is that the practice eats itself. If everyone made promises with zero intention of keeping them, the word would stop doing any work — nobody would beleive it, so making one wouldn't get you anything, so there'd be nothing left to misuse. The act becomes impossible, not just inconvenient. (Collapse kind.)

Flavor two: the reply is that the world keeps running but becomes one you couldn't want. If everyone decided to never lift a finger for a stranger in trouble, the machinery of daily life doesn't break — promises still function, queues still form — its just a world where, next time you're the one in trouble, nobody comes. Nothing logical contradicts; it's a world you couldn't stomach choosing. (Ugly kind.)

Flavor three is the one giving me trouble. If everyone cheated on their taxes, the act of cheating wouldn't become impossible — lying to the tax office works fine as an act no matter how many people do it. What breaks is the background: no revenue, no tax office, nothing left to lie to. But that's not the same as flavor one, is it? In flavor one the thing destroyed is the very tool the maxim uses (the promise). In flavor three the tool (the lie) survives; it's the stage it was built on that goes. (Parasite kind: it only works because it stays rare.)

So, questions. Does this three-way split have established names, or do any of the individual flavors? I have a feeling I'm brushing up against something old and systematic — the "what if everyone did it" move smells like Kant to me, but I only know him at the t-shirt level — but I don't want a label, I want the actual carving: which cases go in which bucket, and what to do with the ones that could fall either way depending on how you describe the act. Is "cheating on my taxes when I feel like it" one act, or just a species of "getting money by whatever means work"? Because if the description is up for grabs, it looks like the bucket a case lands in is up for grabs too, and that seems like it should bother somebody besides me. If the split is real but unnamed, coin me terms and give me boundary cases a novelist can actually put in dialogue. Please don't hand me a name and call it a day — I need to know where the edges are, since my characters are going to fight about exactly those edges.
