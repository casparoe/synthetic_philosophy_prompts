I teach a mid-level undergraduate course on the philosophy of science, and in two weeks I have a single seventy-five-minute session, whiteboard only, on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. My students have had a semester of probability but no philosophy: they can compute distributions fluently but have never asked what licenses a probability assignment in the first place, and most arrive assuming that probability is just mathematics, so no genuine controversy about it can arise. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the material yourself. Specifically, I want the following. First, the order the components must come in: what has to be in place before I can even present the chord problem as a paradox rather than as a puzzle with a hidden typo — in particular, how precisely the principle of indifference needs to be stated beforehand so that the chord constructions count as a refutation of something rather than as three uninteresting answers to three different questions. Second, the carrying example: I'm drawn to leading with the cube factory scenario, where a factory produces cubes and you are told only that the side length is between 0 and 1, since indifference over side lengths and indifference over volumes generate incompatible uniform priors over the same interval; tell me whether that or the random chord problem should carry the session, what the chosen example must have and must lack, and whether the other should appear at all, and if so, where. Third, the misconception map: I anticipate students concluding that the whole thing is ill-posed and therefore vacuous, or that the various constructions simply correspond to different physical randomization procedures, so indifference was never violated, or that appeal to symmetry obviously restores a unique answer — tell me which misconceptions to expect, in the order they will surface, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. Fourth, one question or task I can pose in the last ten minutes that would reveal whether students understand the threat the paradox poses to the idea that ignorance alone can generate probabilities, rather than merely following the geometry. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I assume measure-theoretic reformulations, maximum entropy methods, and any full resolution program — and how to frame that exclusion honestly so it does not feel like evasion. Please answer in American English.
