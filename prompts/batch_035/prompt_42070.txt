Compose a satirical press release, headline plus roughly 600–750 words, in which a technology company announces the end-of-life of its phenomenal consciousness product line. Give the company a plausible name of your own invention — something in the register of an enterprise-software or cognitive-services firm. The piece should read as deadpan corporate PR throughout: boilerplate about listening to our users, a migration FAQ, a grandfathering clause for legacy subscribers, and at least one quoted customer testimonial.

The humor must come from getting illusionism about phenomenal consciousness right rather than caricaturing it, and to that end the release should be written from inside the illusionist position without ever quietly abandoning it:

- The company maintains that the phenomenal properties in question were never actually shipped: what customers received was a functionally discriminative state wrapped in an introspective gloss that misrepresents it as bearing an intrinsic qualitative character. No sentence may tacitly presuppose that the qualia exist — the release cannot, for instance, say that users will "lose the redness of red" — though it may report, with perfect corporate calm, that users continue to judge otherwise, and it should treat those persisting judgments as intended behavior rather than as evidence of a defect.
- The FAQ should field the obvious questions in a way that quietly dramatizes the real pressures on the position: the burden of explaining the illusion without assembling it out of the very ingredients the view denies; the awkwardness of discontinuing a product line the company simultaneously claims was never manufactured; and the awkward fact that, from the first-person side, nothing about the change will be noticeable — a fact the PR spin can only lean into ("Will I notice the difference? No. That has always been our design goal.").
- The customer testimonial should come from a self-described realist subscriber who insists his copy shipped with actual qualia, and the company's response to him should be patient, courteous, and entirely untroubled — his report of ineffable intrinsic character is, after all, exactly the output the introspective interface was built to produce.

A reader who knows the debate — the distinction between phenomenal and functional properties, introspection as a potential source of error, the problem of how to state the illusion without smuggling in what the statement denies — should find the satire fair, in the sense that it exaggerates something the view is genuinely committed to rather than something its defenders would disown, and should be able to tell what is being ironized without the author winking at them. Close with a corporate tagline that lands in both directions: one that functions as marketing copy, and that a philosopher of mind could read as a thesis about consciousness.
