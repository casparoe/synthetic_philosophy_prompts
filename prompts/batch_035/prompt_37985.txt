At a farewell drinks evening last week I watched an argument I couldn't referee, and it has been bothering me since. Marcus, a mid-level engineer on my team, was leaving for another company and had asked our team lead, Vera, for a written reference. The reference praised him for punctuality, keeping his documentation up to date, tidiness, being pleasant in code review — that sort of thing — and contained not a single word about his technical ability, delivery, or judgment. He didn't get the job, he later got hold of the letter, and at the drinks he confronted her.

The exchange went like this. (1) Marcus opened by saying the letter was engineered to say he can't do the work: praising only the trivial virtues is a way of saying there is nothing else to praise, and she meant it as clearly as if she had written it outright. (2) Vera replied that every sentence in the letter was true and individually complimentary; whatever conclusion the recruiter drew was drawn by the recruiter, and if writers owned their readers' inferences, nobody could safely write anything, since any sentence can trigger any association in somebody's head. (3) Marcus came back that she could have cancelled the reading with one line about his engineering, and chose not to — an omission in a reference is a decision, and decisions communicate; and, he added, if she had written something like "his technical work is weak" she would probably face an HR complaint, so wrapping the same message in implication is worse, not better: sabotage with deniability. (4) Vera said not every omission is a signal — she may have written it in ten minutes on a Friday afternoon, and people are forever reading insults into haste that were never put there; she accepts no responsibility for a conclusion she never intended him or anyone else to draw. (5) Marcus closed by saying conventions are exactly what make her move work: reference letters are read against well-known conventions about what a strong one contains, so she used the convention to send the message while keeping the ability to deny having sent it, and that is a con, not a misunderstanding.

They eventually asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered steps, say who is right, who is wrong, and to what degree, rather than just declaring an overall winner. Along the way I'd like your view on the two questions they kept circling: whether a writer can be responsible for something she never wrote, and whether an unintended reading can still count as something the letter communicated. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — is there a principled distinction available here between what the letter said and what Vera meant by it, and if so, does it settle the dispute or just relocate it?
