Two epistemologists — a committed pragmatic encroacher and a committed epistemic purist — have argued for a year about whether practical stakes can affect whether a subject knows, and have finally run an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they should produce.

The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, covering what they agree on about the shared stock of cases (bank-deposit-style cases where the same evidence is assessed under different costs of error, and lottery propositions, where near-certainty seems not to suffice for knowledge), what any adequate theory of knowledge owes them, and what the dispute does *not* turn on; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the encroacher would accept as a fair statement of encroachment, once in wording the purist would accept as a fair statement of purism — covering at least whether stakes affect knowledge itself, whether they affect only warranted assertion or action, and whether the intuition that the low-stakes bank customer "knows" while the high-stakes one doesn't is a datum to be explained or an artifact to be explained away; and (3) the cruxes, i.e., the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one side, with a test both accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance.

At least two cruxes should engage novel cases they construct jointly rather than the standard ones. One should be the *indemnified inspector*: a quality inspector whose employer fully insures her against every consequence of a wrong call — wrong approvals cost her nothing, right ones gain her nothing — while the downstream costs to the firm of an error are enormous; the test is whether her epistemic standing varies with the firm's stakes, her own (null) stakes, or neither. The other should be a *stakes-blind* variant in which the subject's actual stakes are high but she reasonably believes them to be low, testing whether any encroachment tracks actual stakes, believed stakes, or expected costs of acting on error. For each case, both sides should also register what pattern of lay judgments about the case, if it emerged, they would count as evidence against their own view — and if they conclude that part of their residual dispute is merely verbal (for instance, over whether "knows" semantically encodes stakes as opposed to knowledge itself being stake-sensitive), they should say which part, and why, without using that verdict to dissolve the substantive disagreement.
