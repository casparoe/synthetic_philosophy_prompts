I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and I've been asked to write a short position statement for a workshop on risk assessment in criminal justice. Before I commit it to print, I want an audit of whether my views actually hang together. Please read the following as a set of commitments I intend to defend, not as questions I'm asking about the literature.

(1) Calibration is non-negotiable: if a risk score is deployed, it must be calibrated within every group we can feasibly name — race, certainly, but also intersections like race and age band. If the score says 30%, then roughly 30% of people matching that profile should go on to reoffend, for every such group; otherwise the number is simply a lie, whatever else is true of it.

(2) On the COMPAS dispute, my sympathies are with the error-rate side: a decision process that detains one group at a substantially higher false positive rate than another is unfair, and demanding equalized error rates across groups is the right response, not an optional extra.

(3) I do not believe the underlying base-rate difference is purely a measurement artifact. Self-report and victimization survey data convince me that some residual difference in reoffending rates across racial groups would survive even perfectly clean measurement. I take this to be an unfortunate empirical fact, not anything normatively loaded.

(4) I want nothing to do with randomized-threshold fixes — equalizing error rates by flipping coins near the decision boundary strikes me as fairness theater, and I'd insist that decisions be deterministic threshold rules applied to the score.

(5) Relatedly, I think retraining on arrest records as the target variable is a mistake, since arrests are contaminated by differential policing, and the tool should target a cleaner outcome — though I've never supposed that switching targets would erase the base-rate difference in (3).

(6) Finally, a research-taste commitment: despite believing all of the above, my stated agenda is entirely about constraining and post-processing classifiers, not about the upstream social causes I claim are the real problem.

Act as a senior researcher in algorithmic fairness who has seen many position statements fail exactly this kind of scrutiny. Think through what each commitment actually forces me into before you flag anything. Which pairs of these are in genuine tension, which only look to be, and what exactly has to give — ranked by severity, with the cost of each revision stated, and ending with the single change that would clean up the most. Please also tell me how confident you are in each verdict and single out the point in your audit you're least sure of.
