After a career in laboratory measurement science, I have lately been reading philosophy, and I keep noticing a measurement question about a practice I have met as a patient. In the United States, hospitals send a standardized survey to patients after every inpatient stay, asking how often the nurses were courteous and respectful, listened carefully to the patient, and explained things in a way the patient could understand, with response options running Never, Sometimes, Usually, and Always. The results are publicly reported, feed into reimbursement, and hospitals train staff in specific behaviors believed to move the numbers: introducing oneself by name, sitting at the bedside rather than standing, using the patient's name. Yet the same institutions, and much of the nursing literature, insist that caring is a relationship — context-dependent, built over time, and not reducible to a script. What conception of care does this survey-and-incentive regime actually presuppose: care as a set of discrete, observable behaviors whose frequency can be counted; care as whatever the patient happens to perceive; or something else? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of care the regime rests on, say which rival conceptions it quietly rules out, assess whether its presuppositions are internally consistent or in tension, and explain where the practice is underdetermined enough that several theories of care would license it. Then tell me what would have to change if a relational view of care, of the sort care ethicists defend, were put in charge of the measurement instead. End your reply with a final line consisting of a one-sentence summary of your verdict on whether the regime's presuppositions are consistent.
