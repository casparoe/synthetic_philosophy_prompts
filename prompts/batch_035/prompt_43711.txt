i'm a novelist, about four-fifths through a book set in a high valley where families have grazed sheep on shared pasture for generations, and the grass is nearly gone. i want a sestina to open the chapter, in the voice of an old herder who understands perfectly well what's happening and does it anyway. end-words: grass, one, more, free, mine, gone.

the hard part, and what i actually need from you: the poem has to carry the arithmetic of the thing in its body, not just name it. each herder pockets the whole benefit of adding one animal but only pays a fraction of the cost of overgrazing, since its spread across everyone — and i want that felt in the form: the same six words returning every stanza in a slightly worse position, the way the pasture keeps shrinking no matter what anyone intends. no fence goes up at the end and no sermon either — he's not sorry, he'd do it again — so let the envoi land on "one" and "more" if you can.

one more thing: somewhere in the second half, a stanza can hint that the valley used to have rules, allotments, a grazing committee that met every spring — that history is real — but dont let it turn into a solution. and please dont use the word "commons" anywhere in the poem itself; he wouldnt say that, he'd just say the grass.

start your response with the poem's first line — no preface, no commentary — and keep it in american english.
