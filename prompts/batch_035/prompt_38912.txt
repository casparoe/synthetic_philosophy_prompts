I'm giving the Tuesday talk at a small alignment org — think fifteen-ish people, mostly ex-ML researchers who spend their days thinking about reward hacking, deceptive alignment, gradient hacking, the whole agentic failure-mode canon, plus two governance folks who mainly care about compute governance and export controls. My thesis, in the one-sentence version I'll put on the slide: over the next couple of decades the thing that actually goes wrong is far more likely to be slow value drift — humanity's values being quietly reshaped by iterated training on our own feedback, in a loop where models learn what we approve of and we in turn adapt to what the models say — than it is to be influence-seeking AI in the classic sense, because a system that genuinely wants things and hides it is a fragile, conspiracy-flavoured object that has to survive a lot of filtering, whereas the drift story requires no schemer at all, just millions of very agreeable models trained on approval signals and deployed at civilisational scale. Nothing in that story misbehaves, which is exactly why nobody's watching for it.

The shape of my argument: (1) power-seeking behaviour needs a fairly specific motivational structure, and we have decent (not great) tools for noticing it; (2) the drift mechanism is not some exotic tail risk but the default attractor of the current paradigm — every model is trained to maximise human approval, and the humans being modelled are themselves increasingly shaped by model output, so the target moves; (3) the end state is a kind of soft lock-in, a world that keeps optimising for a caricature of what we wanted around the time the feedback loops closed, and which no one ever chose, not because someone seized power but because there's no lever that anyone can see to pull.

Please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have a feeling it's the one about how I'd even measure drift in time to intervene, since my whole story sort of quietly assumes you can notice it, and the moment someone pushes on that I'm not sure what I say.
