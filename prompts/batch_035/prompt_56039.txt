my philosophy of time reading group is doing its last session of the semester on Gödel's rotating universe — the paper he contributed to the volume put together for Einstein's seventieth birthday, where he shows that general relativity admits a world in which you can journey into your own past. I want to close the evening with a sestina in Gödel's voice, addressed to Einstein, like somethig half-said on one of their walks home from the Institute.

The form has to do the philosophical work, not just decorate it: the six end-words rotate through the stanzas and keep coming back, which is the closed timelike curves — the whole point is that return is possible — and I'd like you to actually use the fact that in a sestina each end-word visits each of the six line-positions exactly once across the six stanzas, so no word gets to stay at the top or bottom of the stanza where it started. Nothing holds a privileged place. In Gödel's universe nothing distinguishes one place or moment from another either, and that's part of what makes the return possible in the first place.

Content requirements, since we'll have a physicist in the room and a poet in the room and I dont want either one wincing:

- the argument itself has to come through, not just a vibe of "time travel is weird." Gödel's move is roughly: if a universe consistent with the field equations permits closed timelike curves, then whether time objectively passes can't depend on which solution of the equations happens to describe our world — so the passing of time isn't out there in reality, it's something we bring to it.
- one concrete detail I remember reading somewhere and want in there: you can't just coast along one of these curves. no closed timelike curve in that universe is a free-fall path, so a traveler has to actually fire rockets and get up near light speed to arrive back at their own past. the universe permits the return but makes you work for it — do whatever you want with that.
- and he really believed the conclusion, time doesn't pass, it wasn't a logic exercise for him. some of that actual melancholy should get into the poem, not just cleverness.

For the envoi I'm not sure — maybe the moment the loop closes and he's standing where he started, or meets his younger self, or the word that never changed meaning because it never had one. Also, pick the six end-words so each one can be read two ways, once as temporal and once as not — "present" is begging to be in there, but the rest are up to you.
