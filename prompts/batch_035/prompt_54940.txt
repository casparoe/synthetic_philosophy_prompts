I'm taking an early modern metaphysics course, and we've just moved from Leibniz onto the contemporary identity of indiscernibles literature. Our professor had us read section 9 of Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics (1686) alongside Max Black's famous two-sphere thought experiment, and I'm struggling to see how the two texts are actually supposed to be in conflict. In the Leibniz passage, he insists that "It is not true that two substances resemble each other entirely and differ solo numero," and the surrounding argument seems to run roughly like this: each substance expresses the whole universe in its own way, from its own point of view, and God, in creating, always has a sufficient reason to treat one candidate creation differently from another — so two creations that differed in no respect whatsoever could never both exist, since God would have had no reason to make one rather than, or in addition to, the other.

My problem is with the sentence itself. First, I can't quite pin down what "differ solo numero" is supposed to rule out — is Leibniz just saying there's no bare numerical distinction between substances, or is he making the stronger claim that any two substances must be distinguishable by us in principle? And what exactly is a "substance" doing in this argument, as opposed to just an object? Second, I've read that Black's universe, containing only two qualitatively identical iron spheres and nothing else, is meant as a counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles, but I can't tell whether it's aimed at the same thesis Leibniz asserts or at some later, cleaner reformulation of it. Leibniz's principle seems tied up with divine choice and with each substance expressing the whole universe, while Black's spheres sit in an otherwise empty space — so does Black's scenario even engage the premise that every substance mirrors the universe from its own perspective, or does it quietly change the subject?

Could you walk me through the passage's meaning, the sense of "solo numero," and the role the substance doctrine plays in the argument? Then explain whether Black's two-sphere universe counts as a genuine counterexample to what Leibniz was claiming, or only to a stripped-down version of the principle, and if the latter, which of Leibniz's theological premises does the stripped-down version drop and where does that make it weaker or stronger? I want to write a short response paper on whether the counterexample and the original argument are talking past each other, so any pointers on where the disagreement really lives would help.
