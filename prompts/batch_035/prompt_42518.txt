I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I've run into a puzzle about my own reliability that I can't quite frame, though I suspect philosophy has a name for it. Last year I published a long feature arguing that a widely accepted claim in nutrition science — the one nearly everyone in my field repeats uncritically — rests on shaky evidence and is probably overstated. The argument was careful: I traced the claim to its sources, found the sampling problems, interviewed the skeptics, and my fact-checker found nothing wrong. I still think the reasoning is sound, and rereading the piece I can't identify a single weak link.

But here is the complication. In the years since, three of my other long investigative pieces — ones where I felt exactly the same kind of confidence, built arguments the same way, and had them cleared by editors — have been seriously undercut by later findings. My track record, when I'm honest about it, is that investigations of this type, conducted by me, in this confident mood, turn out wrong more often than right. I now have what looks like decent evidence that the very process which produced my conclusion is unreliable, even though I can't locate any flaw in the conclusion's support from the inside.

My editor says the two attitudes can't coexist: either I really believe the argument, in which case my past failures are irrelevant, or I take my track record seriously, in which case I should pull the piece or at least publish a correction hedging its central claim. A colleague on the desk takes the opposite line: the argument stands or falls on its merits, and retreating because of a statistical fact about my past self is a kind of double counting — the evidence is already all in the article.

Something feels wrong with both of those positions, but I can't articulate the third option. Is it coherent to continue asserting my conclusion publicly while privately giving substantial weight to the suspicion that my reasoning got there unreliably? Does the higher-order fact about my track record give me a reason to change my first-order belief, or only a reason to change how loudly I assert it? And practically: if I can't find the flaw, but I know flaws of exactly the kind I can't find exist in most of my similar past work, what does intellectual honesty require of me here — a correction, a hedge, silence, or standing by the piece? I'd appreciate a framework that takes seriously both the argument itself and the evidence about the arguer.
