Last month my co-founders put me in charge of the agreements panel at the makerspace we started — a volunteer body that rules on when a member's consent to an arrangement, with the space or with another member, is valid. We inherited a record of rulings from the previous chair, and we try to stay consistent with it, which is now my problem: I helped draft the original member agreements, and a hard case has landed in my inbox. The record, briefly: (1) consent to publish a member's project photos in the newsletter, given after being told that only members would see it when the newsletter was in fact publicly posted — ruled invalid; (2) consent to the shared-kiln arrangement, given on a false belief about the scheduling rules that a third member had innocently passed on from an old version of the rules — ruled invalid; (3) consent to displaying a project at the county fair, later regretted, but entered with an accurate understanding of what displaying involved — ruled valid; (4) consent to running the open-house setup, given only after a coordinator threatened to cancel the member's storage slot otherwise — ruled invalid; (5) much the same agreement, given purely because the open house would have failed without that member and no one threatened anything — ruled valid; (6) an agreement given by phone to a 48-hour project-move deadline while the member was on strong painkillers after an accident — ruled invalid and redone once she was sober.

The new case: a member back after a year away has agreed to front the money for materials and cut the display pieces for another member's trade-fair booth, in exchange for a share of the takings, and has already bought the materials. She planned the whole venture around standing weekly access to the shared CNC router. The booking rules were loosened while she was away, unrestricted booking has filled the calendar for months, and her belief in guaranteed access comes from her own memory of the old rules: no one told her anything false, and she never asked. Her partner in the venture, who books the router himself, heard the entire plan, knew how full the calendar was, and said nothing — he assumed she must have arranged something.

What ruling should we issue if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I want, in order: the ruling itself, stated in your first sentence, then the reasoning; the closest analog in the record, with an honest note on where the record genuinely underdetermines the answer; the candidate rules the record fits that come apart on this case, with what each predicts here and which one you would bet on and why; and the further case, of our own invention, that would best separate the surviving rules. Please say plainly what principle each possible ruling would commit the panel to going forward — that is the part I have to defend to the members. No preamble, and don't restate the matter back to me.
