I am a novelist, some years into the craft, now at work on a novel set in the 1630s, centered on a woman who has inherited her father's apothecary shop and who holds, with entire and untroubled conviction, the Galenic theory of the humors. I have also, alongside the fiction, been writing a short essay about a problem I keep running into, and I would be grateful for comments on the notes below — both on whether the argument is any good and on whether it is worth developing further, since I am considering publishing it in a small journal that runs essays by fiction writers on their practice.

I had long been dimly aware of Butterfield's complaint about the Whig interpretation of history, but I only recently recognized its precise novelistic analogue, and it is not what I first assumed. My initial thought was that the Whig temptation in fiction is a matter of plot: the apothecary must be on a journey toward something we recognize, must glimpse, however dimly, the germ of modern pharmacology. That is a real vice, but it is a crude one, and a careful writer avoids it almost automatically. The subtler vice, I have come to think, lies in selection of detail — in where the camera rests. If I show my apothecary weighing out her theriac and omit her prayers, or show her careful record-keeping and omit the astrological reasoning that governed her dosing, I have written a Whig novel even if the plot contains no progress at all. The reader receives a past curated for its relevance to us: every detail selected because it is a seed of the present, every silence a dismissal of what did not become us.

From this I drew a second claim, which I am less sure of: that dramatic irony, the most reliable engine of historical fiction, is structurally Whig. The reader knows the plague is coming, knows the humors are false, knows the century will end with a Royal Society. The pleasure of nearly every historical novel I can think of is the pleasure of watching the past be wrong under the reader's correcting gaze. If Butterfield is right that Whig history studies the past for its relation to the present, then dramatic irony is that relation made into a narrative device — the reader's hindsight standing in for the historian's parliament.

In response I have drafted a working constraint on myself, which I have been calling counterfactual invariance of scene selection: no scene may earn its place primarily because of what it leads to. A scene of the apothecary bleeding a fevered child is admissible only if, had the century gone otherwise — had the Galenic edifice never fallen, had medicine developed as an elaboration of it — the scene would still be worth writing, on grounds internal to the world of the novel. The test is meant to force me to find what is valuable in her life without reference to its destiny.

Here is where the notes end and the doubts begin, and where I would most like your comments. First, I suspect the constraint is self-undermining in a way I cannot quite articulate. "Show the past as it was to itself, indifferent to what it became" is still a prescription about the past's relation to us; the demand for a past that refuses to be our ancestor is as present-directed as the piety it replaces. Am I right that there is a knot here, or is it a merely verbal paradox? Second, I worry the constraint produces an unreadable book. A novel in which no detail is allowed to foreshadow anything is arguably a novel with no principle of selection at all, and selection, not omission, is what a narrative is. If so, the honest conclusion might be that fiction cannot do what Butterfield asks of history — that the novel is constitutively a Whig form. Third, and most tentatively: I have a suspicion that the same knot appears in historical fiction set in living memory, in the temptation to write a character's life as a dress rehearsal for the reader's own politics, and that this suggests my subject is not the seventeenth century but hindsight as such. But I have not worked out whether that extension strengthens the essay or dissolves it into vagueness.

I would value comments on any of this: where the argument is weak, where it merely restates something historians have said better (I am a novelist, not a scholar, and am aware I may be rediscovering truisms), and whether the counterfactual-invariance idea is genuinely workable as a craft constraint or just an elegant way of describing impossibility.
