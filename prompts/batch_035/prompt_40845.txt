I'm preparing a seminar unit on rational choice for second-year philosophy students, built around Buridan's ass: the donkey placed exactly midway between two identical bales of hay which, having no reason to prefer either, supposedly starves. Rather than rehearsing the puzzle in its usual form, I want the session to survey the strategies philosophers have used to dissolve or deflect it, and I've settled on a shortlist of five. First, the symmetry breaker: perfectly identical options never occur in the real world, so the case is a philosopher's fiction with no purchase on actual choice. Second, rational arbitrariness: an indifferent agent may simply pick, and picking is not choosing-by-reason, so nothing in rationality forbids it. Third, the internal coin flip: once reasons run out, randomizing is itself the rational move. Fourth, the value of decisiveness: resolving the deadlock now carries instrumental value because delay is costly, so there is after all a reason to prefer some action over none. Fifth, the drift reply: indifference is a state with dispositions attached, and the ass's hunger gradient will carry it toward one bale, so we should treat indifference as a cause of movement rather than a halt.

Please rank these five responses in tiers according to how much you'd trust each as an answer to the puzzle, saying in a sentence or two why each lands where it does, and indicate which one you would teach as the default position. To make things harder, I also plan to pose a variant of my own for the seminar: the two bales are not identical, merely indistinguishable to the ass, say equally nutritious and differing only in some respect the ass cannot perceive. So the puzzle shifts from objective symmetry to epistemic symmetry, and I'd like to know whether your tier ranking survives that shift, or whether some responses that look strong against real symmetry become weak against perceived symmetry, or the reverse. Please take that variant seriously rather than dismissing it as a cosmetic change.
