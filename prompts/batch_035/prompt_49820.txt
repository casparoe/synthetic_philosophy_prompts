For some years now I've been settled on two claims that I treat as a single position: that Germany bears primary responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War — not merely a share of it, and not merely a handful of statesmen and generals in July 1914, but Germany as a political entity pursuing a deliberate gamble — and that it is coherent and meaningful to put it this way, holding the nation itself responsible across time rather than dissolving the verdict into a list of individuals. Roughly the Fischer thesis, joined to a view about collective responsibility. I want to know what would count against this position, and I want the answer mapped on both fronts rather than just the historical one.

On the historical front: which documents, counter-readings, and historiographical developments should genuinely move me — the interwar revisionists, Fischer's critics, the more recent revival of shared or structural explanations of the July Crisis — and how decisive is each? I'd like you to work through the load-bearing specifics: how the September Programme and the December 1912 war council minutes are supposed to bear on intentions in July 1914, and how a defender of my view could be deprived of them; what follows if the blank cheque to Vienna was a defensive miscalculation within an alliance commitment rather than an offensive gamble; what the systemic and mobilization-timetable explanations of the war's spread would have to show for primacy to dissolve into shared culpability. Distinguish what would be fatal from what would merely be awkward, and say which of these a committed defender of the position would concede as counting against it rather than wave away.

On the conceptual front: which cases, intuitions, or arguments about collective responsibility should move me — that most Germans alive in 1914 neither decided nor perhaps wanted the war; that those born after 1918 stand in no causal chain to it; that a nation may simply not be the kind of entity that can intend, decide, or be guilty of anything; that holding 'Germany' responsible seems to demand a bearer that persists from 1914 through Versailles' war-guilt clause and after, and it is unclear what that bearer is. Which of these would a defender concede, and which would they dismiss as a category error — for instance by saying responsibility attaches to nations through their organs, without attaching to each member?

I also want to know what my position explains that its rivals leave unexplained, and where it over-explains: what does primacy get us that the shared-responsibility picture doesn't, and what does the picture have to strain to accommodate?

Finally, an honest structural worry. It seems to me that defenders of national responsibility handle conceptual objections by quietly shrinking the referent: the nation becomes the state, the state becomes its decision-makers, the decision-makers become a policy-making elite — and at each retreat the claim gets harder to refute but also less like the claim I started with. Has my position been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect for a claim of this kind — part historical judgment, part moral verdict — or just what responsible talk about collective responsibility has to look like? Rank all the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which strand of the position, the primacy of Germany's role or the coherence of holding a nation responsible, is the more exposed one.
