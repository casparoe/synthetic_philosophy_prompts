I run the stats clinic at a coding bootcamp — mostly career changers, solid at Python but their formal stats is basicaly whatever survived a numpy tutorial. It's 18-22 people, one 90-minute evening session, room with round tables, whiteboards, and a projector I'd rather not switch on for the first hour.

Design me an activity where Simpson's reversal shows up in numbers the students generate themselves. That's the hard constraint: no handing out a pre-aggregated table with the reversal sitting there waiting to be noticed — most of them have already skimmed a blog post with the Berkeley admissions chart or the kidney stone trial, and I want them to get bitten by the thing before they recognise it. So don't build the activity on either of those examples, or on batting averages, for the same reason. Ideally the confounding should be baked into the rules of whatever they're doing (seating, ordering, some innocuous-sounding procedural rule) so that when someone finally tallies everything, the aggregate answer and the subgroup answers disagree and the group has to work out which one is real.

My vague seed idea, which you're welcome to improve on or throw out entirely: students individually play some quick game of chance or skill across a few rounds, making a choice each round between two options (name them whatever fits your design), and I rig which conditions they end up in through a rule that sounds logistical rather than statistical. Then one student acts as an analyst who only gets to see the pooled tally sheet and has to make a live call — with actual small stakes, maybe the forfeit being that the losers tidy the room — based on it. I want the moment where the analyst's confident answer, which is arithmetically correct, collides with the subgroup sheets.

Then the debrief: map where in the activity each formal piece lives, what students should conclude about which table answers which question, and — this is the part I care most about — an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that the reversal itself is easy to stage, but the genuinely interesting part, i.e. deciding which marginal you should actually condition on, needs a causal story the game can't supply on its own, and I'd rather you confirm or push back on that than paper over it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I'm worried about tally errors, someone who half-remembers the paradox blurting the name halfway through and spoiling it, and whether the group sizes need to be engineered in advance so the reversal is robust to a few students not showing up.

No lecture up front, and nobody hears the name Simpson until the debrief at the earliest. Give me rules, materials, timings, the tally mechanism, and enough worked-through numbers that I can sanity-check the reversal will actually appear with a realistic turnout.
