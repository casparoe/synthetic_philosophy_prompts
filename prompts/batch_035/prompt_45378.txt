For a few years now I've been arguing about the simulation question with a friend who studied philosophy, and I've settled into a set of positions I'd like to defend at our next meetup. Before I do, I want someone to check whether my beliefs actually fit together. Here they are:

1. Consciousness depends on the pattern of organization, not on what the material is: a sufficiently detailed computer simulation of my brain would have experiences in exactly the way I do.
2. The famous three-way argument is sound: any civilization either dies out before it can run detailed simulations of its own history, or chooses not to, or runs enormous numbers of them — and since the simulated people would vastly outnumber the original ones, we are almost certainly living inside such a simulation.
3. I have never found the doomsday argument persuasive: where my birth happens to fall in the sequence of all humans tells me nothing about humanity's prospects, because I can't treat my own existence as a random sample drawn from that sequence.
4. If I learned tomorrow that we really are in a simulation, nothing of importance would change: my experiences would be exactly as real, and the people around me exactly as conscious and exactly as valuable, as before.
5. Whoever runs our simulation, if anyone, is doing nothing wrong: our history already played out once in the ordinary way, and re-running it — however much suffering it contains — adds nothing morally new.

Go through these commitments pairwise and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ranking the real tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to give and what the revision would cost me. Finally, tell me which single change would restore consistency most cheaply. I honestly don't know where the problem is, if there is one, so please don't soft-pedal.
