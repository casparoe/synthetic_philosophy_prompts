I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've coined a label for an argument form my students keep gesturing at: complexity-theoretic debunking. The argument runs as follows. Cognitive science attributes to humans a task — belief revision, planning, tracking the mental states of many agents at once — whose general form is NP-hard or worse; since humans lack the time and memory that exact solution requires, the faculty cannot be doing what the theory says it does; therefore the theory misdescribes cognition. Before we assess particular uses of the argument, I want the standards fixed. Please write the desiderata a psychological theory must meet to be immune to complexity-theoretic debunking: what it must establish about the task as humans actually confront it rather than in the worst case, what room remains for approximation and satisficing without collapsing into saying the theory was wrong all along, what tractability should even mean for a bounded agent, and what a debunker is entitled to conclude from a bare hardness result. Give the motivating case behind each requirement, and flag within an item any tension you suspect it cannot jointly satisfy with another. Please respond with exactly five numbered desiderata, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and use American English spelling throughout.
