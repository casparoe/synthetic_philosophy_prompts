I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy or linguistics — my degrees are in computer science, and everything I know about this debate comes from engineers arguing about it. So when the following happened, I had no way to referee it, and I'd genuinely like a careful verdict.

At a design review last week, someone offhandedly remarked that large language models had "settled" an old linguistics controversy about whether humans are born with some innate, language-specific mental machinery. That touched off a long argument between two of my colleagues — Maya, who works on our NLP stack, and Jonah, who works on infrastructure. The exchange went roughly like this, as far as I can reconstruct it:

1. Maya said the old argument runs like this: children end up knowing rules of grammar that the sentences they hear underdetermine — nobody tells them which candidate generalizations are wrong — so something must be built in beforehand. LLMs undercut that. A plain neural network with no language-specific structures, trained on nothing but raw text by generic gradient descent, ends up fluent. If a learner with no built-in grammar can do it, the missing ingredient can't have been an innate grammar.

2. Jonah replied that this proves far less than she thinks. Those models are trained on trillions of words; a child hears a few million by school age. So LLMs show that grammar is extractable from enormous data, not that it's extractable from the data a child actually gets. The argument was always about what is learnable from the realistic evidence a child has, not about what is learnable in principle from a corpus a million times larger.

3. Maya conceded the data point but said a child's input is far richer than raw text: it is grounded in objects and events, interactive, accompanied by pointing, gaze, and attempts to communicate. And maybe the innate machinery that learning needs is real but not language-specific — general drives to communicate, biases toward joint attention, and so on. On that reading, she said, the Chomskyan side wins a narrower claim (no dedicated language organ) but loses the broad one (language can be learned by something close to a general-purpose learner).

4. Jonah said that move is cheating. If every useful innate bias gets reclassified as "general cognition," the claim that language needs rich innate structure becomes unfalsifiable — whatever humans turn out to be born with, you call it general and declare a draw. He added a further wrinkle: LLMs are not the pure positive-evidence case Maya described anyway. The models we actually ship were fine-tuned on human preference ratings, which is exactly the kind of corrective feedback children supposedly lack. So the clean experiment hasn't been run.

5. Maya answered that grammaticality shows up in base models before any fine-tuning — next-word prediction alone already yields mostly well-formed sentences — so preference tuning cannot be what carries the grammar. And she pointed out that the size comparison isn't obviously embarrassing for the connectionist side either: the brain has on the order of a hundred billion neurons, and frontier models have on that order of parameters, so it isn't clear the brain is doing more with less in any simple sense.

6. Jonah closed with what he called the deeper point. Even granting everything, fluency is not acquisition. Children converge on the same grammar, with striking speed and uniformity, from meager and messy evidence; no current model has been shown to do anything comparable from comparable data, and the debate is about that, not about whether enormous models can mimic the end state. Until someone trains a model on a child-sized dataset and it succeeds, the innateness argument is untouched — and if it fails, that would be evidence for the Chomskyan side, so the question is at least empirical rather than settled.

Maya muttered that a position you can never lose that way isn't much of a position, and they both turned to me, and I admitted I couldn't referee. Could you do it? Specifically: go through the six moves and say, for each, whether it is right, half-right, or mistaken, and why — including the factual premises, like how big a child's linguistic input really is and how the brain-versus-model size comparison holds up. Then give an overall verdict: do LLMs actually tell us anything about whether language ability is innate, and is there anything both of them were missing? I can follow technical detail fine; just translate any philosophy jargon when it comes up.
