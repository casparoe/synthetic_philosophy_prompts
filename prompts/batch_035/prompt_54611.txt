I've been reading a popular history of philosophy, and its chapter on Leibniz ends with a summary of the doctrine of the best of all possible worlds that I would like to have audited before I repeat any of it. The chapter says, in substance, the following. Leibniz argued that God, being omniscient and perfectly good, was bound to create the best of all possible worlds, and since he did create it, the actual world is the best one — a claim Leibniz defended in the Theodicy and which is why his followers came to be called optimists. Because this world is the best possible, everything in it is for the best: every event, including catastrophes like the Lisbon earthquake, is part of the optimal arrangement and could not have been improved without making the whole worse. It follows, the chapter continues, that we should accept whatever happens with equanimity, since complaining about evil is strictly a mistake, and that trying to prevent suffering is pointless, since whatever happens is already the best. The chapter closes by saying that Voltaire's Candide, written in the aftermath of Lisbon, demolished the doctrine by putting the optimist's motto in the mouth of a fool and inviting the reader to weigh the motto against the corpses, and it adds that no philosopher has since found an answer, so the doctrine is now regarded as refuted.
