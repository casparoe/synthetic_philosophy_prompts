I've been reading about the experience machine and trying to sort out my actual gut reactions, and they don't line up the way I expected. Heres my list. I would not plug into the machine myself, or at least I think I wouldn't. If my kid were dying of something untreatable and painful, I'd want her in the machine for her last few months, and I'd feel like a monster for withholding it, not for providing it. Same for a stranger with chronic untreatable pain who plugs in — that seems rational, maybe even the sensible thing to do. But a bored, healthy person who plugs in to live a more exciting life strikes me as kind of pathetic, and someone who plugs in to believe they're writing the great novel that no one in reality will ever read is somewhere near tragic. And yet I think a woman in a bad marriage who'd be happier believing her husband is faithful, when he isn't, should be told the truth — the fake happiness doesn't really count for her, even though that's basically a low-grade experience machine she never signed up for. One more: I think a person whose only actual ambition is to feel content, who genuinely doesn't care whether anything they believe is true, would be fine in the machine, no tragedy at all.

My tentative thought is that the difference has something to do with whether the person cares about contact with reality — but that feels like it's smuggling in whatever the real question was supposed to be. So: is there a theory of well-being that gets all or most of these verdicts? What does it say about a case I haven't listed, like someone who goes into the machine for exactly a decade, knowing they'll come out and pick their real life back up afterward? And please be honest about which of my reactions you'd have to overturn to get a clean theory out of this, because I suspect at least one pair can't both survive. Answer in American English.
