I don't have any background in this stuff — no political science, no philosophy, just someone who argues with my brother-in-law at family dinners and then thinks about it for a week afterward. His favorite move, whenever the topic of defense spending or nuclear weapons comes up, is to lean back and deliver the old Latin tag about wanting peace and preparing for war. Last Thanksgiving he used it to shut down my suggestion that scrapping a chunk of the arsenal might actually make everyone safer: if you want peace, you prepare for war, therefore you don't cut weapons, and my point was supposedly refuted before I'd finished making it. I had a feeling then, and have a stronger one now, that the phrase is doing about five different jobs at once, but I can't for the life of me pull them apart, and googling it just gets me motivational-poster history and quotes from generals.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Take that one sentence and lay out, as a menu I can actually use at the dinner table, the distinct precise claims it could be expressing — starting with ones so weak that nobody in the argument would deny them, moving through substantive ones, and ending with the strong or even radical readings. For example, it seems like it could be (a) a causal claim that being visibly armed stops others from attacking you, (b) a claim about human nature, that would-be aggressors only respond to strength and read any restraint as weakness, (c) something about leverage — that you need a credible war capability just to sit credibly at a negotiating table, or (d) something specific to nuclear weapons, where maybe "preparing for war" means having a guaranteed ability to retaliate rather than having more warheads than the other guy. I'm not sure those are the right way to slice it; feel free to slice differently. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it could actually be false, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it would genuinely support my brother-in-law's conclusion, since some of the readings seem like they'd justify preparing for war in general while saying nothing about whether *more* weapons are better than *fewer but sufficient* ones.

One more thing I keep chewing on and want woven in: isn't there a live possibility that the slogan gets the causality backwards in some circumstances — that preparing for war is exactly what makes the other side prepare, and everyone ends up less secure? If that's a real possibility, then some readings of the slogan aren't just unproven but actively self-undermining, and I'd like to know which ones survive that worry and which don't. Keep it in plain language, no jargon without a quick explanation, and don't bother with citing books or historians — just the reasoning, laid out so that someone who thinks about this only over turkey can follow it and use it.
