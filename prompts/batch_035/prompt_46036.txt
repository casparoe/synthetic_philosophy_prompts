My metaphysics professor closes her seminar on modal metaphysics with a standing exercise: students propose cases, and she rules on whether the person described could be her. She teaches the Kripke material on origins every year but refuses to lecture on this stretch of the view, so her rulings in the exercise are the only evidence we have. Her verdicts so far:

- The very same sperm fertilizing the very same egg, but the embryo carried to term by a gestational surrogate: still her.
- The same sperm and egg, but the embryo cryopreserved for four years before implantation, so she is born four years late into the same family: still her.
- Her mother's very egg fertilized by a different sperm from her father — her half-sister, with her mother's features but a different temperament and a different life: not her; that's a sibling.
- A person with her exact genome, assembled de novo from a digital DNA file, with no gametes from either parent: not her; a genetic duplicate, she says, like a twin built to order.
- A person with all her memories and personality and no biological relation to her family: not her.

Next week's case, which another student has already submitted: her mother's very egg is fertilized by a different sperm from her father — one that, by an astronomical fluke of the recombination lottery, carries exactly the same haploid genome as the sperm that actually fertilized that egg. The resulting zygote is therefore identical, base pair for base pair, to her actual zygote; the person develops in her mother's womb and lives a biography that matches her actual one in every respect we would ordinarily cite in identifying her — same family, same schools, same memories acquired in the same order. Could that person be her?

I care less about the bare prediction than about what lies behind it. Please lay out the candidate rules that fit her verdicts so far — I can half-see at least three: one on which both particular gametes are essential; one on which the particular ovum is essential and the sperm matters only through the genetic material it delivers; and perhaps a best-candidate rule on which overwhelming overall similarity carries the day. Correct or extend that list, note which tempting rules her earlier rulings have already ruled out, and say what each surviving rule predicts for next week's case. Then give your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her track record. After that, tell me which further case would best separate whichever rules survive her ruling — for instance, if she says yes, I would want a case that distinguishes the ovum-plus-genome reading from the best-candidate reading. Finally, be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration would change your mind about the prediction.
