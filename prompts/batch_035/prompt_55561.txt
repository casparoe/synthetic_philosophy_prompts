Two friends of mine got into a long argument after a reading group on Dennett's idea that the self is a "center of narrative gravity" and I couldn't referee it, so maybe you can. You're basically a professional philosopher of mind, so please take it seriously and maybe brainstorm a bit before commiting to verdicts.

(1) Maya said the upshot is deflationary: just as a center of gravity is a posit we invent to keep track of a physical system, the self is a useful fiction the brain's user-interface spins up. So when she says "I regret what I did at 20," there's no deep fact about who the "I" is that did it -- it's narrative bookkeeping all the way down.

(2) Ben said the analogy actually cuts the other way: centers of gravity are not fictions in physics, they're perfectly respectable theoretical posits with real explanatory power. Nobody thinks a comet's center of mass is "unreal" just because no atom sits there. So Dennett's move, if anything, makes the self MORE real than eliminativists wanted -- a real abstract object that unifies the data.

(3) Maya pushed back: a center of mass has an exact definition and, in principle, an exact location you can compute. "The self" has neither. So the analogy is trading on vagueness -- it sounds rigorous because it borrows physics clothes for something that can't be computed at all.

(4) Ben answered that plenty of respectable posits can't be computed either: a species' average fitness, an ecosystem's equilibrium, a market price "absent shocks." If being non-computable made something unreal, most of the special sciences would be about nothing.

(5) Then Maya went for what she thought was a reductio: okay, then fictional characters have selves. Sherlock Holmes is a center of narrative gravity that organizes the stories as well as anything organizes mine. And the journaling app she's used for a year -- the one that says "we've noticed you sleep badly after talking to your mother" -- is spinning up a center of gravity about her that's arguably more stable and better documented than her own. If the theory hands out selves that easily, she said, it changed the subject from what we wanted explained.

(6) Ben said no -- for him the reductio goes the other way: the theory wasn't supposed to vindicate "there is no self," it was supposed to explain why it's so useful to talk as if there is, and if it explains that equally for Holmes and the app, fine, that's a feature, not a bug. Maybe "self" is like "center of the story," and there's nothing shameful in stories having centers.

Can you adjudicate move by move -- say who's right at each step and why, not just who wins overall? I'd especially like to know whether Maya's point in (3) is a real problem for Dennett or a confusion about how analogies work, and whether (5) and (6) are both sliding past some distinction (fictional vs. real narrators? self-models vs. selves?) that a careful answer should restore. Also flag where either of them seems to be assuming something Dennett wouldn't grant. Thanks!
