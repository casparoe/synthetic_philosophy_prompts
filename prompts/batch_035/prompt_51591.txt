The exam question was:

"In her work on echo chambers and epistemic bubbles, C. Thi Nguyen draws a distinction between two ways a person's information environment can be epistemically distorted. (a) Explain the distinction, making clear what role trust plays on each side of it. (b) Which of the two is more resistant to correction, and why? (c) Does the more resilient of the two require anyone to be actively deceived or manipulated, or can it arise among fully sincere participants? Defend your answer."

The student's answer, verbatim:

"An epistemic bubble is a social structure where you just don't get exposed to the other side's views. The people in it aren't hostile to dissent — dissent simply never reaches them, like living in a town with no newspaper from the neighboring county. An echo chamber is different because the other side's views do reach you, but they arrive pre-discredited. The echo chamber trains its members' trust so that outside voices are marked as untrustworthy in advance: anyone who speaks against the group's core beliefs is taken to be either corrupt, or brainwashed, or in on a conspiracy, and this discrediting happens before you've even heard what they have to say. So trust is doing the work in the echo chamber case — it's a redistribution of trust away from outsiders and toward insiders — whereas in the bubble trust never really comes up, you just have an absence.

(b) The bubble is much more resistant to correction. Because the bubble is a sealed environment, the members never encounter contrary evidence at all, so nothing can ever shake them. Echo chamber members, by contrast, are exposed to contrary voices all the time — that's partly what defines them — so all it takes is enough repeated exposure and the wall of discredit will eventually crack. This is why echo chambers tend to collapse over time while bubbles persist.

(c) Echo chambers don't require manipulation in the sense of a scheming propagandist. Since everyone in the echo chamber sees the same evidence as everyone else — the discrediting explanations are available to members too, e.g. they can plainly observe that defectors get called corrupt — the members are being irrational, but not deceived. Sincere participants can sustain one by each doing their honest best with the trust structure they inherited. So the answer to (c) is yes for sincerity, though the members are still epistemically irrational, since they have access to the same facts as outsiders and persist anyway."
