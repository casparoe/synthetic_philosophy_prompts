My brother and I keep circling this thing and I want to know if it has a proper name in philosophy. Some beliefs seem to bring about their own truth. If I believe my shy new coworker would love to be invited to lunch, and I invite her, and we end up friends for life — was my belief already true before I acted, or did acting on it make it true? If the belief had been false and I'd acted anyway, maybe it would have become true anyway. Same with a coach who believes a mediocre runner can finish a marathon, trains her accordingly, and then the runner finishes. And there's the mirror case: everyone believes a small bank is about to fail, so they all withdraw their money, and the bank fails. Meanwhile a belief about the number of craters on the far side of the moon can't work like this at all — nothing I do about it changes anything.

So my question: does this family of beliefs-that-affect-their-own-truth have an established name and literature? I know "self-fulfilling prophecy" from sociology, but I have a feeling that's narrower — about public predictions rather than quiet beliefs — and I've heard "performative" used for utterances rather than beliefs, so I don't want to just grab the nearest label. I want to know what the nearest concepts actually are, which one fits which of my cases, and where the edges are: is the bank run the same phenomenon as the coworker, or does it matter that one is about a single person and the other about a whole population coordinating? Is a belief that merely improves its own chances (the coach) the same as one that flat-out manufactures its truth, or is that a distinction philosophers already draw?

Here's the part I'm actually loosing sleep over. A philosophy-major friend said my cases are basically the raw material of some huge fight between two American philosophers around 1900 — a charming psychologist who argued that true beliefs are the ones that work for you, and a grumpy mathematician who thought that was an outrage and eventually renamed his own theory with a deliberately ugly word so nobody would confuse the two. Is that right? If so, did anyone ever make the obvious objection that beliefs like my coworker case are a tiny corner — beliefs that get to be true partly because you hold them — and that building a theory of all truth out of them is like founding a theory of pregnancy on the one famous exception? Or is the connection subtler than that? Please answer in American English, by the way — I'm getting a mix of spellings from other sources and it's driving me nuts.
