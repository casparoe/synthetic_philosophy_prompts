At the magazine where I work as a copy editor, a colleague has been campaigning to abolish our style guide, and her argument runs as follows. She read that David Lewis, in his 1969 book Convention, analyzes conventions as arbitrary solutions to coordination problems: everyone drives on the right (or the left) not because that side is inherently better but because nearly everyone conforms, expects nearly everyone else to conform, and has reason to keep conforming given those expectations, all of which is common knowledge. She says the rules of grammar and usage are conventions in exactly this sense — English could have settled on different past-tense forms, different constructions, a different set of homophone distinctions, and the ones it settled on were arbitrary. So, she concludes, there is no more such a thing as grammatical or stylistic correctness than there is a correct side of the road, and our job should be to record actual usage rather than enforce anything; the style guide is a kind of superstition. The editors who disagree with her mostly just insist that standards matter, which has not been helping. I would like a proper audit of her analogy before I decide what I think.

Please lay out explicitly what maps onto what: what in the case of usage rules plays the role that the side of the road plays, what plays the role of the coordination problem itself, and what plays the roles of the common knowledge, the alternative equilibria, and the preference for general conformity that Lewis's account requires. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Some disanalogies I suspect matter: a side of the road is a single, salient, easily grasped alternative, while a language's conventions are vast, tacit, and acquired by exposure rather than choice; driving conventions are backed by explicit law and visible enforcement, while usage conventions are policed loosely and unevenly; and drivers all want the same thing, while speakers and writers have partly conflicting interests — some want to be understood by everyone, others want to be understood by a select few, and some signal education, class, or group membership precisely by departing from or adhering to particular usages. Tell me which of the disanalogies are load-bearing for her conclusion and which are incidental. I would also like the point about Lewis's own view confronted directly: on his account a convention, once established, does generate reasons to conform — the arbitrariness of which equilibrium we landed in does not mean there is no reason to conform to the one we are in. Does that alone refute her conclusion, or does it just relocate the argument — for instance, to the question of which forms of writing involve genuine coordination payoffs and which function instead as markers of group membership? Finally, if the driving example is the wrong carrier for this debate, say what would be a better analogy for the work usage norms actually do, and what conclusion, if any, about the fate of our style guide that better analogy would support.
