"Nāgārjuna argues that all phenomena are empty of intrinsic nature. Explain what this claim amounts to, why dependent origination is his central argument for it, and what the tetralemma in the opening examination of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā is supposed to accomplish. Then explain the doctrine of the two truths and why it is supposed to block the charge that Madhyamaka is a form of nihilism."

Here is the student's answer, transcribed in full:

"Nāgārjuna's claim that all dharmas are empty is the claim that nothing possesses intrinsic nature (svabhāva): no dharma exists from its own side, independently of conditions, designations, and other dharmas. His central argument is that anything with intrinsic nature would have to exist in a fixed way, since to have an essence would be to be what one is independent of everything else; but everything arises dependently, and what arises dependently cannot have intrinsic nature. This is why he identifies dependent origination with emptiness.

The tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) at the start of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā considers four possibilities for how any dharma could arise: from itself, from another, from both, or from neither — that is, causelessly. The first horn fails because a thing that arose from itself would already exist, making arising pointless; the second fails because if cause and effect were intrinsically other, nothing would connect them and anything could arise from anything. The remaining horns fail for similar reasons. Since all four alternatives fail, Nāgārjuna's own position must be the fourth one: given that nothing has intrinsic nature, nothing really arises at all — arising is merely apparent, and in the ultimate sense things happen without causes. Causation is just a scheme we use to organize experience conventionally. Reality itself is indeterminate: neither existent nor nonexistent nor both nor neither.

This is where the two truths come in. Ultimate truth is that all dharmas are empty; conventional truth is the world as it appears to the unenlightened, which is strictly false but pedagogically necessary, since the Buddha first taught in conventional terms as a ladder to be kicked away. The charge of nihilism is thus mistaken: Madhyamaka does affirm a positive ultimate truth — the emptiness of all dharmas — and this is the final nature of reality, the way things really are beneath appearances."
