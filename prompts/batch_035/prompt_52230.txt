I am a recently retired pharmacologist — thirty-one years in medicines regulation and clinical-trial oversight — and since retiring I have joined a philosophy discussion group that meets at our public library. Our next topic is state paternalism, particularly the "nudging" literature, and I have been asked to prepare a diagnostic instrument for the session: not an essay, but a way of sorting members by what they actually believe about specific cases, so the discussion doesn't collapse into people simply trading labels.

From my years in drug regulation I bring a background assumption I would like the instrument to interrogate: in regulating medicines we never demanded that each individual user of, say, a codeine-containing cough syrup be individually irrational; population-level evidence of predictable errors was enough to justify blanket rules. Whether that is defensible paternalism, excusable because it merely fixes decision inputs, or inexcusable overreach is exactly the kind of question the instrument should sort people on.

Please construct a "which position on paternalism do you actually hold?" quiz with the following specifications.

Structure. Eight questions, each presenting one concrete case with two or three answer options. No question may use the standard philosophical labels ("soft paternalism," "libertarian paternalism," etc.) or ask whether someone "accepts paternalism"; the questions must sort by responses to cases only. Then provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

The distinctions that matter to me, and which the questions should make people come apart on:

1. The target of intervention: (a) decision inputs — warnings, education, disclosure (the paradigm of informed consent, which I spent my career on); (b) decision architecture — defaults, ordering, placement, opt-out design, which leave beliefs untouched; and (c) preference shaping — cultivating tastes, making the healthy option the pleasant one, public campaigns that change what people want. Someone might accept all warnings but balk at re-architecting cafeterias, or accept defaults but not taste-cultivation. At least one question must separate each pair of these.
2. The standard of justification: whether the intervention must be endorsable by the chooser's own reflective preferences (would she thank us later?), or may appeal to her predicted welfare as we judge it, or may appeal to a wrong-prevention rationale that is not about her welfare at all.
3. The granularity of justification: whether paternalistic rules need only population-level evidence of predictable decision failures, or must be justifiable case by case against each regulated individual.

Cases. Use at least six drawn from this list: automatic pension enrollment with an opt-out; placing fruit at eye level and desserts at the back; a per-ounce tax on sugared drinks; mandatory motorcycle helmet laws; outright prohibition of a recreational opioid; graphic warning labels mandated on cigarette packaging; a mandatory cooling-off period before payday-loan signing; a public campaign that makes smoking socially unattractive; and a blanket prescription-only rule for a cold remedy that some users take quite rationally.

Leaves. The scoring key must include, at minimum: the Millian anti-paternalist position; soft paternalism on the Feinberg-style contrast (intervention permissible only to secure adequately informed, voluntary choice); hard paternalism; libertarian paternalism of the Thaler–Sunstein kind; and the non-paternalist wrong-prevention position of the sort Seana Shiffrin has defended, on which restricting someone's means (as with drug laws) can be justified without appeal to her own good at all. Build the logical space from the questions first — the combinations of answers they permit — and then locate these named positions within it, rather than starting from the list of names. Note any answer combinations the space allows that no listed position occupies, and briefly diagnose each empty cell: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive for reasons worth stating, or merely unexplored? Also flag explicitly any pair of the required leaves your questions cannot in principle separate, rather than pretending the instrument distinguishes them.

Format. Please return everything as a single JSON object with this schema:

- "questions": an array of eight objects, each with fields "id" (Q1–Q8), "case" (the concrete scenario), "options" (array of objects with fields "label" and "text"), and "target_distinction" (which of my three distinctions the question separates people on);
- "leaves": an array of objects with fields "name", "proponents" (may be "unnamed"), "answer_pattern" (the option labels that lead to this leaf), "one_line_description", and "awkward_commitment" (the single implication of this position that its defenders find hardest to swallow);
- "unoccupied": an array of objects with fields "answer_pattern", "why_empty" (one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "unexplored"), and "diagnosis";
- "inseparable_pairs": an array of objects with fields "leaves" (the two leaf names) and "why" (why the questions cannot pull them apart).
