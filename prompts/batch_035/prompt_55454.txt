I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy background, and I keep getting into arguments at work about "revealed preference" — the idea that what people do shows what they actually want, better than what they say. I've been going by gut on cases and I want to know if there's an actual principle behind my verdicts, so here they are:

- We shipped infinite scroll even though like 70% of survey respondents said they'd hate it. Time-on-site went up 40%. My verdict: users preferred it, the survey was noise.
- My friend has been saying for two years he wants to switch to a Rust shop. Bought three books, never opened them, never applied anywhere. Verdict: he doesn't really want to, he just likes the idea of wanting it.
- I keep saying I want to cut back on phone use, and I keep not doing it. By the same logic as the friend case, scrolling is my real preference. But this one feels wrong — it feels like a bug in me, not a preference. (Though my friend would probably say the exact same thing about himself.)
- Users click "accept all cookies" on our consent wall in under two seconds, almost nobody opens settings. Verdict: this one I'm NOT sure shows they don't care about privacy — the button is big and pre-selected and declining means a worse experience. But then what exactly makes it different from the infinite scroll case?
- A smoker who's tried to quit five times and calls himself desperate to stop. Verdict: his smoking doesn't reveal a preference for smoking, even though obviously he keeps choosing to buy cigarettes.

So: is there one principle that gets all or most of these, or do I have to give some of them up? If the answer is something like "behavior reveals preference only when X," I want to know what X is, concretely enough that I could apply it to a fresh product decision — for instance, does paying for our premium tier reveal users prefer it, when we made the free tier deliberately worse last quarter?

Also, since I'm going to repeat whatever you tell me in my next argument: please seperate what's basically settled among people who actually study this from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested take as if it were the consensus. I'd rather know where the fights are than win on a technicality.
