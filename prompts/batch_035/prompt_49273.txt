I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized online gaming company, and I've been asked to sit on the small committee that signs off on promotional mechanics. Marketing wants to run a provably fair coin-flip jackpot: players pay a fixed entry fee, a coin is flipped repeatedly, and the payout doubles with each successive heads until the first tails ends the round. Because the expected payout is unbounded, marketing argues the promotion is irresistibly attractive to players and has projected acquisition numbers on that basis. Finance has countered that our balance sheet caps any realistic payout at a few million dollars, which they say makes the true expected value small and calculable. My two colleagues have dug in on opposite sides: one holds that any positive expected value means we should run it (with the liability cap disclosed in the terms), the other thinks something is deeply off about a game whose appeal rests on an infinite number that neither a player nor our company will ever actually experience. I recognize the shape of this from a blog post about the St. Petersburg paradox, but I have no philosophy training and want the reasoning done properly rather than hand-waved. Draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete recommendation on the entry fee and liability cap we should set. It should give the real reasoning behind the verdict, not just "the expected value is large" or "the expected value is small once you truncate," it should address whether the liability cap genuinely defuses the problem or merely relocates it, and it must give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side loses.
