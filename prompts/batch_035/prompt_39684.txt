I teach the discussion sections for a course on contemporary moral theory, and we have just finished the aggregation problem in Scanlon's contractualism: whether, and under what conditions, a large number of individually weaker claims can outweigh one person's stronger claim, and what the sheer number of claimants contributes if anything. For the next section I want a decision tree that takes each student from a commitment everyone in the room shares — that each person's suffering gives us a reason to prevent it, and that one person's stronger claim normally defeats another's weaker one — to a named position: thoroughgoing aggregation (the utilitarian leaf), prioritarian aggregation, Scanlon's own individualist view, Taurek-style non-aggregation, and threshold or lexical views on which claims may combine but only past a cutoff.

Requirements on the branches. Each node must be a concrete case or comparison a student can answer without knowing which position it feeds — for instance a choice between preventing a single death and preventing an enormous number of headaches, or a chain of pairwise choices in which each step seems clearly right but the endpoints seem clearly wrong (the transitivity objection). At least one branch must isolate whether the student's resistance to aggregation is grounded in the separateness of persons — claims belong to individuals and cannot be summed across them — or in doubts about the structure of value, namely whether betterness must be transitive at all; if these two routes to skepticism cannot be landed in distinguishable leaves, say so explicitly rather than papering over it. At least one branch must handle the tie-breaker question, whether numbers can matter between claims of exactly equal strength, since a view can deny aggregation outright while still letting numbers break ties, and I want that possibility represented rather than collapsed into the utilitarian leaf. Build the tree from the logical space first — which questions, in which combinations — and only then locate the named positions in it, noting each unoccupied combination with a sentence on whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag honestly any pair of leaves no case can cleanly separate; I suspect threshold aggregation and Scanlon's official view are difficult to pull apart, and I would like to know whether you agree or whether some case can do it. Use no more than ten branch questions, and no question may simply ask the student which theory or theorist they favor — the point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they came in liking.

Begin your response with the tree itself, with no preface and no restatement of this request; put the commentary — unoccupied combinations, inseparable leaves, and any advice on how students should read their path — after the tree.
