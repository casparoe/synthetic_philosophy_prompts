I write a column on science and ideas for a general readership, and my next piece is about how Gödel's incompleteness theorems became the most overworked results in mathematics — conscripted to prove the existence of God, the impossibility of machine minds (the Penrose-style argument), and the general unreliability of scientific or rational inquiry. I'd like to close the column with a villanelle, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than merely decorate it.

The two refrains should be the two sentences our culture keeps hearing. The first refrain is the popular overreach — in substance, that Gödel showed truth outruns proof, so we are all entitled to our favorite unprovable truths. The second is an austere statement of what the theorems actually license: that a consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, and that such a system leaves some arithmetical truths unprovable within it. As the refrains alternate through the tercets, each return of the overreach should latch onto the genuine feature of the theorem it exploits — the real gap between truth and provability in arithmetic — and each return of the austere statement should scrupulously decline to export that gap to minds, gods, or science as a whole. The turn should arrive in the final quatrain, where the refrains share a stanza for the first time: there the reader should see that the misreading cannot simply be laughed off, because it grows out of the correct reading the way a rumor grows out of a fact.

Accuracy matters — someone with a logic background should find every line defensible, and the poem should not pretend the theorem is simpler than it is. A proper villanelle, nineteen lines.
