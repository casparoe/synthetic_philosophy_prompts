I am at work on my third novel, in which roughly a fifth of the text is written in a private language that two siblings invented as children and still speak to each other in adulthood. The language is genuinely comprehensible in principle — its grammar is regular, and its vocabulary is built from English roots by systematic transformations — but I have deliberately supplied no glossary, no explanatory footnotes, and no other translation apparatus, beyond whatever the surrounding English narration allows an attentive reader to infer. An old friend who teaches semiotics keeps pressing on me Hofstadter's discussion, in Gödel, Escher, Bach, of where a message's meaning resides: the frame message, which tells the receiver that a decodable message is present at all; the outer message, the decoding instructions implicit in the message's structure, which lead the receiver to identify the system; and the inner message, the content the sender actually meant to convey. My friend insists that the last of these is what really matters, and that by withholding a decoder I am only making my readers' access to the inner message needlessly laborious.

Please argue against my friend. Make the case that, for a literary work, the bulk of the meaning carried by my invented-language passages resides in the frame and outer messages rather than the inner one — that what matters most is the reader's recognition that something decodable is being deliberately withheld, and the labor of reconstructing the siblings' private code, not the propositional content the invented sentences happen to encode. Along the way, please address the obvious objection: that the passages could then be replaced by gibberish that merely looks like a decodable cipher, with nothing lost. If you think that objection defeats the position in its strongest form, say so plainly and argue instead for whatever division of labor among the three layers you can defend.
