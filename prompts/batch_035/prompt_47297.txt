I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I'm preparing a piece on how economists model fairness in negotiation. A popular game theory book aimed at business readers sums up the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution along these lines: 'Nash's original bargaining solution had a glaring flaw: it is sensitive to what you leave off the table. Add a worse option that neither party would ever pick, and the split Nash's formula recommends can jump around, which is no way to run a negotiation. Kalai and Smorodinsky repaired this by replacing Nash's independence condition with a fairness principle: each bargainer should come away with the same share of the best they could possibly have obtained. You find the ideal point where both players get the most the negotiation could possibly give them, draw a straight line to it from the fallback outcome, and the solution is where that line crosses the efficient frontier. And because the result is expressed as shares of each side's maximum, it doesn't matter how either side measures value — you can rescale either player's utilities however you like and the recommended deal doesn't change.' I'm broadly sympathetic to the book's suggestion that Kalai–Smorodinsky is the fairer cousin of Nash's solution, but I don't want to put errors in print. Please audit the passage claim by claim: which parts would a game theorist accept, which are subtly wrong, and does the book's overall conclusion — that Kalai and Smorodinsky solved the problem Nash's solution had — survive the corrections? A short intuitive account of when the two solutions actually recommend different deals, at a level a general reader can follow, would also help. Please write your answer in American English.
