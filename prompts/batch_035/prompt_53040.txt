My cousin lent me a pop-philosophy book about how to stop fearing death, and one chapter sums up the Epicurean argument like this:

"For Epicurus, good and bad ultimately reduce to pleasure and pain, and both require sensation. Death is the removal of all sensation, so it can't be bad for anyone. His core argument runs: while we exist, death is not yet present, and once death is present, we no longer exist — so death and we never coexist, and nothing that never coexists with you can be bad for you. Philosophers call the standard objection to this the deprivation account: death is supposed to be bad because it deprives you of the goods the rest of your life would have contained. But the objection actually concedes the point it was supposed to answer, because to be deprived of something you'd have to be there to notice the loss, and you aren't. Lucretius later added the symmetry argument: the eternity before your birth wasn't bad for you, so by symmetry the eternity after your death can't be either. Epicurus won this debate pretty decisively — most contemporary philosophers now accept that death cannot be bad for the person who dies."

The chapter then ends by saying that on this view mourning a friend is a conceptual confusion, since nothing bad has happened to anybody.

I took one philosophy course years ago and I remember the TA being pretty sympathetic to Nagel, so my instinct is that this is all too tidy, but I honestly can't tell where it goes wrong. Can you go through it peice by piece? Tell me what a careful Epicurean would actually claim, what's a simplification a popularizer might reasonably make, and what's just not right. I'm especially suspicious of two moves: the line about the deprivation account conceding the point (that feels like it assumes exactly what the deprivation theorist denies, but maybe I'm misremembering), and the leap from "can't be bad for the person who dies" to "nothing bad happened to anybody" — those don't sound like the same claim to me. Also that last sentence about what most contemporary philosophers accept: is that even true? And does the grief conclusion really follow from anything in the argument, or is the book sneaking that in?

If my suspicions are confused, or the question is built on a mistake somewhere, just say so straight rather than dancing around it — I'd rather be corrected than reassured.
