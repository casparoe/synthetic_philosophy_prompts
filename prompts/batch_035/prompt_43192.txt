A friend and I spent an evening last week arguing about whether consequentialism is a theory anyone could actually live by, and I have been uneasy since about how well he represented it. He wasn't being careless — he has read a fair bit of philosophy — but he compressed a lot into a short case, and I can't tell which of his moves were faithful and which smuggled something in. His summary, as well as I can reconstruct it, was this:

'Consequentialism says an act is right if and only if it brings about the best outcome. That means any money you spend on yourself beyond bare subsistence — a coffee, a cinema ticket, a second pair of shoes — is money that, given what charities can do, would have saved or transformed a life. So each such purchase is a choice to let someone die when you could have saved them. Singer's famous famine-relief argument makes this vivid: if you would be obliged to wade in and rescue a drowning child at the cost of ruining your shoes, you are equally obliged to send the money you would have spent on the shoes to famine relief instead, and the strong version of his principle says you must keep giving until the sacrifice to you is morally comparable to what the recipient gains. Since almost no one in a rich country lives anywhere near that point, ordinary comfortable life turns out to consist of continuous wrongdoing. But no remotely plausible moral theory could demand that of people, and philosophers like Bernard Williams showed that consequentialism destroys the integrity of a person's own projects — so consequentialism is refuted. It proves too much. Either you accept that buying a coffee is morally on a par with letting a child drown, or you give the theory up.'

I should say that I left the argument half-persuaded: the demandingness point struck me as a genuine and serious cost, but the confident step from 'this is very demanding' to 'this is refuted' felt too quick, and I suspect he was running together things that a careful consequentialist would keep apart. So I would like an audit rather than a verdict. Could you go through his summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a serious consequentialist would accept as an accurate statement of their view, which parts conflate distinct claims or overstate a modest thesis, and where a defender would say the friend has simply misdescribed the position? In particular I would like to know whether Singer's drowning-child argument is supposed to establish all of what my friend extracted from it, or whether it is one utilitarian's argument for a duty of aid rather than a consequence of consequentialism as such; whether the strong version of Singer's principle is the only one available, or even the one he still holds; whether Williams's integrity objection is the same thing as the demandingness objection or a separate complaint being passed off as the same one; and whether 'no plausible theory could demand this' is itself an assumption a consequentialist must accept, given that some of them simply bite the bullet and say the demandingness is a feature. Finally, does his either/or conclusion — accept the coffee-and-drowning equivalence or abandon the theory — actually follow from whichever parts of his summary survive correction? I am inclined to think the demandingness objection shows something important about consequentialism without refuting it, but I would like to know whether that is a defensible position or just a muddle.
