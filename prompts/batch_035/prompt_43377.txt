I am a novelist, currently at work on my fifth book, narrated in close third person by a live-in caregiver named for no one in particular but modeled on several women I interviewed. Drafting her voice has left me with a conceptual itch I cannot scratch. In scene after scene, the family she serves believes her completely about what happened — the date of the fall, what the specialist actually said, which pill was refused, how long the confusion had been going on — but the moment she ventures what it means, the temperature of the room drops. When she suggests that the matriarch is not depressed by her diagnosis but terrified of becoming a burden, the daughters exchange a look as though she had overreached; the interpretation is treated as not hers to offer. A friend who spent twenty years as a hospital nurse confirms the shape of this from her own life: her factual reports were never once doubted, while her judgment about what a patient's behavior signaled was routinely relayed upward until a physician re-pronounced it in his own words, at which point it became actionable.

Stated as generally as I can manage, the pattern is this: certain people are granted full credibility as witnesses but essentially none as interpreters of what they have witnessed — they are trusted to say what happened and not trusted to say what it amounts to. The deficit is selective in a way that puzzles me, because it does not feel like ordinary disbelief. And there seems to be a complementary figure, common enough in fiction and in life: the credentialed person who is believed about meanings — diagnoses, motives, the deeper significance of events — while his factual slips are indulged as harmless eccentricity, so that the two kinds of authority come apart in opposite directions. As a novelist I need to know whether my narrator's situation is an instance of something general or an idiosyncrasy I have projected onto the world, and I would like to understand the phenomenon precisely enough to dramatize it rather than merely gesture at it.

So: does this division of labor — full epistemic credit for reporting, none for interpreting — have an established name, or does either half of it? A philosopher acquaintance muttered something over dinner about testimony and credibility but refused to be pinned down, and I would rather not bluff my way through a literature I have not read. Please organize your reply under four headings, in this order. First, "Nearest concepts" (roughly 250 words): the established terms from epistemology or philosophy of mind that come closest to the pattern, stated plainly enough for a layperson. Second, "Where they fall short" (roughly 150 words): what exactly remains undescribed once those concepts are applied, given that the credibility deficit here is selective rather than global. Third, "A label, if warranted" (roughly 150 words): either a term from the literature I have missed, or your own coinage, with a definition careful enough to exclude the mere circumstance of being occasionally wrong. Fourth, "Two boundary cases" (roughiy 200 words total): one case showing where the concept should end, and one testing whether my caregiver example genuinely belongs to it or is better described by something else. I am not after a bare name; I want to know what the thing is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
