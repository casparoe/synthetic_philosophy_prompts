I'm in a graduate reading group working through philosophy of quantum mechanics, and we've arrived at the Born rule problem in the Everett interpretation. There's an asymmetry the group keeps circling that I'd like you to explain carefully. On the one hand, I understand why the naive answer — count the branches and treat them as equiprobable — is a non-starter: the decomposition of the wavefunction into "branches" is not a well-defined operation, since any branching can be refined or coarsened, so "how many worlds" has no fact of the matter. On the other hand, the Born weight attached to each branch is supposed to be perfectly well-defined. I'd like to understand how both of these can be true at once — how the number of branches can be vague while the squared amplitude attached to each is exact — and what work that asymmetry does in the wider argument.

Second, and more importantly, I want to understand where the two main strategies for recovering probability in Everett actually locate it, because as far as I can tell they locate it in different places. In the decision-theoretic program associated with David Deutsch and David Wallace, the argument seems to run before the branch: a rational agent facing a measurement is shown, via constraints on her preferences, that she must care about the weights rather than the branch counts, so probability re-enters as a fact about rational decision-making rather than about what physically happens. In the self-locating uncertainty approach of Charles Sebens and Sean Carroll, the argument instead runs after the split but before the observer knows which branch she is on — probability there re-enters as credence about one's own location among already-existing successors. Are these genuinely competing answers to the same question, or are they answering different questions (one about what to care about in advance, one about what to believe in the interval after the world has split)? And is either entitled to be called a derivation of the Born rule, as opposed to a demonstration that, given that one cares about amplitudes at all, squaring them is the only coherent way to weight them?

Please explain this at a level suitable for someone comfortable with the basic formalism of decoherence but not fluent in decision theory. In particular, I'd like the exact role the failure of branch counting plays in each strategy: is it the thing that forces probability to be amplitude-based, or merely an obstacle each program routes around in its own way? And if there's a standard objection to each — the circularity worry against the decision-theoretic proof, or the worry that self-locating uncertainty presupposes an agent confined to a single branch and so can't apply before branching — please state those plainly too.
