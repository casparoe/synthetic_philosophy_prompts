I'm taking a graduate seminar on social choice theory, and we have spent nearly four weeks on what our course outline calls the Harsanyi–Sen debate. The impasse has begun to feel almost structural to me, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than another round of adjudication. The dispute, in brief: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says that if each individual's preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the social preference relation does too, and if social preference respects unanimity, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities — and with an added equal-treatment condition, an unweighted sum. Harsanyi took this to vindicate utilitarianism from surprisingly weak premises. Sen and others have resisted, on at least two fronts: that the theorem smuggles in cardinal utilities without any interpersonal comparability, so the "sum" adds numbers that live on incommensurable scales; and, following Diamond's objection, that evaluating prospects by expected utility runs together two different questions — what is prudent for one person facing risk, and what is fair when the risk is distributed across different people — so a society can rationally prefer a fair lottery to a certainty for its worst-off individual even when the lottery has lower expected utility. Our seminar read Weymark's later reconstruction, which distinguishes a strictly utilitarian reading of the theorem from a weaker, agnostic one, and Broome's defence of Harsanyi; but my sense is that the two camps have not really engaged each other in decades — each side restates its favourite counterexample or its favourite axiom, and nothing moves. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the stalled disagreement is not about interpersonal comparability at bottom but about what a representation theorem is for: whether the vNM axioms encode substantive norms of rationality that social evaluation should inherit, or merely a convenient mathematical form in which any complete ranking can be dressed. If that is right, the two sides are answering different questions under one name, and no formal result will settle it. Is my diagnosis right? Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a hidden formal question doing the work? Please give reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, and tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress here — a new theorem, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question — or whether no progress should be expected, and why. Please answer in British English.
