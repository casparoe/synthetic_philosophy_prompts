I've been trying to map my convictions about when doubting something is legitimate and when it isn't, and I'd like to know whether any single principle captures them. My verdicts, such as they are:

1. My neighbor refusing to serve on a jury on the grounds that she cannot rule out being a brain in a vat: not a legitimate doubt — barely a doubt at all.
2. My old professor spending a whole seminar eliciting exactly that hypothesis and probing what would count against it: perfectly legitimate, even valuable.
3. A woman who checks her door locks forty times a night because she cannot be certain they are locked, despite overwhelming evidence that she has just checked them: pathological, though I am honestly unsure whether to call it illegitimate — it seems more like a defect in the doubter than in the doubt.
4. A physicist who, in 1890, treats it as settled that any anomaly in planetary motion reflects an unmodeled asteroid or a measurement error rather than a flaw in Newtonian mechanics: legitimate, indeed rational, at the time — and yet
5. the same stance, held in 1910 after decades of accumulating anomalies, had become illegitimate, and Mercury's perihelion deserved the doubt it eventually got. So whatever certainties these are, they can expire — but seemingly not on any schedule I can state.
6. Moore raising a hand and concluding that external things exist: sound but pointless. It doesn't touch the skeptic, yet I cannot articulate what a proof of the external world was supposed to do differently, especially since
7. I have no objection at all to someone asserting, before a blood draw, that they know they have hands, and
8. I would let someone say they know they have never been to the moon — true, ordinary, and yet not supported by anything in their experience that distinguishes it from mere memories.
9. A conspiracy theorist doubting the moon landing: illegitimate — but everything I come up with by way of explanation (the doubt would require far too much else to be wrong) seems to apply equally to the physicist in (4), whom I called legitimate.
10. A mathematician who, after seriously internalizing formalism, briefly doubts whether two plus two equals four and then returns to arithmetic: I want to say this doubt was legitimate in a way (1) was not, even though it concerns something more basic than anything in (1)–(9).

My tentative principle: a doubt is legitimate when, and only when, some live practice contains a check that could in principle settle it — where the philosophy seminar counts as a live practice but the jury room does not. This handles (1) versus (2), but it makes the difference between (4) and (5) mysterious, since the available checks had not changed between 1890 and 1910, and it is unclear what it could even mean to run a check on the items in (6)–(8), where I doubt the notion of checking applies.

Please repair this principle or replace it with a better one. State it precisely enough to deliver verdicts on: a climate scientist asked by a journalist whether she can be sure the thermometers are not systematically miscalibrated; an AI safety researcher who cannot stop wondering whether her memories of the past month were fabricated; someone who doubts their own name only at a border crossing, where it matters legally, but not elsewhere; and someone who, after a religious conversion, doubts that the earth is more than a few thousand years old while still using radiometric dating to schedule a flight, in the sense of trusting the equipment it is embedded in. Then tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts your best principle fails to accommodate, and which verdict you would advise me to surrender first.
