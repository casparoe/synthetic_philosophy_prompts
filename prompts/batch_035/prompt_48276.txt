So, bit of background: I'm a software engineer, about fifteen years in, mostly backend, and for the last several years I've been working on a giant legacy monolith that handles our payments. Everybody complains about it, nobody is allowed to touch the core, and every week there's the same argument in our standups: do we keep adding special cases so the thing limps along, or do we finally do the rewrite my colleague keeps pushing for. Back in university I took a philosophy of science course where we read Lakatos, and recently I caught myself thinking his whole apparatus describes our codebase suspiciously well. What I retained from the course is the broad strokes: research programmes with a hard core you never give up on, a protective belt of auxiliary assumptions that takes the hits, the positive and negative heuristics, and programmes being called progressive or degenerating depending on whether they keep predicting genuinely new stuff or just endlessly patch in response to surprises. But that was over a decade ago and I remeber it the way you remember a lecture you crammed for, so re-explain whatever parts of Lakatos you need as you go — don't assume I can reconstruct the details.

What I want is a translation into the vocabulary I actually use day to day: long-lived codebases, architectural invariants (the Linux kernel people's rule about never breaking userspace, or in our case rules like all writes go through the saga coordinator and the public API never changes), backwards compatibility shims, regression suites, technical debt, the strangler-fig approach where you carve the new system out around the old one, the constant triage between fixing the root cause and bolting on another special case. Map each Lakatosian element onto whatever fits: what plays the role of the hard core in a software project, what is the protective belt, what does a degenerating problemshift look like in a commit history, what's the software counterpart of a novel prediction if there is one. I want the mapping to be honest rather than clever — where something only fits if you squint, say so, and if an element has no counterpart at all, don't invent one.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Two questions in particular. First: in science, rival programmes compete to explain the same phenomena, and Lakatos (at least as I remember him) has this story where rationality is mostly retrospective — you only find out years later whether sticking with a degenerating programme was vision or stubbornness. In software you can fork, copy, and run the old system and the rewrite side by side for years, keeping whichever performs better empirically. Does that dissolve the interesting part of the Lakatos debate, or is the engineers' endless rewrite-vs-refactor fight (the folklore that big rewrites always end in disaster, versus the claim that incrementally patching a rotten core is throwing good money after bad) still tracking a genuine disagreement that just looks different at this scale? Second: what in Lakatos has no expression at all in my vocabulary, and in your judgement is that because my vocabulary is missing something (and what I'd have to add to say it) or because the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked? And please don't smuggle software assumptions in and call them translation — if the fact that code is copyable is quietly doing all the work in my intuitions, I want that flagged.
