Not sure where else to ask this, so here goes. I do incident reviews at work (workplace safety stuff, nothing dramatic), and I keep running into the same asymmetry. When someone's shortcut causes an injury, everybody says he "knew exactly what he was doing" and "chose to cut corners", even though the injury was the last thing the guy wanted. But when the same kind of shortcut happens to prevent an accident, or just saves everybody a pile of time, nobody says she knowingly brought that about -- she "got lucky" or "stumbled into it". Same knowledge, same foreseen-but-not-aimed-at consequence, opposite verdict on whether it was on purpose. I mentioned this to a friend who did a philosophy undergrad and she said "confirmation bias", but that doesn't seem right, because it's not about looking for evidence, it's about whether the word "intentionally" sticks depending on whether the outcome was bad. There's also a wrinkle I can't find words for: when the bad side effect lands mostly on the agent himself (guy takes a shortcut, mangles his own hand), people get alot more reluctant to say he did it on purpose. So the asymmetry seems to need the victim to be somebody else.

So my questions: does this pattern have an actual name, what's the closest established thing to it, and how do people carve up the space here -- is the mainstream view that this is a bias that should be corrected, or that ordinary folks are tracking something real about what intention is? And if the self-harm wrinkle has been written about, how is it usually explained? Don't just give me a label and call it a day, I want to know how it relates to nearby stuff like judging people more harshly because the outcome was bad anyway.
