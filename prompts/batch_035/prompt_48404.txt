"Explain the methodological tension in microhistory between its aspiration to recover the perspectives of ordinary, often illiterate people and its reliance on atypical subjects preserved through atypical records. Using Ginzburg's Menocchio as your example, discuss the objection that microhistory ends up writing the history of exceptional individuals rather than 'history from below' in any meaningful sense, and how microhistorians have defended themselves against it."

Here's the student's answer, pasted as recieved:

"Microhistory developed in Italy in the 1970s as part of the broader Annales program of serial history, so Ginzburg and Levi inherited Braudel's commitment to writing the history of ordinary people at large scale. Ginzburg's The Cheese and the Worms studies Menocchio, a sixteenth-century Friulian miller interrogated by the Inquisition, and uses the trial records to reconstruct the mental world of a peasant. The tension is that Menocchio was not a typical peasant: he was literate, he read books, and he held unusual cosmological ideas (he imagined the universe originating through a process of curdling, like cheese in milk). But Ginzburg's defense is that this does not matter, because Menocchio is statistically representative: his patterns of reading and belief were common among millers, a class that was dispropor­tionately literate, and what holds for literate millers holds broadly for the peasantry. This is what microhistorians mean by the 'exceptional normal.' So microhistory really is history from below in the strict sense, since 'history from below' and 'microhistory' are two names for the same method: E.P. Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class is essentially a microhistory of English laborers. It is true that the Inquisition records that make Menocchio visible are biased toward exceptional cases, since heresy trials only capture people who voiced heterodox opinions. But we can correct for this bias by aggregating large numbers of Inquisition trials and comparing Menocchio against the base rates. Overall, then, microhistory's answer to the typicality objection is that representativeness can be recovered statistically, and this is what licenses its claim to speak for the silent majority of early modern peasants."
