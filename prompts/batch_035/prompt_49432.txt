I'm a novelist, about two years into a book set in a small mill town in the Northeast, and I've hit a snag that turned out to be more interesting than the plot problem it originally was. The premise: the town's founding story everyone tells is about a flood in the late 1920s, when the mill owner supposedly opened his gates and sheltered the whole town, and it's a point of genuine pride — there's a plaque, a little reenactment every ten years, people name their kids after the guy. But I've been going through the local newspaper archive on microfilm for research, and the record says something different: the owner turned people away the first night and only relented after a fire at a boardinghouse, and the "everyone" he sheltered was mostly the mill's core workers while the immigrant families across the river sat in a schoolhouse for a week. Nobody in town remembers any of that, and a few people I interviewed got actively upset when I brought it up.

Here's what I want, and it's not help with the novel itself — it's a thought experiment I can use to pressure-test what the novel is actually about. Build me a scenario (it can be built out of this flood or something parallel to it, realistic, no more than a paragraph) engineered so that the community's memory and the documentary record conflict in a very specific way: the memory has to be false about the facts, but it can't just be a flattering lie the powerful told and everyone swallowed. I want the memory to be doing some genuine work that the record structurally can't do — maybe it's how people with no access to the archive transmitted what actually mattered to them, or it encodes a real grievance in distorted form. And don't cheat by stipulating the answer: don't bake "and the memory was justified" into the scenario, let the tension fall out of the details.

After the scenario, walk through both sides. What does someone who thinks the record should win whenever memory and archive conflict get out of the case, and what does someone who grants collective memory its own kind of authority get out of it? Then the part I care about most: tell me exactly which feature of the case is doing the work of making each side seem right, and what happens to my intuitions if I flip or remove that one feature — say, if it turned out the archive was compiled decades later by the mill owner's grandson, or if the distorted memory is actively hurting the descendants of the families who were left out that week. And finish by telling me how the strongest defender of whichever side comes out worse would attack the case. I'd rather the scenario feel like it could actually happen somewhere next Tuesday than have it be clever.
