My nationalism reading group (lol) has been stuck on the same fight for basically a year and at this point I want a diagnosis of the standoff, not another rerun of it. Side one, the modernists: nations are recent, an 18th-19th century product. They lean on the Hobsbawm invented-tradition stuff — Scottish clan tartans largely codified in the 19th century, royal ceremonies that look ancient but were staged recently — and on Anderson's point that a nation is an imagined community because you'll never meet almost any of your fellow members, and what made that kind of imagining possible was print capitalism: newspapers and novels running thousands of strangers through the same shared chronological frame every morning. Side two, the skeptics: that's chronological snobbery. Plenty of premodern peoples had the relevant thing — shared language, origin myths, sacred texts, a sense of being an us against the foreigners. They point at medieval chroniclers complaining about the cruelty of neighboring peoples, at communities that held a collective identity across centuries of statelessness (Jews, Armenians, the Poles between the partitions), and ask whether its really plausible that a feeling that durable was invented by the printing press around 1780. And here's the thing: after a year both sides have fully absorbed each other's examples and nobody has moved an inch. The modernists just say fine, those are ethnic or religious communities, not nations, because they lacked mass participation and a claim to popular sovereignty; the other side says that's moving the goalposts — you've defined nation so only modern ones qualify and then triumphantly discovered nations are modern.

My working hypothesis is that a lot of this is a verbal dispute about how much of the package you need before you get to use the word, but I also suspect there's a real causal question hiding underneath: were premodern identities load-bearing causes of the later nations, or just interchangeable raw material that print capitalism could've worked into nations from basically anything? So what I want from you is a real diagnosis — verbal, substantive, or two different questions (one about the word, one about the causal story) traveling under one heading? And what would actually count as progress here? Like, is there evidence that could exist that would move one side — or is the deeper problem that archives mostly record elites, so we can never check what the medieval peasant actually felt and both sides are just extrapolating? I'd honestly settle for being told the question is badly posed, if you can say why.
