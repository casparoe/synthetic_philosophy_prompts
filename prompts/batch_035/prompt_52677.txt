You play a commited Dennettian — you think the taxonomy is basically right, you're willing to concede that the boundaries between rungs are fuzzy and that real animals straddle categories, but you will not give up the core idea that these are four genuinely different ways of getting smart, ranked by how cheaply they let you make mistakes. Here's my opener: the whole taxonomy is rigged by where you put the cut between mind and no mind. A Darwinian creature, on your own telling, doesn't learn anything at all — its smarts were paid for by its dead relatives. But natural selection only ever acts on lineages, never on the individual, so calling the individual organism a Darwinian *mind* is like calling a single lottery ticket a gambler. The actual learner at the bottom rung is the population. Then when you move up to Skinnerian creatures, suddenly it's the individual that does the learning — you've swapped the subject of the learning halfway up without flagging it. So it's not one ladder, it's two ladders glued together at totally different grains: population-shaped at the bottom, organism-shaped after. And the Gregorian rung has the same problem in reverse, since half the mind of a Gregorian creature lives out in its tools and its culture, not inside its skull. So which is it — is the mind the organism, the lineage, or the whole cultural loop? Pick one and stay consistent, because I think each choice makes one of your four rungs collapse into its neighbor.
