I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and in three weeks I'm presenting at our department's weekly colloquium a paper defending a fairly strong version of epiphenomenalism: qualia are real, irreducibly phenomenal properties that are causally idle with respect to the physical world, arising as byproducts of certain neurophysiological processes without feeding back into them. My central claim is that the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment is not the embarrassment it's usually taken to be. On my view, when I judge "I am having a reddish experience," the judgment is a physical state whose content is carried entirely by physical mechanisms; the quale itself plays no causal role in producing or informing the judgment, but the covariation between quale and judgment is guaranteed by a common physical cause, so the epistemic situation is no worse than ordinary cases of reliable indicator-based knowledge. I further want to argue that the self-stultification objection — that epiphenomenalism undercuts my own grounds for asserting it — confuses causal explanation with epistemic justification, though I'm aware this move is more often asserted than argued.

The audience is my own department, and I know it well. There's a senior faculty member who is a committed representationalist and thinks the whole puzzle dissolves once you accept a phenomenal-concepts strategy: he'll say that "quotational" phenomenal concepts make the appearance of an explanatory gap a cognitive artifact, and that my position buys the anti-physicalist's framing wholesale. There's a second faculty member, an identity theorist from the older Australian materialist lineage, who is skeptical of any non-reductive talk and will press me on whether "byproducts with guaranteed covariation" is just re-descriptive epiphenomenalism with the metaphysical costs and none of the benefits. Several of the graduate students are sympathetic to illusionism and will likely ask why I don't just go the Frankish route and drop the qualia altogether, treating my "phenomenal residue" as a poorly theorized intuition rather than a datum. And there's a postdoc with a neuroscience background who tends to ask the deflate-the-stakes question of what, if anything, my view predicts or rules out empirically. What I do not expect, and what I would find most dangerous, is a well-prepared questioner pressing the acquaintance-based reply: that if my judgment about the red quale is not causally connected to the quale, then the covariation story gives me at most reliably true beliefs, never knowledge by acquaintance, and that this concedes the game to the skeptic about other minds.

Act as a seasoned philosopher of mind who has sat on many such panels and seen which objections actually land. Prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me: the six or seven objections I'm most likely to get from this particular audience, in rough order of how much damage each could do, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it (representationalist, identity theorist, illusionist, neuroscientist), followed by a two-minute reply I can give verbally and a candid note on where the reply is soft. For each, flag whether I should concede something up front rather than fight the whole battle. Include the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks — I believe it's the acquaintance one, but tell me if you think there's a worse one lurking, perhaps involving the causal closure premise itself or the coherence of "guaranteed covariation" without causal constraint. Think carefully about this audience before writing: these are the objections they will actually raise, not generic anti-epiphenomenalist arguments from the literature. Keep the whole prep sheet under 1,200 words, and order the entries so the most damaging objection comes first rather than the most likely one.
