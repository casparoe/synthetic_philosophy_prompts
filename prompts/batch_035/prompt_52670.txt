I am a novelist who has been drafted into something slightly out of my depth: a small residential retreat for working writers, at which each year someone must lead a two-hour evening session on a single idea from philosophy, on the understanding that no one present has read any philosophy since university. This year the idea is the extended mind thesis, and this year the someone is me. Plan the session for me. Do not explain the thesis to me here; I half understand it and can fill in the rest. What I need is the plan.

Some things you should know about the room. Twelve novelists and essayists, verbal, combative, allergic to jargon, and — this is the relevant fact — devoted to their notebooks. Every one of us keeps one: character notebooks, commonplace books, the draft journal one revisits for ten years while a novel refuses to finish. Several of us have said, only half joking, that we think in them rather than with them, and at least one member of the group lost a notebook in a house move some years ago and speaks of it the way other people speak of a death. That attachment should be both the hook and the trap, and I suspect the whole session turns on how it is used.

So, the plan I need: the order in which the pieces must be introduced, and what depends on what — I can see that some notion of a belief as a standing state rather than an inner episode has to be in place before any comparison with a notebook can do work, and I want the sequence to make that feel necessary rather than like throat-clearing. Then the carrying example: I am inclined to offer my own stalled-novel notebook as the case, but you should tell me whether it has the features the example needs or whether it invites trouble, and if it is the wrong notebook, what the right one must have and lack. Then the misconceptions, in the order this particular audience will form them: I can predict the sentimental reading (the notebook as part of my soul, which collapses into metaphor exactly where we need precision), and the dismissive one (we all use tools, so the thesis proves everything and therefore nothing), and I can predict the novelist who says that when she consults her notebook she experiences herself retrieving information, not believing something — I do not know where in the sequence that objection should be met or whether it should be met at all. Tell me where each misconception is best absorbed, and which one I should let the room fight about rather than resolve. I also want one question I can put to the group at the end that would tell me whether they have understood the difference between the notebook merely mattering to their minds and being a constituent of them, rather than having merely been moved. And I want an honest account of what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — the session is two hours, there is a fire in the grate, and I would rather say plainly that some debates are being left at the door than pretend they were never there.
