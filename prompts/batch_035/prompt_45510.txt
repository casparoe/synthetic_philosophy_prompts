My friends Dana and Marcus have been fighting about Sen's liberal paradox for like a week now and they want me to referee, which is rough because I can follow maybe 60% of it. Before I get to their argument, here's the setup they're working with, so you're on the same page.

Sen's classic example: two people, Prude and Lewd, and one copy of a racy novel. Three options: Prude reads it, Lewd reads it, or nobody reads it. Prude's ranking: nobody reads > Prude reads > Lewd reads (he hates the book but would rather slog through it in disgust himself than have Lewd enjoy it). Lewd's ranking: Prude reads > Lewd reads > nobody reads (he'd get the most fun out of Prude being scandalized, but he'd still rather read it himself than have it trashed). Now say we're the social planner and we respect individual rights in a minimal way: each person gets to decide whether *they themselves* read it. So Lewd's own preference puts "Lewd reads" above "nobody reads," and society honors that; Prude's preference puts "nobody reads" above "Prude reads," and society honors that too. Chain it together and society ranks Lewd reads > nobody reads > Prude reads. But hold on: *both* individuals rank "Prude reads" above "Lewd reads." So unanimous preference says the opposite of what respecting rights just forced on us. That's the impossibility — some combination of rights-respecting, Pareto, and consistency has to give.

OK, their actual fight:

(1) Dana says the theorem is a straight reductio of classical liberalism: if even this tiny society can't satisfy minimal rights plus Pareto plus coherent aggregation, then rights can't do the foundational work liberals want them to do, and in practice Pareto will always win, so rights will always get trimmed whenever enough people care about your business. She thinks that's just the fate of "it's my choice and no one else's" as a doctrine.

(2) Marcus says she's misdiagnosing it. The engine of the paradox is that the preferences *reach into someone else's sphere* — Prude cares whether Lewd reads, Lewd cares whether Prude reads. If the planner only counted each person's preferences over their own conduct, there'd be no cycle. So the lesson is about meddlesome preferences, not about rights being incoherent.

(3) Dana pushes back that this is a fix by amputation. Social choice theory isn't in the business of auditing the *content* of preferences, and once you let a planner decide which preferences are too nosy to count, you've built the thing liberalism was supposed to prevent. Also plenty of "other-regarding" preferences seem legitimate (I care a lot whether my neighbor beats his dog), so "keep it to your own sphere" isn't a clean filter even in principle.

(4) Marcus then goes practical: real rights are waivable. Prude can just... choose to read the novel. Or Lewd can pay him to. If rights are things you can exercise, trade, or waive, the Pareto-superior outcome is sitting right there, no planner needed. On this telling there's no deep conflict, just a bargaining problem, and the "paradox" is an artifact of modeling rights as rigid ranking-constraints instead of as options people hold.

(5) Dana's last word: if rights are tradeable options you've basically reinvented the market, and where that lands depends on initial endowments and bargaining power, which is fine for a novel but creepy for the rights we actually don't want sold (votes, bodily autonomy). And anyway, she says, the theorem is general — it's about any social aggregation, so one clever contract in the book case doesn't dissolve it.
