my housemate Dev waded into a freezing canal last month to drag out a stranger whose kayak had flipped, wrecked his phone, got mild hypothermia, missed a job interview. at dinner afterwards two of our friends got into it about whether what he did was "above and beyond" morally speaking, and it got pretty heated so I'll try to give both sides their best shot.

(1) Lena said it was a textbook case of something supererogatory -- morally good to do, but not something he was required to do, so he deserves extra credit for it.

(2) Marco said he doesn't think that category makes sense. either dev had a reason of morality to do it, strong enough to make it wrong to skip, or he didn't. if morality says "rescue the stranger," standing there filming on your phone instead is at least somewhat wrong, and if morality doesn't say that, dev did a nice thing but nothing morally creditable beyond, like, returning a lost wallet. "supererogatory" wants to be better-than-required, which marco says is grading on a curve -- as if morality hands out gold stars for difficulty.

(3) Lena said the risk is what makes it optional: the reason we don't blame someone for not jumping into a freezing canal is that a rescue with a real chance of you dying is too much to demand, so the sacrifice is exactly what buys the permission. that's why fire fighters are owed things and people who sit out risky rescues aren't villains.

(4) Marco said that proves too much and too little. too much: if the sacrifice is what makes it optional, then the more you'd suffer, the more optional it gets, so morality demands less of people for whom helping is harder, which seems backwards. too little: if dev could've done the same rescue at zero risk -- say with a rope and a life ring right there -- he'd be a total jerk not to, so it's not the cost that matters but some threshold of danger. and once marco put it that way he claimed the whole label collapses: dev didn't do anything *extra*, he just did the required rescue while swallowing a prudential cost, which happens all the time (getting up at 5am to drive your friend to the airport isn't heroic). "supererogatory" is just what duty looks like when it hurts, i.e., not a distinct category at all but the name of an overlap between a moral reason to act and a prudential reason not to.

(5) Lena came back with the flip side: if jumping in was genuinely permissible to skip, then the guy who stood there filming did nothing impermissible either, which just sounds wrong -- he's a bad person even if not blameworthy. she said maybe there's a space between "wrong" and "fine," and supererogation is the positive side of it (there's supposedly a mirror category too, acts that are bad-but-permissible, she half remembered a term for it from an ethics class). marco said if "supererogatory" is just a label for a gap between two seperate scales (blameworthiness and admirability) then it does zero explanatory work, it's just bookkeeping.
