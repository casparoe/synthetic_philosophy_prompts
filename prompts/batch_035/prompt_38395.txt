I've been following the decision-theory discussions on the rationalist corners of the internet for over a decade now — Newcomb, updatelessness, functional decision theory, and the recurring fights about Pascal's mugging — and there's a structural worry I've never seen cleanly resolved rather than waved away: every proposed acausal trade with partners much larger than ourselves has the shape of Pascal's wager. A minuscule probability of influencing an astronomically larger pool of value, multiplied out so that it dominates the decision. Critics say this is just the wager with cosmology swapped for heaven; defenders say the logical correlation between decision procedures does work that miracles and prophecies don't. I want to see where the difference actually lives, so I'm asking for a constructed case rather than another round of assertion.

Build me a thought experiment meeting these specifications. The scenario: an agent like you or me faces a potential acausal trade with a hypothetical civilization or coalition of vastly greater scale — so much larger that even a tiny probability of their cooperation outweighs everything in the agent's light cone. Constraints: (1) no anthropic uncertainty and no "I might be inside their simulation" moves — the agent knows full well it is the small, local party; (2) the logical correlation should be achievable by counterparts who model each other no better than a careful stranger reading the other side's published reasoning — no superintelligent predictors, no perfect copies; (3) the small agent's side of the trade must be an action of ordinary human scale — a career choice, a donation, a research priority — not signaling to the stars or building an AI to act on our behalf; (4) the scenario must not stipulate that the trade is rational; the verdicts have to come out of the theories, not go into the setup. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, and it should be one you could describe to a skeptical friend without them laughing at you.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. Walk through what causal, evidential, and functional decision theory each say about the case and why they say it. Then identify which structural feature of the case does the work of separating a defensible acausal trade from Pascal's wager and from Pascal's mugging — is it the boundedness of the payoff, the trackability of the logical correlation, the fact that both sides gain rather than one side threatening, or something else entirely? Tell me what happens if you clip the payoff distribution so the far side's cooperation no longer dominates the calculus: does the case collapse into an ordinary modest trade, or does it vanish? And finish with the strongest attack a defender of refusing-the-trade — someone convinced that acting on tiny probabilities of astronomical value is a decision-theoretic pathology we should train ourselves out of — could mount against your case, and whether the case survives it. I'm not looking for a survey of who has argued what over the years; I want the reasoning actually worked through, including where it gets uncomfortable.
