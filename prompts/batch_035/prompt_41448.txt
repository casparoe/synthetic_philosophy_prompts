For about half a century, philosophers have been arguing over whether there is a morally significant difference between doing and allowing — the killing/letting-die distinction, as it shows up in medical ethics, triage, and the doctrine of double effect's neighborhood. The shape of the impasse is by now familiar: one side points to matched pairs of cases, where the motive, the effort expended, and the outcome are held fixed and the only difference is whether the agent brings the harm about or merely fails to prevent it, and claims that our reluctance to treat these identically is explained by confounds — special duties, background expectations, salience — rather than by the doing/allowing distinction itself. The other side concedes the matched pairs but insists that even in them the agent who merely allows harm stands in a different relation to the outcome: the harm flows through the world rather than out of the agent, and that difference in itself matters. Neither side has moved in decades, and each appears to regard the other's central move as question-begging. My hypothesis is that this is not a substantive moral disagreement at all, but a deeper one about what counts as a moral datum: one side treats the verdicts we deliver on carefully matched cases as the data any theory must answer to, while the other treats those same verdicts as artifacts — intuitions we happen to have before theorizing, which a correct theory should explain away. Is that diagnosis right? Or is some hidden question doing the real work — perhaps about the individuation of actions (whether "refraining" is an event at all), or about whether "allowing" picks out a single category rather than a family? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a new kind of case, a conceptual distinction, results about how people divide the terrain — or an argument that no progress should be expected here.
