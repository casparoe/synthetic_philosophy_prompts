Compare the thesis that mental states (or more generally, one's overall occurrent conditions) are luminous with the KK principle, i.e., the thesis that if one knows that p, then one knows that one knows that p. The two are often run together: Williamson's margin-for-error argument against luminosity, built on someone gradually feeling warmer as a bath gets hotter, looks like a dressed-up version of the standard counterexamples to KK, and one sometimes hears anti-luminosity described as just "KK failure applied to experience." But are the two claims actually equivalent, or merely related? Pay attention to at least two structural differences: first, luminosity is defined via being in a position to know rather than actually knowing, so an agent could fail luminosity while still satisfying KK on some restricted reading, or vice versa; second, anti-luminosity is usually a local thesis about a specific range of conditions and times, whereas KK is standardly formulated as a fully general schema about any proposition at any time. Given these differences, work out what the logical relationship between the two theses really is: does the margin-for-error argument against luminosity straightforwardly generate a counterexample to KK (say, by applying it to the condition that one knows that one feels cold), or are there extra premises needed? And could a philosopher coherently hold that some conditions are luminous while rejecting KK, or reject luminosity while endorsing KK — for example, by endorsing a safety-based account of knowledge on which self-knowledge and environmental knowledge come apart? Please justify your verdict with concrete examples rather than just mapping the definitions.
