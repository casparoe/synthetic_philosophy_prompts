Last week our department hosted a long-running dispute, and as the bystander who was asked to referee, I find myself unable to do so cleanly. The participants were a colleague in political theory and a younger economist who has been working through the interwar socialist calculation debate with unusual care. The exchange ran through six distinct moves, and I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication of each, together with an assessment of what, if anything, both parties overlooked.

The economist opened by insisting that Mises's 1920 argument settles the matter: without genuine market prices for producer goods there is no common denominator against which alternative production plans can be compared, so allocation under socialism cannot be rational even in principle; and Lange's so-called market socialism, which retains markets only for consumer goods and labor while capital goods prices are set by the planning board, leaves the decisive prices untouched. (2) The theorist replied that this caricatures Lange: prices set by trial and error, raised where there is shortage and lowered where there is surplus, are not commands but feedback-driven adjustments, structurally identical to what the Walrasian auctioneer accomplishes, so the board needs no more information than the market itself uses. (3) The economist answered that this conflates the existence of equilibrium prices with their discovery — Hayek's 1945 point being that the relevant knowledge is not given to anyone but generated on the spot, in response to local and constantly changing circumstances, and that a board adjusting to aggregate shortage statistics receives stale summaries rather than the on-the-ground knowledge that markets transmit through price movements. (4) The theorist countered that the epistemic argument proves far too much: enormous corporations coordinate hundreds of thousands of employees across continents using administratively set internal transfer prices, so large-scale planning manifestly works; if tacit knowledge were as inaccessible as claimed, firms above some modest size would collapse, and they do not — which suggests the true obstacles to socialist planning are matters of bureaucratic cost and incentive design, i.e., engineering problems, and that Mises's original impossibility claim was simply an overreach. (5) The economist rejoined that the firm argument runs backwards: firms can plan internally precisely because they face external market prices and the discipline of bankruptcy, which means they are islands of planning that depend on a surrounding market sea, and a single entity spanning the whole economy would have nothing outside itself from which to learn and no selection mechanism to correct its errors. (6) The theorist's closing move was more methodological: he observed that the economist's objection had now shifted from the absence of a common denominator, to the impossibility of discovery, to the absence of selection pressure, and he demanded to know which single thesis was actually under test — if each formulation retreats when refuted, the socialist need only ever answer the currently strongest version, and the debate dissolves into an engineering discussion about institutional design.

Please adjudicate each of the six moves in turn, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating; indicate who is more persuasive overall and about precisely what; and say what, if anything, both parties are missing — in particular, whether some hinge of the original debate (for instance, whether the economy in question is stationary or changing, or whether the disagreement is ultimately about knowledge rather than incentives) would allow a cleaner verdict than either of them managed to reach. I should note that I have no stake in the outcome myself; I am chiefly interested in whether the individual dialectical moves succeed or fail on their own terms.
