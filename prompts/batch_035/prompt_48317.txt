I've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere — LessWrong in its heyday, the decision-theory-adjacent corners, the periodic posts on the value of information — and I now run a weekly decision review at a three-hundred-person company that builds logistics software. Several times a week a team comes to me with some version of the question "should we find out more before we decide?" Run another A/B test before rolling out the checkout change; commission a customer study before the rebrand; run a load test against production before the holiday traffic; do reference calls with a vendor's former customers before signing. The stakes range from a week of engineering time to an irreversible pricing commitment; the teams want an answer within a day; and the people who would apply whatever I come up with are mid-level product managers with no decision-theory background and no patience for anything that smells of a seminar.

What I want is a decision procedure my PMs can actually run, built out of the theory of the value of information — I. J. Good's result that acting with free, relevant information in hand is never worse in expectation than acting without it, together with the practical structure around that theorem: information has value only insofar as some possible result would change the action; its value is capped by the stakes; and the theorem's assumptions (the information is genuinely free, it arrives before the decision, it is used to update rather than to rationalize, and gathering it does not itself alter the situation) are precisely the assumptions that fail in practice. The procedure should translate all of this into an ordered checklist: the questions to ask about a proposed information-gathering step, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure returns a flat "no, skip the study"; and the point at which it returns "yes, but only for this subset of outcomes." The idea that a test is worth running only if some result would actually flip the decision seems to me the load-bearing insight of the whole framework, and I want it to survive the simplification. The procedure must also mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it must hand off to a human exactly where the question is one of values rather than facts — above all, whether the worst case is tolerable, which no theorem answers.

Then run the procedure on three proposals, chosen to stress it:

1. A two-week A/B test of a redesigned checkout flow before full rollout, where the cost of testing is a slower rollout and a small conversion hit on the control branch.
2. A six-figure customer research study before a rebrand that senior leadership has already all but committed to, where I suspect the study is wanted as cover rather than as input.
3. A load test against production infrastructure two weeks before peak season, where gathering the information itself carries a risk of an outage — that is, the act of finding out changes the thing you are trying to find out about.

On case 2, I expect the procedure to answer "don't run it" or "run only a stripped-down version," which is an answer I will not enjoy relaying. So tell me also how to distinguish "the information has no value because no result would change the decision" from "the information has no value because the decision has already been made and the study is theater" — the first is a fact about the decision, the second is a fact about my organization, and I need the procedure to be honest about which of the two it can actually detect.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire; what it does about numbers nobody has (our PMs will not produce priors, and any procedure that demands them will simply be ignored); and where the value-of-information framework is the wrong lens entirely. I'm told there are cases in which more information is harmful even when used perfectly, and I would like to know whether my weekly queue is likely to contain any.
