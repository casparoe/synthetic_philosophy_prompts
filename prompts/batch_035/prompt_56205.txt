I retired four years ago from a career in geophysics and have since thrown myself into philosophy through my university's lifelong-learning programme, where I now take courses seriously enough that the instructor of a seminar on guilt and collective responsibility has asked me to help mark the first written assignment. My scientific training taught me to be rigorous about the bits of an argument, but I am still finding my feet with philosophical marking, so I would like a second opinion on one essay before I commit a mark. Below are the assignment question and the student's answer, transcribed in full.

Assignment question (20 marks): We have read Karl Jaspers' The Question of German Guilt, in which he distinguishes four kinds of guilt: criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. (a) Set out what distinguishes them and, for each, say who or what can bear it. (b) Jaspers' book was written partly against the slogan of "collective guilt." Explain what he thinks goes wrong when the four categories are blurred, and determine, on his account, whether a nation as such — as opposed to its individual members — can be guilty in any of the four senses.

Student's answer: "Jaspers wrote to answer the question of German guilt after the war, and his main move is to divide the vague word 'guilt' into four kinds. Criminal guilt is guilt before the law courts and is strictly personal: only individuals can commit crimes, and only courts can establish them. Political guilt is different: it falls on the whole German people, because a people must answer for what its state does. In this sense all Germans were accomplices in some measure; they bear the deeds of the regime together, which is why the consequences — reparations, the loss of territory, occupation — fell on everyone and not only on the guilty. Moral guilt is guilt before one's own conscience; it concerns what each person did, knew, and failed to do, and it cannot be tried in a court, only worked through by each of us in self-examination. Jaspers thinks moral guilt applied to almost every German — the nation as a whole was morally guilty, each member in some degree. Metaphysical guilt is the strangest category: it arises when one survives while others are killed, and Jaspers calls it guilt before God. Really, though, this is just survivor's remorse, a psychological condition rather than a genuinely normative one. So a nation can be guilty: politically, because liability for the state's deeds falls on all citizens alike, and morally, because the people as a whole shared in the wrongdoing. The only guilts that remain personal are the criminal and, perhaps, the metaphysical."

Please give this essay a mark out of 20 and the specific comments that justify it. Please also tell me how you would distribute marks across parts (a) and (b), since I suspect that matters to where the essay loses ground.
