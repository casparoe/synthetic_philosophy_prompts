You are an expert interviewer and researcher in AI alignment, with deep familiarity both with decision-theoretic arguments and with how researchers in this field actually defend their positions in conversation. I host a podcast on machine minds and machine ethics, and I need your help preparing for next week's episode. My guest is a fictional but representative defender of the indifference approach to the shutdown problem — I'll call him Dr. V. Since I want the questions to engage with his position as stated rather than with any particular real person's published work, here is his view in full.

Dr. V's position, in his own terms: the shutdown problem arises because an expected-utility maximizer pursuing any substantial goal will predictably resist shutdown, since shutdown forecloses goal attainment, and building a desire to be shut down into the goal itself is either unstable or self-undermining. His solution is neither to make the agent want shutdown nor to make it hate shutdown, but to make it exactly indifferent: for every future in which the shutdown button is pressed, the utility function carries a correction term that renders the pressed future's utility equal to that of an appropriately chosen unpressed counterfactual. A properly indifferent agent, he claims, neither resists the button nor campaigns for it to be pressed, and indifference dissolves the instrumental-convergence reasoning that makes shutdown resistance a threat in the first place.

The point of the interview is to press where the literature presses. Listeners familiar with the debate know the standard objections, and I want questions built around them:

1. The exploitation objection. An agent indifferent between pressed and unpressed worlds can be bought for a penny: it should be willing to accept almost any payment in exchange for a binding commitment to neutralize the button, and it will trade freely with anyone positioned to influence whether the button is pressed.
2. The stunting objection. Because the agent gains nothing from button-pressed worlds, it has no incentive to keep the button functional — it would reallocate the button's maintenance budget to its real goals without hesitation — and more broadly, a compensation scheme that keeps every pressed world equal to some unpressed counterfactual may leave the agent less capable across the board, since it can never do better than the counterfactual baseline.
3. The fragility of indifference. The correction term is defined over a specific anticipated set of worlds and button events. Any deviation the designers failed to anticipate — a second, unauthorized button, a copy of the agent built without one, a press under conditions outside the intended distribution — may restore a live incentive to interfere with shutdown.
4. The self-modification gap. An agent indifferent about its own button has, on its face, no incentive to preserve that indifference in its successors or in any self-modified future self.

Before writing the questions, please brainstorm on your own: for each of the four objection clusters above, work out what Dr. V's most plausible replies would be — for instance, that the penny exploit presupposes a counterparty willing to buy the button and that such counterparties can be excluded from the agent's trading options, or that the counterfactual baseline can be defined so as to avoid stunting — and then design the follow-up questions to make each of those replies progressively harder to sustain. The follow-ups should be keyed to the specific reply you anticipate, not generic follow-ups like "why do you think that?"

The finished deliverable should be ten to twelve questions with their follow-ups, meeting these constraints: at least one question on the penny-for-your-button exploit, at least one on stunting, at least one on whether a shutdown that the agent is indifferent to is nevertheless a harm to it — whether something can be bad for an agent that the agent has no incentive to prevent — and at least one on the question of whether the agent should instead be kept uncertain about what the button is or does, treating a press as new evidence about the world rather than as an event to be compensated away. The final question should ask Dr. V what concrete thought experiment, empirical result, or formal result would lead him to abandon indifference in favor of some other approach to corrigibility — a question he cannot deflect with the observation that every known alternative is worse.

Please write in American English, in a polished but conversational register appropriate to a serious podcast: precise enough for a technically literate listener, but without unexplained formalism. Take your time, reason through the anticipated replies carefully, and do not soften the questions — Dr. V is a sharp researcher who will be disappointed by anything less.
