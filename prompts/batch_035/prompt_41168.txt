Write an essay on whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. The orthodox answer is yes: money and effort already spent are gone, and only forward-looking consequences should count. But test that orthodoxy against three cases: (i) you finish a tedious novel because you promised the friend who gave it to you a discussion of it; (ii) a government continues a war partly on the ground that stopping now would mean the soldiers who died sacrificed themselves for nothing; (iii) a negotiator who is known to throw good money after bad — to honor sunk costs come what may — makes more credible threats and extracts better terms over a lifetime of bargaining than a clean-slate reasoner does, so an agent built to honor sunk costs may outperform one built never to. For each case, say whether the rationale is really forward-looking in disguise (keeping a promise, signaling to others) or a genuine vindication of honoring sunk costs. Also weigh whether the time-slice view of persons — on which you are, in effect, a sequence of short-lived agents rather than one extended chooser — strengthens the orthodox answer, or shows that the ordinary advice to ignore sunk costs smuggles in an assumption about who the patient of the decision is. Reach a definite verdict: either the fallacy is always a fallacy, or specify the conditions under which honoring sunk costs is rational and explain why those exceptions do not swallow the rule.
