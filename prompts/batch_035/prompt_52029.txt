Last quarter our board put me in charge of drafting our company's written doctrine for responding to ransomware and other extortion, after a competitor of ours was paralyzed for three weeks over a six-figure demand. A colleague on the strategy team, who reads a lot of decision theory, talked the working group into the argument below, and when I extended it one step further it began to prove things I'm fairly sure are false. I cannot locate the failure, and I would like your help.

His argument: (1) When we can settle a policy in advance, we should choose the one whose consequences are best for us once everyone can predict it — including its effect on who gets threatened in the first place. (2) A threat is issued only if the threatener predicts compliance; a victim whose settled policy is to resist is therefore never threatened at all, and resisting costs such a victim nothing. (3) Ransomware gangs choose their targets the same way — on predictions of who will pay — so (2) applies to them as much as to anyone, and our doctrine should be a public, unbreakable refusal to pay or negotiate. (4) My extension, which seemed to follow: since the logic turns only on threateners acting on predictions about us, it does not stop at ransomware. We should adopt visible policies of maximal resistance on every front where we could conceivably be threatened; and where our resolve is genuinely weak — say, if employees' families were threatened — we should install surrogate goals, publicly binding ourselves to treat some designated decoy, a fund we would burn on demand or a rival firm we would defend, as being as important to us as the firm itself, so that every threat, on any front, meets the same resolute response.

The conclusions this forces: a hospital should refuse to pay for stolen patient records even when refusal foreseeably costs lives; parents should refuse a kidnapper's demands; every company should answer a patent troll's letter exactly as it would answer a hostage-taking. Each step looked fine to me while I wrote it out — (2) in particular, which seems right in every case I can imagine. So which step do I reject? Is there a principled difference between the threats for which (2) is true and the threats for which it is false, or does the argument instead collapse in some quieter way I have not named? And what does the rejection cost — if I give up the step you indict, do we lose deterrence altogether in the ransomware case, or retain some version of it on some other basis? I would most like to keep (1); tell me what keeping it buys and what giving up the rest costs.
