I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for a long time -- Overcoming Bias in the late 2000s, LessWrong ever since, and in recent years the Alignment Forum -- and one post from that corner that I keep returning to is Scott Garrabrant's well-known taxonomy of Goodhart failures. Paraphrasing from memory, it divides the ways a proxy measure breaks down under optimization pressure into four kinds: regressional, where the proxy is a noisy correlate of the goal and selection on the proxy partly selects for noise; extremal, where the proxy-goal correlation held in some midrange of the data but collapses once you push into the tail; causal, where the correlation between proxy and goal was real but intervening directly on the proxy severs it, so moving the proxy doesn't drag the goal along; and adversarial, where some agent notices you are optimizing the proxy and arranges to look good on it without being good. The framing I've also seen in that community's follow-up discussions -- that when you condition on a proxy, you also condition on its imperfections -- has always struck me as exactly right, and it supplies the first premise of an argument I've now half-convinced myself of, to my own alarm.

The argument runs as follows. (1) In any real system, a proxy's correlation with the goal is imperfect: never exactly one, always some gap. (2) Wherever the correlation is imperfect and anything conditions on the proxy, regressional Goodhart occurs -- no deliberate optimization is needed, just taking the cases where the proxy reads high, since those readings will include a disproportionate share of noise. (3) Every actual deployment of a metric involves conditioning on it: thresholding, ranking, allocating by score -- otherwise it wouldn't function as a metric -- so regressional Goodhart is present in every real-world case. (4) But the same conditioning is exactly what happens in the other cases too: pushing into the tail is what extremal Goodhart is, destroying the correlation by acting on the proxy is what the causal kind is, and the adversarial gamer exploits the very imperfection that produced the regression in the first place. (5) So all four kinds are simultaneously present in every real instance; no observed system wears a label saying which kind it is. (6) A classification whose kinds always co-occur is not a taxonomy of distinct phenomena but four names for one phenomenon described from different angles. (7) Therefore the fourfold taxonomy is idle bookkeeping, and the alignment literature that assigns different remedies to the different kinds -- tighter estimators for the regressional sort, distrust of extrapolation for the extremal sort, worry about agents for the adversarial sort -- is trading on a distinction without a difference.

My honest reaction is that this proves too much: the taxonomy has been genuinely useful to me for years, and I suspect the answer lies in denying (6) -- that co-occurrence at a single operating point doesn't mean the kinds fail to differ in how they respond to changes in pressure, or in the counterfactual structure of the failure -- but I cannot for the life of me articulate what exactly is supposed to be wrong with the slide from (5) to (6), and I can equally imagine the real problem being that (4) quietly equivocates, lumping together conditioning that merely happens with conditioning whose point is selection. Tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked so innocent when I first wrote it down, and say what rejecting it costs me -- because if I have to give up (2) or (3), the whole picture of Goodhart as ubiquitous that I've carried around from a decade of reading these blogs goes with it, and I'd like to know that in advance.
