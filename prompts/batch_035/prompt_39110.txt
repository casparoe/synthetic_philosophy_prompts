I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been reading around Keynes's Treatise on Probability after a colleague mentioned that his notion of "uncertainty" is nothing like the uncertainty I deal with in probabilistic systems. I've got a rough grip on his basic picture — probability as a logical relation between a hypothesis and the evidence for it, rather than a frequency or a betting price, with the consequence that some probabilities can't be assigned numbers and can't even be ranked against each other — but I'm stuck on his separate notion of the "weight" of an argument. As far as I can tell, two arguments can support their conclusions equally strongly, while one rests on a lot of relevant evidence and the other on very little, and Keynes flags weight as a distinct dimension that his formal calculus doesn't capture. Please explain, carefully and in British English: first, what weight is supposed to add over probability, and why Keynes thinks an increase in relevant evidence always raises weight even when it leaves the probability unchanged or even lowers it; second, the question Keynes himself raises about whether new knowledge can ever diminish weight — I gather his initial slogan about weight always increasing turns out to be less tidy than it sounds, and I'd like to understand where the complication comes from; and third, whether the kind of "uncertainty" he emphasises decades later in his economics — situations where we simply have no basis for forming any probability at all — is best understood as the low-weight end of the scale from the Treatise, or as something categorically different, given that in the Treatise there are already probabilities that are non-numerical and mutually incomparable. As a rough anchor, it would help to contrast weight with the nearest things in my own world, such as the width of a Bayesian credible interval or the confidence score a classifier attaches to a prediction, and to say explicitly where those analogies break down.
