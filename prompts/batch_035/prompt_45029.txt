I teach an introductory ethics course for second-year students with no philosophy background beyond a semester of applied topics. Next week I have a single seventy-five-minute seminar on the procreation asymmetry — roughly, the thought that creating a person whose life would go badly is seriously wrong in a way that creating a person whose life would go well is not correspondingly required, so that the badness and the goodness of new lives cannot just be weighed against each other on a single scale. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the asymmetry itself. In particular, tell me: (1) the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why — especially whether the distinction between making existing people better off and bringing into existence people whose lives go well has to be secured before the asymmetry is even stated, and whether my co-teacher is right that the non-identity problem should open the session or whether it should be cut entirely; (2) what the carrying example should be — I am tempted to use a municipal government choosing between subsidizing fertility treatment for involuntarily childless couples and expanding a pediatric ward, since both options are sympathetic and neither side is a cartoon, but assess whether that case actually has the right features or whether it smuggles in confounds (the beneficiaries on one side exist and on the other do not) that will confuse more than they carry, and propose a replacement if so; (3) the misconceptions students will predictably form and where in the sequence each should be headed off — my own guesses are that the asymmetry will be heard as flat antinatalism, as the claim that additional happy lives are not good at all, or as simply total utilitarianism running in reverse, and I would like to know whether to expect others; (4) one question I can pose at the end that would show me whether they can apply the asymmetry to a case they have not seen, rather than merely nodding along to it; and (5) what I should openly refuse to bring in at this level — the repugnant conclusion, average versus total views, anything else — and how to say so in one honest sentence without making the students feel shortchanged. Bear in mind that the students arrive directly from a unit on Singer's famine argument, so their working picture of morality is entirely about duties to people who already exist; I want to know whether that inheritance is scaffolding to build on or a habit I have to break in the first ten minutes.
