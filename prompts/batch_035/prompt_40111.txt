I'm giving a talk at my city's Effective Altruism meetup next month arguing that the neglectedness factor in the importance-tractability-neglectedness framework is doing something sneakier than it appears. Neglectedness is usually operationalized as how few resources currently flow to a cause, but in practice it functions as a proxy for the marginal impact of an additional dollar or hour, and the inference from one to the other smuggles in an efficient-markets assumption: that if a cause were genuinely high-impact at the margin, resources would already have flooded in. That assumption is obviously false in some places (wild-animal welfare, pre-2020 pandemic preparedness) and plausibly true in others, so ITN-based cross-cause rankings are quietly comparing numbers computed under different implicit assumptions about how fast capital and labor move. My proposed repair is to split the single neglectedness factor into two: an absolute-resource term and a friction term capturing why resources fail to flow even when the cause looks good — funder irrationality, measurement problems, coordination failures, moral circle limits. The audience will be about fifteen people: mostly long-time meetup regulars who have internalized 80,000 Hours-style cause profiles and will feel I'm attacking the community's shared calculator, a couple of economics graduate students who know the efficient-markets literature and will enjoy pressing on exactly that, and one person who works in global health logistics and thinks the whole framework is EA navel-gazing compared to just doing straightforward cost-effectiveness estimates. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections roughly in the order they'll arrive, phrased the way each of these groups would actually phrase them, ranked by how much damage each does to my case, with a short reply to each, a note on where to concede and where to hold, and the softest point in my own position that I should pre-emptively defend rather than hope nobody notices.
