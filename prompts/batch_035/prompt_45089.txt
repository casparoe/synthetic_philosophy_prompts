I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole about how other democracies do things, and something struck me as strange. In the UK you can't actually vote for or against the Prime Minister at all — the ballot just lists your local MP candidates, and the PM is whoever ends up commanding a majority in the Commons. So the head of government is basically twice removed from the voters, and the governing party can swap that person out mid-term through its own internal rules with no input from the electorate. This has happened a bunch of times in living memory, sometimes handing the country a new PM chosen by dues-paying party members who are a tiny fraction of the population. Germany has an interesting twist on this too: parliament can't just remove a chancellor, it has to elect a replacement in the same vote. Meanwhile in the US, removing a president mid-term is nearly impossible by design — impeachment needs a supermajority in the Senate and is treated as an emergency measure for grave misconduct, not a routine fact of political life. And it cuts the other way with election timing: presidential systems fix the date so the incumbent can't game it, while a PM can often call a snap election when the polls look good. So my question is what theory of democratic mandate the parliamentary setup presupposes. It seems like it must rest on the idea that the mandate belongs to the party and its programme rather than to a person — that voters are choosing a government, not a leader. But is that even consistent with how these systems campaign now, where elections get sold to voters as a personal choice between two named leaders? And is the presidential side genuinely commited to a personal mandate, given that you can win the presidency while losing the popular vote? I'd also like to know whether the parliamentary practice actually requires the party-mandate theory or whether several different views of representation would license it just as well, and what would concretely have to change in the practice if a country really started treating the mandate as personal.
