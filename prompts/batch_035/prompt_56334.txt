For context, here is the exam question as it appeared on the midterm:

Question 4. The Uniform Determination of Death Act provides that "An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead." Critics have complained that death, of all states, should not be the kind of thing that can be established by disjunctive criteria, as if "dead" meant one thing in the ICU and another thing at the accident scene. (a) Explain the rationale behind the whole-brain criterion as the President's Commission understood it, making clear how it differs both from the circulatory criterion and from a "higher-brain" criterion keyed to consciousness or personhood. (b) Explain how donation after circulatory death (DCD) puts pressure on the word "irreversible" in clause (1). (c) In light of (a) and (b), assess whether the Act's disjunction tracks a single concept of death or two.

And here, transcribed verbatim, is the student's answer:

"The whole-brain criterion was developed by the Harvard committee in 1968 and then given legal form by the President's Commission in 1981. The Harvard report already defined death as the death of the person rather than the body, and the Commission continued this line: the whole-brain criterion is ultimately grounded in the loss of the person, since once consciousness is permanently lost the person is gone, and the brain stem is written into the statute only as a diagnostic safety margin so that death is not declared in marginal or transient cases. This is what separates whole-brain death from a persistent vegetative state like Karen Ann Quinlan's, where the brain stem continues to function: the patient in a PVS is not dead, because the lower brain still works. The circulatory criterion is older and more conservative: it identifies death with the stopping of the heart and breathing, and it remains the default outside the hospital and in most of the world. Part (b): in DCD, the patient has severe neurological injury but does not meet brain-death criteria, so the transplant team waits until the family agrees to withdraw support, the ventilator is removed, the heart stops, and after a short waiting period of a few minutes death is declared so that the organs can be recovered before they deteriorate. The waiting period is chosen to make sure the heart does not restart on its own. In this setting, irreversible cessation just means permanent cessation: once the heart will not restart spontaneously and nobody intends to restart it, the cessation counts as irreversible in the sense the Act requires, which is why DCD is uncontroversial from a legal standpoint. Part (c): the two clauses of the Act can come apart in time. A patient on a ventilator can lose all brain function while the heart continues to beat, so clause (2) is satisfied hours or days before clause (1) ever could be; conversely, in DCD clause (1) is satisfied while some brain cells are still alive. Since the same patient can satisfy one clause and not the other, the Act must be tracking two distinct concepts of death. Therefore there are in fact two deaths, and the law should say so explicitly rather than pretending there is one."

A few notes on where my own hesitation lies, so you can check me rather than defer to me: I suspect the answer conflates the Commission's actual rationale (loss of integrated functioning of the organism as a whole) with a personhood rationale the Commission pointedly declined to adopt; I suspect the Harvard attribution is a misreading; the "irreversible means permanent" move in part (b) strikes me as the exact conflation the question was fishing for rather than a resolution of it; and the inference in part (c) from divergent timing to two concepts looks like a non sequitur dressed up as a conclusion. But I may be over-reading a compressed exam answer, and I want the grade and comments to reflect what the student actually got wrong, not what I would have written.
