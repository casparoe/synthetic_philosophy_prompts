Quick background before the ask: I'm teaching a second-year political theory course, and next week's single 50-minute lecture has to carry the whole negative vs republican liberty distinction (no discussion section till the week after, so it all rides on this one session). About 60 students. They've all had the Berlin two-concepts thing in intro, but thinly — they mostly remember "negative = no interference, positive = self-mastery" and, depending on their politics, that positive liberty is either communism or therapy.

What I want from you is a plan for the lecture, not the lecture itself. Specifically:

1. Ordering. What depends on what? My instinct is to set up Berlin's negative liberty as the foil first and then complicate it, but I can see that backfiring — if non-domination arrives as just an amendment, they'll file it as a footnote, and if it arrives as a revolution, they'll file it as a mood. Take a position on the sequence and defend it.

2. The carrying example. The classic one is the slave with the perfectly considerate master who never actually interferes. I'm half worried it's doing too much at once (historical baggage, students going numb, the rhetorical question "would you trade your freedom for this?" doing the work instead of the concept). My alternative is a modern case: the at-will employee whose boss could fire her tomorrow for no reason and never has. Decide whether to keep the classic, replace it, or pair them — and tell me what whatever example you pick must have, and what it must *not* have, to do its job.

3. Misconceptions, with locations. The ones I'm bracing for: (a) students shelve non-domination under positive liberty because both sound "deeper" than the plain negative kind; (b) reading non-domination as "nobody is stronger than you," which turns it into a claim about power parity rather than arbitrary power; (c) treating it as a welfare claim — the considerate master is fine because the slave is comfortable. For each, say where in the sequence it should be headed off, not left to the reading.

4. A check. One question I can ask in the last five minutes that would tell me whether they actually got the distinction vs. just learned two new jargon words.

Also be honest about what to cut at this level and why. I'm tempted to gesture at the republican tradition for pedigree (Machiavelli, the Atlantic republics) and at the Pettit-style reply to the objection that non-domination collapses into non-interference once you count threats and latent powers as interferences. Tell me wether either earns its minutes.

One more thing: I know there are live scholarly fights about whether republican liberty is genuinely a third category or a refinement of negative liberty, and I don't want a survey of positions. Pick the framing you'd actually bet on for this audience and commit to it. No readings — the syllabus is already set.
