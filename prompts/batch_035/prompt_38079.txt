My housemate, who is doing a philosophy of mind course, keeps bringing up the color phi phenomenon at dinner, and we've ended up in a genuine argument that I'd like to see settled in writing. The setup, as she explains it: in the color phi illusion, two dots in different colors (say, red and green) are flashed in quick succession at different positions on a screen, and subjects report seeing a single dot moving from the first position to the second while changing color midway — even though no dot is ever displayed in the middle location, let alone a color-changing one. The philosophically interesting question is when the apparent midway color change is experienced. Dennett distinguishes two readings of what the brain might be doing: on the "Stalinesque" reading, the brain holds back information about the second flash, edits it into a smooth narrative, and only then presents the edited version to consciousness, so that experience itself contains the illusory midpoint; on the "Orwellian" reading, the subject momentarily has a veridical-ish experience of two discrete flashes, but a memory-rewriting process kicks in almost immediately afterwards, overwriting the original with the motion-and-color-change narrative, so the subject sincerely but falsely reports (and remembers) having seen the change.

I find the Stalinesque story almost unbelievable, since it seems to require the brain to know about the second flash before consciously registering the first, and my housemate thinks the Orwellian story is equally absurd because it requires a subject to have had an experience that is then wiped without a trace — she asks, rhetorically, what could even count as evidence that such an experience occurred, if no report, memory, or behavioral trace of it survives? For her part, she accuses me of smuggling in a "Cartesian theater" where a finished edit is screened for an inner audience, and I suspect her position relies on an over-strong verificationism about consciousness.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Take a clear stance on whether the Stalinesque or the Orwellian interpretation is preferable, or whether the dichotomy itself is confused (Dennett himself, I gather, argues the two interpretations are indistinguishable in principle and that this indistinguishability undermines the idea that there is a determinate fact about "when" the color change is experienced). Where helpful, you may bring in related material such as the apparent time-order effects in Libet-style experiments or the cutaneous "rabbit" illusion, but keep the focus on the phi case. The essay should weigh the strongest considerations on each side, engage directly with the worry that either story posits something undetectable, and end with a defended verdict rather than a shrug.
