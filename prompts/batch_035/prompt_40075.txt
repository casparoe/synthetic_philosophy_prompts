So here's my situation. I write novels — mid-list, small press, you almost certainly haven't read me, that's fine — and earlier this year I got roped onto the board of a nonprofit that runs a rural residency for writers. Six cabins, four weeks each, historically no strings attached: you show up, you sleep, you stare at a lake, you write or you don't. Some of our best alumni did their best work there and some spent the whole month recovering from burnout, and honestly we've never been able to tell the two apart until years later, which is kind of the point of the place, I always thought.

Anyway, money. We're broke, as residencies are, and a foundation has offered to fund five residencies a year for three years on one condition: residents must commit to a "structured working schedule" of at least 30 hours a week of logged writing time, with a deliverable — a minimum word count or a completed draft — submitted at the end, failure to meet it means we have to return a prorated chunk of the grant. The foundation's people were very polite about it but the logic was basically: we're paying for the production of literature, not for people to nap in the woods. One board member (a retired management consultant, love him, exhausting) is strongly in favor and says our old model is just romanticized leisure funded by other people's generosity. A couple of the poets are ready to walk over it. I'm supposed to write the recommendation memo that goes out before our next meeting and I keep going back and forth.

The thing I keep chewing on is that this is obviously not just a logistics question. The donor's conditions are basically the Protestant work ethic with a spreadsheet — work as a calling, output as the measure of worth, idle hours as something that has to be justified. And there's a real critique of that whole picture: the karoshi phenomenon in Japan, where people literally die from overwork, is one endpoint of taking logged hours as the measure; the French 35-hour week experiment was, whatever its economic ups and downs, an attempt to say that the point of an economy is to protect time that isn't work. On the other hand I don't want to pretend the retreat is sacred — writers are professional procrastinators, "reverie" can be a euphemism for drinking, and a funder asking what they get for their money isn't crazy. And there's a middle position some of us have floated: take the money but define "deliverable" loosely (e.g. a process journal or an end-of-stay conversation instead of a word count) and see if the foundation bites.

So what I need from you: draft the memo I can circulate to the board. It has to actually recommend something — accept the conditions as offered, reject the grant, or counter with a modified version — with the reasoning laid out so the pro-structure faction can't just dismiss it as poetry-people sentimentality, and with a fair shake to the strongest point on the other side (which I think is that accountability isn't inherently anti-art, plenty of great books were written under contract deadlines). If you can weave in the broader question — whether unstructured time is genuinely productive in a way that output metrics can't see, or whether that's a story leisure-class institutions tell themselves — without turning the memo into a philosophy lecture, that would be great. Keep it to something a board member will actually read before a Thursday meeting. And maybe don't quote any dead germans at me directly, paraphrase, it reads less pretensious.
