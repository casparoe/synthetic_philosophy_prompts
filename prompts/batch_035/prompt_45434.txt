I teach a 200-level course on contemporary moral problems at a mid-sized public university. Next week's unit is on Peter Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1972), including the shallow-pond case. There are 19 students enrolled, and we meet once for 75 minutes; the students will have read the paper beforehand. I would like to spend most of the session on an activity rather than on lecture or discussion, and I want the activity designed so that the students run into the philosophical problem through their own choices, not through anything I tell them.

The effect I am after is this: the students should discover, from their own recorded behavior, which factors actually govern their willingness to bear costs for strangers, and only afterwards see how those factors line up against the two versions of Singer's principle. To arrange this, the game's parameters should vary independently across rounds: (a) the size of the cost a rescuer must bear, (b) how many other players are equally well positioned to rescue, and (c) whether the need is presented as a single vivid person or as a statistical aggregate. In addition, every student should enter the game with a private, token-funded project of their own that pays off at the end only if they retain enough tokens, so that at some point each student faces a rescue decision that would, if made, forfeit the project outright.

Please provide the full design: rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, a payoff scheme requiring no real money, and instructions for what I should record about each round. Then a debrief plan that does three things. First, it should use the recorded choices, round by round, to ask whether giving tracked the cost parameter or instead tracked (b) and (c) — the factors Singer insists are morally irrelevant. Second, it should locate, for the class as a whole, the point at which students crossed, or refused to cross, the line between Singer's strong principle, which forbids sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, and the weaker version he concedes to those unwilling to accept the strong one. Third, it should map the first moment a student asks, in effect, why me rather than them onto the debate over fair shares: whether my duty is lessened by others' noncompliance, and whether what looks like an agreed division of labor is really just mutual excuse.

For reference, the strong principle reads: "if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it." The qualified version, offered later in the same discussion, reads: "if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything morally significant, we ought, morally, to do it."

Finally, include a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle it, and a candid note on the features of the real problem — its scale, its irreversibility, and the fact that the need never stops presenting itself — that a one-off classroom game with tokens cannot reproduce, so that I know in advance which parts of the idea I will have to supply myself in the debrief.

Please write the entire answer in American English.
