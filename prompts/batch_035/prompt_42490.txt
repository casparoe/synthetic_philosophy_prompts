I run a philosophy of biology seminar for grad students and advanced undergrads, and this spring I want to break our usual routine. Every time we do the units of selection debate, we end up doing the same museum tour: Dawkins's gene-level bookkeeping, group selection's death and rehabilitation, kin selection and inclusive fitness, Okasha on evolutionary transitions, Godfrey-Smith on Darwinian populations. It's all good material but the students come away thinking the interesting stuff ended sometime around 2010, and I half suspect that's true and half suspect it's very far from true. So instead of another survey, I want the seminar to actually pick one or two open questions and work on them for real — a dozen students, one semester, only a couple of them with any real population genetics chops (there's one grad student whose actually comfortable with the Price equation, the rest are armchair types like me).

Help me build that agenda. What are the genuinely unresolved questions in this literature right now, and which two or three should we prioritize? Candidates I'm considering include: whether the kin selection / multilevel selection "equivalence" results show the two frameworks are just notational variants or whether one of them carves the causal structure better; whether the "bookkeeping" arguments for genic selection ever establish more than an accounting convention, and whether accounting conventions can themselves matter scientifically; how far the evolutionary-transitions framework stretches when you push it past organisms — to cancer lineages within a body, to clonal selection in the immune system, to gene drives that hijack meiosis; and whether Godfrey-Smith's idea of ranking populations on a continuum of "Darwinianness" actually dissolves the units question or just restates it more vaguely. Feel free to add things I'm not listing, and to tell me which of my candidates are effectively settled or were never really open in the first place.

Defend the ordering — I care more about the reasoning than the list. What makes a question worth a semester of mostly non-mathematical philosophy students: is it upstream of the others, is it tractable without heavy formal machinery, has it been neglected while everyone relitigates the same Dawkins-vs-Gould stuff? And where would you say a question looks open but is really just ambiguous words? One more thing: at the end, tell me straight how confident you are in the ranking, and single out the point you'd be least surprised to find out you got wrong — I don't want to march my students into a question that some paper I haven't read already put to bed.
