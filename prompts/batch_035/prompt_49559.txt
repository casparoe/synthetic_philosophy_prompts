**Heritability is not what the number sounds like**

Every few months an internet argument lands on a number: heritability. "Height is 0.8 heritable in developed countries," someone says, and then both sides use it wrong. Side one reads it as "80% of any given person's height comes from their genes." Side two reads it as "so interventions can't do much, it's mostly genetic." Both readings are wrong, and the way they're wrong is actually the interesting part.

What heritability actually measures: take a population, measure a trait, and decompose the *variation* in that trait. How much of the variance tracks genetic differences between people in that population, how much tracks environmental differences. It's a variance decomposition over a population, not a statement about any individual, and not a statement about "genes vs. environment" in some general sense.

The analogy I want to run with is from work. We have a dashboard for request latency. Latency varies across requests. Some of the variance maps to which code path a request hits — some handlers are just slow — and some maps to network jitter. Suppose that split came out 80/20, code vs. jitter. What would that tell you? In this deployment, under this load, code differences dominate the spread. It would NOT tell you: (a) that any single request's latency is "80% caused by code" — each request's latency is caused by whichever handler it actually hit and whatever the network actually did; (b) that the split would survive moving the cluster to a congested network. It wouldn't — same code, new environment, the decomposition flips; (c) anything at all about why our average latency is worse than a competitor's. That's a comparison between two deployments, and it needs its own decomposition, which could come out any way at all even if both deployments are 80/20 internally.

Height is the textbook case because we can watch the number move. In rich countries, where nutrition and childhood disease are roughly equalized, genetic differences dominate the variance and heritability comes out high. In a population where some kids are malnourished, environment dominates and the number is much lower. Same trait, same biology, very different number — because heritability is a property of the population-environment mix, not of the trait.

And that's what makes the second wrong reading backwards. "High heritability, so environment doesn't matter" gets it exactly inverted: high heritability often shows up *because* the environment has been equalized. The genetic variance was always there; it only dominates once the environmental variance shrinks. (I've seen this connected to why the height number went up over the 20th century as malnutrition receded — I'm hazy on the details and whether that's a documented pattern or something I half-remember, so someone who knows should check me.) Also, and this is the part people on side one miss: even a perfect 1.0 heritability within a population says nothing about differences *between* populations or groups, because those are seperate populations and each needs its own decomposition. This is, I gather, the actual technical content behind the standard "within-group heritability doesn't license between-group claims" line, which usually gets asserted rather than explained.
