A guy in my logic study group has turned Skolemist on us and none of us can quite put a finger on what's wrong with the argument. Here it is as he tells it:

(1) Downward Löwenheim–Skolem: any countable first-order theory with an infinite model has a model whose domain is countable (it can literally be the natural numbers with some funny interpretations of the symbols).
(2) ZFC, if consistent, is a countable first-order theory with infinite models.
(3) So there's a countable model M of ZFC.
(4) ZFC proves that the reals are uncountable, so M satisfies "the reals are uncountable" — the version of the reals living inside M comes out uncountable according to M.
(5) But that set is a subset of M, and M is countable, so there are only countably many of those "reals." Period.
(6) So the same collection is both countable and uncountable, which just means countability isn't a property of collections at all — it depends on which bijections exist where, i.e. where you're standing when you count.
(7) And since anything worth calling a mathematical object can be built inside ZFC, there's always a standpoint from which everything comes out countable. So the whole hierarchy of infinite sizes is bookkeeping from some perspective or other, not a fact about the things themselves.

He says even Skolem drew a lesson sort of like this from his own theorem, which doesn't help our mood.

What gets me is that the same move seems to run backwards. Upward Löwenheim–Skolem says a theory with an infinite model also has models of every bigger infinite size, so there's also a standpoint from which "the reals" come out absurdly enormous. If sliding to the countable model gets "everything is really countable," sliding to the huge ones should get "everything is really enormous," and I don't know how he holds onto (7) while that's on the table. It feels like his argument proves way too much — cardinality theorems don't act model-relative when we actually prove them, and if the argument worked it seems like it would wreck first-order logic wholesale, not just deflate the infinities.

So: which step do I reject? My instinct is that something fishy happens between (4) and (5), like "countable" switches meaning on us mid-argument, but he insists both claims are just plain true, and the annoying thing is that as far as I can tell he's right about that too. Walk me through where it actually goes wrong, why the bad step looked fine (it looked fine to me for a week), and what the standard escape routes each cost — e.g. insisting cardinality is only ever relative to a model, or saying second-order quantification fixes it, or whatever you'd say the "from outside M" in step (5) is secretly doing. If you think (6) is where the real slide is, from "depends on standpoint" to "isn't real," say that too, but tell me what Im giving up if I keep some absolute notion of countability and I only ever get to use it from a standpoint I can't formalize.
