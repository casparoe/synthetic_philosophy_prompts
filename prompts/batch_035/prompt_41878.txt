I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but a question from my own life got me reading philosophy papers, and I've discovered that the debate I wandered into has apparently been stalled for about forty years. The question is whether genuine self-deception is even possible. Here's the paradox as I understand it: to deceive someone, you have to know the truth, since you need it to construct the lie; but if you're both the deceiver and the deceived, you'd have to know and not know the same fact at the same time, which looks like a contradiction. One camp answers that the puzzle only looks paradoxical because we insist on a single unified mind: roughly following Sartre's idea of bad faith and Davidson's suggestion that the mind can be partitioned, they say the intention to deceive lives in one compartment and the resulting ignorance in another, so self-deception is real, intentional, and just structurally odd. The other camp, the deflationists, think that's overengineering: what actually happens, they say, is ordinary motivated reasoning — you never form an intention to lie to yourself, you just give convenient evidence more weight, avoid situations where inconvenient evidence might show up, and ask yourself "can I believe this?" instead of "must I believe this?" The bias does the work cold, with no little inner schemer required.

I came to this through an embarrassingly concrete case: for about six months I believed my side project was two weeks from shipping, and every week I found some reason to reset the estimate. When I looked up the literature to figure out which camp I belonged to, I found that the intentionalists and the deflationists have been trading the same points since the 1980s without either side conceding anything, and the exchanges have a scripted quality: one side says "your cold biases can't explain the selectivity and timing of real cases," the other says "your partitioned mind is a mystery mechanism invented to save the paradox," and repeat. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is at bottom a verbal disagreement: the intentionalists are protecting a richer concept — the self-deception we hold people responsible for, the one connected to agency — while the deflationists are studying a psychological process, and each side keeps refuting the other by its own standard for what deserves the name.

Please tell me whether that diagnosis is right, or whether there's a genuine substantive disagreement hidden here instead — maybe about the facts of how biased belief formation actually works, or about whether "intention" itself picks out something coherent at the level below conscious deliberation. In answering, describe what each camp actually claims rather than just labeling them, and use their positions as the evidence for your diagnosis. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress on this debate: a stipulated split between two concepts, some specific empirical finding about motivated reasoning, a formal model of bias, or a change in the question — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please write your answer in American English.
