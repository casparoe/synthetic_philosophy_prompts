My book group spent a month on Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, and we finished with a dispute between two members, Nadia and Theo, that I was asked to referee and couldn't. Some background the argument presupposes: Keynes forecast that, a hundred years on, productivity gains would essentially solve the economic problem, that the work week would shrink to around fifteen hours, and that society would undergo a moral revaluation — the pursuit of money as an end in itself would come to seem a semi-pathological appetite, and people would admire instead those who had learned to live wisely and pleasantly. We had also read his 1938 talk My Early Beliefs, in which he reflects on the influence of G. E. Moore's ethics on his Cambridge circle — the conviction that what ultimately matters is states of mind, friendship, and the enjoyment of beauty rather than rule-following or worldly success. With that in place, the exchange went as follows.

(1) Nadia opened by saying that Keynes's forecast refutes itself within his own essay, because he conceded there that some human needs are insatiable: needs satisfied not absolutely but only in comparison with what others have. Once you admit that, the economic problem can never be retired, because however much absolute abundance arrives, people will still compete for relative position, and relative position is definitionally scarce. Money-love therefore stays rational indefinitely, hours don't fall, and the moral revaluation never gets its trigger.

(2) Theo replied that this treats the essay as a prediction about human nature when it is really a prediction about consequences of productivity, and that by the productivity measure it came astonishingly close: output per hour grew roughly on the trajectory Keynes extrapolated, and hours did fall steeply for decades — just unevenly, and with a plateau. The residual gap between a fifteen-hour and a forty-hour week, he said, is explained by coordination failures and institutional bundling (employers sell labor in fixed lumps; positional spending on housing and schooling ratchets; norms equate hours with virtue), not by anything Keynes got wrong about what people ultimately want. So the diagnosis survives as an institutional one.

(3) Nadia said the hours question was never the point: the real prophecy was the moral one, and that one failed flatly. Keynes expected abundance to make society embarrassed by wealth-seeking, and what we instead have, with the deadline essentially upon us, is perhaps the least embarrassed wealth-worship in history. If the revaluation was the load-bearing prediction, its failure cannot be excused by partial success on a proxy.

(4) Theo answered that the revaluation did occur — in one population. Keynes's own circle, and Bloomsbury generally, actually lived the good life he described: they treated friendship, conversation, and beauty as the serious goods and money as a tool. In the 1938 talk Keynes more or less admitted his error, which was assuming the mores of a tiny group of Cambridge apostles raised on Moore would generalize to mankind. So Keynes was right that the good life he described is available under abundance and chosen by real people; what failed was contagion, not content. And since Keynes himself diagnosed this in print eight years after the essay, Theo argued, it is unfair to score the forecast against a blindness its author had already corrected.

(5) Nadia's last move was to say that if this is the defense, the essay is not a forecast at all but a sermon — an exhortation dressed in the borrowed authority of extrapolation — and a sermon that presupposes an audience that doesn't exist shouldn't get credit for being half-right. Theo's closing reply was that using forecast as a rhetorical device to make a moral possibility vivid is exactly what the essay set out to do, that making a possibility vivid to people who might adopt it is how such possibilities get adopted at all, and that the fact we spent our book group's month arguing about it is the essay still doing its work ninety-plus years later.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is basically sound, basically mistaken, or right about one thing and wrong about another, with your reasoning. Where it matters to the verdict, indicate what you take the Moorean background to be doing in the essay. Then say who, if either, is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed — in particular whether there is a reading of the essay under which Nadia's opening concession about relative needs and Theo's closing claim about rhetorical purpose can both be correct.
