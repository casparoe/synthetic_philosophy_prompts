I sit on a municipal Commemorative Names Review Panel, and we are deadlocked on a case I have to put in writing. The case: Cobb Avenue, named after a Progressive-era mayor, Hollis Cobb, who created the city's water system, its park network, and its professional civil service — and who also authored the city's racial zoning ordinance and directed the clearance of two Black neighborhoods, correspondence later published showing he regarded the clearance, not the water works, as his proudest achievement. The avenue runs directly through land emptied by that clearance; descendants of the displaced families still live nearby. Our panel has narrowed the dispute to three questions we cannot resolve by intuition, and the memo must take a position on each:

1. The honorific question. Defenders of the name argue that honorific naming fades into mere reference: most residents under forty cannot identify who Cobb was, and a name spoken by people who don't know its referent performs no act of praise. One commissioner has proposed formalizing this as a "stranger test": if a typical resident asked to explain the name would shrug, the name no longer honors anything, and the offense claimed by the descendants mislocates the wrong. The memo must say whether this test is sound, or whether it proves too much — whether forgetting can itself be the injury, so that the remedy for a dead name is re-educating it into a live one rather than leaving it inert.

2. The record question. The local historical society proposes retaining the name with a substantial interpretive installation, on the ground that renaming is archival erasure: the street is itself a document, and we do not rewrite documents we regret. Opponents reply that a street is not read like a document; it is spoken, typed, and dispatched, by people who never consented to invoke the man daily. The memo should address this asymmetry directly — between an installation that only those who seek it out will encounter, and a name that is compulsory in everyday speech.

3. The divisibility question. A hybrid has been floated: that the panel can honor Cobb pro tanto — for the water works and the parks — while explicitly disclaiming the zoning, in the text of a plaque. The memo must say whether honor is divisible in this way, or whether honoring the man for anything necessarily honors the man.

Please draft the panel's decision memo: a concrete recommendation (full renaming, retention with the interpretive installation, or a hybrid — for example, renaming the street while relocating the name to the city museum with a full account of the clearance), an implementation the city could actually schedule, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, not a token paragraph. The memo should close with a separate section marking which of the positions taken rest on points of broad agreement among people who study the ethics of commemoration, and which rest on genuinely contested ground — the divisibility of honor, in particular, should not be presented as if there were a settled answer when there is not.
