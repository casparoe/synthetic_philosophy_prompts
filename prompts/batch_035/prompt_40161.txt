I've been asked to circulate a position statement before a philosophy of mind reading group takes up eliminative materialism, so the others can press on it. Before sending it around, I would like an audit of whether the views I've listed actually hang together. Here is the statement as drafted:

1. I accept eliminative materialism about the propositional attitudes, in the vein of Paul Churchland: "belief" and "desire" are theoretical posits of folk psychology, that theory is deeply mistaken about the causes of behavior, and strictly speaking there are no beliefs or desires — in humans, in animals, or in machines.
2. I hold that introspection provides no counterexample to this. Our apparent awareness of our own attitudes is itself shaped by the folk theory, so "I can just tell that I believe things" carries no more weight than any other fragment of that theory.
3. I nonetheless expect belief/desire vocabulary to remain permanently entrenched in ordinary life as a stable, useful fiction — closer to "the sun rises at six," which we keep saying even though astronomy has shown there is no rising, than to "phlogiston," which vanished once chemistry caught up.
4. I think self-deception is a real and important phenomenon, and the best analysis of it is roughly this: the person holds a belief they are motivated not to avow, that belief quietly influences their behavior and their sincere testimony, and it coexists with another belief they do avow.
5. I am a realist about sensations: there really is something it is like to be in pain, and I take eliminative materialism to be silent on this — its target is the attitudes, not qualia or felt states.

Please go through these pair by pair and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look to be, what the cheapest repair for each genuine tension would be and what that repair costs, and finally which single revision to the set would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and show the reasoning at each step rather than just issuing verdicts. One further point: if some pair turns out to be in tension only given an auxiliary assumption I haven't listed, so that the tension could be dissolved by adding a clause rather than giving up any commitment, flag that explicitly — I'm aware that can look like cheating, and I'd like to know when the cheapest repair is of that form and when it is not.
