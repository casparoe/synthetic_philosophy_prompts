I'm a graduate student deciding whether to spend the next decade of my career on string-theoretic model-building, and my adviser keeps defending his confidence not with predictions or data but with what he calls indirect indicators: the absence of any alternatives, the surprising coherence of the internal requirements the theory has to satisfy, and the fact that successful theories in nearby territory were developed by similar communities in similar circumstances. Before I either adopt this style of argument or dismiss it as motivated reasoning, I want the full specification it has to meet, because I suspect the criteria are where the real content is, not the examples.

So write the desiderata list: what a methodology of confirming theories without new empirical tests would have to deliver to count as genuinely extending our evidence rather than rationalizing our commitments. The requirements I'd expect include, at least, an account of how the indicator correlates with truth independently of our desire for the theory to be true (the no-alternatives argument, for instance, only works if theory-space is somehow constrained, so the method owes us a story about why the community's failure to find alternatives tracks the world rather than the sociology of the field); an answer to the look-elsewhere worry, since a theory that can absorb any future result -- as critics say the string landscape can -- doesn't gain support from its predecessors' merely not being refuted; a treatment of the base-rate question, i.e., how often in the history of physics have non-empirical indicators of this kind been followed by empirical vindication versus quietly abandoned; and a way of making the confidence graded and decision-relevant, since a justification that yields only all-or-nothing belief can't sensibly guide something as costly and revisable as a career choice. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and say whether you think it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp -- the empiricist camp treats base rates as everything, the string-theory camp treats them as beside the point, and I want to know which side that disagreement actually reflects the mathematics of confirmation and which reflects turf.

Finally, flag any pair of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied: in particular whether a method can be at once sensitive to base rates (which pull toward pessimism, given how many beautiful theories died) and robust enough to license real confidence in a theory with a landscape of solutions. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly -- that joint unsatisfiability would itself be the most useful thing I could take to my adviser, more useful than any verdict about string theory in particular. Don't bother scoring Bayesian terms or Popperian falsificationism against the list in detail; I know those positions, and it's the requirements that are new to me.
