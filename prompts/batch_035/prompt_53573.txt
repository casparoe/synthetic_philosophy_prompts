A friend who did graduate work in economics talked me into an argument last night that I still can't get out of, because every escape I tried he had an answer for, and the whole thing now seems to prove far more than he intended. Here is the argument as he gave it, numbered as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) All the facts there are about how well someone's life is going for her — all the facts about her utility, in the economist's sense — are exhausted by the facts about which options she would choose, including which gambles she would accept.
(2) Whatever choices a person makes, however rich the data, are compatible with rescaling her utility function: multiply by any positive constant, add any constant, and every one of her choices remains exactly as rational as before. Choice data fix the scale only up to that transformation.
(3) So no facts about Ann's choices together with facts about Bob's choices fix whether Ann's headache is worse for her than Bob's is for him. Making that comparison requires, in addition to both scales, the transformation relating them, and nothing in either person's choice behavior supplies it.
(4) A claim on which no possible evidence could bear, even in principle, is not a claim with a truth value — it isn't false, it's empty.
(5) Therefore interpersonal comparisons of utility are empty: "Ann's pain is worse than Bob's" says nothing, and the same goes for every cost-benefit analysis, QALY weighting, or utilitarian calculus that leans on them.

So far, I thought, this is just the old ordinalist line my friend traces back to Robbins, and I assumed the standard responses would work. They didn't, and the argument started eating things it shouldn't. First I tried the doctor with two doses of morphine: her choice of which patient to treat ranks Ann's and Bob's states on a single scale, so the missing transformation is supplied after all. He answered that the doctor's ranking is the doctor's — one more preference system, hers, resting on her beliefs and her concern — and that to call it a measurement of Ann's and Bob's utilities is to smuggle in a third person's scale precisely where the comparison was supposed to be between the other two. Then he extended the argument in a direction that really unsettled me:

(6) Nothing in the reasoning was special to distinct persons. My choices now fix my current scale only up to the transformation; my choices in 2015 fixed that scale the same way; and no behavior of mine fixes the transformation between the two. So "I am better off now than I was then" is exactly as empty as "Ann is worse off than Bob." The only comparisons with truth values, he says, are the ones a single chooser makes at a single moment among the options then before her.

At that point I tried the move I had read was Harsanyi's — that I can rank the alternatives of being Ann with her migraine and being Bob with his stubbed toe, and that this ranking puts their states on a common scale after all. He said it just re-describes the doctor case with me in the doctor's chair: it measures my extended preferences, and he can rescale those as freely as anything else, which tells us nothing about Ann and Bob. I then pointed out that his argument seems to spare one thing I thought it would swallow: my preference between mild contentment for sure and a fifty-fifty gamble between agony and euphoria appears to fix a genuinely cardinal fact — that contentment lies exactly midway between agony and euphoria — for a single chooser at a single time. He conceded this, but only by noting that this is precisely the case in which there is a single chooser, which he had been granting all along.

I can see three places a refusal might go, and each seems to cost something I want. I could reject (1) as too thin: there is more to how someone's life is going than what she would choose — suffering has an intensity that shows up in no choice — but then my friend will ask how those extra facts are ever detected by anyone, and his answers to the doctor and to Harsanyi look as though they will swallow whatever I point to. I could reject (4) as covert verificationism: there were truths about the temperature of stars before thermometry, and truths about the relative mass of two objects before anyone placed them on the same balance, so "no possible evidence" must be doing enormous work, and I cannot tell whether it is doing the same work in every case. Or I could accuse the move from (3) to (5) of an equivocation on "utility" between the representation we build out of choices and the quantity that representation is supposed to represent — though he will reasonably reply that he was entitled to fix his terms at the start, and that the emptiness of interpersonal comparisons was his conclusion, not a hidden premise.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the meaningfulness of comparisons within a single life across time — if (6) is right, even my sense that I was better off after leaving my old job loses its content — but I would also like to understand what the doctor's ranking is of, if not Ann's and Bob's utilities. Please take a definite position on whether the argument is valid, and if so which premise is least costly to give up, or if not, exactly where the flaw lies and why the step looked acceptable. And please say how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis, singling out the point you are least sure of.
