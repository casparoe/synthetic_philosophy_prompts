My friend and I have been arguing for two years about whether a morally bad work can still be good art, and it has gone exactly nowhere, so I want a diagnosis of the standoff itself, not a verdict on who's right.

The positions, concretely: she holds that a moral flaw is an aesthetic flaw. Her favorite example is a film that gets you rooting for a war criminal: if it does that skillfully, the skill is the problem, not an excuse for it — you were persuaded well toward something awful, and being persuaded *well* is precisely what makes it worse. I'm on the other side roughly as follows: the craft is the craft. The same machinery that made you sympathize with the monster could have been pointed at a sympathetic character, so the excellence is real and just badly deployed — like a great engine in a car someone's driving toward a cliff. The engine doesn't stop being great because the driver is a maniac.

Here's the frustrating part. On actual cases we barely disagree. We both think a certain infamous Nazi propaganda documentary is repulsive, and we both think you're kidding yourself if you deny it's effective. We both think a clumsy, moralizing novel can be bad art even though its heart is in the right place. So the fight isn't about the cases — it's about how to book them. When we each say the work is or isn't "good," is that one scale with moral and aesthetic ingredients mixed into it, or two scales that can come apart? And if two scales, is there any fact of the matter about which one gets to own the word "good"?

My working hypothesis, offered as just a hypothesis: it's mostly verbal. By "good art" she means something like the whole package as it operates on an audience, and I mean something like the achievement of the artistic ends taken by itself, and we keep colliding because English hands us a single adjective for both. But I'm honestly not sure, because sometimes it feels like there's a genuine normative question underneath — something about whether audiences *ought* to adopt the whole-package view, which wouldn't be verbal at all.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, substantive-in-disguise, or the two of us quietly answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose it using what each of us actually claims (as I've laid it out above), and then tell me whether anything would count as progress here — a distinction we haven't found, a test case that would force one of us to move, or a good argument that no progress is to be expected and we should just split the word and go home. I'd rather not go home.
