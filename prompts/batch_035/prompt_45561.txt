I teach the Putnam/Burge stretch of phil language most years, and here's the package I've settled into — tell me whether it hangs together, because I honestly don't know which piece I'd give up. (1) Twin Earth: in 1750, before anyone knew the chemical composition of anything, my twin on Twin Earth (lakes full of XYZ, which looks and behaves just like water) means XYZ by "water" while I mean H2O, and we beleive different things, despite being molecule-for-molecule identical. (2) Burge's arthritis patient: the guy who thinks arthritis can flare up in his thigh still means arthritis, so externalism holds for ordinary terms generally, not just the science-y ones. (3) I accept the slow-switching cases: if you move to Twin Earth as a kid and never catch on, over the decades your word "water" silently comes to mean XYZ, and nothing available to you would tell you so. (4) But I'm also a pretty hard-line causal theorist about belief: beliefs are the states that, together with desires, cause and explain behavior — and since my twin and I are physically identical, our behavior will be identical, and the internal cause of it is the same in both of us; whatever is in my head pushing my limbs is in his too. (5) And I think rationalizing explanations are genuine explanations, not a loose overlay on the causal story: "he drank it because he believed it was water" is true and explanitory only if he believed it was water, not if he believed it was XYZ. (6) Last thing: I'd have said belief states themselves are internal — what's in my head is in my head — even though what they're about isn't. So: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like it, how bad is the real one, and what's the cheapest fix? In particular, is (6) a genuine solution to the trouble between (4) and (5), or just a restatement of it? Don't rank everything as equally bad — I want to know which commitment actually has to move.
