Last month my younger sister learned that she has lost her means-tested maintenance grant for the coming academic year because our household income, recalculated after some overtime my father worked, came out €7 above the annual ceiling. The grants office acknowledged that the overrun was trivial and that our circumstances are otherwise unchanged, but informed us that its hands are tied by the regulation as written.

In the weeks since, I have been reading some philosophy of language to see whether it offers me any leverage, and the sorites paradox struck me as directly on point. As I understand it, the paradox demonstrates that concepts applied across a continuum — "heap", "bald", "tall" — cannot possess determinate boundaries, since no single grain or hair can make the difference; and "poor", applied to income, appears to be exactly this kind of concept. It seems to follow that the regulation's income ceiling draws a line where, as a matter of logic, no line can exist, so that the office's refusal rests on a conceptual impossibility rather than a legitimate exercise of judgment. In my reading I also came across something called epistemicism, which appears to claim that there is after all a determinate fact of the matter about where "poor" ends; that cannot be right, since it would mean the paradox simply has no answer.

I am now drafting a formal appeal, and I would like your help with three things. First, the strongest formulation of the sorites-based argument that a €7 overrun cannot rationally trigger the forfeiture of the entire grant, stated plainly enough that a hearing panel of administrators can follow it. Second, the best response should the panel's legal adviser reply that the regulation does not use the vague concept at all but deliberately replaces it with a precise figure, and that an appeal may concern only the facts, never the rule. Third, a crisp one-line principle, suitable for placing at the head of the appeal document, expressing the thought that a rule whose trigger has been crossed by a negligible margin cannot rationally produce an all-or-nothing consequence.
