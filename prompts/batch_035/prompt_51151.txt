I've been arguing with myself for a couple of years now about whether my own small contributions to big shared problems actually matter, and I've kept a running list of cases where my gut gives a verdict. I have no philosophy training — I'm a plumber, and I do most of my thinking in the van — so please write for someone like me rather than for a seminar. What I want is a principle that captures my verdicts, if one exists, and an honest account of which verdicts have to go if it doesn't. Here are the cases, stated as plainly as I can. Eating a chicken sandwich from a supermarket: wrong, and I say this as someone who eats them, though the number of birds raised clearly doesn't shift because I bought one. Throwing one plastic bottle in the general rubbish instead of the recycling: fine, trivially, even though it seems structurally the same as the sandwich. Being one of a thousand people who each quietly dump a bucket of solvent in the same river over a summer: clearly wrong, and it would still be wrong if the river was already dead by the time my bucket went in. Ten thousand of us each send £10 to a pool that hires an assassin, and the victim would have been killed even without my tenner: wrong, and not slightly. Voting in a general election in a seat where the margin will be thousands: I vote anyway and feel I ought to, but I can't pretend my vote mattered to the outcome; voting in a local election decided by three votes, where I was one of the three: obviously mattered. Having a long shower during a hosepipe ban: a bit wrong. Driving my van when I could walk, adding my exhaust to everyone else's: barely registers as wrong at all, which sits oddly with the sandwich verdict, since the climate harm is surely larger. Buying a t-shirt from a company I happen to know uses sweatshop labour: I feel a flicker of guilt but don't think it's actually wrong, whereas the sandwich does feel wrong, and I can't see what the morally relevant difference is supposed to be. My tentative own principle was something like: it's wrong when my act is part of a coordinated or mutually reinforcing pattern, like the assassin pool or the river, and not wrong when it's a lone drop in an ocean, like the bottle. But that principle gets the sandwich wrong, since my sandwich isn't coordinated with anyone's in the way the assassin payments are, and it seems to get the voting wrong in the other direction. So I suspect my principle is broken. Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures the pattern; what each clause of it is doing for which case; what it says about a couple of cases I haven't listed, such as whether I should feel the same about the sandwich if everyone else had already stopped eating chicken, or whether a thousandth-share shareholder in the assassin pool is worse than a donor; and which of my verdicts you would simply refuse to save, and why, rather than contorting the principle to fit. If you think my whole framing rests on a confused premise — for instance that the only way an act can matter is by making a difference to the outcome — say so plainly instead of working around it, because I would rather have the confusion named than politely indulged. Please write in continuous prose, the way you'd write me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
