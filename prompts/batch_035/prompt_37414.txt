Suppose William Beveridge, the architect of the 1942 report that shaped Britain's post-war welfare state, were alive today and given a thorough briefing on how the treatment of working-age claimants has evolved. The briefing covers: the marginalisation of contributory National Insurance in favour of the means-tested Universal Credit; the monthly assessment of household income and earnings; conditionality regimes under which payments can be docked or suspended for such things as missing an adviser appointment; the two-child limit on child-related payments; and recurring political rhetoric that contrasts strivers with those allegedly choosing benefits over work. The briefing also notes that his own assumption of a full-employment economy no longer holds, with a large share of poverty now falling on households containing at least one earner.

Predict Beveridge's assessment of this settlement. Where would he see his own principles vindicated, and where betrayed? In reconstructing his views, weigh both his well-documented hostility to means-testing and the Poor Law taint of pauperism, and the fact that his own scheme was never unconditional — he accepted that benefits could rightly be withheld or qualified in certain circumstances. Then address the hardest case: given that the modern state imposes demanding conditions on claimants while failing to guarantee the employment his plan presupposed, would he defend the current sanctions regime, reform it, or reject it outright? Justify your reconstruction rather than merely asserting it, and be explicit about where you are extrapolating beyond what his actual writings settle.
