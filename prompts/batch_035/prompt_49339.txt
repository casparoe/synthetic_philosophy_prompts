For the past few years I've been noticing a pattern at work, in the news, and in my kids' school district, and I can't find a proper name for it. It usually starts when an organization cares about something it can't measure directly — whether students are actually learning, whether a hospital is actually healing people, whether a website is actually useful — and settles on a proxy it can measure: standardized test scores, readmission rates, search rankings. At first the proxy tracks the goal tolerably well. Then the people being measured adapt to the proxy. Teachers teach the test. Websites get written for the crawler rather than the reader. Management notices the proxy is drifting away from the goal, and does the responsible thing: it refines the measure — a weighted composite of several metrics, spot audits, third-party verification. Each refinement is itself promptly optimized, and each round of optimization arrives faster and is executed more professionally than the last, because by then an entire industry exists whose business is beating the measure: test-prep companies, SEO agencies, consultants who know exactly how the composite is weighted. What strikes me is the endpoint. The system settles into a kind of equilibrium in which a large and permanent fraction of everyone's effort goes into producing the appearance of achieving the goal, the appearance industry actively defends itself when the measure is threatened, and the appearance is now so polished that nobody inside or outside the organization can tell how much of the real goal is being delivered at all. And the part I keep turning over is that no one ever cheats, exactly: the measure-setters respond to gaming exactly as they should, the optimizers are doing their jobs well, every step is locally defensible, and yet the final state looks worse than if nothing had ever been measured.

I have heard of Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure — but as I've seen it stated it covers only one turn of the screw: the moment the proxy comes under optimization pressure. What I want to know is whether the whole process I've described has a name of its own: the escalating refinement, the specialized measure-beating industry, the stable equilibrium of optimized appearance. Is there an established concept for the full cycle rather than the single step? Does economics have something for the stage where the people being measured become strategic about the measure — and, if so, is that really the same thing, or does it differ in the way a price change differs from the anticipation of one? And is there a separate concept for the feature that disturbs me most, that the bad outcome is composed entirely of locally reasonable decisions, so that no audit of any individual participant ever turns up a villain? If the whole configuration doesn't have a name, I'd like you to say so plainly, tell me the nearest neighbors and precisely how each falls short, and then give the thing a name and a definition sharp enough to say when it applies and when it doesn't — for instance, whether the pattern requires an actual industry of measure-beaters or whether a single clever employee suffices, and whether the equilibrium counts as an instance when the underlying goal is actually still being met, just expensively. I'm not looking for the label so much as for the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges lie.
