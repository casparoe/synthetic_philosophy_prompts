Quick context: I'm in a reading group that's half decision theory people, half epistemic logic people, and the fight about logical omniscience comes up every few months and goes absolutely nowhere. Everyone knows all the moves, nobody budges, and I'm starting to wonder what kind of disagreement this actually is, so I want a diagnosis rather than a rerun of the arguments.

Here's the shape of it as each side plays its hand. The logic side: if knowledge isn't closed under consequence, the whole Kripke machinery stops working — distribution of K over conjunction, the way we model common knowledge, the completeness results. So treat it as an idealization, like frictionless planes in mechanics. The economists back them up on the Bayesian side: nobody literally conditionalizes on all their logical consequences, but a model has to assume competence somewhere or it explains nothing, and if you dislike the idealization, produce the model without it. The other side thinks this is not an idealization but a scandal: an agent who believes all consequences of her beliefs isn't an idealization of us, she's a different kind of thing entirely. And the proposed fixes all have well-known costs that the anti-fix people recite like a liturgy — impossible worlds can be fitted to anything, so the model ends up with no constraint on it and tells you nothing; the awareness literature just relabels the problem (fine, she was unaware of p — but why was she unaware, that's the phenomenon we wanted); weakening the logic (relevant, paraconstructive [sic], whatever) blocks some closure principles but not the one that actually bites in real life, which is that consequences are just expensive to compute. So the stall: everyone agrees nobody is logically omniscient, everyone agrees the ideal models are indispensable in practice, everyone agrees every repair is costly, and it's been like this since roughly forever.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the fight is fueled by an analogy that does more work than anyone admits. Frictionless planes are safe because the neglected forces are small or decouple from the ones you care about; in the logical omniscience case the neglected factor — computational cost — is huge and is arguably the very thing we're trying to model, so it's not clear the idealization is of the same kind at all, maybe not even the same genus. If that's right, then possibly the two camps aren't disagreeing about one thing: one group is building a model of a standard of rationality (where idealizing is fine, norms are norms) and the other wants a model of actual inference (where the shortcut IS the signal), and they've been talking past each other under one heading for decades. But I'm not sure, because the anti-idealization people also keep making normative claims ("a rational agent wouldn't be caught dead believing p while denying an easy consequence of p"), which sounds like they want the norm too, just with a speed limit attached.

So: is this a verbal dispute about the word "idealization," a substantive empirical-looking question about whether computational cost is noise or signal, two projects with one name, or bedrock intuitions about what formal methods are even for that nobody can argue for? And given the diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a principled restriction of closure (say, to rules that are cheap to apply) that isn't just a patch, a general account of when idealizations are safe that the frictionless-plane analogy is one instance of, or should I expect nothing to move and, if so, why?
