I'm a master's student in philosophy of science, and my supervisor compressed the whole debate about counterfactual versus interventionist theories of causation into one seminar aside: "The old counterfactual theory, associated with David Lewis, held that C causes E when, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred either. It was more or less demolished in the 1970s by cases of pre-emption and overdetermination, where the effect plainly occurs but depends on nothing in that simple way. The interventionist theory, developed by Judea Pearl and James Woodward, replaced it: C causes E just in case some possible intervention on C would change E, where an intervention is a manipulation that alters C and leaves every other cause of E alone. Since causation is now tied to possible manipulations, the theory is at bottom about what we could control, and it quietly reduces the metaphysics of causation to a methodology for finding causes out." I'm inclined to think this is basically right, and that interventionism simply won the argument. Is it? Please go claim by claim: which parts would Lewis accept as a fair statement of his view, which would Woodward accept of his, where does the paragraph conflate two claims the theories carefully keep apart (for instance, over what an intervention is for Woodward — an actual manipulation by anyone, or something else?), and does the closing reduction of metaphysics to methodology follow from anything interventionists actually claim? Finally, tell me whether pre-emption genuinely demolished the counterfactual approach, as the paragraph implies, or whether that is a standard objection being presented as a settled refutation.
