I teach a seminar on formal epistemology, and this semester we're doing a unit on informal mathematical reasoning — Fermi estimates, back-of-envelope calculations, the kind of arguments people in crypto and computer security make when they say a hash function "has no backdoor" or that a pseudorandom generator "looks random" without any proof. My hook for the students is a case a friend of mine in finance uses: you estimate the value of two portfolios by noting portfolio A looks like about $2M and portfolio B looks like about $2M, and you conclude the combined thing is about $4M — even though both are stuffed with the same stocks, so if the market tanks they tank together. Treating them as independent is unarguably wrong as a matter of probability theory, and yet the estimate feels fine, and everyone from physicists to security auditors reasons this way constantly. There's a line of work (mostly coming out of the AI safety world, e.g. the "heuristic arguments" literature and attempts to formalize the presumption of independence) that wants to make this style of reasoning precise, and I want my students to evaluate those attempts against a spec rather than one at a time.

So what I'd like from you is the spec itself: the list of desiderata that any adequate formal account of heuristic argument — a "heuristic estimator" that takes some argument and outputs a number or a credence, vindicating the reasoning-by-approximation we all actually do — has to meet. Think hard about which requirements are genuine and which are just prejudices imported from one approach: should such an account be sound in the sense of never producing confident garbage, or does demanding soundness immediately kill the whole project (since the two-portfolios trick gives wrong answers in the tails)? Does it need to capture the distinction between an argument that's merely unverifiable and one that's actually untrustworthy, like the difference between trusting SHA-256 and trusting a backdoored RNG? Should it generalize over the agent's epistemic state, i.e. two people with the same evidence and priors who run the same heuristic argument should get the same output? Should it license using arguments you don't fully trust, the way security people rely on standard assumptions? And which pairs of your desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable — that's the part I most want to see, since if the list can't be satisfied that's itself the interesting philosophical result, maybe the informal practice is just not formalizable.

Don't give me a mealy survey of what different frameworks might maybe satisfy. Commit: tell me which desiderata you actually endorse, which you'd toss out and why, whether the whole project is coherent or whether "heuristic argument" names a family of things no single formalism can cover, and finish with your honest verdict on whether the formalization program can succeed or whether it's chasing something that dissolves under analysis. My students will score the actual proposals against your list in class, so keep the weight on the requirements and the motivating cases rather than on reviewing specific frameworks, though you can gesture at how logical-induction-style approaches or heuristic estimators would obviously do. Also, if you think one of my desiderata suggestions above is confused or question-begging, say so directly — I'd rather find out now than mid-lecture.
