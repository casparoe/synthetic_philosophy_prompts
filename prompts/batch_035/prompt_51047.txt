I'm preparing to lead a book club discussion of Jane Austen's Emma with a philosophical slant, and I'd like your help constructing the reading. The lens is Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator from The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and my working hypothesis is that Emma Woodhouse is a brilliant simulator of other minds who exercises the imaginative half of Smith's mechanism — projecting herself into others' situations — almost entirely for manipulation (matchmaking, managing Harriet, flattering herself about her own discernment) while being nearly immune to the reflexive half, the discipline of viewing her own conduct as a well-informed bystander would. Mr. Knightley then looks like the impartial spectator made flesh: the one person whose judgments Emma treats as authoritative even when they sting, which is precisely her relationship to no other character, including her father. And the Box Hill episode — Emma publicly mocking Miss Bates, then being rebuked by Knightley and revisiting the scene with mounting shame on the drive home — looks like a textbook dramatization of the agent having to lower her conduct and claims to the level that a spectator can go along with. But I can also see ways the novel would resist being reduced to an illustration, and I want a reading that is honest in both directions.

Please take the role of a critic equally at home in Austen scholarship and in the Scottish Enlightenment, and before writing the essay, briefly sketch two or three candidate readings of the novel through this lens and tell me which you'll pursue and why. Then write the essay, and please attend to the following. First, get the Smithian machinery precise: the two-way adjustment between agent and spectator, the internalized spectator that Smith says we each carry, and the distinction between wanting to be praised and wanting to be praiseworthy — Emma conspicuously cares about the first, and one natural reading of her maturation is a shift toward the second, which I'd like you to assess rather than assume. Second, address whether Knightley can really occupy the spectator's role given that he is a particular, interested party — a neighboring landowner, an old family friend, and a man quietly in love with her — and whether Austen is thereby showing something Smith underplays: that the impartial spectator has to be assembled out of actual, partial spectators. Third, give Miss Bates her due: she spends the entire novel systematically diminishing her own claims so that others can sympathize with her without strain, which makes her a near-perfect Smithian agent and a strange foil for the heroine. Fourth, note where the free indirect narration itself does the work of the impartial spectator, and where the reading strains — for instance, whether Emma's reform is genuine moral growth or is triggered by romantic disappointment, and whether Austen lets her off the hook Smith would not.
