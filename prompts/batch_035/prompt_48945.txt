My brother-in-law is one of those energetic amateur debaters, and his signature move in any religion argument is slapping the table with ex nihilo nihil fit — nothing comes from nothing — and then running the kalam cosmological argument off it: whatever begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist, therefore something outside the universe caused it. Last weekend my cousin, who's a physics grad student, tried to shut this down with quantum fluctuations — virtual particle pairs popping out of the vacuum — so the principle is just empirically false, physics has moved on, end of story. And then my old philosophy TA, who was somehow at the same dinner, muttered that the whole fight was over an ambiguity in "nothing" and left before explaining what he meant. I've been turning it over since and I can't fix it on my own.

So what I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims that could actually be hiding in that one Latin line. Is it a definitional truth (if something "comes from nothing," maybe it just... begins to exist, in which case the principle is trivial and can't do any argumentative work)? A causal principle about efficient causes? A claim only about material causes — which seems awkward for my brother-in-law, since his own theology says God created the world ex nihilo, so presumably "from nothing" can't mean what the slogan naively sounds like it means? A statement about conservation laws that physics could in principle have found to be false (and did the quantum stuff actually falsify it, or is a quantum vacuum with its fields and its structure not "nothing" in the relevant sense)? Or is it really a disguised principle of sufficient reason, in which case it's doing way more work than the surface grammar suggests?

For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's supposed to be a priori or empirical, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it supports the kalam conclusion my brother-in-law wants, or the cousin's rebuttal, or neither. And if you can, say something about which reading the *other* version of the cosmological argument, the contingency one, actually needs — I've been told that version doesn't even require the universe to have a beginning, and I genuinely don't see how "nothing comes from nothing" could ever do that job. Lay it out as a list I can print and bring to the next family dinner, verdicts included.
