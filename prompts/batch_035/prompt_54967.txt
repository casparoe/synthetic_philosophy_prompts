Exam question:
Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" presents a regress intended to trouble the justification of deductive inference. Reconstruct the argument's structure, explain what the regress is commonly taken to show about the relation between premises and rules of inference, and assess whether the regress poses a genuine problem for the justification of deduction or whether it rests on a misunderstanding that can be dispelled.

Student's answer:
In Carroll's dialogue, the tortoise accepts the first two propositions of a simple Euclidean argument — that things equal to the same are equal to each other, and that the two sides of this triangle are equal to the same — but refuses to accept the conclusion that the two sides of the triangle are equal to each other. Achilles writes down the conditional that if the first two propositions are true, the conclusion must be true, and the tortoise accepts that as a hypothetical, yet still won't grant the conclusion. So a new conditional has to be written, then another, and the notebook grows without end. The point is that the tortoise treats the rule of inference as just one more premise to be accepted or rejected at will, and once that move is allowed, no argument can ever get a conclusion out of someone who is determined not to draw it.

This was a direct attack on Russell and Whitehead's logicism in Principia Mathematica, which tried to reduce mathematics to a set of axioms, and Carroll showed that project could not get off the ground because any axiomatic system must contain infinitely many axioms to license even one inference. The regress shows that every deduction requires infinitely many premises, so no finite mind could ever be entitled to a deductive conclusion; at best, deduction is psychologically compelling rather than rationally binding. In this respect Carroll's regress is essentially Hume's problem of induction run in reverse: Hume showed that induction can only be justified circularly by appeal to induction's past success, and Carroll shows deduction is in exactly the same position, since justifying a rule of inference would require a further inference governed by that very rule. So the two kinds of inference end up on a par epistemically, and the choice between them is pragmatic. The standard resolution, defended by Russell in his paper on denoting, is to distinguish a conditional connective inside the object language from the act of inference itself, treating the tortoise as equivocating between them.
