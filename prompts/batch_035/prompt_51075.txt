At the end of a long seminar, a colleague and I found ourselves on opposite sides of a factual question — whether a body of data we had both read did or did not support a certain conclusion — and when I suggested we keep at it, he proposed that we agree to disagree, and the room visibly relaxed. I have been uneasy about the phrase ever since. It sounds like a courtesy, but it seems to be quietly doing serious epistemological work: invoked between two people who regard each other as epistemic peers, it settles, in four words, a question that the epistemology of disagreement has not managed to settle at all. Unpack the precise claims a person could be expressing by saying we should agree to disagree, and lay them out as a menu, ordered from the trivial to the radical. A number of candidate readings suggest themselves, though you need not stop at these:

1. A mere report of the fact that we do disagree, bundled with a social convention for moving on to coffee.
2. A subjectivist claim that the matter at issue is one of opinion rather than fact.
3. A claim of evidential underdetermination: given the shared evidence, more than one credence in the proposition is rationally permissible.
4. A steadfast claim: that my own reasoning is entitled to a special weight even against an acknowledged peer.
5. A conciliationist claim in disguise: perhaps the "agree" in the phrase is doing the real work, so that it mandates meeting in the middle rather than each standing pat.
6. A pragmatic claim: whatever the epistemics, further argument is not worth its cost in time or collegiality.
7. Something in the neighborhood of Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that agents who start from a common prior and whose posteriors are common knowledge must in fact hold the same posterior — under which the phrase, for idealized agents, would describe a flat impossibility.

For each reading, say plainly whether it is a platitude, defensible but contested, or false; keep separate what it claims about what we will do, what we may do, and what we ought to do with our credences; and say explicitly whether, if true, it would actually justify what my colleague used it for, namely ending the exchange with neither of us moving at all. Note also which readings would license asymmetric outcomes — one of us walking away confident, the other hesitant — since some precisifications seem to permit that and others do not. Close by telling me which reading the room was most plausibly invoking when it relaxed, and whether my unease about the phrase, as opposed to my colleague's use of it, is warranted.
