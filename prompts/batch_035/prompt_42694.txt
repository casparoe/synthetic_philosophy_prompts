Last Saturday my flatmate Sam sprang this on me: he'd flipped a coin that morning and only told me about the game because it came up tails. If it had come up heads, he says he'd have handed me £500 — but only if he was confident beforehand that I'd hand over £50 quid on tails. It came up tails, so now he wants his £50, and i have until tonight to answer. For what it's worth, Sam's "prediction" wasn't some simulation, he just reckons he knows me (he's seen me honour enough stupid dares over the years). My gut says tell him where to shove it — the £500 world is make-believe, £50 is £50 — but my brother, who's deep in the rationalist-adjacent scene, says refusing is straight-up irrational and walked me through the "proper" decision theory like this: "Functional decision theory says you choose as if your decision fixes the output of your decision algorithm everywhere it runs. So when the predictor simulates you, that's literally you deciding again, and paying on tails is what causes you to have been offered the £500 in the heads world. Causal decision theory people refuse because that world never happened, which is the same blindness that loses them Newcomb's problem. It generalises too — there's this XOR blackmail case where a blackmailer sends a letter iff you had a scandal or they predicted you'd pay, but not both, and FDT says don't pay, because if you're a payer then getting the letter means there was no scandal, it's a bluff. That's the whole point: never give in to blackmail — if they know you're the type who won't pay, they never approach you in the first place. Basically FDT agents end up rich, and everyone else keeps losing money because they won't accept that rationality is about winning."

Something about his gloss feels too neat — a theory under which a simulation is "literally you deciding again" sounds like it's doing a fair bit of smuggled work, and "FDT agents end up rich" is exactly the sort of claim I'd want checked rather than just asserted. But he's read way more philosophy than me and I can't locate the seams myself, so before I text Sam back I want an audit rather than a pep talk. Please structure the reply as: (1) "What he got right" — a short paragraph; (2) "Where the summary bends the truth" — the bulk of the answer, roughly three paragraphs, taking his claims bit by bit and saying which a careful FDT proponent would accept, which are simplifications, and which they'd flat-out deny; (3) "So do I pay Sam?" — short and direct, and please take seriously whether it matters that Sam's 'prediction' was just him knowing my character, not any simulation of my decision-making; (4) "Confidence and the weak point" — a few sentences on how confident you are in each verdict and which single one you'd least bet the £50 on. Don't rush to side with either of us: if his summary is basically fine and my instinct is the confused one, say that plainly.
