For some years now I have been reading Wittgenstein on my own — no formal training in philosophy, just the texts, secondary reading, and long walks. What has stayed with me most is his picture of religious belief: that it is less like holding a hypothesis and more like a picture one lives by, something that shows itself in how a person acts, regrets, and prays rather than in anything they would assert as a claim about the world. I also keep returning to his complaint, in his comments on Frazer's Golden Bough, that treating other people's rituals as primitive mistakes is itself a misunderstanding: the ceremonies are not bad theories about how the world works, but practices with their own grammar, intelligible only from inside the life they belong to.

Here is the question I cannot settle by myself. Many people now talk to AI chatbots as spiritual companions — they tell them their guilt, ask them for guidance, and some use them as a kind of confessor. I want to know what Wittgenstein would have made of that. Not the trivial answer that he died in 1951 and would have found the technology baffling; I mean his reaction to the practice itself. Would he say that telling your sins to a machine is simply a different practice from telling them to a priest — a new form of life with its own grammar, about which nothing is lost and nothing is fake? Or would he say that it only looks like confession, and is really something else wearing its clothes, perhaps self-management with religious decoration?

In thinking about this I have come up with a rough distinction of my own, and I would like you to test it against Wittgenstein rather than merely refine it: some practices seem load-bearing in a life — they do the work of holding a person together, as confession, prayer, and mourning arguably do — while others seem decorative, borrowing the outward form while the real work is done elsewhere, in therapy, in medication, in willpower. My worry is that Wittgenstein's notion of a form of life cannot by itself tell us which side of that line a new practice falls on, because it seems designed to describe practices that are already settled, not to pass judgment on ones being born. If that is right, then his philosophy of religion, however deep, is silent at exactly the point where our century would need it to speak.

So I would like you to do three things. First, reconstruct from what he actually wrote — the lecture on ethics, the remarks on Frazer, the scattered notes on religion collected after his death — the materials for an answer: what he took religious belief to be, and where he thought a practice's meaning resides. Second, argue both sides of the chatbot-confessor case as he might have: the reading on which it is a genuine new form of life that could, in time, grow its own depth; and the reading on which it is a counterfeit whose very convenience disqualifies it. Third, tell me whether my load-bearing/decorative distinction survives contact with his thought. Is it something he would have accepted? Something he would have dissolved — for instance, as asking a practice for a justification it could not give in either direction? Or something genuinely new that his tools simply do not reach? If you think the distinction rests on a mistake, say so plainly; I would rather lose it than keep it by convincing myself that Wittgenstein would have been on my side.
