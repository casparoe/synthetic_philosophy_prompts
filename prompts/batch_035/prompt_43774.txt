I teach a philosophy of medicine seminar, and every year I bring in a physician-philosopher colleague from the med school — I'll call her Dana — who runs this diagnostic parlor game with the students: she gives verdicts on a grab bag of conditions, disease or not a disease, and the students try to reverse-engineer the rule she's using. I've got four years of her verdicts written down in my notes, and next week she's coming back with three new cases I've managed to learn about in advance. I want to walk in with predictions so the students and I can test our theories of Dana in real time, instead of just reacting.

Her past rulings, verbatim from my notes:

- metastatic melanoma: disease
- a clean tibial fracture from a ski crash: disease
- schizophrenia with persistent delusions: disease
- two weeks of crushing sadness after a divorce: not a disease
- a benign mole: not a disease
- mild nearsightedness, fully corrected with glasses: disease, but barely (she really did qualify it like that)
- male-pattern baldness in a 55-year-old man: not a disease
- prolonged grief that still wrecks a patient's functioning four years after a bereavement: disease

And here are next week's cases:

1. a 40-year-old woman who is infertile, married, deliberately childfree, and genuinelly unbothered by the infertility;
2. a violinist whose stage fright is so severe she can no longer perform, with no other symptoms and nothing clinically detectable going on;
3. a healthy 52-year-old going through menopause with severe hot flashes.

Predict Dana's verdict on each of the three. Then tell me which rule you think she's actually running — Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism, a normativist line on which disease-hood depends on the condition being bad for you or disvalued, a Wakefield-style harmful dysfunction analysis, or something else entirely. As far as I can tell at least three of those fit her track record equally well, so I want the competing readings laid out side by side and then your best guess with your actual reasons. And finally, invent one further case of your own that would do the most work splitting the leading candidates apart — I might email it to her beforehand if it's good enough.

Please write your answer in American English. I know half the philosophy-of-medicine literature is British and the mixed spellings in my notes are bad enough already.
