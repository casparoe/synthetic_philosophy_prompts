I'm a staff engineer with no philosophy background, but I've been volunteered to run the "critical thinking" module at our company's annual tech-lead offsite: 12 participants, one 90-minute block, and a mandate that it be hands-on rather than a slide deck. I got interested in this after our promotion committee had a strange meeting: we voted "meets expectations" on each individual competency for a candidate, and then, when someone asked for a straight up/down verdict on the candidate as a whole, the majority voted no. Nobody could articulate why that felt wrong but also strangely defensible, and I've since learned there's a real structure behind it: when a group decides by taking majority votes on separate premises, the group's premises can logically commit it to a conclusion that the group's direct majority vote on the conclusion rejects. I want the offsite activity to make people hit that wall themselves, in a realistic engineering setting, before I name the phenomenon or explain the formal side.

Here's the scenario I'd like you to build around: an incident review board for a fictional outage in a payment-processing service. The board must decide whether to file the incident as "process failure" (which triggers a mandatory runbook rewrite and blameless-postmortem follow-up) or "acts-of-god failure" (no follow-up), based on three premises: (1) the on-call engineer deviated from the documented runbook, (2) the runbook, had it been followed, would have prevented the outage, and (3) the deviation was causally connected to the damage. I want the participants' underlying judgments pre-engineered through role cards — each card states what that person's investigation found and how confident they are — so that the distribution comes out the classic way: a majority accepts each premise individually, but only a minority accepts the conjunction, and the direct verdict on the final classification therefore flips relative to the premise-by-premise path. With 12 people I imagine four boards of three, though I'm open to other groupings if you think they'd expose the structure better.

Please give me the full runnable package: role cards with their fact patterns and confidence levels, the materials and facilitation script, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and some payoff structure that creates real engagement without real money (reputational stakes for the fictional boards would be fine, e.g., each board's "review quality" is scored by the others). Critically, I want one phase where boards discover the contradiction on their own, and a second phase where they must pick — or invent — an aggregation rule and then live with its consequences on a fresh case that stresses whatever rule they chose. Then the debrief: map the specific votes onto premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation, connect it to how our actual promotion and hiring-loop scoring works, and be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what features of the real philosophical problem no classroom simulation can reproduce.
