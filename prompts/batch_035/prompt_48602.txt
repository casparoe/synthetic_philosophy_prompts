I teach a graduate seminar that spends several weeks on the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. One of my students has fallen into the habit of issuing one-line verdicts on every example the seminar raises: this use of the past serves life; that one corrupts it. Her track record so far, in the order the cases came up:

1. A decaying regime that rallies support by invoking a few grand images of national greatness — since greatness happened here once, the present order must be preserved — while itself producing nothing. Her verdict: corrupting.
2. A settled town that keeps its founders' church in repair and repeats the same festival every year out of plain love of the place, making no claim that the past licenses its future. Verdict: serving.
3. A generation that indicts its fathers' world wholesale, finishes its prosecution, and then, with nothing further to build, settles into cynicism. Verdict: corrupting.
4. A generation that prosecutes in exactly the same way but converts the indictment into new institutions. Verdict: serving.
5. A scholar who writes a scrupulously neutral chronicle of his city, assigning equal weight to everything and taking no side. Verdict: corrupting.
6. An architect who builds without consulting the history of building, largely ignorant of what her precursors attempted. Verdict: serving.
7. A savant whose command of the record is so complete that every fresh undertaking feels belated to him, and who greets all present activity with irony. Verdict: corrupting.

Next week I mean to put three further cases to her. I have a suspicion about how she will rule, but I do not trust it, and I would like your analysis before class.

(a) A civic movement still in its vigorous founding years, membership growing and energy abundant, that commissions the same kind of neutral chronicle the scholar in (5) wrote — this time of the movement's own early period — and whose members now spend their meetings reciting the record of what they have already done, taking equal delight in every item and no side in any of it.

(b) A village in visible decline — the mill closed, the young gone to the cities — whose inhabitants nonetheless maintain exactly the piety of the town in (2): the same festival, the church kept in repair, plain love of the place, no claim about the future. The decline began before the piety did, or at least cannot be traced to it.

(c) A retired man who has read enough history to conclude that nothing essentially new ever happens, that every apparent beginning is a return; who preaches neither reform nor despair but stillness, holds no mission, and is, by every report, content.

I want three things from you. First, the rule she seems to be implicitly running, together with any rival rules that fit her record just as well. Second, what each of those rules predicts for (a), (b), and (c) — and, if they diverge, your best guess as to her actual verdict, with your reasons. Third, the single further case that would best separate the rival rules: the one she could not answer both ways.

Whatever you say above, end your reply with one final line, and nothing after it, of exactly this form:

\answer{(a): <label>; (b): <label>; (c): <label>}

where each <label> is either life-serving or life-corrupting, giving your best-guess prediction of her verdict on that case.
