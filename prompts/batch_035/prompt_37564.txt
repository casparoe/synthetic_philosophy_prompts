so here's my situation. I write novels — the kind that follow one person across forty-odd years, which is basically biography with the serial numbers filed off. Somewhere along the way I picked up what I guess is a genuine philosophical position, roughly the Plutarch view: that people acquire moral understanding not from principles or arguments but from lives — from sustained acquaintance with concrete people, real or invented, whose choices you watch play out over decades. Plutarch pairs a Greek with a Roman and trusts the reader to triangulate; the medieval saints' lives did the same job with fewer parallels and more miracles; and I'd claim the modern novel is just the same machine with lower stakes and better dialogue. There's a philosophy version of this, exemplarism — the idea that the admirable person comes *before* the criteria for rightness rather than after — but I came at it backwards, thru writing, thru watching what readers do with my characters.

That's the position I want stress-tested: moral learning runs through lives, and explicit moral theorizing is mostly downstream rationalization. Tell me what would count against it. Not the cheap stuff like "hagiography lies" — I mean, if the view is false, what should I expect to observe that I don't? What findings, from anywhere — psychology, history of education, whatever — would show that narratives of lives don't transmit moral judgment but something else, like identification or aspiration or just taste in people? How bad is it for me that readers of the very same life come away with opposite verdicts — I get letters about my own protagonist, half calling him a hero and half a warning, and I genuinely cant tell which half misread, or whether misreading is even possible on my view? Is it fatal that the biography tradition has served propaganda and nationalism as happily as it served moral education — Plutarch himself got mined for exactly that? And the question I most want you to take seriously: where would a defender of the view draw the line between a genuine defeater and a reader just reading badly, and has the whole thing been insulated to death by that move — the way "the right kind of exemplar" always quietly means "the one that produced the lesson we already liked"? Give me the defeaters ranked, fatal to merely awkward, say which ones the view's defenders would concede versus laugh off, and be blunt about whether I should drop this or whether its one of those views that can lose every skirmish and still be basically right.
