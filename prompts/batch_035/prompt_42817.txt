I'm a novelist four years into a book whose narrator is a novelist writing the very book I am writing — a fact I've stopped apologizing for. I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a weeklong workshop on self-reference in fiction. The room will be split: half working novelists who distrust theory and tune out anything that sounds like a lecture, half logicians and devoted readers of Hofstadter who will wince at any casual use of "strange loop" or "tangled hierarchy." Write the remarks, roughly five minutes long. They should explain the notion of a tangled hierarchy and of the inviolate level accurately but concretely, using Escher's Drawing Hands as the governing image: the two hands draw each other, yet the sheet of paper they appear on is drawn by a hand on a level they can never touch, and that undrawn level is what keeps the picture legible instead of collapsing into gibberish. Then put the idea to work on the question the novelists in the room actually care about: when a novel folds back and makes its own author a character, what plays the role of the inviolate level — the reader, the ending, the author's eventual death — and what does it buy the book that a cheap mirror trick wouldn't? Bear in mind that opening remarks about self-referential fiction risk becoming an instance of their own subject, so either keep them deliberately clear of that trap or make the falling-in earn its keep; tell me which you chose and why, and make the result land with both halves of the room.
