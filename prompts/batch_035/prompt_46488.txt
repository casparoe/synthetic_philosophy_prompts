My roommate and I have been going at it for a week about whether she should quit her PhD. She's four years in, miserable, and her advisor says one more year gets her done. I keep telling her to cut her losses and she finally quoted her dad at me: don't throw good money after bad. And it sounds like it settles things, but the more I think about it the less sure I am. So help me take that proverb apart. What are the actually different claims it could be making? There's obviously the definitional one — money already spent can't count as a reason for future spending — which seems true but trivially so, and maybe doesn't by itself tell you what to do at all. Then there's some stronger version, something like "continuing is always irrational if the only reason is what you've already put in," and I don't even know if that's true, because couldn't past investment be evidence about what finishing would be like? Like if she's already learned the field, that's a real reason the fifth year is cheaper for her than a first year would be for someone else. And then there's all this other stuff I keep half-remembering: quitting costs that are themselves future costs (explaining the gap, lost career specific capital, her own rules about finishing what you start and whether breaking them makes her less able to trust herself later). Do those count as honoring sunk costs or are they just regular forward-looking reasons wearing a costume? Plus my econ prof always said the ski trip story as if it proved everything, and I have a feeling the proverb does different work in the textbook version vs the dad version. Give me the readings as a list I can actually use in the argument — which are platitudes, which are defensible, which are false, and crucially which one is doing the work when someone uses it to END an argument. And if the strong reading turns out to be false, tell me honestly what I should be saying to her instead, because "your dad's proverb is imprecise" is not gonna land. (one more wrinkle: she'd also be leaving behind a funded year, so continuing costs her mostly time not money — does the proverb even apply to time?)
