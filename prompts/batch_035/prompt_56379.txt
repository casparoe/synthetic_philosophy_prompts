I'm in a history of science course right now and we just did the Newton/Leibniz calculus priority fight and the Darwin/Wallace thing, and the professor kept saying multiple simultaneous discovery is basically the norm, not the exception -- same idea arrives "in the air" once the groundwork is there. But then I realized almost every scientific institution we have is built for a world of solitary discoverers anyway. The Nobel goes to at most three people and never posthumously, patents reward whoever gets there first, laws and units and species get named after one guy even when several people plainly got there at nearly the same time. So my question for a paper I have to write: what view of discovery and credit do these priority practices actually presuppose? Do they require thinking of a discovery as the achievement of a particular individual, or could they be justified even if you believe discoveries are basically inevitable once the background knowledge exists? And is the whole thing even consistent -- like, the same community that loves the "standing on the shoulders of giants" line also treats being first by six months as everything. Also wondering whether the practices might be underdetermined here, i.e. several different theories of credit would licence them anyway, and if we took the "discoveries are inevitable" view seriously, what would actually have to change -- Nobel rules, patents, naming, all of it?
