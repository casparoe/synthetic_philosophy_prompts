I'm a graduate student writing a paper that has drifted from environmental law into what I suspect is really the philosophy of biology, and I'd like help working out the philosophical commitments that a certain legal practice quietly makes. The practice is this. Under statutes like the U.S. Endangered Species Act, protection attaches to named species, and the official lists are updated to follow the taxonomic literature. When taxonomists split a widespread species into several, on the basis of reinterpreting already-collected genetic and morphological material — no new field data, just a published revision — the legal status of the daughter forms changes automatically: a newly recognized, narrow-range form can become endangered overnight, and a lumping can dissolve protection without a single change in the animals on the ground. At the same time, the agencies administer hybridization in what looks like the opposite spirit. The red wolf has been legally protected since the early 1970s, even though its very status as a species is contested (some taxonomists argue it is itself of ancient hybrid origin); coyotes, which have spread into the same forests of eastern North Carolina where the wolves were reintroduced, receive no protection; the two interbreed freely and produce fertile offspring; and the recovery program treats this interbreeding as a threat to the species — wolf–coyote hybrids are not protected as wolves, and the agencies work to keep the two populations apart, on the rationale that the protected category would otherwise be absorbed into the unprotected one.

So here is my question. What view of species does this bundle of practices presuppose? On its face it looks almost typological: the law seems to protect something like the purity of a lineage, and to count the blending of lineages as a loss even in cases where the animals' genes persist in the hybrid population — this from a discipline that officially rejected essentialism long ago. It also seems to presuppose that species membership is determinate enough that each individual animal can be assigned to one side of a boundary, even though the biology of hybrid zones and intergrading populations says the boundary is exactly where things blur. And it appears to presuppose that taxonomy discovers facts about these boundaries, since a published paper alters legal consequences without any further finding by the administering agency — even though taxonomic splits are, at least arguably, theory-laden choices among competing species concepts rather than pure discoveries. Are these commitments mutually consistent? Can the whole practice be reconstructed as resting on one implicit theory — some genealogical or lineage-based species concept plus an added purity assumption, perhaps — or is it instead philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival species concepts, so that the agencies are in effect choosing a species concept without ever saying so? I'd like you to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and to tell me what would actually have to change in its administration if the implicit view were replaced — say, by a similarity-based grouping, or by an openly conventionalist view on which drawing the boundary is partly a decision rather than a discovery.

Finally, as a matter of epistemic hygiene: throughout your answer, please say how confident you are in each of your major claims, and single out the point in your reconstruction of the implicit theory that you are least sure of — I would rather know where the ground is soft than receive a seamless analysis.
