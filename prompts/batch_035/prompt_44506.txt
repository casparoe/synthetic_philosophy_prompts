My book club is doing Ender's Game next month and I keep getting stuck on the Orson Scott Card question, so I want a proper reading of it through the work-vs-artist debate. The usual move is "separate the art from the artist", but it strikes me that people run two quite different cases together: the case where the art itself is morally compromised (the views are in the text) and the case where the artist is compromised but the text seems clean. Card is a weird instance of the second, because the novel is basically a hymn to radical empathy with the Other — Ender destroys the buggers precisely because he understands them too well, and loves what he understands — while the author has spent decades and real money campaigning against gay marriage. So the book's central theme and the author's public life pull in exactly opposite directions, and the failure is a failure *of* the thing the book is *about*.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Read the novel through the immoral-art debate and tell me whether the standard machinery (autonomism, moralism, the middle positions) even applies when the objectionable bit isn't in the text but in the author's activism — is that a distinct case the philosophers have missed, or just the ordinary one with extra steps? Is there something specially uncomfortable about loving an empathy-book whose author is publicly failing at empathy, in a way that's not captured by talk of prescribed responses or works "manifesting" immoral attitudes? And push back the other way too: is it maybe just bad criticism to read Card into the text at all — the novel doesn't know who wrote it, so why should the club care?

I want specific scenes doing real work — the Fantasy Game with the Giant's Drink, the hive-queen ending, maybe the Peter/Valentine mirror in which empathy and manipulation get blurred — not just vibes about the author. And feel free to bring in Speaker for the Dead if it helps, since Ender spends that whole book literally becoming a professional understander of his enemies. I'm taking this to a book club, not a seminar, so I'd rather it come out honest and messy than with a tidy verdict either way. One of our members is an ethicism-holdout and another thinks caring about authors at all is sentimental nonsense, so I need ammunition for both directions.
