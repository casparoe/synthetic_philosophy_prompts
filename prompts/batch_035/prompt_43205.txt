I'm taking a political philosophy course this term, and the unit on global justice has left me with a pile of gut verdicts that I can't line up into anything coherent, so I'd like help doing reflective equilibrium on them — figuring out what principle, if any, captures what I actually think about whether compatriots have special claims on us that strangers abroad don't.

Here are my verdicts on cases. My government spends the equivalent of $50,000 on disability benefits for a compatriot when the same money, routed through a cost-effective health program abroad, would save several lives: I think the domestic spending is not merely permissible but required, and I don't feel the least bit guilty about it. Coast guard rescuers pull citizens from the water first when they can't save everyone: fine, obviously. Public university places reserved for citizens over equally qualified foreign applicants: fine. But now: an emergency room turns away a foreign tourist who will die without treatment, on the ground that citizens come first: clearly not fine — the partiality that seemed innocent in the other cases suddenly looks monstrous. A private citizen donates $500 to a food bank for locals rather than to a charity that would save a child's life abroad: I'm uneasy calling this wrong, but I'd never say the local donation is *better*, whereas I do implicitly think the government's disability spending is better than the foreign aid it crowds out — and I can't see why the scale of the agent should flip the moral valence. Telling a lie to a foreigner is exactly as bad as telling the same lie to a compatriot, so whatever special claims exist, they don't extend to the whole of morality, only to some distributive corner of it. And my special duties to my family feel different in kind from anything I owe compatriots — I never chose my nation, I don't even know most compatriots, and I'd resist saying co-citizenship is just a scaled-up family. My tentative principle was something like: partiality is legitimate inside relationships of reciprocity and mutual cooperation, and co-citizenship is one such relationship, while employer-tourist and stranger-stranger relations are not. But I suspect this principle does worse on my own cases than I initially thought — for instance, the foreign tourist is inside a web of reciprocal cooperation with us (trade, treaties, legal protections) in a way a stranger abroad isn't, yet my verdict there is the most anti-partial of all.

So here's what I'd like. Work through the cases, tell me whether one principle (or a small set of rival candidates) captures the pattern — consider seriously the idea that the relevant distinction is not the relationship but the *role*: that citizens and officials acting in the name of a state may weigh compatriots' interests more heavily, while private individuals acting as such may not, and say whether that can be made to square with my feeling that the government's choice is not just permitted but positively right. State your favored principle precisely, clause by clause, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it overturns and whether I should mourn them. Then apply it to two cases I haven't listed: a legislator who votes for tariffs that cost foreigners a lot to benefit compatriots a little, and a doctor from a poor country who emigrates to a rich one, on the argument that she owes her compatriots (who subsidized her training) her labor.
