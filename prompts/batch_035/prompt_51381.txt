So I write novels — third one just went to copyedits, which means I've been staring at my own sentences so long I've started having thoughts about them, which is never good. Here's what's bugging me, and I want someone who actually knows philosophy of language to sort it out. It's about when words in and around a novel actually DO something versus when they're just... words. Like, performatives, I think is the term? Utterances that perform rather than describe. Anyway. I've made a list of cases from my own books and my gut verdicts on each, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them.

1. In the book I just finished, a priest character pronounces two other characters husband and wife. Verdict: that worked. Those two are married — in the story, sure, but married, full stop, as far as anyone inside the book is concerned.

2. Same book, the acknowledgments: "For my husband — this one is yours." Verdict: that also worked, but in the real world. I actually gave him the book. Me, the author, not a character.

3. First-person narrator in my second novel tells the reader "I promise you, everything in these pages is true." Verdict: nothing happened. No one promised anyone anything. It's not even false, it's just inert somehow. Feels different from both 1 and 2 and I can't say why.

4. My second novel had a phrase in it for a specific kind of bad date (my agent made me cut it, dont ask). My friends who read the drafts still use the phrase. Verdict: I did something real when I wrote it. I coined a word. That feels like the realest speech act on this whole list, realler than the fictional wedding even, and that embarrasses me slightly.

5. That same book has a minor character who is very obviously my old landlord, and the narrator calls him a thief — he wasn't one, he was just a jerk about the water heater. Verdict: if that had survived to the printed book, something would have happened to the real landlord. A real accusation got made, by me, somehow, even though it's "just" the narrator talking about a "fictional" character.

6. Also in that book, the narrator rants about a real senator being on the take. Verdict: nothing. It's the narrator being the narrator, it's satire, and I genuinely don't feel that I accused anyone of anything. I can't reconcile this one with 5 and I've stopped trying.

7. The standard all-persons-are-fictitious disclaimer at the front. Verdict: I sign my name under it and it does something legal-ish I suppose, but in the landlord version of the book it would have been a lie, and yet I still don't feel like I'd have been lying, exactly. No idea what's going on there.

8. The movie adaptation of my first book: the actor playing a judge sentences a character to five years. Verdict: nothing real, obviously. But also exactly like case 1 — the character gets sentenced in the fiction — and that feels right to me, I get no wobble between those two at all.

So: is there one principle that sorts these? My half-guess is that it's something like who is speaking, to whom, and in wich world — but every time I try to make that precise it tells me the narrator's promise in 3 did something (I don't think it did), or it tells me the landlord accusation in 5 is nothing (I don't believe that either).

What I want from you:

- State the principle — or two candidate principles if one can't do it all — in plain language, not jargon.
- Show it handling all eight cases, and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn. I suspect 4 and 5 together are a headache for anything simple, so tell me if one of them has to go and which.

- Tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't thought of. Specifically: (a) if I put an author's note at the end of the next book apologizing to my hometown for how I portrayed it, is that a real apology, performed by me, or a description of the sentiment behind one? (b) if I use a real letter my grandmother wrote as an epigraph, and in it she promises my grandfather something, did she promise it all over again by being reprinted, or is that promise somehow frozen or used up by now? and (c) when I read my own dialogue out loud at a book event — the priest's wedding lines, say, in the character's voice, with the audience knowing full well it's me reading — did anything get pronounced, and to whom?

One more thing. I gather Austin had some line about how speech acts in fiction are somehow hollow — parasitic on normal use, I think the word was — and if that's really the answer, then fine, but then I want to know why my gut says cases 2, 4 and 5 are exceptions, or whether my gut is just wrong about all three.

Oh and please answer in American English — no "whilst" or "amongst", and it's "toward" not "towards". I read enough British copyeditors this month.
