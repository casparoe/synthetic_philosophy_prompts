I teach a political philosophy seminar, and every year the corruption unit goes the same way: I put the easy case on the board (customs official, brown envelope, everyone nods), and then the whole class falls apart once we get to clientelism and gift-giving. My own views on this are honestly a mess, which is partly why I keep testing them on Dana, who teaches anthropology down the hall and has very firm instincts about this stuff. After enough dinners together I've collected a track record of her verdicts, and here's the thing: I can predict her on some cases with embarassing accuracy and on others I genuinely have no idea. Her rulings so far: a city building inspector who takes cash to overlook code violations is corrupt, full stop, no discussion. A rural doctor in southern Europe whose patients' families bring her wine and homemade cakes at Christmas, and who ends up giving those families slightly longer appointments, is not corrupt -- Dana basically thinks you'd have to be a sociopath to call that corruption. An old-style ward boss who got the snow plowed on streets that turned out the vote for his machine, and who never pocketed a dime himself, is not corrupt either. A senator who quietly slips an earmark into a bill for a project a major donor wants is corrupt. And a customs official in a country where everyone expects a dash to expedite paperwork that people are legally entitled to anyway is, she says, corrupt -- this one suprised me a little given her usual cultural-relativist reflexes. I keep telling her that by a strict Weberian impartiality standard half her "not corrupt" verdicts flip, and she waves me off with something about Weber being an artifact of one particular kind of state.

Next week we're having dinner and I want to walk in with a case that actually tests her. Here it is: a hospital administrator in rural Kenya who decides how to distribute a limited supply of vaccines, and who quietly reserves early slots for her relatives and for people from her home village. She takes no payment for any of this, she sincerely believes she would be a monster to let a stranger jump ahead of her mother, most of the staff and patients expect exactly this behavior from someone in her position, and the ministry's formal protocol says allocation should be strictly need-based. So: (a) given her track record, what is Dana most likely to say about this case? (b) What are the different rules that could each rationalize that track record? I can see at least a personal-enrichment rule (no profit, no corruption), a narrower office-selling rule where corruption requires treating role duties as a commodity for sale, and some version of a local-norms rule where corruption means violating the standards the society itself applies to the role -- and I'd want you to say which of these (or something else entirely) actually fits her best, including whether any of her past verdicts strain a given rule, since her call on the dash case does funny things to the pure local-norms reading. And (c) design one further case, described concretely enough that I could actually put it to her over dessert, where those rules come apart cleanly enough that her answer would tell me which one she's really running. Don't just tell me what she *should* say -- I want a prediction I can win an argument with.
