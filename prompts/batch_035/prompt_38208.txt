I am a retired condensed-matter experimentalist who has lately taken up reading in decision theory, and Shubik's dollar auction, together with what psychologists call escalation of commitment, has made me look with new suspicion at the renewal panel I still sit on, which decides each year which of our institute's long-running experiments continue. Our precedents: we renewed a flagship spectroscopy campaign for its tenth and final year, with nine years and most of its budget already spent, a modest remaining cost, and an expected value of the last tranche that was only slightly positive; we cut a climate-model intercomparison in its second year of four when its costs doubled and the value of what remained fell clearly below the remaining cost; we renewed a thirty-year seismic time series through a budget crunch, although its standalone value for the coming year was modest, because an interruption would have destroyed the scientific worth of all thirty years of records; and we cut a small detector prototype that was perhaps ninety percent complete and needed only a modest top-up, after a rival group published the measurement it was built to make. Next month we decide on the long-baseline experiment: roughly halfway done, with about $250 million sunk of a projected $500 million, whose remaining $250 million buys a result that a competitor's recent measurement has largely anticipated on most, though not all, of its science goals. On a fresh estimate the remaining tranche is worth somewhat less than it costs, and if we stop now the instrument can be repurposed for a different measurement that the community genuinely wants. Predict our verdict — or tell me what it should be if we mean to stay consistent with ourselves — by first identifying the rule we have implicitly been running: fresh-eyes expected value with sunk costs ignored; something on which past investment legitimately raises the marginal value of continuing, as with near-completion or the way the seismic series was only worth continuing because of what came before; or plain dollar-auction escalation, where the more already spent, the harder it is to walk away. Give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, say honestly what principle we would be adopting with each possible choice if our precedents underdetermine it, and tell me which further case, were it ever to come before us, would best tell those rules apart.
