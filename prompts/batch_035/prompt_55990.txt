I hold a view about trolley-type cases that combines the doctrine of double effect with a refinement I've come to regard as essential, and I want to know what would count against it. The core idea, descending from the intention/foreseeing distinction in the double-effect lineage that goes back to Aquinas on lethal self-defense, is this: an agent may bring about foreseen harm only when the good she aims at does not causally depend on that harm occurring. I call this the dependence test, and I take it to sort the familiar cases better than either plain double effect or any "using a person as a mere means" formula. Turning the trolley from the five onto the one is permissible, on my view, precisely because the five are saved whether or not the one dies — were he to leap clear at the last instant, the diversion would still have saved them. The footbridge case and the loop variant, where the one's body stops the trolley, come out impermissible, because there the good plainly depends on the harm; transplant fails in the same way. I like that the test explains why many people who accept diversion balk at footbridge and loop without resting anything on contested claims about what is "part of the agent's intention," and that it predicts a sharp asymmetry between standard diversion and loop that a straightforward maximizing view, or a pure doing/allowing distinction, cannot reproduce. What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: cases whose intuitive verdicts the dependence test gets wrong (including cases where the harm depends on the good rather than the good on the harm — e.g., a victim who dies only because the agent diverted, where the five would have been saved anyway); arguments that the test collapses into a rival principle or into consequentialism under some description; the worry that "depends on" comes in degrees, and that any line drawn across that spectrum is arbitrary; empirical results on order effects and demographic variation in trolley judgments, and how much those should trouble me; and the charge that by explaining away contrary intuitions as bias I have insulated the view into unfalsifiability. For each defeater, say whether I, as a committed defender, should concede it as genuine evidence against the view or resist it, and be honest about which of these the view's actual rivals could not easily absorb themselves. Please end with a single final line of the form "Verdict: ..." containing your bottom-line assessment of how well the dependence test holds up, in no more than twenty words.
