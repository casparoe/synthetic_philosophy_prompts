I'm TAing a 200-level metaphysics course — 18 students, one 75-minute section, a room with movable desks. We just covered the special composition question, i.e. under what conditions do some things compose a further thing, and the students have mostly decided it's verbal bookkeeping: whether the bolt-and-nut assembly on the table is one object or two is just a way of talking, nothing worth fighting over. I was complaining about this to the instructor over coffee and she said stop assigning more reading and make them do something instead. So: I want an activity where they run into the question themselves, before anyone names it for them. The moment I'm after is the one where every physical fact about a setup is fixed — positions, what's glued, what's resting on what, what's touching — and they still catch themselves genuinely unsure, and arguing with each other, about how many objects are sitting there. And then a second moment, where they notice that no further observation would settle it: the missing fact isn't a physical one, and no measurement is coming to the rescue.

I can bring materials: a duffel of junk — a chipped teacup that's been glued back together, a loose stack of books, a handful of hex nuts, a bag of sand, some velcro strips, a dead phone. Have them stage scenes with that, or propose your own materials if you think something else works better. What I want from you is a full design: setup, roles (I'm imagining some students act as registrars who have to issue a list of the objects in a scene and commit to it, and others as auditors of some kind — but you tell me), a timeline that fits 75 minutes, and a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money. Here's the trap I want built into the scoring: the participants should be rewarded for something, so their choices have stakes and they invest in them — but the payoffs should quietly never depend on how many objects they counted. They argue hard about the count, and then discover the count did no work anywhere in the game. That's the engine of the whole thing.

Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the real positions — nihilism, unrestricted composition, the restricted answers like composition only occurring in living things — and onto the argument from vagueness that people use to push toward unrestricted composition. Two things I want you to be honest about. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I assume some students will refuse to take the counting seriously at all, and others will just pick a convention quickly and thereby miss the point — how do I handle that in the moment without just telling them the answer? Second, which part of the actual debate the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. My own guess is the activity can generate the felt arbitrariness of any cutoff, but can't reproduce the sorites-style reasoning that makes unrestricted composition look like the only principled option — if you agree, say how I bridge that gap in the debrief without it collapsing into a lecture, which is exactly what I'm trying to avoid.
