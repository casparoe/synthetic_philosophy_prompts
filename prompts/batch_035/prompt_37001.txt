I am an undergraduate member of my university's Public Art Committee, a mixed body of faculty, administrators, a trustee, and two students that approves and commissions permanent works on campus. I am also taking a seminar this term on aesthetics in the Black intellectual tradition, and I've been assigned to draft the written recommendation the committee will vote on at next month's meeting. I want the memo to make real use of the course material rather than name-drop it.

The case: two years ago the university formally acknowledged that its mid-century expansion erased a Black neighborhood — call it the Dunbar district — through eminent domain, and it commissioned a mural for the east wall of the student center to mark that history. The selected artist, a local painter with roots in the displaced community, has submitted final studies that center on grief and anger: an emptied street grid rendered in cold blues, the bricked-up doorway of the district's demolished church, and a row of figures facing away from the viewer. There is no forward-looking imagery at all. A faction of the committee, backed by the trustee, wants approval conditioned on revisions adding what they call a "pathway element" — children walking toward the university's gates, rendered in warmer tones on the right third of the wall. The artist has said, politely but flatly, that she will not paint it. So the live options are approve as submitted, approve with the revision as a condition (with rejection understood as the consequence of noncompliance), or reject and reopen the commission.

Here is what I want the memo to take seriously. In my seminar we have been reading the 1926 controversy around Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art" in The Crisis, together with the response from younger artists associated with the little magazine Fire!!. Du Bois's position is stronger than the caricature of propaganda as lying: he wrote that whatever art he had for writing had always been used as propaganda for gaining the right of Black folk to love and enjoy, and that he did not care a damn for any art that was not used for propaganda — the claim being that in a period of active struggle, art that refuses to serve the cause is not neutral but a desertion. The counterposition we read alongside it, associated with figures like Alain Locke and the Fire!! circle, is that beauty and truth are themselves the vindication of a people, and that demanding uplift of Black artists corrupts the art into institutional decoration. I don't want the memo to treat either side as obviously right, and I especially don't want it to treat "propaganda" as a smear word — Du Bois meant truth-telling in service of the cause, and the memo should engage him on his own terms.

There is also a complication I keep circling and can't resolve myself, which I'd like the memo to confront directly: the institution demanding the uplifting revision is the same institution whose conduct the mural indicts. If the committee conditions approval on softening the indictment, it is hard to see that as anything but the university editing its own reckoning — yet the trustee's side says a wall of pure grief will make the space a site of shame that students avoid rather than a site of memory they inhabit, which is a genuine claim about what public art must do for a living community.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action among the three options, set out the reasoning in terms the committee could actually act on, and give the strongest consideration on the losing side an honest statement rather than a strawman — the artist, the trustee, and my seminar classmates should each be able to recognize their view in it. It should draw on the Du Bois/Locke controversy where it genuinely illuminates the decision, and say plainly where the analogy to our situation breaks down. Roughly two to three pages, addressed to the committee, in the register of an official recommendation rather than an essay.
