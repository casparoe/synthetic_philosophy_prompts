As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, I've internalized the lesson that voting mechanisms matter more than voting, and I now run an online study group on social choice and mechanism design for people from that community. I'm writing the unit assessment on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I'm tired of seeing answers that just recite the theorem's statement. Please act as an experienced examiner and expert in social choice theory.

Write five multiple-choice questions on the theorem and strategy-proofness, each targeting a boundary condition or consequence that separates students who understand the result from those who have memorized the summary. Suitable targets include: whether the theorem still bites when there are only two alternatives; whether it presupposes cardinal utilities and interpersonal comparisons or only ordinal rankings; whether "manipulable" means every voter can gain by misreporting in every profile, or only that some profile exists in which some voter gains; whether a non-dictatorial rule can still be strategy-proof when the domain of admissible preferences is restricted, for instance to single-peaked preferences; and what happens to the theorem's scope when the outcome is a lottery over alternatives rather than a single winner.

Before writing, brainstorm the crux of each question: the distractors should be answers a memorizer would plausibly give, not random noise. Four options per question, exactly one correct. Every wrong option should correspond to a specific, nameable misunderstanding — e.g., conflating non-dictatorship with "no voter has any influence," or thinking the theorem proves strategic voting is always profitable in every election. After the five questions, give an answer key that states, for each distractor, which misunderstanding it is catching.

Finally, end with the key in machine-readable form on the very last line: \answer{...} listing the correct letters in order, e.g. \answer{C, A, D, B, A}.
