The exam question was as follows:

Question 3 (25 points). State the doctrine of double effect, carefully distinguishing the conditions it places on an action that has both a good and a bad effect. Explain how the doctrine has been used to defend the contrast between the permissibility of a bystander's diverting a runaway trolley from five workers onto a track where it will kill one worker and the impermissibility of pushing a large man off a footbridge into the trolley's path, thereby stopping it and saving the five. Then present the strongest objection you can to the doctrine's treatment of these cases, and either defend the doctrine against that objection or concede that the doctrine fails.

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

The doctrine of double effect (DDE) was introduced by Philippa Foot in her 1967 paper "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of Double Effect," which also gave us the trolley problem, including the famous case of the large man pushed from the footbridge. The doctrine descends from Aquinas's discussion of private self-defense and holds that an action with both a good and a bad effect is permissible provided that (1) the act itself is not wrong; (2) the agent intends only the good effect, the bad effect being merely foreseen; (3) the good effect is not achieved by means of the bad effect; and (4) there is proportionality between the good and bad effects.

Applying the doctrine: in the bystander case, the agent who throws the switch intends only the redirection of the trolley; the death of the one worker on the side track is a foreseen side effect, not part of her plan. In the footbridge case, by contrast, the agent must intend the large man's death, or at least use his body as a means of stopping the trolley, so the doctrine correctly rules it out. The doctrine at bottom just restates the familiar distinction between doing and allowing: the bystander who fails to act allows five to die, whereas the footbridge agent does something that kills. That is why our firm intuitions about the two cases have the content they do.

Consider next the loop case, in which the diverted trolley would continue on to kill the five unless it is stopped by the large man's body, which happens to be on the side track. Here, too, the doctrine permits diverting, since the agent's aim is merely to send the trolley onto the side track; the man's death is still only foreseen, not intended, and it is not as though she could not have preferred it otherwise.

The strongest objection is this: since the bystander knows with certainty that the one will die if she throws the switch, the death is part of what she intends, and a distinction between intention and mere foresight cannot be sustained where the foreseen outcome is certain. This objection fails, however. The bystander does not desire the one's death, and what intention amounts to is what the agent desires to bring about. Since she would prefer a world in which the one escaped the side track unharmed, she does not intend his death, and the doctrine survives.
