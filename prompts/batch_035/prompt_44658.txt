A friend from my rationality meetup has been getting into forecasting and measurement stuff lately, and at last week's meetup he gave an impromptu five-minute talk on Goodhart's law. I wrote his summary down afterwards, roughly: "Goodhart was a Bank of England economist who noticed that whenever a statistical regularity gets used as a target for policy, it breaks down — the moment the government started targeting the money supply in the early 80s, the stable relationship between money growth and inflation fell apart, which is why the whole monetarist experiment collapsed. Generalized: any measure that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure. The mechanism is that people game the metric — teachers teach to the test, hospitals admit the easy patients, so the metric stops tracking the thing you actually cared about. Which is why we can basically never have good metrics at all: every proxy diverges from its target the moment you lean on it, so numbers are only trustworthy as long as nobody's acting on them."

Most of this sounded right to me, but that last sentence felt too strong, and something about "the mechanism is that people game it" bugged me too — I have a vague memory of LessWrong posts distinguising different *kinds* of Goodhart, where some versions don't involve anyone scheming at all, just noise and selection effects. So: can you go through his summary bit by bit — what's accurate, what's a subtle conflation or overstatement, and whether his "no good metrics ever" conclusion actually follows from the parts that are true? Also, is his phrasing of the law the original one, or a later paraphrase that's doing slightly different work than what Goodhart himself claimed? I'd especially like to know where the strongest defensible version of his point ends and the overshoot begins, since I'm bringing this up at the next meetup and don't want to correct his errors by making the opposite ones.
