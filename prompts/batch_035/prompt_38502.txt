Drafting a piece for a small philosophy blog (mixed audience, some longtermists, some decision theory people) on whether the simulation argument should make us less fanatical about the far future. Before I send it to my editor I'd like comments. What I mainly want to know: does the move in paragraphs 3-4 actually work or am I cheating somewhere, and does the ending land or does it just sound clever. Also flag anything factually embarassing. I'm not trying to publish in a journal so I don't need it airtight, just not obviously wrong.

Here's the draft:

---

**Title: The far future might be someone else's past**

The case for fanaticism about the far future goes like this. However small the probability, the value at stake in securing a flourishing civilization for billions of years is so astronomical that it swamps any comparison with present-day goods. Reduce existential risk by 0.0001% and, on expectational reasoning, you've done more good than a thousand ordinary careers, provided the future you're securing is long and rich. Call someone fanatical when they accept this -- when they'll trade large certain benefits for tiny probabilities of astronomical ones. Plenty of people think the decision theory here is broken, that we should reject unbounded aggregation, that Pascal's mugging shows the whole framework is unstable. Those objections all attack the decision theory. I want to suggest a different kind of attack, one aimed at the quantity rather than the machinery.

The simulation argument (in the Bostrom trilemma version) says that one of three things is true: civilizations like ours almost never reach the technological maturity needed to run detailed ancestor simulations; or mature civilizations that could run them choose not to; or we are almost certainly living in one right now. The first disjunct is bad news for longtermism anyway. So the interesting case is: suppose we grant that mature civilizations do run ancestor simulations. Then, conditional on us having any future descendants at all, we are very likely simulated.

Here's the move. Ask what our far future is, if we're an ancestor simulation. Two cases. Case one: the simulation is faithful and runs to completion -- the simulators want to see how our whole history went. But that means our entire future is a replay of something that already happened at base level. Whatever we do -- colonize the galaxy, or cook ourselves -- has already been done, in the original. Our choices matter enormously to us, obviously, and to the other people in here with us. But "making the far future go well" turns out to be the wrong description of what we're doing. The far future already went however it went, before we were ever run. There's nothing left to secure; there's only a record being inscribed. Case two: the simulation is partial -- the simulators are interested in, say, the transition to machine intelligence, or the period up to some cutoff, and shut us down after that. Then our far future is bounded not by physics or by our choices but by base-level curiosity and compute budgets. In both cases the astronomical-value tail that does the fanatic's work is much thinner than the naive calculation assumes. And note the shape of this: it's not a discount on the probability of a long future, it's a discount on the very idea that the length of our future is up to us.

Paragraph four is where I feel shakiest. The obvious comeback is that simulations are morally real: simulated suffering hurts, a simulated galaxy-full of flourishing lives is flourishing lives, so if case one is true and the replay is long and good, the fanaticism just reattaches itself -- securing that long good future still matters, even if it's a rerun. I try to answer that our relation to the outcome is different -- we're not steering toward the outcome, we're exhibiting it -- but honestly I'm not sure that difference does any moral work, and a reviewer who pressed there would hurt. There's also a smaller comeback I just wave at: maybe the thing to do, if you're an ancestor sim, is be the kind of civilization simulators don't shut down early, which arguably reintroduces a fanatical structure one level up. I mention it in one sentence and move on, which I suspect is a mistake.

The conclusion I draw is deliberately modest: the simulation argument doesn't refute fanaticism, it dampens it, and from a direction the standard objections never come from. The usual attacks say your decision theory is too aggressive. This one says your ledger miscounts what's on offer -- the far future is either someone else's past or someone else's product. A fanatic with a thinner target is less of a fanatic, even if they never notice the difference.

So, questions: (a) does the faithful-replay vs. truncated-sim dichotomy actually exhaust the cases, or is there an obvious third option I'm missing (e.g. simulations that fork, or that simulators re-run with tweaks -- I keep going back and forth on whether re-runs break the dichotomy or just multiply case one)? (b) is the "the far future already went" line coherent, or does it smuggle in some metaphysics of time it can't cash (base level and our level presumably share a time dimension, or do they?). (c) where would the longtermists in my readership actually push back hardest, and (d) be honest about whether the ending is doing philosophy or just doing rhetoric. Also tell me if the tone is too cute throughout, my editor says I do that.
