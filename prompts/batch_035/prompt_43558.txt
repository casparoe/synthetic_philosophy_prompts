So it's April, which means the taxation-is-theft posts are back on my feeds, and instead of scrolling past this year I actually sat down and tried to formalise the argument, and now I'm annoyed because I can't find the broken step and it seems to prove a lot more than the people posting it want. Here's what a friend and I got it down to after a few rounds on a forum thread:

(1) Theft is taking something that's yours, without your consent, under threat of force. Mugger, burglar, pickpocket — no disputes there.
(2) For consent to count it has to be actual: you signed it, said it, or would affirm it if asked. Tacit consent from silence or from staying put is fake consent — otherwise the mugger has your consent because you handed over the wallet rather than your legs, and the protection racket has the shopkeeper's consent because he keeps paying up.
(3) Nobody actually consents to taxation. I never signed the social contract, I was born under it; voting isn't consent, since a majority of other people can't consent on my behalf and my one vote binds nothing anyway; and love-it-or-leave-it is exactly the racket move from (2).
(4) What the state takes in tax is mine before it takes it. Pre-tax income is my labour's product; the state doesn't create it, it just claims a cut.
(5) The state takes it anyway, under threat of force — try withholding and see what happens.
(6) So taxation is theft, in exactly the sense the mugger's taking is.

Each of (1)-(5) looks fine to me when I stare at it on its own, which is the usual sign I'm about to embarass myself. The problem is where else the same chain goes:

(7) Run (2)-(3) on property instead of tax. The squatter who rejects your deed never actually consented to your land claim; the title system is enforced against him under threat of force with no actual consent from him. So enforcing property rights against the non-consenting is also robbery — which means the nightwatchman state the tax-is-theft crowd wants is funded by theft and operates by theft.
(8) My friend says fine, he bites the bullet all the way to market anarchism: private defence agencies, fees instead of taxes. But run (2)-(3) again — the agency enforces its norms on anyone who never consented, and someone who never signed on gets enforced against all the same, just under a different letterhead. He says that's different because you can switch agencies, but I never consented to the switching costs either, and per (2) "you can exit" is the racket move again.

So either the whole thing proves far too much, or something in the chain is fake. I don't want to conclude my local council and a cat burglar are morally indistinguishable, but I also can't see which premise to give up that doesn't cost me something I care about. For what it's worth: I want to keep (2) — the mafia point is the strongest thing that side ever produced — and I want to keep saying the mugger clearly wrongs me. I'm much less attached to (4), but I can't quite see what's wrong with it, since it seems weird to say my salary isn't mine before the state touches it.

Give me a committed answer: name the specific premise or inference to reject, explain why it looked fine, and tell me honestly what rejecting it costs — including whether my friend's anarchist retreat actually escapes the problem or just inherits it with worse branding. Don't give me a menu of options and tell me to pick one.
