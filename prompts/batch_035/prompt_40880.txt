I recently came across a parlor game that is supposed to explain why people stay in things long past the point of sense. A dollar bill is put up for auction. The highest bidder wins the dollar and pays whatever they bid; the twist is that the second-highest bidder must also pay their full bid and gets nothing at all. Everyone is told the rules in advance, and yet the bidding routinely climbs past a dollar anyway. People invoke it to explain renovations that never end, lawsuits nobody can drop, and wars nobody can quit — the phrase I keep hearing attached to it is that once you're in for a penny, you're in for a pound.

My brother-in-law, who plays a lot of poker, thinks the fuss is overblown. On his telling, every bid already made in that game is like chips already pushed into the pot: spent, unrecoverable, and therefore irrelevant to what anyone should do next. The only question a rational player ever faces is forward-looking — from this point on, is the next bid worth it? Understood that way, he says, the game contains no paradox, just a lesson about ignoring what you've already paid, and the sensible move is to walk away early and never look back. He generalizes it to life: the reason to quit a dead-end job, a failing project, or a bad degree program is exactly the reason to fold a hand — the chips in the pot don't get to vote.

Something about this bothers me, though I can't pin it down. In poker, the pot is indifferent — the chips just sit there waiting for whoever wins. In this game, my past bids seem to matter prospectively: every dollar I've bid is now a dollar the other person loses if they finish second, which means the past hasn't merely happened to me, it is aimed at my opponent, changing what staying in is worth to them and thus what they'll do. That doesn't feel like dead chips. But he's the one who plays cards for money and I don't, so I may just be fooling myself.

Could you audit his analogy properly? Please set out what maps onto what — what plays the role of the pot, the chips, folding, the other players, the showdown — and then say where the mapping breaks down. I'd like you to separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion (walk away early, treat everything already spent as gone) from the ones that don't. Then tell me whether his conclusion survives once the broken parts are on the table, or whether the poker picture was quietly carrying it all along. And if you think the auction is better compared to something else entirely, I'd like to hear what, and what the better comparison predicts about the real-life cases people attach to this game.
