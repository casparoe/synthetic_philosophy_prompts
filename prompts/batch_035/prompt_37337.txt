I'm a high school senior writing an extended essay on constitutional monarchy in parliamentary democracies, and I've noticed that almost everything I read ranks the interpretations the same way: either the monarch is a harmless ornament, or she is a dormant guardian whose reserve powers exist for genuine emergencies, and the guardian view is treated as the serious one while the ornamental view is what you tell tourists. For my essay I want a thought experiment that reverses this ranking: a scenario in which the monarch who does exactly what the conventions literally require, and nothing more, ends up protecting the constitution better than a monarch who uses the reserve powers as a guardian would.

Here are the specifications. The setting should be a realistic, contemporary Westminster-style parliamentary democracy: no coups, no violence, no futuristic technology. The government must advise the monarch to do something she has at least a serious constitutional argument for refusing — prorogation timed to dodge a confidence vote, a dissolution that resets the electoral clock in the government's favour, assent to a measure moving election oversight into the governing party's hands — but the impropriety must be genuinely contestable, not blatant. If the advice is nakedly unconstitutional, refusal becomes trivially right and the case teaches nothing. And the reason compliance comes out ahead must not be that the advice was secretly fine. It must be that invoking the reserve powers, even correctly, damages the institution that carries them: build in realistic evidence within the scenario — polling, editorials, statements from constitutional lawyers — that intervention in this contestable case would hand the governing party a legitimacy grievance ("an unelected monarch blocked your elected government") that it would ride to an election victory and then use to strip the reserve powers by statute or referendum, replacing the Crown with a figurehead appointed by the prime minister. The guardian spends the backstop in the least compelling emergency it will ever face, and the public then lives without a backstop at all.

Please deliver, first, the scenario itself, no more than two paragraphs; second, a walkthrough of what each of three positions says and why: (i) the strict ceremonialist, who holds that the monarch must act on ministerial advice in all but a literal constitutional breakdown; (ii) the reserve-powers guardian, who holds that the Crown may refuse advice when refusal is necessary to avert a subversion of the constitution; and (iii) the constitutional-insurance view, on which the monarchy's value lies precisely in being a check that is never cashed. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdict if that feature is stripped out, and how a defender of the guardian view would attack the case. I can already imagine one line of attack — that a backstop never used in a real emergency is not a backstop at all, so if the conventions never license refusal in a contestable case, the guardian conception was incoherent from the start — and I'd like to know whether that reply actually works or just restates the problem. You may draw on real episodes for texture, such as the 1975 Australian dismissal, the 2008 Canadian prorogation request, or the 2019 United Kingdom prorogation litigation, but the scenario must stand on its own, since I'm testing the doctrines rather than anyone's memory of events.

One request about how you write this up: please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area. What written constitutions and established conventions require, and what happened in those historical episodes, are settled; what the reserve powers are actually for, whether a doctrine of never using them in a partisan context is coherent, and whether an unused check is a real check are live disputes. Please don't present any of those as the consensus view, and flag clearly where constitutional scholars themselves disagree.
