I'm a third-year PPE student, and last week's seminar in my module on justice and the law was on prostitution policy: the Nordic model, under which buying sex is a criminal offence but selling it is not; the German and Dutch regimes, which legalise the trade under licensing and registration; and New Zealand-style full decriminalisation. Twice in one hour someone reached for the slogan 'sex work is work', and I left unsure what it actually claims. A classmate used it against the Nordic model: criminalising the buyer means treating an industry's customers as criminals, which we do for no other trade. I then used it myself to argue for decriminalisation over licensing, and my tutor replied that the slogan is either trivial or false — trivial if it means only that money changes hands for services (a contract killing also involves money changing hands), and false if it means sex work resembles other occupations in every respect that matters to the law, since what is being sold is access to the body. She then posed a question I could not answer: Germany and the Netherlands also treat it as work, subject to licences and registration, so if the slogan is satisfied by a licensed regime, it does not choose between the German model and the New Zealand one; and if it is not satisfied by a licensed regime, I do not know which reading does that work.

One thing that struck me afterwards: here in England and Wales the slogan is already true in the narrowest sense — selling sex is not itself a crime, although brothel-keeping, kerb-crawling and controlling prostitution for gain are — and nobody thinks that narrow truth settles anything about how the industry should be governed. That makes me suspect the slogan has more readings than the seminar gave it credit for.

What I would like is a menu I can take back to the next seminar. Please lay out the distinct precise claims 'sex work is work' could be expressing, ordered from the platitude to the most radical, and for each one say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or plainly false. Then say, for each reading, whether it would actually support the two uses it was put to on Monday: hers against the Nordic model, and mine for decriminalisation over legalisation. Finally, give me your verdict on the tutor's dilemma: is there a middle reading that is neither trivial nor false, and, if so, does it discriminate between the German and New Zealand models — or does the slogan simply underdetermine the policy question? If the latter, please be honest about it: I would rather discover that the slogan does less work than the seminar assumed than keep leaning on it.
