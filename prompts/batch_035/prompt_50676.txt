i keep running into the same wall in arguments about human enhancement, and i want a diagnosis of the wall, not another round of the argument itself.

The debate, quickly: is there a morally important line between therapy (getting someone back to normal — antidepressants for someone who can't get out of bed, a cochlear implant for deafness) and enhancement (pushing past normal — stimulants for focus in someone without ADHD, gene edits for extra muscle)? One side says the line is obvious and does real work: health systems owe you therapy but not enhancement, and going past normal starts to smell like cheating, or eugenics-lite. The other side says the line is a mirage: normal is just a statistic about the species, glasses and vaccines already push us past what our ancestors' bodies could do on their own, and if the whole line rests on species-typical functioning then it's morally arbitrary — being born below the median on some trait isn't obviously more of a tragedy than being born at the median when everyone could be moved up. And every case gets mirrored in a way that makes the line look weird: growth hormone for a hormone-deficient kid counts as therapy, for a short-but-healthy kid it counts as enhancement, but it's the same injection and the parents have the same worry both times.

The thing is, all the arguements have been on the table for decades and nobody moves an inch. My working theory, coming at this as a software engineer: both sides are actually arguing about defaults, not about a line. Therapy is restore-to-factory-settings, enhancement is changing the config, and people only think a config change needs extra justification because they're implicitly treating the factory settings as something that was chosen — by evolution with intentions, by God, by Nature-as-engineer. If you don't buy that there was ever a spec author, the moral difference evaporates, and no amount of case-juggling will bring it back. So the impasse would be a disagreement about a hidden metaphysical assumption, smuggled in under the heading of medicine.

Is that right? Or is it something else — a verbal dispute about the word 'health', a hidden empirical question about what species-typical functioning actually tracks, or two groups answering different questions under one name (one about what an insurance system should pay for, one about what kinds of people it's good to become)? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually claims, not a survey of the literature. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me honestly: is there anything that would count as progress here — a distinction someone hasn't made yet, a reframing, anything — or is this a debate that stays alive precisely because the question is ill-posed?
