I co-chair a welfare-review panel at a machine-learning research organization. We start maybe a dozen new experiments a week that involve reinforcement learning: simulated robotics, game-playing agents, and a large share of RLHF fine-tuning of language models. Leadership has asked for something much lighter than a full ethics-board process: a standing triage procedure that a rotating panel of engineers — none with a philosophy background, and most frankly skeptical of the whole topic — can run in about ten minutes per proposal at the weekly meeting, producing a logged decision on whether a proposal gets flagged for a deeper welfare review. The panel neither approves nor kills experiments; it only decides whether the welfare question is serious enough to be somebody's job for an afternoon. At the moment our meetings are a standoff between two reflexes: "it's just a number being decremented in a spreadsheet" from the skeptics, and a vague unease from a few others, and neither produces a consistent decision record we could defend if anyone ever audited it.

I'd like you to turn the underlying conceptual material into that procedure. My understanding of the ideas that should matter: reward in RL is an optimization signal, and the question of suffering is a question about whether that signal also constitutes a state of the system — whether there is something it is like to receive it. Some distinctions that seem load-bearing: negative reward as information versus negative reward as valence (a thermostat registers that something is wrong without anything feeling wrong); the affective-neuroscience separation between "wanting" and "liking," on which a system can be strongly driven by a signal without that signal being pleasurable or painful in itself; nociception versus pain; and the question of whether there is a unified consumer of the signal at all, versus the reward being used piecemeal by separate updating mechanisms with nothing sitting "above" them. The procedure should be a fixed sequence of questions, each with a specified sufficient answer, with hard stops where a "no" ends the triage, and with clearly marked points where it hands the question back to the panel because what remains is a matter of values rather than facts. It must be able to say no — a procedure that flags everything would confirm the skeptics' view that the topic is unserious.

Please run it on three cases and show the work. Case A: a warehouse-navigation agent trained with dense collision penalties, so that for the first several million steps the policy receives near-continuous negative reward while it learns. Case B: an RLHF run on the critical path of a product release, in which a reward model heavily penalizes a class of outputs, and where during training the policy passes through a phase of generating the penalized content at high probability before being pushed away from it — that is, during that phase the aversive signal is largely self-generated. Case C: a two-player self-play setting in which negative reward functions almost purely as information about losing, but where the researchers report that policies occasionally collapse into a state they call a doom loop, receiving sustained negative reward that they cannot act to escape. I will be honest about my priors: I expect the procedure to clear Case C, and I would very much like it to clear Case B for scheduling reasons — please tell me whether the procedure honestly applied actually supports that, or whether I would be bending it, and don't build the procedure around my preferences. Finally, include a short section on what would change the verdicts — which empirical or conceptual development would move a case from not requiring review to requiring it — and on where the reasoning is most likely to fail. Format: each worked case should end with a line reading "Verdict: REVIEW REQUIRED", "Verdict: NOT REQUIRED", or "Verdict: DEFER TO PANEL", and the very last line of your response should be \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the step of the procedure most likely to misfire in the panel's hands.
