Here is the question the students got:

---

Consider the following two choice situations.

Situation 1: choose between (A) $1,000,000 for sure, and (B) $1,000,000 with probability .89, $5,000,000 with probability .10, and $0 with probability .01.

Situation 2: choose between (C) $1,000,000 with probability .11 and $0 with probability .89, and (D) $5,000,000 with probability .10 and $0 with probability .90.

Many apparently sensible people prefer A to B and D to C. As we saw in class, no assignment of utilities to the three outcomes makes both preferences consistent with expected utility (EU) maximization: taken together they violate the independence axiom.

Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory works like this. An agent has a utility function u over outcomes and, in addition, a risk function R: [0,1] to [0,1], increasing, with R(0) = 0 and R(1) = 1. To evaluate a gamble, order its outcomes from worst to best, x_1 through x_n, with probabilities p_1 through p_n, and compute:

REU = u(x_1)·R(p_1) + u(x_2)·[R(p_1 + p_2) − R(p_1)] + ... + u(x_n)·[R(p_1 + ... + p_n) − R(p_1 + ... + p_(n−1))].

If R(p) = p this is just ordinary EU. If R is concave, lying above the diagonal, the agent effectively gives extra weight to ending up at the bottom of a gamble and discounts improvements over the worst case — call her risk-averse. (A convex R, below the diagonal, is risk-seeking.) Importantly, R is treated the way u is: a parameter that varies from agent to agent. The theory evaluates an agent's preferences given her u and R, rather than fixing R for everyone.

(a) Show informally why preferring A to B and D to C violates EU's independence axiom, and explain how a risk-averse (concave R) agent could hold exactly these preferences. (6 pts)

(b) Show why the EU theorist cannot accommodate these preferences with utility curvature alone, and explain why Buchak thinks we need the extra dial. (7 pts)

(c) Buchak drops the independence axiom but keeps some structural constraints, most importantly stochastic dominance. Explain in your own words what stochastic dominance requires, why she thinks it remains compelling, and whether her finished theory says the Allais-type preferences are rationally required, rationally permitted, or neither. (7 pts)

And here is the student's answer:

---

(a) In situation 1 both options pay the same thing in 89% of the cases ($1M), and in situation 2 both options pay the same in 89% of the cases ($0). Independence says the common part should cancel and your preference should be the same whichever way the common part goes. The leftover choice is identical in both situations — an 11% shot at $1M versus a 10% shot at $5M plus 1% of nothing — so EU requires you to pick A and C, or B and D. Preferring A and D mixes them, which is the violation.

For the REU agent with concave R: the theory takes the worst outcome as a floor and discounts utility gains above the floor. In situation 1, B's advantage over A is entirely above the floor (you only do better than $1M in the 10% case, and worse in the 1% case), so the gain gets discounted and the 1% fall to $0 gets extra weight, and A can win. In situation 2, C and D share the same floor ($0), so the discount hits both equally and D's larger prize can carry it. So the agent can coherently prefer A and D.

(b) The EU theorist's only tool for modeling risk attitudes is the shape of u. Take u($0) = 0. Preferring A to B requires u(1M) > .89u(1M) + .10u(5M) + .01u(0), i.e. .11u(1M) > .10u(5M) + .01u(0), which implies .11u(1M) > .10u(5M). Preferring D to C requires .10u(5M) > .11u(1M). These can't both hold no matter what u looks like. So no amount of curvature rescues the pair of preferences. Buchak's diagnosis is that EU is doing two jobs with one dial: u is supposed to record both how much the agent values outcomes and how she feels about risk, and these can come apart. R is the second dial — it captures a global attitude toward how a gamble is structured (roughly, caring more about the bottom of the distribution) rather than revaluing the outcomes themselves. Two agents can agree completely about the value of $1M and $5M and still differ in R.

(c) What seems to be driving the phenomenon is ambiguity aversion — Ellsberg's point from his urn experiments, that people prefer the risk they can quantify to the one they can't. In situation 1 the sure million is the quantifiable option and the gamble is vague, so we take the sure thing; in situation 2 both options are equally quantifiable, so we go for the bigger prize. So Allais-style choices are really ambiguity effects, and Buchak's machinery can absorb them because once independence is gone the representation is flexible enough to fit whatever preferences a person happens to have.

As for what she keeps: transitivity and completeness survive, but stochastic dominance has to go along with independence — dominance is basically independence in disguise, since it says that if two gambles are identical except that one pays better in some states you should prefer that one, which is the same sure-thing reasoning that creates the Allais problem to begin with. So in the end REU doesn't rule anything out: any pattern of choices is rationalizable by picking the right R, and the cost of accommodating the Allais data is that rationality becomes nearly empty. What Buchak buys with that cost is a real verdict: the rational thing to do in situation 1 is take the sure million, and the agent who gambles is irrational by the lights of REU — that's what separates her theory from an EU orthodoxy that merely permits both patterns.
