Our department runs an annual event where each of us circulates a short position statement — a blunt, numbered list of commitments in a single problem area — and the rest of the group spends an afternoon attacking it. Mine this year is on meaning and rule-following, and before I send it around I'd like a consistency audit, since I've been assembling the list piecemeal and I genuinely don't know where it's weakest. Here it is. (1) I'm a naturalist about meaning: what fixes what someone means are natural facts about that person, and there are no irreducibly normative or non-natural semantic facts. (2) What fixed what I meant by the word plus, back when I learned arithmetic, is roughly my dispositional and behavioral history; I'm aware of the finitude and error problems for this, but I've always regarded them as technical — the kind of thing refinements like a competence/performance distinction can absorb. (3) Meaning is essentially normative: to mean addition by plus is to be answerable to a standard my future applications can fail, which is why the claim that since I meant addition and not quus, the answer to 68 + 57 ought to be 125 strikes me as expressing a genuine ought, not a mere prediction about myself. (4) The communitarian reading of Wittgenstein is mistaken: a lifelong solitary castaway could perfectly well mean things by his words; the community's role in language is causal and epistemic, never constitutive. (5) At the same time, I accept the private language argument itself — no term gets its meaning through inner ostension to a purely private sensation. (6) I reject the skeptical solution: the demand for a fact that constitutes my having meant addition is a legitimate demand, and there must be a straight answer to it. (7) Meaning supervenes on the individual's total natural history: two creatures alike in that respect cannot differ in what they mean.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a survey of the literature. Go through the commitments pairwise, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, give the cheapest fix for each, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. A few things I'm already half-worried about, though I don't trust my own diagnoses: two colleagues insist that (4) and (5) are flatly inconsistent — is holding both really as incoherent as they claim, or is there a reading on which they sit together? Someone else told me that (7) simply begs the question against the skeptic, but I can't see that (7) asserts anything the skeptic denies, as opposed to something he merely challenges — is that distinction doing real work or am I flattering myself? And I'd like to know whether the optimism in (2) about technical refinements can survive (3) at all, or whether the two of them force a choice I've been refusing to notice. Finally, if the whole package only stands because I'm quietly using the word normative in two different senses across the list, please say so plainly.
