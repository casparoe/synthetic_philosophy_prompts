My sister's a nurse and at dinner last week she and her husband got into it about something from her work. A colleague of hers had a lab report and the analyzer printout both saying a patient was O-negative, and was completely sure, but re-ran the typing by hand anyway before a transfusion. Her husband said the double-check was silly: either the colleague knew the patient was O-negative, in which case why re-run anything, or she didn't. My sister said that's not how it works — when a mistake means someone dies, the standard you have to meet to *count* as knowing goes up; the same evidence that would let you skip the recheck for a routine wristband would be reckless here. He said then "knows" just means "confident enough for current purposes," which turns knowledge into a fact about our convenience rather than about the patient's blood. She said no — it's still a fact about the blood, but what it takes to be entitled to treat it as settled moves with what's riding on it, and that's not convenience, it's what knowing is *for*. He came back that if the standard moves with the stakes, the sentence "she knows the patient is O-neg" could be true said by the day-shift nurse and false said by the night-shift supervisor about the same person with the same evidence, which is absurd. She said lots of words work that way — "flat," for one — and he said a table being flat-enough for a glass isn't the same as blood being typed-enough for a transfusion, since only one of those has a right answer independent of our purposes. I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who's right at each step, and where (if anywhere) they're both missing something?
