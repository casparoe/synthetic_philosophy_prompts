I am an actuary who has spent two decades in pricing and capital modeling, and I have recently been reading about the debate between egalitarians and prioritarians in distributive ethics. I can follow the philosophy with effort, but I keep involuntarily translating it into the only vocabulary in which my intuitions are reliable: expected utility, concave transformations, risk aversion, certainty equivalents, diversification, and stochastic orderings. I would like you to perform that translation systematically and then tell me what it destroys.

First, restate the two positions. Prioritarianism, as I understand it, says benefits to the worse off matter more, and is often glossed as summing a concave transformation of individual well-being levels rather than the levels themselves. Telic egalitarianism says inequality is bad in itself, even if nobody is treated unfairly and nobody gains from removing it. Then translate the leveling-down objection — that a view on which equality is intrinsically good must count it as an improvement, or at least not a worsening, when we level down by taking from the better off and giving nothing to anyone else — into my terms. I suspect the natural translation is that egalitarianism works like an agent who is risk-averse toward some giant social lottery, except that no person bears that risk: risk aversion in my world is always somebody's attitude toward their own uncertain outcomes, and the concavity of a social welfare function belongs to nobody. Tell me whether that is a fair translation or whether I am smuggling an assumption, as I would be if I called a market price a utility.

Second, use the translation to locate the dispute precisely. Concave order in my toolkit ranks distributions with equal means; leveling-down deliberately changes the mean, so the ordering goes silent exactly where the objection bites. Does that silence simply restate the objection, or does it show that the objection rests on something the ordering cannot express? And where does prioritarianism come out — is the concavity of the social welfare function supposed to be the impersonal analogue of my policyholders' risk aversion, or is it meant to be something else entirely? I have read that Parfit insisted the priority of the worse off is not the diminishing marginal utility of resources: even holding the size of the benefit fixed, it counts for more when it lands on someone with a lower lifetime level of well-being. In my vocabulary, concavity of a utility-of-wealth function does double duty — it generates both diminishing marginal utility and risk aversion — and I cannot separate them the way he wants to. So tell me whether the actuarial vocabulary can express a concavity that is about how much a benefit matters to someone without being anyone's attitude toward risk, and if not, whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim is less clear than it looks.

Please write continuous prose only, as in a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Begin with a one-paragraph verdict on whether the dispute survives the translation, and then develop the two parts in order.
