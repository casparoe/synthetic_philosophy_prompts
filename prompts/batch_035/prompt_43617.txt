quick context: i'm a novelist, about 40k words into a book about a retired type designer, Marta, who plays a decades-long game with her granddaughter. every year on the girl's birthday Marta hands over one hand-drawn glyph, and the girl — no context, no word around it, just the mark on the card — has to say what letter it is. the first one, from when the girl was six, was a perfectly ordinary lowercase 'a'. each year's version is a tiny nudge from the last: the terminal shortens, the bowl sinks, the spine leans. i want the final payoff scene to be the year the girl looks at it and says, honestly, "i don't know what that is." but i don't want this to be vibes — i want the philosophical engine under the book to be a real thought experiment, so here's what i need from you.

construct the scenario to spec, and then prove it works. the spec:

1. it has to be realistic. nothing a working type designer couldn't do with a pen or ordinary font software (they can literally interpolate between two glyphs, so use that if you want). no brain scans, no weird sci-fi.

2. the case has to make two accounts of letter identity give opposite verdicts on the same glyph. account one, the reader's account: a mark is an 'a' iff fluent readers of the latin script read it as one without hesitation in ordinary conditions. account two, the lineage account: a mark is an 'a' iff it descends from paradigm 'a's thru a chain of individually unremarkable design moves, the kind of moves a working designer would sign off on. in my book i want a single glyph that is an 'a' on one account and not on the other — you pick which way, but tell me why that direction makes the better novel, too.

3. the verdicts have to be generated, not stipulated. don't just assert that readers stop recognizing it — give the mechanism. (reading is fast and greedy and leans hard on context, so think about what happens when you strip the context away. and think about the fact that readers happily read a blackletter 'a' and a geometric sans 'a' as the same letter even tho those two glyphs share almost no geometry with each other — the case has to keep style and identity from doing each others jobs, or a critic of the experiment will say it collapsed into a case about style.)

4. after the scenario itself — keep it to one paragraph, and keep it something i could actually adapt into a scene — walk me thru both accounts and what each says about the last glyph, tell me which single feature of the setup is doing all the work, and what happens to the verdict if i remove that feature. then give me the counterattack: if the lineage theorist loses, what's their best comeback (maybe something about how the double-story 'a' and the single-story 'a' already force our hand), and if the recognition theorist loses, what's theirs. i need to be able to put both comebacks in the granddaughter's mouth eventually, so make them good ones.

one more thing, since you clearly know the old cognitive-science chatter about how every 'a' shares some abstract letter-spirit that no single drawn 'a' exhausts: tell me at the end whether your thought experiment supports that picture or whether it makes trouble for it. my editor keeps asking me what the book is "about" and i want to be able to answer her.
