So I'm a dev, no philosophy training, but I got into a genuinely heated slack argument about sunk costs last week and it made me realize my own gut verdicts are all over the place. The textbook line (which a couple people in the thread kept repeating) is that sunk costs should never matter, only future costs and benefits, full stop. I mostly buy that. But when I list out actual cases, my verdicts don't line up, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures them or whether I'm just rationalizing my own bad habits.

My cases and verdicts:

1. My team spent 6 months building a feature nobody asked for and the roadmap has moved on. PM wants to ship it anyway because "we're too deep in to waste the 6 months." Verdict: obviously the sunk cost fallacy, kill it.

2. Old legacy codebase, ugly, everyone complains, no tests. Someone proposes a full rewrite from scratch because the current code is garbage. I think continuing to patch the ugly thing is correct, and honestly part of my reason is "there's 10 years of accumulated bugfixes baked into that mess, a rewrite throws all that away." Verdict: this one feels like it's NOT a fallacy, even though it sounds structurally like case 1.

3. Two hours into a three hour movie I'm hating. My buddy says I paid for the ticket so I might as well stay. Verdict: fallacy, walk out, the money's gone either way.

4. My sister is 3 years into a PhD, kind of miserable, job prospects meh, and one of her stated reasons for staying is that leaving now would make the 3 years "for nothing." Verdict: I actually think this is pretty reasonable? I can't fully condemn it and it bugs me.

5. Friend in a ten year relationship that's gone flat — no toxicity, just coasting — and when asked why he stays, "a decade in" is genuinely one of his reasons. Verdict: I wince, but again I can't bring myself to call it flat-out irrational, even though it's the same shape as case 4.

6. A company keeps funding a dying product line because "we've already sunk $40M into it." Verdict: textbook fallacy, textbook.

7. I keep going to a kickboxing gym I've paid a year membership for, even in weeks I don't enjoy it, partly because the year is paid for. Verdict: fine, no objection. (Is this even sunk cost reasoning or is that just... economics?)

My tentative attempt at a principle: sunk costs matter when they've left behind something durable that still does work — the bugfixed code, the shared history, the two-thirds-finished degree — and don't matter when they're just gone, like money paid or hours already endured. This saves 2, and maybe 4 and 5, and it still condemns 1 and 6. But then case 3 vs case 4 bothers me, because they're both "endure misery to reach a completion," and I say walk out of the movie and stay in the PhD. Is the difference that the PhD leaves a credential and the movie leaves nothing? That feels like a different principle than the durable-residue one and I'm not sure they can both be true.

So: is there one principle (or maybe a short list of them) that captures most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts would you tell me to just give up on — I suspect 5 is the one holding me hostage? Please state the principle precisely enough to make predictions, tell me what it says about a couple of cases I haven't listed (e.g. half-built house I no longer love, or finishing a 900-page novel I'm 700 pages into and hating), and be honest about which of my gut calls your principle has to overturn rather than accommodate. Also happy to be told the textbook is just right and all my lenient verdicts are motivated reasoning — my coworker Dave certainly thinks so.
