My political economy reading group has spent the better part of a year on the socialist calculation debate, and we've reached the stage where the two most committed members -- one broadly in the Mises-Hayek tradition, one defending something close to Oskar Lange's market-socialist answer -- are no longer convincing each other but merely refining their objections. Before we dissolve into reminiscence about Soviet shortages, I want us to try a structured close. The two of them have agreed, in principle, to write the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between their positions would produce, and I've been asked to brief them on what that document should contain.

Please draft that joint statement. It should be something both a dedicated Austrian-school economist and a dedicated Langean could sign without wincing at any line. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered section of claims both accept -- about the theoretical problem itself (what exactly is being calculated, what prices do, what equilibrium means or fails to mean in this context), about what the historical record of centrally planned economies does and does not show, and about which of the classic objections have been answered and which haven't. Second, a disagreements table stating precisely where they part ways, with each disagreement phrased in wording that the side holding that view would endorse as fair -- I'm particularly interested in whether their disagreement over whether computation can substitute for the price mechanism is empirical (a question about information processing and incentives) or conceptual (a question about what kind of knowledge tacit, dispersed, revealed-only-through-choice knowledge is and whether it can be collected at all). Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, and for each crux, some concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it -- for instance, how a firm's internal planning works, or what the behavior of large corporations or modern algorithmic matching systems suggests about the feasibility of non-market coordination -- with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you find that some portion of their dispute is merely verbal -- e.g. turns on different definitions of "socialism" or of "rational economic calculation" -- please identify which part and why.

One caution: don't let the agreed section collapse into platitudes both could sign but neither cares about, and don't let the disagreements table smuggle in verdicts; the point of the document is that each side would accept the description of the *other* side's position as accurate, not as refuted. End the document with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in at most ten words, the single crux the parties jointly identify as the one whose resolution would most likely change at least one of their minds.
