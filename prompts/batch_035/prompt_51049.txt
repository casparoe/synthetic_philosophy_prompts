Write a satirical staff handbook, roughly 600 words, for the Office of Commemorative Consistency in a small fictional republic whose constitution provides that where the nation's collective memory conflicts with the documentary record, memory governs "all matters of ceremony, schooling, and national self-regard," while the archives govern everything else. Format it as numbered sections in a deadpan bureaucratic voice, with an appendix of forms.

The jokes should come from the real dynamics of collective memory rather than from invented ones: that memory is selective and present-serving, smoothing events into usable narratives; that it is transmitted through anniversaries, monuments, and school lessons rather than documents; that it is plural, so different communities remember the same event differently; and that revising a settled memory feels like an affront even when the records are unambiguous. Build the Office's procedures around these facts—for example, a protocol for when two districts commemorate incompatible versions of the same battle, a form an archivist must file before noting that a cherished date cannot be right, a rule about how often the founding story may be re-rendered, and a clause on what happens when the records are simply embarrassing. The satire should stay deadpan and legible without winking: a reader who knows the literature on memory versus history should find it fair, and a reader who doesn't should still follow it. Do not resolve, inside the handbook, the question of whose memory counts when the nation does not agree with itself. Write the piece in American English.
