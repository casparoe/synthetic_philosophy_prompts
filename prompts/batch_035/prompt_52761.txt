Write a sestina in the voice of the night porter of Hilbert's Grand Hotel, set on the night a coach carrying countably many new guests pulls up to an already full hotel. The management's solution — move every guest from room n to room 2n and lodge the newcomers in the odd rooms — is the porter's problem: he is the one with the cart of keys. Make the sestina's machinery do the mathematical work. The six end-words are the hotel's fixed stock (rooms, keys, the ledger, the stair, the guest, the night), and the poem, like the hotel, admits no new end-word after the first stanza: every later stanza is only a permutation of the original occupancy, which is the whole point — the trick is a reassignment, not the creation of room. The envoi, where the six end-words are packed two to a line, should be the moment the last straggler reaches the desk and is housed without a single new word being spent. The porter should be capable and weary rather than awed, and the final line should let the finitist's worry stand on its own: he has performed the shuffle every night for as long as there have been nights and never once seen it finished, so the vacancy lives in the ledger, not in the hallway. Someone who knows the debates over actual infinities should find the poem technically honest; someone who doesn't should still find a good poem about a man, a cart, and an endless corridor.
