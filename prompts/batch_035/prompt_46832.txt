I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, currently serving as a teaching assistant on an upper-level course called Virtue, Character, and the Situation, mostly taken by third- and fourth-year philosophers with a few psychology majors. The course lead has delegated the section of the final exam on the situationist challenge to the TAs, and I've been asked to draft one essay question for it. The exam is sat in ninety minutes; this question carries twenty marks, and candidates should be able to answer it in about 800 words. Our students have, by this point, all memorised the standard dialectic: the Milgram set-up, the seminary-student study in which a manipulation of how much of a hurry the subjects were in — rather than the topic they had been assigned to speak on — predicted whether they stopped for a man slumped in a doorway, the inference from such results to the scarcity of robust, cross-situational traits, and then the stock replies: the appeal to a distinction between virtues and mere personality traits, the concession that full virtue is rare (which, as the sharper students sometimes note, Aristotle seems never to have denied), and the localist retreat to traits that are stable only within types of situation.

The trouble is that a candidate can reproduce all of that and still have no view on the actual evidential relation between the experiments and the theory. What I want the question to force is the following thought, which almost none of them can yet articulate: the situationist experiments were designed to test broad traits in single-shot, between-subjects designs, on unselected subjects, in situations the subjects had no reason to regard as tests of anything; so it is unclear whether such experiments could ever discriminate between 'few people possess robust traits' and 'this design could not detect robust traits even if most people possessed them'. So the question I want should require the candidate to say what an observation internal to one of those experiments — some pattern in the data, some feature of the design — would have had to look like in order to count in virtue ethics' favour, and then to say whether the experiments as run were capable of producing that pattern at all, and if not, what evidence would have been capable of discriminating between the two hypotheses. It should be unanswerable by adjudicating the debate through the rehearsed replies: reciting the catalogue of studies and the catalogue of responses should earn very little, and the marks should go to the candidate who can state the epistemology of the dispute.

Please produce three things, all in British English. First, the essay question itself, phrased so that the crux is the evidential one and not merely 'who wins the debate'; you may seed it with a one-sentence sketch of one experiment, but the question must not depend on citing any specific study, author, or work, and no bibliography is expected of candidates. Second, a model answer of roughly 750 words that takes a definite position rather than surveying options — ideally one that distinguishes which version of virtue ethics the experiments genuinely threaten from which they leave untouched, and that says what discriminating evidence would look like. Third, a marking scheme of twenty marks built from five must-hit points of four marks each, with, for each point, a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it, and a closing sentence that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific move that marks the difference — the kind of thing that shows a candidate has understood the epistemology rather than memorised the positions.
