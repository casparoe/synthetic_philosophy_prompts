I teach a mid-sized introductory epistemology course (about 30 students, one 75-minute session) and I've decided I'm done explaining the lottery and preface paradoxes on the board. Design an in-class activity in which the students generate both paradoxes themselves before anyone names them. I'm imagining something with two phases: one in which each student individually holds a "ticket" and must decide whether to publicly commit to the claim that their ticket will lose, and one in which the class's individual commitments get pooled into a single collective artifact — a mock book of assertions, a group report, a joint statement — that they then have to sign or disown as a body. I'd like real, structured stakes rather than hypotheticals: some payoff scheme (points, tokens, a class-wide bonus that pays out or doesn't) that makes the individual commitment decisions genuinely costly to refuse and genuinely uncomfortable to make. The students should end up holding, in their own hands, the joint conclusion that they can see is a contradiction — and only then should the paradox be named.

A few constraints. No lecture up front, and the names "lottery paradox," "preface paradox," and the probabilistic acceptance rule literature should stay out of the room until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the formal problem: the individually high probability of each claim, the closure or conjunction step that turns many acceptable assertions into an unacceptable one, and the difference in structure between the lottery case (where the inconsistency comes from the known odds) and the preface case (where it comes from the author's own self-assessment). I also want the debrief to give the students a live choice among the standard escapes — rejecting the acceptance rule, accepting contradictions, distinguishing belief from assertion — and to make them feel the price of each, not just hear it catalogued.

Finally, be candid about the design itself: tell me where it is most likely to fail on a first run (students treating the tickets as a joke, the pooling phase dissolving into negotiation, the shy ones free-riding on the bold ones' commitments) and what I should do when that happens. And tell me what would lead you to abandon this design in favor of a different one — which specific failure mode, if it showed up, would tell you the activity can't carry the weight and the paradox has to be staged some other way, and what the fallback design would be. I'd rather redesign now than discover mid-semester that the game only produces the paradox because I nudged it there, since then the students learn that much less.
