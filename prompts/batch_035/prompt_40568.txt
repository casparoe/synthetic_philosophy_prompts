I teach a graduate seminar on explanation in the social sciences, and a colleague in sociology has sent round an argument against what she calls "values as causes." Its target is Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and I cannot decide whether it is a serious objection or a cheap one dressed up in serious clothes. Here it is, as she states it.

(1) If an explanation is to be more than a redescription, it must be possible to establish that the cause was present without presupposing that the effect occurred; otherwise the explanation is untestable and empty.
(2) In Weber's thesis, the cause is a psychological condition: the anxiety over election, the sanctification of everyday labor as a calling, the hostility to idle enjoyment and to wealth that is merely enjoyed rather than used — the disposition he is famous for calling innerworldly asceticism — prevailing among ordinary Calvinist believers.
(3) Weber's evidence that this condition was prevalent among the population, as opposed to merely preached at it, consists of sermons, catechisms, and manuals of practical divinity — texts establishing what believers were told — together with the occupational and confessional statistics with which the essay opens, which show at most that Protestants were concentrated in business and skilled trades, not why.
(4) So the attribution of the ethic to the population rests on the very conduct it is supposed to explain: we count the ethic as widespread because methodical, ascetic, profit-oriented conduct was widespread, and then explain the conduct by the ethic.
(5) The explanation therefore collapses into: these people behaved as they did because they had the dispositions that produced that behavior.
(6) And nothing in the reasoning is special to Weber: every explanation that invokes values or culture as a cause infers the values from the conduct they are invoked to explain, so every such explanation is untestable and empty.

She concludes that Weber's thesis, and cultural explanation in general, is a redescription of what it claims to explain. My colleague is content with this; I am not, but I also cannot locate the defective step. I will mention two features of the argument that I think any adequate answer must take seriously.

First, if the argument is sound, it does not stop at culture. When I explain a student's arriving at my seminar out of breath by her desire not to be late, my evidence for the desire is, in the main, the running itself — the very behavior the desire is cited to explain. My colleague replies that the individual case is different, because there the inference concerns a particular subject who can be interrogated, observed in other circumstances, and so on. I find that boundary unstable, but I cannot say precisely where it breaks, or whether it breaks in a way that also breaks her step (6).

Second, I would like to keep (1) in some form: I do believe that explanations which cannot be checked against anything are empty. But I suspect the requirement I actually want is weaker than the one my colleague needs for (5) and (6) to go through, and I would like to see exactly where the extra strength enters the argument.

Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked acceptable when it was introduced, and what rejecting it costs — including what my colleague's position and mine must each give up.
