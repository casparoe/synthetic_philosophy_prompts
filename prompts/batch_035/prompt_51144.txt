My roommate and I have gotten semi-obsessed with Lewis Carroll's tortoise argument, the one where you try to get a stubborn tortoise to accept a conclusion by writing down every rule as an extra premise, and it just asks why the new premise applies, forever. Neither of us studies philosophy or anything, I just like arguing with her. Over the past few weeks we've gone through a bunch of scenarios and I finally have her positions written down in my notes app, but there's a new case coming up and I honestly can't predict what she'll say, even though I've read her verdicts like ten times now.

Her rulings so far: (1) From "it's raining" and "if it's raining, the street is wet," you can conclude "the street is wet," and she says you don't need to say the rule out loud — the step is just there, done. (2) But she insists there IS something you're doing when you make the step, some actual rule-following, and that's what the tortoise story shows: you can't convert rule-following into one more line on the page. (3) A guy who accepts both premises but just refuses the conclusion, without giving any reason, is irrational — not merely disagreeable, but actually making a mistake, she's firm on that. (4) A chess engine computing a move: she says it doesn't "follow the rules" of chess the way a person does, because it doesn't grasp anything, it just computes — but the move is still legal, obviously. (5) A parrot trained to squawk the conclusion whenever it hears premises of that shape: she was genuinely torn on whether the parrot is *doing* the inference, but said the conclusion is still true regardless of the parrot.

The new case that's splitting us: imagine a proof assistant that mechanically verifies every step of some long mathematical proof against a set of formal rules, and outputs "verified" — but nobody involved can say what makes those rules truth-preserving. (Say the proof and the rule-set were just found on an old hard drive, in some notation nobody fully understands except how to mechanically apply it.) Does the proof establish its conclusion? She hasn't ruled yet. I'm less interested in the answer itself than in the rule she's implicitly running underneath verdicts 1–5. As far as I can tell there are at least two candidates that both fit her record: one where what matters is the actual premise-to-conclusion pattern (truth carries through regardless of anyone grasping anything, so the proof works), and one where some kind of grasp or endorsement of the rules is doing the real work (so it doesn't). What does each rule predict about the hard drive proof, which one is the better bet given her verdicts so far, and what scenario should I spring on her next to actually tell the two rules apart?
