I'm in a philosophy reading group that has spent several sessions on the debate between deflationary and substantive theories of truth, and at the last meeting two members — call them Dana and Miguel — got into a dispute I was not able to referee. Both are competent amateurs who have read enough of the literature to make genuinely decent moves, and neither is obviously strawmanning the other. I'll reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can, numbered by move; please take the positions they stake out as they are stated, not as the best possible version anyone has ever defended.

The common ground they accepted at the outset: the deflationist holds that there is nothing more to truth than is given by the instances of the equivalence schema — the proposition that snow is white is true if and only if snow is white, and likewise for every other proposition; truth, so conceived, is not a deep property whose hidden nature it is the task of a philosophical theory to uncover.

(1) Dana opened by arguing that deflationism is unstable as a philosophy of logic. If all there is to truth is the equivalence schema, then the concept cannot do the work logic assigns it: validity is standardly characterized as necessary truth-preservation, and the step from a valid argument plus true premises to a true conclusion runs through truth as a genuine property. A concept that is exhausted by its schema cannot bear that weight, so deflationism saws off the branch that logic itself sits on.

(2) Miguel replied that Dana had mislocated the function of the truth predicate. On the deflationary view, its whole utility is as a device of generalization: it lets us say with one finite sentence that everything the witness testified is true, something we could not say by enumerating her assertions one by one. Expressive indispensability of that kind is entirely compatible with explanatory emptiness; it is only against the inflated picture that a concept must earn its keep by revealing a property.

(3) Dana pressed on the normative side. Belief is standardly said to aim at truth: one ought to believe that p only if it is true that p. But if truth is not so much as a property, then belief aims at nothing, and the normative characterization of the mental collapses. The deflationist owes us either a retraction of the norm of truth or a reconstruction of it that does not quietly reintroduce the property she started by denying.

(4) Miguel said the reconstruction is straightforward: state the norm schematically — for each proposition p, do not believe that p unless p. Each instance is fully intelligible without the truth predicate; only the general statement over all propositions needs it, and that is exactly the generalizing role the deflationist has already assigned to it. No property is invoked, and nothing normative is lost.

(5) Dana then shifted to the Liar. The deflationist claims the equivalence schema holds without exception. But take the liar proposition — the one that says of itself that it is not true. Its equivalence instance states that that proposition is true if and only if it is not true, which is a contradiction. So either the schema is not universal, in which case there is more to truth than the deflationist admits, or it is universal and inconsistent. Either way, deflationism presented as a complete theory is refuted by a two-line argument.

(6) Miguel's penultimate move invoked Tarski: the undefinability theorem shows that truth for a sufficiently rich language cannot be defined within that language, which is precisely what one should expect if truth is a logico-linguistic device rather than a substantive property; meanwhile the inflationist owes a definition, and Tarski showed none is available. As for the Liar, it is a problem for every theory of truth, so it counts against none of them.

(7) Dana closed by denying the symmetry claim. Tarski defined truth perfectly well for formalized languages, from a metalanguage; that we can always do this, but only by climbing to a richer language, is evidence that truth is a genuine property of sentences that outruns any fixed schema — the hierarchy is a discovery about truth, not a limitation of our vocabulary, and Miguel cannot both lean on Tarski and refuse the lesson.

Both left convinced they had won, and the rest of us could not honestly say who had. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of moves (1) through (7), state who is right, to what degree, and why, flagging any move that is right in what it asserts but wrong in what it denies (or vice versa). Then give an overall verdict on who fared better. Finally, identify anything of substance that both of them missed — in particular, whether their disagreement over the Liar and over Tarski really turns on the deflationism question at all, or whether some of those moves could have been adjudicated on ground both of them already share.
