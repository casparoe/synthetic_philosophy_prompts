a friend of a friend who did his phd on kripke walked me into this at a party and i've been chewing on it for a week. the setup is just the standard necessary a posteriori stuff, so nothing exotic, but the argument he built out of it seems to prove way too much. here it is as he gave it to me:

(1) 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are rigid designators: in every possible situation where they designate anything at all, they designate the same planet, Venus. (2) So 'Hesperus is Phosphorus', if true, is true in exactly the situations where 'Hesperus is Hesperus' is true — namely all of them. That's just what rigidity plus co-reference buys you. (3) 'Hesperus is Hesperus' is knowable a priori by anyone who understands it; no observation needed, you can see it's true from its form alone. (4) A sentence's truth conditions are what determine what it takes to know it — knowing that p is just being in the right kind of state with respect to the conditions under which p. (5) 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'Hesperus is Hesperus' have the same truth conditions, by (2). (6) So 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is knowable a priori by anyone who understands it, same as (3). (7) But the astronomers who first established that the evening star and the morning star are one and the same understood exactly what they were asking, and they needed decades of careful observation to settle it. (8) So either they somehow had a priori knowledge all along and the observations were theater, or something above has gone wrong.

and here's the part that makes me think it really does prove too much: run the same argument on 'heat is the motion of molecules' or 'water is H2O' and you get that chemistry and thermodynamics were, in principle, knowable a priori by anyone who understood the terms. which is insane — the whole point of the necessary a posteriori was that science discovers necessities, and this argument seems to show that once something is necessary, nothing empirical was ever needed to know it. necessity eats the a posteriori.

i've stared at (4) and (5) the most because they're doing the transfer work, but i can't actually see whats wrong with either of them — if two sentences are true in all the same situations, in what sense could knowledge of one be a different kind of thing than knowledge of the other? if i have to pick something to give up, i'd honestly rather keep (7) (the babylonians really did learn something from looking at the sky) and keep the necessity from (2), so i guess i'm hoping (4) is where the rot is, but when i try to say out loud why (4) is false it keeps sounding like im denying that knowledge is about truth conditions, which feels like denying everything.

which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? and is the answer the same for the Hesperus case and the water case, or does something extra show up when both sides of the identity are natural-kind terms instead of proper names? also he swore there's a version of this that bothered kripke himself, so if you know which bit of the machinery was supposed to handle it, i'd like to hear it.
