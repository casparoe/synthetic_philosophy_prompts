A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Kelly betting, and he thinks he won while I think his argument proves far too much. The occasion was a coin flip: a genuine, one-time offer to bet at odds of 1.2 to 1, where staking a fraction f of one's bankroll multiplies it by (1 + 1.2f) on heads and (1 − f) on tails. He worked out that the Kelly fraction here is a twelfth of the bankroll, that staking exactly double that leaves the long-run growth rate equal to that of not betting at all, and that anything beyond it drives the growth rate negative. From this he derived, in numbered steps, a conclusion I cannot refute but refuse to accept: (1) rational staking is governed by the long-run growth rate of the bankroll, because wealth compounds multiplicatively and a bettor faces not one wager but a long, open-ended series of them; (2) the Kelly fraction uniquely maximizes that growth rate; (3) any policy that systematically stakes above Kelly is beaten almost surely in the limit, since the over-staker ends below the Kelly bettor with probability one, and staking beyond twice Kelly makes the growth rate negative, which is as good as a guarantee of ruin; (4) real lives are just such open-ended series — every investment, career move, and insurance premium is another wager on a bankroll that will face further wagers — so no one ever makes a truly last bet, and the long run always arrives; (5) therefore staking more than Kelly on any real monetary decision is irrational, namely choosing a strategy that does worse almost surely; (6) and since the justification runs through membership in the series rather than through any wager's own repetitions, the cap binds each decision taken singly and cannot be lifted for a wager that happens to be someone's last. His conclusion: whoever stakes above Kelly in any real decision is irrational.

Here is what I cannot swallow. A relative of mine, an elderly man with no heirs of his own, is dying. He has liquid savings of $120,000, no bets ahead of him, and a single all-or-nothing aim: funding a treatment for his nephew that costs $200,000, which he cares about in a threshold way — reaching it is everything, falling short by a dollar is worth no more than falling short by a hundred thousand. The flip described above is genuinely on offer to him once, after which his affairs are wound up. For him, staking less than about 56% of his savings guarantees the treatment is never funded, since only a win can carry him over the threshold; his sensible stake is just above 56%, the least that clears it on heads — far beyond twice Kelly, in the very region my friend's argument describes as guaranteed ruin. It seems to me he would be rational to take it, and the argument plainly means to forbid this.

So tell me where the argument goes wrong: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I can see that my friend has a retreat available — dropping the repetition route and re-deriving the cap from Bernoulli's old idea that utility in wealth is logarithmic, which caps even a single wager at the same fraction with no appeal to a series — and I want to know whether that retreat rescues his conclusion about my relative, or whether it breaks precisely on the threshold. For my own part, I would like to keep step (5) for my own investing, where I really do face an open-ended series, while denying the final conclusion for my relative's last wager; but I want to know whether that line is principled or merely a convenient place to stop, and what exactly I am conceding if I admit that the rational stake depends on the bettor's utility and horizon rather than on the odds alone.
