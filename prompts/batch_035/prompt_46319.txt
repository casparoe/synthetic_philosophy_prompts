Every argument I get into about ethics eventually hits the same wall: someone tells me my view is "just bias" — you only think inheritance is fine because you'll inherit, you only think that policy's wrong because it'd cost you money, whatever — and my only comeback is "no, I sincerely believe it," which persuades nobody. And the annoying part is I don't have a principled way to sort moral bias from plain moral preference, and I'm fairly sure the people doing the accusing don't either. Pre-theoretically we run genealogies only on views we already dislike — nobody psychoanalyzes their own egalitarianism — so I can't just trust my intuitions here. So before I argue about this again I want the full spec.

Write me the requirements list: what any method of telling moral bias apart from moral preference would have to deliver. The cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it can't just assume. Some of what I think belongs on the list (add whatever I'm missing):

1. It has to nail the paradigm cases — the slaveholder's moral defense of slavery, the guy whose moral principles always, coincidentally, point at his own interest. But "the view serves the believer" can't be the test, because by that test every woman who supports women's rights is biased (women as a class gain from it), which seems flatly wrong. So it has to handle the case where sincere conviction and self-interest align — including through a whole movement — without writing the movement off as self-serving.

2. It can't smuggle in a first-order moral theory. If "biased" just means "believes the wrong things," the method is useless for actual disagreements; it just relabels them. But then I worry: without any grip on moral truth, what does the method even detect? One thing I've noticed is that explaining why someone holds a belief only debunks it if the explanation is insensitive to whether the belief is true (nobody debunks physicists by pointing out they have careers in physics). In morals there's no truth-check to fall back on — so are the no-smuggling requirement and the requirement that it deliver verdicts in live controversies even jointly satisfiable? That's the part I most want to see: if some subset of the requirements can't be met together, say so.

3. It can't flag every minority view as bias. Every moral advance started as a minority view held mostly by people it benefited or who identified with its holders. The method should be able to say "biased but correct," or at least shouldn't have to choose in advance between labeling dissent bias and giving up.

4. It can't collapse partiality into bias. I'm allowed to favor my own kids; a method that flags every self- or group-serving preference as bias proves too much — it would condemn loyalty, gratitude, patriotism, all of it. So where exactly is the line between legitimate partiality and corrupting bias, and can the method draw that line without a moral theory in tow?

5. It should keep apart at least three things that constantly get run together: process bias (the belief was formed badly — rationalized after the fact, absorbed from whoever raised you), standpoint (the belief was formed from a particular position — which, per standpoint theorists, sometimes *improves* moral knowledge, since the people subject to a practice see things others miss), and mere interest alignment (the view happens to serve you). Only the first looks clearly bad. Does the method need to detect these seperately? I think so, but I might be wrong.

Then a short scorecard against what's actually on offer: the follow-the-incentives heuristic (discount any moral view held by people it benefits), reflective equilibrium, the Rawls-style idea that we should rely only on moral judgments formed under conditions favorable to forming them accurately, ideal observer theory, and the flat genealogy move. And tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out candidates — my money's on no-smuggling, since almost every method on that list quietly settles the controversy first and then calls the loser biased. But tell me if im wrong. No table needed, bullet points are fine, and don't skimp on the motivating case behind each requirement — that's where I keep getting lost.
