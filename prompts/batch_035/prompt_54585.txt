I maintain a small open-source side project — a tool that helps individual donors compare charities by expected impact. The user inputs a budget and a few cause preferences, the tool pulls cost-effectiveness estimates, and it outputs a ranked list with confidence intervals. I'm a backend engineer by trade; my formal training is entirely in computer science and statistics, and my philosophy education consists of occasionally reading blog posts. Last month a user filed an issue that has been living rent-free in my head ever since. Their argument, roughly: if there's any non-negligible probability that the future contains infinitely many morally relevant beings (an infinite universe, infinite simulations, whatever), then the expected value of essentially every charitable action is infinite or undefined, and a tool whose entire job is to print out "A ranks above B" based on finite point estimates is philosophically fraudulent. A colleague who minored in philosophy mentioned in passing that there's a whole literature on how aggregative ethics breaks under infinities, and gestures at things like discounting, orderings that compare the largest terms first, and some way of regularizing infinite sums — but he couldn't explain what any of those moves actually commit you to, and I don't have the background to reconstruct it myself.

Please describe the most important implications of infinite ethics for a project like mine — a piece of software that must output a definite ranking from finite inputs. I'm not asking for a reading list or a literature survey; I want the consequences that actually bear on design decisions. For instance: does it matter whether my aggregation is a plain sum versus something that compares the dominant terms first? Should the tool refuse to rank two options whose expected values are both infinite, or flag such pairs rather than silently tie-breaking them? Is the pragmatic move of conditioning on a finite horizon (e.g., truncating at a thousand years) defensible, or does it quietly assume away exactly the cases that make infinity a problem in the first place? And, importantly, which of these implications are robust across most reasonable responses to infinitude versus which depend on picking one specific, contested resolution — because as an engineer I need to know what can be hard-coded as a default and what should be exposed as an option with a warning label. Assume I can't spend more than a weekend on the philosophy; I need the version that survives being implemented in a few hundred lines of Python.
