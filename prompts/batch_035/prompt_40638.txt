A while back I reread the Republic, Book II, the part where Glaucon basically dares Socrates to defend justice as worth having for its own sake, and tells the story of a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible and uses it to seduce the queen and take the throne. It got under my skin, and since then I've been trying to write down where I actually stand on the whole "why be moral?" thing. I've ended up with a list I half believe and half suspect is incoherrent, but I genuinely can't see where the problem is. Here's the list:

1. Morality is a cooperative scheme for mutual advantage. That's the only grounding I accept — no free-floating moral facts, no divine commands. The rules that bind us are the ones everyone does better under if everyone follows them.
2. If someone asks why be moral, the only answer I take seriously is the prudential one: cooperation pays, defection eventually gets punished, reputation is worth something. "Because it's just wrong" doesn't answer anything as far as I'm concerned.
3. And yet: if a person really had a working ring — genuinely undetectable, zero chance of ever being caught by anyone, ever — I still think they shouldn't use it. Not "it would be nice if they didn't," but shouldn't. If they walked into a vault with it, I'd want to say they did something they had decisive reason not to do.
4. I want to say the ring-user isn't just blameworthy but irrational — the same category of error as believing both p and not-p. That matters to me. The ring should come out as a mistake, not merely a vice.
5. I refuse to ground any of this in guilt or shame. To me those are consequences, just internalized ones. Fear of your own conscience is still fear, and fear of consequences is exactly what the ring deletes.
6. I also don't buy bootstrapping. I can't accept "you should keep the deal because you made the deal," or "the practice of promising is what makes promise-keeping required." A practice can't generate it's own authority — that authority has to come from somewhere outside the practice.
7. Last one, and this one has never bothered me even though it maybe should: I'm fine with morality binding me on particular occasions against my interests. If I promised, I should keep the promise even on a day when keeping it costs me more than anyone gains from it.

Go through the list and tell me which of these are in real tension, which only look like they are, and how bad each real one is. For each real one, say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix would cost me elsewhere on the list. Then tell me whether there's one single revision that would clean up the most, and be specific about which number has to go or get weakened. I don't want a lecture on the history of the question or a reading list — I know the usual names — I want an audit of my seven items. Please write it in American English ("behavior," "favor," etc.), it's going into a doc I'm sharing with some Americans who will absolutely complain otherwise.
