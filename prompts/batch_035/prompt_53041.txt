I sit on the animal welfare oversight panel of a marine research institute that houses Octopus vulgaris and cuttlefish for cognition research. Our welfare policy was drafted with vertebrates in mind and is silent about cephalopods, and we now face a concrete case that forces the issue: a proposed study requires chronically implanting recording electrodes through the mantle, involving repeated handling, incisions, and post-operative monitoring. Whether the protocol goes forward, and under what anesthesia and analgesia requirements, turns on a prior question the panel cannot punt on: what evidence would count as establishing that these animals feel pain, rather than merely detecting and responding to noxious stimuli? The proposal is on the agenda for our Thursday meeting, and our director has asked the panel for a written standard she can apply to this and future protocols, not a one-off ruling.

We split down the middle last week, and the split is philosophical rather than factual. One faction, drawing on the spirit of Turing's 1950 move of replacing an unanswerable inner question with a behavioral test, argues that felt pain should be inferred from flexible, cost-sensitive behavior: an animal that gives up a valuable resource to avoid a noxious stimulus, tends an injured area, or self-administers analgesia when given the option is behaving the way a suffering creature behaves — and, they add, this is essentially the kind of evidence we rely on even for fellow humans, and largely for lab mice as well. The other faction counters that behavior is underdetermined: a nociceptive reflex arc with no accompanying experience could produce the same response curves, so behavioral convergence should trigger, at most, further physiological study (nociceptor mapping, neural correlates), not the protections themselves. They also warn that tests calibrated to what human pain expression looks like invite false negatives in animals whose behavior is alien to us — cuttlefish passing their skin through color changes rather than grimacing.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete evidential standard, with criteria specific enough that a reviewer could actually score a protocol against them, and specify what the standard requires for the implantation study in particular — including a recommendation on anesthesia, analgesia, and any conditions short of rejection. It must also engage honestly with the strongest point on the losing side, whichever side loses. Have the memo lead with its recommendation in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and no restating of the panel's question back to us.
