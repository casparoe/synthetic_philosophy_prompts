For most of my working life I was a quantitative geneticist in agricultural research, where heritability was a daily working tool rather than an object of contemplation. Since retiring I have taken up the philosophy of science, and this term I have agreed to serve as an external grader for an introductory philosophy of science course at a nearby university. The course grades on the letter scale A–F with pluses and minuses. I would appreciate your assistance in evaluating one student's examination answer.

The examination question read as follows:

"In quantitative genetics, the term 'heritability' has a technical meaning. Define it, and explain why it is said to be a property of a population in a given range of environments, rather than a property of a trait, still less of an individual. Human stature is often estimated to have a heritability of about 0.8 in contemporary developed countries. In light of your definition, discuss what such a figure does and does not support: in particular, whether it constrains what environmental intervention could achieve, and whether it licenses any inference about the causes of differences between groups."

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Heritability, as the term is used in quantitative genetics, is the proportion of the phenotypic variance in a trait, in a particular population, that is attributable to genetic differences among the members of that population. Broad-sense heritability includes all genetic variance — additive, dominance, and epistatic; narrow-sense heritability includes only the additive portion, which is the part that predicts response to selection. Because it is a ratio of variances, heritability lies between zero and one and is a property of a population in a given range of environments, not a property of the trait in itself: the same trait may have different heritabilities in different populations, or in the same population under different conditions. Nor is it a statement about individuals: to say that height has a heritability of 0.8 is not to say that 80 per cent of any particular person's height is caused by their genes. There is no fact of the matter as to how much of one person's height is genetic and how much is environmental; heritability partitions variance, and variance is a property of populations, not of persons.

The example of human stature shows why high heritability does not mean immutability. Stature is highly heritable in contemporary developed countries, yet average adult height in many of those countries rose by several centimetres over the twentieth century — a change far too rapid to be genetic, and generally attributed to nutrition and public health. A trait can therefore be highly heritable at a time while its population mean shifts substantially in response to environmental change. High heritability tells us how much of the presently observed variation is associated with genetic differences; it does not tell us how malleable the population mean is.

Indeed, if environmental differences within a population were eliminated altogether, environmental variance would fall to zero and heritability would approach one. In a perfectly uniform environment, the trait would be, in effect, genetically determined, since whatever differences remained would have to be traced to genetic differences.

As for differences between groups: heritability within a group plainly settles nothing by itself about the causes of differences between groups. But if a trait has been found highly heritable in every population in which it has been studied, then genes must be regarded as the leading candidate for explaining differences between those populations as well; for if environmental differences between the groups were doing the work, we would expect heritability within the groups to be depressed. Within-group heritability, while not decisive for between-group questions, thus gives those questions a definite nudge in the direction of genetic explanation."
