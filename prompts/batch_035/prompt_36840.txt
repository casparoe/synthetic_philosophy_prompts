I am a professor preparing a position statement on moral responsibility for a collected volume, and before I submit the manuscript I would like a rigorous audit of whether the package I have arrived at actually hangs together. After some years of teaching and writing on the free will problem, my view has settled into the following five commitments:

(1) I accept Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: nothing can be causa sui in the required sense; to be truly morally responsible for what one does, one would have to be responsible, through some prior choice, for the way one is — one's character, motives, and habits of deliberation — and any such prior choice would itself have issued from a way one is that one did not choose; regress; therefore no one is morally responsible in the strong, desert-entailing sense for anything they do.

(2) I hold that there are nevertheless real differences in degree of responsibility among agents in ordinary contexts: the person who acts after sustained reflection is more responsible for what she does than the person who acts on impulse or under compulsion, even though neither is responsible in the ultimate sense that (1) shows to be impossible.

(3) I hold that reactive attitudes can be fitting in a robust sense: resentment at betrayal and gratitude for kindness can get things right about their objects, in something like the way perceptual beliefs can, and are not merely useful policies we adopt for regulating behavior.

(4) I hold that the everyday practices of praise and blame remain justified, but only instrumentally — as tools of moral influence, character formation, and coordination — never as the giving of what is deserved.

(5) I hold that some people are, as an objective matter, better or worse than others — more just, more cruel, more generous — and that this is a fact about how they are, not about anything they were responsible for bringing about.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what the least costly repair would be in each case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating all of them as equally serious, indicate for each what would have to give, and identify the single revision that would restore the most coherence overall. I confess I cannot myself see where the real pressure lies — my students give me different answers every year — so please do not assume I have a preferred sacrifice in mind.
