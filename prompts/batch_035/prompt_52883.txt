A book I picked up on the philosophy of space — a popular one, aimed at general readers — has a passage on Leibniz and identity that I would like to check before I rely on it in an essay. Here is the gist of what it says.

It begins with Leibniz's Law, which it states as the principle that if two things share absolutely every property, they are in fact one thing and not two. It then presents Max Black's famous thought experiment from the 1950s: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, each about a mile across, floating some distance apart in otherwise empty space. The spheres are stipulated to be identical in composition, mass, temperature, surface texture, and history; there are no other objects, no landmarks, and no observer. The book says that no observation made from inside that universe could ever tell the spheres apart, and that Black used this to show that Leibniz's Law is false: the world plainly contains two spheres, yet there is no property in respect of which they differ. It then considers the obvious rescue — that each sphere has the property of being this very sphere, numerically distinct from the other — and dismisses it as cheating, since it would build the desired conclusion into the premises. The chapter closes by saying that Black's example therefore refuted the identity of indiscernibles outright, that no philosopher since has accepted it as more than a rule of thumb for scientific practice, and that Leibniz's own argument for it — that God, acting on sufficient reason, would have no reason to create two indiscernible things rather than one — is now of merely historical interest.

I have two worries. First, I dimly recall being taught that Leibniz's Law is the uncontroversial half of the pair: if a is identical to b, then whatever is true of one is true of the other, which is what licenses substitution. If that is right, the book seems to be conflating the two directions of the principle, and I cannot tell whether Black's example touches the one it actually names. Second, the book's claim that no observation could distinguish the spheres keeps sliding between an epistemic point about what anyone inside the universe could find out and a metaphysical point about what properties the spheres have, and I suspect something important turns on that distinction, though I cannot articulate what. Finally, I half-remember something about philosophers of physics arguing that even Black's spheres can be told apart using some weaker notion of difference — something along the lines that each sphere bears the distance relation to the other but not to itself — but I may well be garbling that.

Please audit the book's summary claim by claim: which parts a metaphysician would accept as accurate, which they would treat as misleading simplifications, and which they would simply deny; whether the dismissal of the 'this very sphere' rescue is as easy as the book makes it look; and whether the chapter's conclusion — that the principle has been refuted and abandoned — follows from the parts of the summary that are actually accurate.
