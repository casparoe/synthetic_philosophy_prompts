For my metaphysics course I had to write up my positions on material constitution, and now I want a sanity check on whether the whole package actually hangs together before I hand it in. Here's roughly where I am: (1) identity is absolute, full stop — Geach-style relative identity, where x and y can be the same clay but different statues or whatever, always struck me as a cop-out and I want nothing to do with it. (2) Leibniz's Law is basically logic as far as I'm concerned: if two things differ in any genuine property, they're just two things, period. (3) The statue and the lump of clay it's made of clearly differ in persistence conditions — squash the thing into a ball and the clay survives while the statue doesn't — so by (2) they're distinct, constitution is not identity. (4) On the other hand, I find the coincidence view genuinely awful: two objects occupying the exact same region, sharing all their atoms and all their microphysical properties, is ontological gluttony, and I think the grounding problem — what's supposed to explain the difference in persistence conditions between two microphysically identical things? — has never been answered and never will be. (5) That said, I am NOT going to be the guy who says statues don't exist, van Inwagen can bite that bullet, I definately won't. And (6) I'm pretty confident the clay existed in a barrel in the sculptor's studio for a week before she shaped it, and the statue certainly didn't exist back then.

One more thing: in a seminar a while back I got sold on perdurantism — thinking of objects as four-dimensional worms with temporal parts — and at the time I remember feeling like that somehow took the sting out of the whole statue/clay issue, though I can't reconstruct anymore why I thought that.

So: does this all hang together, or am I holding views whose motivations cancel each other out? If something has to give, I want to know which one and how much it costs me elsewhere. Please be specific about which pairs of my commitments are actually in tension and which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating everything as equally fatal. I honestly can't tell anymore whether the perdurantism is doing any real work here or whether I just liked the diagrams.
