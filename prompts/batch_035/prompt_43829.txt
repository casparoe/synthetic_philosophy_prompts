My department runs a small evening reading group on philosophy of mind, and last month's meeting dissolved into a two-hour argument between two of our members — call them Daniel and Marissa — about the Gödelian argument against mechanism, the one Lucas and Penrose made famous. I mostly stayed out of it, and at the end they asked me to referee by email. I'd rather send them a considered adjudication than my half-understood impressions. Here, as best I can reconstruct it, is the exchange, move by move.

(1) Daniel opened with the classic setup. Take any machine whose mathematical output is generated by some formal system strong enough for arithmetic. If that system is consistent, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem hands us a sentence — the one that says of itself that the system doesn't prove it — which the system cannot prove but which we, reasoning about the system, can see is true. So for any machine there is a mathematical truth we can grasp and it cannot; hence the mind is not a machine.

(2) Marissa replied that the argument only gets going if the machine's system is consistent, and more importantly that it only works if we can see it is consistent — an inconsistent system proves everything, its Gödel sentence included. And that seeing, she said, is exactly what is at issue: to conclude that the Gödel sentence is true but unprovable-for-the-machine, you must know the system is sound, and neither the machine nor, she claimed, the human demonstrably has that knowledge in the relevant case.

(3) Daniel said this concedes his point rather than resisting it. By the second incompleteness theorem the machine can never establish its own consistency, but he can establish his: he can reflect that he has never knowingly affirmed a contradiction, and that reflexive access to his own consistency is precisely the kind of self-knowledge no formal system can have.

(4) Marissa called that introspective overreach. Never having knowingly affirmed a contradiction is not the same as being consistent — humans harbor inconsistent belief sets all the time without noticing, and the history of mathematics is full of axiom systems that looked fine until they weren't. So Daniel's premise is an idealization quietly swapped in for a fact about an actual, fallible reasoner.

(5) Daniel's last move was the Penrose-style one: grant all the fallibility points and consider instead an idealized human mathematician, one who never errs and can survey any proposed machine. Either that idealized reasoner is a formal system, in which case it has a Gödel sentence it cannot prove but that we, reasoning about it from outside, can see is true; or it is not a formal system, in which case mechanism is false anyway. The dilemma survives the quibbles about human frailty.

(6) Marissa answered that the dilemma does not survive, because the outside perspective is doing all the work. When we reason about the idealized reasoner's Gödel sentence, we are entitled to the conclusion only if we know that reasoner's system is consistent; and if that system is at least as strong as ours, then by the second theorem we cannot establish its consistency except from a yet stronger system — at which point the question simply recurses about the stronger system, and about us. The argument, she concluded, needs a reasoner who is stipulated to be sound at every level, which means it establishes something about idealized reasoners nobody has ever met and nothing about human beings.

At that point the host's dog needed walking and the meeting broke up.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is basically right, basically wrong, or right about something narrower than it claims, and justify the rating. Then say who, if anyone, comes out ahead overall, and identify any point that neither of them raised that a careful philosopher would want put on the table.
