In a philosophy of mind seminar, my professor dismissed the Turing test using an analogy that traces back to Searle's own responses to critics of the Chinese Room: a computer simulation of a hurricane doesn't blow anyone's hat off, a simulation of digestion doesn't break down carbohydrates, so why would a simulation of understanding understand anything? On this picture, a program that passes the Turing test is like a flight simulator — it can imitate the outward behavior of a mind with arbitrary fidelity while lacking the inner causal reality, and no amount of conversational polish changes that. My professor leans on this to conclude that the Chinese Room shows the Turing test tests nothing: passing it establishes only that you are a good simulator, and being a good simulator is all the test could ever measure.

Can you audit the analogy? I'd like the correspondence table first — what in the Chinese Room setup is supposed to play the role of the simulation, what plays the role of the wind and rain that the simulation lacks, and what plays the role of the thing being simulated. Then the places the mapping breaks. In particular, I keep tripping over one counterexample: a simulation of a calculator actually calculates, and a simulation of addition actually adds. Does the simulation analogy simply presuppose that understanding is like weather rather than like multiplication — a causal-physical phenomenon rather than a formal one — and is that presupposition doing all the work? If so, it looks like the analogy needs understanding to be non-computational in order to show that it is. And given that weak point, does my professor's conclusion about the Turing test survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way?
