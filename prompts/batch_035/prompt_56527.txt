I am a recently retired climate modeler — thirty years of working with coupled ocean–atmosphere systems, the last fifteen mostly on uncertainty propagation and ensemble design. Since retiring I have been reading political philosophy seriously for the first time, and I have become captivated by the boundary problem: the question of who belongs to the demos and is therefore entitled to a say in collective decisions. Coming from the natural sciences, my instinct is that the problem is only hard because the philosophical proposals are under-specified, and that it should yield to a properly formulated causal criterion.

The position I have arrived at is roughly this: the demos for any decision should consist of all and only those whose interests are causally affected by that decision in a non-trivial way, where "non-trivial" means clearing some threshold of stakes rather than being a mere butterfly effect. My life among chaotic systems makes me well aware that in a literal causal sense every decision affects everyone a little; that is exactly why I build in the threshold. On this view, constituencies should be decision-specific rather than fixed: a town's zoning question enfranchises its residents and near-neighbors but not distant strangers, while questions with global externalities — and here my professional background makes me think of emissions more than most — enfranchise everyone, including arguably those not yet born.

I want to test this position the way I would test a model, so treat it as the object: what would count against it? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some I already half-see: the threshold looks uncomfortably like a tuned parameter that can be adjusted to save the theory from any awkward constituency; the boundary-drawing decision itself affects people, launching a regress over who votes on who votes; and the criterion seems to enfranchise tourists, prisoners, and children while offering no obvious grip on why the unborn count but the dead do not. Which of these would a committed defender concede as genuine costs, and which would they wave off? Does the position predict anything that fixed-constituency and coercion-based rivals do not — some real case where the causal-stakes criterion gets the franchise right and they get it wrong — or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? And if it has, I want an honest verdict on whether that is a defect for a principle of this kind, or just what philosophy looks like from the inside.
