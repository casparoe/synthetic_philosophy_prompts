Read a piece in a policy magazine last week that summed up Amartya Sen's famine work roughly like this: 'Sen's great discovery was that famines are never really about food. In Bengal in 1943 there was plenty of rice around — people starved because merchants hoarded it and the colonial government, which could have acted, chose not to. The same story explains Ireland in the 1840s and Mao's Great Leap Forward: famines are not natural disasters but political crimes, governments starving their own people. And that's why, as Sen showed, no famine has ever occured in a functioning democracy.'

I came away feeling like I'd learned something, but a couple of things bug me. Like, the Irish famine started with the potato blight destroying the actual crop — that seems like a food problem if anything is? And 'crime' sounds like it needs intent, and I genuinely dont know whether Sen thinks the British in Bengal intended anything, or just didn't care.

So: how much of this summary would Sen himself sign off on? Go claim by claim and tell me which parts are accurate, which are inflated or conflated — especially what his entitlement approach actually says about *why* the specific Bengal victims starved, whether he thinks food shortage is never the cause of a famine anywhere, and whether 'political crime, governments starving their own people' follows from his analysis or goes well beyond it. Also, the democracy claim at the end: did he demonstrate that, or assert it, and is it the kind of thing that could survive one bad counterexample? I'd like to know what I can actually trust from that piece before I repeat any of it at dinner parties.
