I teach an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility and I'm writing the take-home final. Draft one essay question on standing to blame and the hypocrisy objection, along with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard positions — forfeiture-style accounts, inconsistent-standards explanations of why the hypocrite's blame is objectionable, and accounts on which loss of standing makes blame improper to express rather than unjustified or false. Instead, it should put two cases the students have not seen at the center, and demand that they use the cases to test the accounts rather than summarize them.

The first case is the clean-hands accuser: someone who has never committed the wrong in question and intends never to, but who, when pressed, concedes that in the wrongdoer's circumstances — the same pressures, incentives, and weaknesses of will — they would very likely have done the same. The second is standing by proxy: the close friend of a victim blames the wrongdoer, but the victim has forgiven, no longer wants the matter raised, and asks the friend to drop it. Has the friend's standing been extinguished by the victim's forgiveness, merely outweighed, or was it never the friend's to begin with?

The question should force students to say whether the hypocrisy objection has any grip on the clean-hands accuser at all, and if not, whether that shows the objection was never really about hypocrisy but about something else — fairness, identification with the wrong, or the blamer's own susceptibility to it. It should also make them commit on whether loss of standing renders blame false, unjustified, or merely improper to express, and to trace what each answer implies for the proxy case.

For the marking scheme: separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, assign marks to each, and note in one line the way students typically go wrong on each move — for instance, treating the hypocrisy objection as refuting the truth of the blamer's judgment rather than the propriety of their blaming.
