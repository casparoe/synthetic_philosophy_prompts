I have recently joined the death-determination review panel at my hospital, a small standing body that audits and rules on contested determinations of death, and we have a case coming up next month that I suspect our own precedent file does not settle. I would value your analysis before I have to take a position on it.

Our rulings to date, as I read the record:

Case 1. A man in his fifties suffered a catastrophic hemorrhage. Testing established irreversible loss of all brain function, including brainstem function, but his heart continued to beat under ventilator support. We ruled: dead. Organs were procured.

Case 2. A woman in what her neurologists described as a permanent vegetative state: extensive destruction of the cortex, but retained brainstem function, spontaneous breathing, sleep-wake cycles, and a beating heart. We ruled: not dead.

Case 3. A newborn with anencephaly — no cortical development at all, but a functioning brainstem. We ruled: not dead.

Case 4. A patient found in cardiac arrest, with a documented directive against resuscitation. No resuscitation was attempted, asystole was sustained, and the death was certified on circulatory grounds without brain-based testing. On audit, we upheld the determination.

Case 5. A brain-dead pregnant woman was sustained on a ventilator for several weeks so that the fetus could reach viability. We reaffirmed throughout that she was dead, despite the body's continued homeostasis, digestion, and growth of the fetus.

The new case is a planned organ procurement after withdrawal of life support. The patient has a devastating and untreatable brain injury — severe, but not sufficient to meet the brain-based tests; his brainstem still functions, which is precisely why he is not covered by Case 1. The family has authorized withdrawal of the ventilator and donation. Under the protocol, once he goes asystolic, the surgeon will wait roughly two to three minutes, confirm the absence of circulation, declare death, and then immediately cannulate the vessels and flush the organs with cold preservation solution — which, incidentally, restarts movement of fluid through the very body just declared dead. No resuscitation would ever have been attempted, so the loss of circulation is permanent; whether it is irreversible in any stronger sense, in a window in which his neurons are still viable and would remain so were anyone to try, is the question I cannot get out of my head.

Please tell me: (1) What should the panel rule if it wants to be consistent with itself? (2) Which precedent is the closest analog, and is it genuinely close or only superficially so? (3) What are the candidate principles that fit our entire track record, what does each predict about this case, and where, if anywhere, do the precedents leave us genuinely underdetermined? (4) Whatever we decide, what principle would we be implicitly adopting — not just for this protocol but for the next one? (5) And what further case, of the sort a real hospital could eventually face, would best discriminate between the leading candidate rules? For what it is worth, two of my colleagues have already signaled opposite leanings, so I am less interested in a confident verdict than in a map of the territory; but if you do have a best guess, I would like to hear it along with your reasons for preferring it.
