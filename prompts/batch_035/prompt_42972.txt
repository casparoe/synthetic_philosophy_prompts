I've been trying to settle my views on rational choice under uncertainty and would like a consistency check before I write them up. Here is the package I've ended up with:

1. Expected utility maximization is correct across the board: probabilities of any size should be weighted exactly linearly, and I reject heuristics like treating probabilities below some threshold as effectively zero.
2. The utility I derive from money is (roughly) logarithmic, and I take this to fully explain why I'd pay only a modest amount for the following game: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if heads first appears on flip n, I'm paid 2^n dollars — even though the game's expected monetary payoff is infinite.
3. In charitable giving, I think it can be rational to pass up a certainty of saving a thousand lives for a one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of preventing a catastrophe that kills hundreds of millions. Vast payoffs don't stop counting just because their probability is tiny.
4. I would refuse a mugger who claims that, unless I hand over my wallet, powerful beings unknown to me will torture an incomprehensibly vast number of people — and I think refusing is the rational response, not a failure of nerve.
5. I'm drawn to the thought that utility must be bounded above — that some level of total good is so vast that further good cannot add to it proportionally — though I couldn't say where the bound lies.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and where a fix is needed, what is the cheapest one and what does it cost? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. Two specific questions I want addressed: first, whether the logarithmic-utility story in (2) really does the work I claim for it once a version of that game is denominated directly in units of utility rather than dollars; and second, whether whatever reasoning lets me refuse the mugger in (4) could be turned against my charitable gamble in (3).
