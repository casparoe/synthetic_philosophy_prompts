I'm presenting at a workshop next month where I'll defend what I take to be a restrained version of the Adams thesis: the probability of a simple, unembedded indicative conditional is its conditional probability, and Lewis's triviality results don't refute this -- they show something about what happens when you embed conditionals or demand a single probability function do too much work at once. My own angle, for what it's worth: the proofs all require the conditional probability to be computed on one fixed algebra, and once you let the relevant epistemic state shift with what's being conditionalized on, the "collapse" Lewis derives stops being a reductio and becomes just the observation that conditionals are context-dependent in a way indicative sentences standardly aren't. I admit that's half-baked.

The audience: a philosophy department with maybe two formal semanticists who know the triviality proofs line by line, a couple of epistemologists sympathetic to conditional probability but suspicious of giving up truth conditions, and a logician who thinks the whole debate is confused because we never fixed what "the probability of a sentence" is supposed to mean before we started proving things about it. Nobody there is a card-carrying Quinean, but there's general institutional fondness for bivalence.
