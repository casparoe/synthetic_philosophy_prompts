I'm taking a philosophy of mind course, and our professor wants us to write essays arguing that some theory of consciousness is adequate only relative to a checklist we have to build ourselves first. Take the role of a professional philosopher of mind and help me draft that checklist for theories located in the access-vs-phenomenal-consciousness debate. Begin with some brief brainstorming before committing to the final list. The desiderata should cover at least the following, though you may add others: (i) explaining cases that seem to show phenomenal consciousness overflows the capacity-limited contents that global workspace theory says get broadcast (e.g., the Sperling partial-report setup); (ii) handling postdictive phenomena, where the moment-to-moment contents of experience appear to depend on what arrives slightly later (e.g., the cutaneous rabbit); (iii) respecting the access/phenomenal distinction without either collapsing it by stipulation or positing an untestable second layer of consciousness; and (iv) accounting for subjects who cannot report their experiences at all. My professor also insists on a requirement she calls "demotion discipline": when a theory reclassifies some everyday experience as not-really-conscious, it owes us an account of what that experience then is, rather than quietly deleting it. Please say which desiderata you think are non-negotiable and which are camp prejudices (e.g., favors of overflow defenders or of workspace theorists), whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and finish with one paragraph on how global workspace theory does against your list.
