I've been going to my local rationalist meetup for years and last month two of the regulars, both much more mathy than me, got into a long-running fight about Kelly betting that never got resolved and now flares up every time anyone mentions the word "bankroll." I'll try to reconstruct it because I'd love an actual adjudication.

Dave's position was the standard one: if you're making a long sequence of bets where your stake is a fraction of your current bankroll, EV maximization leads to ruin almost surely, because the single best outcome (putting everything on the highest-edge bet and winning) has positive EV but the multiplicative dynamics kill you in the long run. Kelly, he said, isn't a heuristic, it's just the mathematically correct answer to repeated multiplicative betting: maximize the geometric growth rate of your wealth and you end up richer than anyone else with probability approaching one, given enough bets. He even brought up the ergodicity framing -- that the whole confusion comes from people computing ensemble averages (expectations over parallel universes) when what matters to a single person over time is the time-average growth rate, and that this dissolves the need to just arbitrarily assume log utility. Kelly follows from the dynamics of the situation, not from anyone's psychology.

Mira said this was way oversold. Her first point was that Kelly is famously equivalent to maximizing expected log wealth, so if you don't happen to value wealth logarithmically, the "mathematically correct" framing is question-begging -- Dave is smuggling in a utility function and calling it a theorem. Second, the ergodicity stuff only applies when your wealth genuinely follows that multiplicative process with no outside income, no borrowing, no ability to sit out rounds -- real people aren't closed multiplicative systems, so "what Kelly says" isn't pinned down by the setup. Third, in actual betting your edge is estimated with error, and betting full Kelly on an overestimated edge is punishing, which is why serious bettors use fractional Kelly, and she claimed that once you go fractional, Kelly isn't a decision rule anymore, it's a family of rules with a parameter you choose for reasons Kelly itself doesn't supply -- at which point, she said, the whole thing collapses back into ordinary decision theory and Dave is just a person who happens to like log.

Dave's comeback: the equivalence with log utility doesn't show what Mira thinks, because the point of the geometric framing is that growth rates are a property of trajectories, not of preferences -- if you compare any fixed betting fraction against Kelly over a long enough horizon, Kelly overtakes it with probability one, which is a dominance claim that holds regardless of what you enjoy. Mira's reply to that was that "given enough bets" is hiding an infinity, that real people have finite horizons and charitable bequests, and that probability-one dominance over unbounded time doesn't transfer cleanly to the fifty years an actual human has, especially since Kelly is known to be more volatile in expectation than people realize in medium runs. They basically went around this loop three times and then started arguing about whether "geometric rationality" as a general decision-theory program (using it outside betting, like for career choices) was a real generalization or vibes.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'm especially confused about whether the dominance argument really is preference-free, and whether the finite-horizon point is a real objection or just pedantry, and I'd take any thoughts on whether the whole disagreement is actually substantive or whether they're using "Kelly" to mean three different things.
