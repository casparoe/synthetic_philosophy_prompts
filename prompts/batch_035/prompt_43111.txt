I teach a nineteenth-century political philosophy course, and this term I am building a unit on Mill and empire around the awkward fact that the author of On Liberty spent his entire career, from 1823 until the Company's dissolution in 1858, as an administrator of the East India Company, drafting dispatches for the governance of India. To frame the unit, I pulled a popular intellectual history of liberalism from a general-audience series, and its handling of Mill struck me as smooth enough that my students would have no reason to doubt any of it. Before I decide whether to assign the chapter, I want an audit of its central paragraph, which reads as follows:

"Mill is often called the father of liberalism, but his liberalism stopped at the water's edge. He spent his whole working life as an official of the East India Company, and in On Liberty he openly declares that despotism is a legitimate form of government for barbarians — a category into which he placed the people of India. The harm principle, the centerpiece of his political philosophy, simply did not apply to them: on his view Indians were, politically speaking, children, incapable of governing themselves, so the protections Mill demanded for citizens would be wasted on them. He went further, arguing in his Indian writings that despotic rule was positively good for such peoples, since only autocratic management could drag a stagnant society into the modern age. Mill was, in short, a straightforward apologist for empire, and his case for British rule in India differs only in ornament from the arguments later used to justify every colonial venture of the following century."

Please go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what a scholar of Mill's political thought would accept as stated, what they would call a simplification, and what they would call a misrepresentation. In particular I want to know whether the second sentence trades on something Mill actually says — I recall the passage in the introductory chapter of On Liberty about despotism over those in a backward state of improvement, but I do not recall Mill naming India there, or anywhere in that book — and whether the fourth sentence is a fair description of what Mill actually committed to writing about India, as against his parliamentary testimony and his defense of Company administration. I also want your view on the final verdict: does the conclusion that Mill was a straightforward apologist follow from the parts of the paragraph that are accurate, once the misrepresentations are corrected, or does the correction change the character of the verdict even if not its direction?

Please do not give me a survey of possible readings and leave the choice to me. Commit to a definite overall judgment — fair, unfair, or fair-in-substance-but-slanted — state it explicitly, and defend it, including where you stand on whether the honest version of this paragraph would leave Mill looking substantially better, substantially worse, or roughly the same to a reader who knows nothing else about him.
