So background: I'm a retired geochemist, did 35 years mainly on trace element analysis, and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of science and the whole rationalist-adjacent reading pile. Aumann's agreement theorem keeps showing up everywhere I look. As I understand it, the claim from his famous little 1976 paper is that two agents with the same priors and common knowledge of each other's posteriors can't agree to disagree -- their estimates have to coincide. Fine as math, I take it. But people then use it to say things like "persistent disagreement between two smart honest people is proof somebody's being irrational or has different priors," and that feels like it's doing way more work than the theorem licenses.

My old colleague and I have coffee every week and disagree about tons of factual stuff (right now: whether vitamin D supplementation above deficiency levels does anything for healthy adults), and we joke that between us we've experimentally refuted Aumann. But the joke got me actually wondering where the theorem breaks for people like us, and I can't tell if the answer is boring ("humans just aren't Bayes-rational, next question") or interesting.

So here's what I want from you: an actual number, your credence, in this proposition -- that a persistent disagreement between two honest, intelligent, genuinely trying people, on a purely factual question, after full disclosure and real discussion, necessarily shows that at least one substantive assumption of the theorem fails, in a way that can be named and isn't just "humans are bad at this." Not a hedge, a number.

Then decompose it for me like I'd decompose an error budget: your probability that the common prior assumption is the real binding failure in practice, your probability that common knowledge is basically never achieved between humans no matter how long they talk, your probability that the theorem, even where its assumptions technically hold, is vacuous -- tells you disagreement shouldn't happen but gives you no idea which of you to update, and so is useless as a guide -- plus any other terms you think belong in the calculation. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, then show how they combine into your top-level credence. (I realize the terms might not be independent; tell me how you're handling that.)

And two more things. First, which of those terms is your final number most sensitive to -- where would a little movement change the answer a lot? Second, my specific case: same priors seems doomed for us, he's ex-chemistry and I'm ex-geology and our fields drilled different intuitions into us. Give me your credence that if my colleague and I both committed to some honest protocol -- state evidence, state reasoning, no strategic withholding -- our vitamin D disagreement would shrink to something like agreement over the course of a year. And be honest if you think the honest answer is "I basically can't apply the theorem to you two at all, here's why." I'd rather have a well-defended 0.4 than a lecture that starts with "it depends."
