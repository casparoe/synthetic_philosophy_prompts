Some background before my question, since the number I want depends on it. My spouse and I have recently been arguing about whether I have a genuine moral obligation—not merely a praiseworthy kindness—to take my aging mother into our home rather than arrange assisted living, even though doing so would strain our finances and, I believe, our children's stability. My spouse was raised in a society where an adult child's duty to parents is assumed to run that deep; I was raised somewhere far more individualist, where adult children owe parents affection and perhaps money, but not their lives. What disturbed me was not that we disagreed but that neither of us could produce a consideration the other would recognize as evidence rather than as a restatement of a starting point. I later learned that nearly all research in moral psychology is conducted on Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic samples—WEIRD populations, as the acronym goes—who turn out to be global outliers: unusual in treating harm and fairness as the whole of morality, where most societies also treat loyalty to kin, deference within hierarchy, and certain purity concerns as moral matters in their own right. That is a descriptive finding; my question is normative, and I want it as a number.

Please give me your credence that there is a fact of the matter in cases of this kind—some determinate answer to whether an adult child in circumstances like ours is obligated as I've described, such that at least one of us is simply wrong. Decompose it into whatever terms you think belong, but at minimum: (1) your credence that moral realism of some form holds for at least part of this domain; (2) conditional on that, your credence that obligations to parents fall within realism's scope rather than in a conventional zone, like which side of the road to drive on, where cross-cultural spread is no threat to objectivity because there is nothing to be objective about; and (3) your credence that our disagreement reflects one of us being closer to the truth, rather than there being no truth to be close to. A friend suggested that, in assessing (3), I should calibrate against domains where we independently know there are facts of the matter yet populations still differ—numerical estimation, causal attribution—where spread across cultures is treated as evidence about the populations, never as evidence that there is no answer. I cannot tell how much weight that analogy deserves, so tell me where you think it bends.

Give a number and a one-line justification for each term, then the combination, then the term you are least confident in and what would shift it. Finally, tell me which of the three terms my spouse and I most plausibly disagree about—since whether our argument is winnable, rather than merely understandable, hangs on exactly that. If you would precisify "fact of the matter" differently than as an answer idealized, fully informed agents would converge on, say so and proceed with your own precisification.
