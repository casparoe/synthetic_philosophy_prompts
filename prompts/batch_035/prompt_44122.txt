I'm doing grant evaluation for a small private foundation that has decided to fund work premised on a possibility most mathematicians dismiss: that our foundational theories — Peano arithmetic, or ZFC, or even quite weak fragments — are in fact inconsistent. The board has read enough to be uneasy. Edward Nelson spent years trying to prove elementary arithmetic inconsistent and, shortly before his death in 2011, announced a proof of the inconsistency of Peano arithmetic that had to be retracted after Terence Tao found a flaw. Vladimir Voevodsky, a Fields Medalist, said late in his life that he could not rule out the inconsistency of Peano arithmetic, and his turn toward univalent foundations and machine-checked proof was partly motivated by distrust of informal standards of correctness. And on the other side, there is the comforting observation associated with Harvey Friedman's grand conjecture: that nearly every theorem of ordinary mathematics published in a top journal is arithmetical in statement and apparently provable in a very weak fragment of PA — elementary function arithmetic — so perhaps almost all real mathematics would survive even if the strong theories collapsed. The board takes no position on the probability; it just wants to know what would be worth doing if one assigned it a non-negligible credence, say a few percent.

The budget is about $3 million a year for four years, enough for eight to ten researchers drawn from proof theory, philosophy of mathematics, and formalization software engineering. I've identified five candidate lines of work, but I don't trust my own ordering, and I want yours, with the reasoning exposed:

1. Damage assessment: systematic reverse-mathematical surveys of how much of published mathematics survives in successively weaker fragments — a "disaster map" telling us which results stand even if PA or ZFC falls, and where the genuinely strong assumptions hide.
2. Ordinal analysis: pushing consistency proofs for stronger and stronger theories into weaker metatheories, resuming a program that has largely stalled beyond the impredicative systems of the 1990s.
3. Formalization at scale: funding machine-checked libraries and tooling, on the theory that even if it can't rule out inconsistency, it can localize and quarantine the damage when one is found.
4. Inconsistency-tolerant foundations: developing paraconsistent or otherwise degradable foundational systems in which a discovered contradiction would not trivialize the subject.
5. Epistemology of the question itself: what, if anything, justifies our confidence in Con(PA); how Gödel's disjunction should be amended to admit a neglected third horn (the axioms are inconsistent); whether "absolute consistency" is even a coherent target; what rational credence in inconsistency a well-calibrated agent should hold.

Please give me a priority ordering of these programs (and tell me if the list itself is misdrawn — whether some line is secretly two, or two are secretly one). For each, I want the criterion-by-criterion case: how much it reduces expected damage versus how much it reduces expected probability of disaster (these come apart — formalization is pure insurance, ordinal analysis is pure prevention); tractability for the kind of researchers we can hire versus for mathematicians who won't work on it; neglectedness relative to what academia already supports; and, crucially, what each program implicitly bets on — what crux about the foundations or about the sociology of mathematics makes it rational to fund it rather than its rivals. Then tell me which alleged stakes of the inconsistency question are overrated: would an inconsistency in PA actually matter to working mathematicians, or does the Friedman-style observation that ordinary mathematics lives in weak fragments mean the practical stakes are near zero? Finally, describe two or three concrete developments in the next four years — a proof-theoretic breakthrough, a formalization milestone, an embarrassment in some subfield — that should move a program up or down the list, so the board can evaluate the portfolio when it reviews the grants. I care more about the reasoning that connects the ordering to the decision at hand than about completeness.
