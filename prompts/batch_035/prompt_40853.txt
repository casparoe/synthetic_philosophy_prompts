I'm on the clinical ethics committee at a medium-sized NHS trust — nurses, a couple of consultants, a chaplain, a lay member, no philosophers — and we keep getting advance directive cases where the patient's earlier self and their current self seem to be pulling in opposite directions. Last weekend I had a massive argument about this with my oldest mate, who did a philosophy MA and has opinions about everything. He was going on about Dworkin's old point that when Margo writes a directive refusing treatment should she get dementia, we honour it because it protects her critical interests, the story she tells about her whole life, not just how content the demented Margo happens to feel day to day. Then he flipped and did the Dresser side: the person sitting in the day room smiling at visitors arguably isn't the same self that signed the thing, so why should a stranger's document from fifteen years ago outrank the person actually in front of us? And then a third position: someone like Jaworska would say we're pretending late-stage dementia patients have no cares at all, when actually they often still have values and attachments we could ask about directly if we bothered. We went round in circles until 1am and I realised my committee needs something we can actually use, not a seminar.

What I want is a decision procedure we can run in a real case discussion — we get about twenty minutes per case, the attending consultant has to sign off on whatever we conclude, and nobody on the committee will ever read the underlying literature. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a firm no (e.g. the directive plainly doesn't apply to the clinical situation in front of us), where it defers to the now-patient's own current wishes, and the points where it honestly gives up and hands the decision to the committee as a value judgement rather than pretending the flowchart settled it. I'd like it to build in the thought that the patient's current cares, even with advanced dementia, are evidence worth gathering rather than noise to be overridden — but also to say what to do when the current patient seems happy and the directive still bites, since my mate insists that's the whole point of writing one. Please be upfront about which steps are just judgement dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases for me. One: a woman with moderately advanced dementia, generally cheerful, who wrote a directive five years ago refusing antibiotics for life-threatening infections, and her family says she'd never want her current self to be bound by it. Two: a directive written decades ago, before a second marriage and a grandchild, that says no to a treatment which now seems to cut against everything the patient's life has since been about — a case where following the old self feels wrong in the opposite direction, and I want to see whether the procedure notices the difference. Three: the vague one, a directive that just says no extraordinary measures with no elaboration, for a patient who now can't discuss it. I want to see where the procedure gives an answer I won't like and says so.
