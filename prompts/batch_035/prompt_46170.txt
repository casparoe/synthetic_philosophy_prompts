A friend of mine, who does data engineering at a large logistics firm and is active in democratic-socialist politics, keeps pressing an argument he considers fatal to the socialist calculation debate: Mises and Hayek claimed that rational allocation at the scale of a whole economy is impossible without genuine market prices, yet any big-box retailer or e-commerce platform routinely plans the production, warehousing, and distribution of hundreds of thousands of distinct items across a continent, with no internal markets at all — procurement, staffing, inventory, and routing are all decided administratively, and the system works well enough to keep the shelves stocked. His conclusion: the calculation problem is an engineering problem, now largely solved, and the debate is a historical curiosity. He sometimes adds that if a firm can do it under the handicap of shareholder extraction, a democratic polity could do it better.

I want a serious audit of the analogy, since it's doing a lot of work in our arguments. Specifically:

First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the corporate case is supposed to play the role of the socialist commonwealth, of the central planning board, of market prices, of the consumer, and of the worker? Be candid about any slots with no occupant.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and — more importantly — sort the disanalogies into those that matter for his conclusion and those that don't. For instance, it may be irrelevant that firms are hierarchical rather than democratic, if his claim is only about the possibility of calculation rather than the desirability of the arrangements; but the fact that the firm is embedded in external markets looks like it might be doing a great deal of the work, since the corporation plans its production against prices it takes as data rather than computing an allocation for the whole system. Where exactly does that leave the analogy — is the firm a miniature planned economy, or more like a single massive market participant whose internal allocation is cheap precisely because the allocation problem that markets are good at solving is still being solved, just outside the firm's walls?

Third, assess whether his conclusion survives the audit. It seems the analogy conflates at least three distinct claims that got run together in the original debate: that calculation without prices is impossible in principle (the Mises claim), that the relevant economic knowledge is dispersed, tacit, and cannot be surveyed by any center (the Hayek claim), and that planning at scale fails for reasons of computational complexity. The corporate case, if it shows anything, seems to bear on the third claim and perhaps to leave the first two untouched — the firm's planners are consuming market prices, and the firm is disciplined by bankruptcy and competition in a way a commonwealth with no exit is not. But be fair to my friend: say whether anything survives on his side, including whether the analogy at least shifts the burden of proof on complexity, and what a Lange-style reply (planning by trial and error, iterated adjustment rather than solving equations all at once) would add.

Finally, if the analogy is a poor carrier for his conclusion, say what a better analogy for the corporate case would be, and whether that better analogy could be retooled to support some weaker version of what he wants to argue.
