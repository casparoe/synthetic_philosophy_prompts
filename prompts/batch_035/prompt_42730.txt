So I've been going down a rabbit hole about this thing my brother showed me, I think it's called the centipede game? Two players take turns, and on your turn you can either grab the pot or pass, and if you pass the pot grows and it's the other guy's turn, and this goes on for some fixed number of rounds until the game just ends. I have zero academic background, I just think about stuff like this in the evenings, but I've noticed I keep issuing verdicts to myself and I'm not sure they add up, so I want to know if there's some principle that captures them all or whether I'm just inconsistent.

Here's how my gut rules, case by case:

1. Last round, you can either take the big pot or pass and get nothing while the other player gets everything. Take it, obviously. Even a saint takes it. I'm totally with the textbook here.
2. A version with only 2 rounds. My head says grab at round 1, because the round-1 grab is only two "obvious" steps away from case 1, but my gut says that's leaving nearly everything on the table for a tiny sure thing, and I half think the gut is right. This is the one I keep flip flopping on.
3. A version with 100 rounds where the pot ends up huge. My gut says passing for the first couple dozen rounds is genuinely rational, not just a lucky mistake, and I refuse to believe the right move is take at round 1 because "he'd take at round 2, so I should take at round 1" all the way down.
4. In chess, when the computers have worked out a rook endgame to the last move and the website says "mate in 34," I think that kind of backwards reasoning from the end of the game is completely sound. It doesn't bother me at all there, and honestly I trust it more than most things.
5. We played a version where after every round we flipped a coin, and tails ended the game on the spot. My verdict there is that passing early felt MORE rational, not less, even though you'd think adding a random chance of losing everything would make me grab sooner.
6. A version with no known end at all, like we play it every Sunday forever. Passing/cooperating is clearly right there, no one would even argue.

The tension is pretty visible: 3 and 4 both seem right to me, but the reasoning that makes 4 airtight (work backwards from a known end, everyone rational, everyone knows everyone's rational, stacked up as many layers as you need) applied to 3 gives me "take at round 1," which I won't accept. And then 5 goes the opposite direction from what I'd have predicted about myself, which makes me wonder if the coin is doing something important or if I'm just being weird about coins.

My own half-baked theory is that backwards reasoning is fine when the end is close, like 2 or 3 steps away, and becomes bogus somewhere along the way when you chain too many "he knows that I know that he knows" steps together. But that feels like a fudge I invented to save my verdicts, not a real principle, and I can't say where the cutoff is or why there'd be one at all.

So: is there one principle that gets all six, or close? If it has to choose between saving my verdict on 3 and saving my verdict on 4, which should go and why? And whatever principle you land on, please tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (a) a version where the pot stops growing after round 10 and just sits there getting passed along till round 100, and (b) a version where we each get to announce out loud, before the game starts, what we're planning to do, with no way to bind ourselves to it. I'd also take suggestions for a seventh case that would really stress-test whatever principle you come up with. Write it for someone who never took a class in this, no math if you can avoid it, and definately no reading lists.
