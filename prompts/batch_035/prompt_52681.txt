I've been rereading Moby-Dick alongside Machiavelli, of all things, and I want to see if a reading holds up. In chapter 25 of The Prince Machiavelli says that "fortune is the arbiter of one half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less," and he compares fortune to "one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains" — the point being you build dikes and embankments during the calm weather, so that when the flood comes it can be channeled. His whole picture is that virtù is adaptive: the successful man reads "the spirit of the times" and adjusts his methods, and the man who can't change course when the times change is ruined, because fortune being changeful and men being fixed in their natures, agreement between the two is luck with an expiry date.

So here's my question: what happens when you read Ahab through this? My first pass is that Ahab is virtù that refuses to be virtù — he has the energy, the boldness, the capacity to bend an entire crew to his will (that quarterdeck scene, nailing the doubloon to the mast, is basically a masterclass in manufacturing consent), so the raw material of virtù is all there. But he does the one thing Machiavelli says guarantees ruin: instead of adapting to fortune, he declares war on it. He treats the whale not as a flood to be channeled but as a personal enemy, which is a category error Machiavelli's framework can't really process — fortune isn't the kind of thing you get to hate. And the counterweight is Ishmael, who survives exactly the way Machiavelli's adaptive man survives: by accommodating, not conquering — he ends up floating on Queequeg's coffin, literally riding out the flood on something that was meant for death. The dike, after a fashion.

But I want to know whether this reading is actually faithful, or whether I'm forcing it. A few places I suspect it resists. First, Machiavelli is talking about princes and political survival, and Ahab's quest is private — revenge, maybe knowledge. Does the fortune/virtù framework even stretch that far, or does applying it to a whaling captain distort both? Second, there's a wrinkle about the whale itself: Ishmael keeps insisting we can't actually know whether Moby Dick is malicious or just a dumb brute following instinct, and the crew (and Ahab) project malice onto him. Is the novel maybe more epistemically humble about fortune than Machiavelli is — treating it as not just ungoverned but fundamentally unreadable? Third, and this is the one that bugs me most: Machiavelli implies the prepared man survives, but the Pequod isn't exactly undefended — they're competent whalers, Starbuck keeps urging the prudent course, and it doesn't matter. So does Ahab's fall confirm Machiavelli (the fixed nature meets changing circumstances and is ruined) or quietly undercut him (no amount of dikes helps when the flood is bigger than the river metaphor allows)?

Where does the lens genuinely illuminate, where does the novel refuse it, and is there something here — maybe about the difference between channeling fortune and taking it personally — that Melville dramatizes and Machiavelli's chapter can't say on its own? Also, if you think the reading collapses entirely, say so plainly and tell me what breaks first. No need to quote the novel from memory, just work from the well-known scenes.
