I'm taking a set theory course this semester and we just got to the axiom of choice. My professor presented it with the classic boots and socks picture that I gather goes back to Russell: if you have infinitely many pairs of boots, you don't need any fancy axiom, just say "take the left boot from each pair" and you've got your choice function handed to you. But with socks there's no left or right, no rule that distinguishes one sock from its partner, and the professor's point was that the axiom of choice is exactly the innocent-seeming claim that even then you can "pick one sock from each pair." The way she told it, the socks case is just the boots case minus the lucky rule, so if the boots are fine the socks should be too, and the whole thing is basically obvious.

Since then the analogy has been doing a lot of work in my head and in our problem set discussions. When people in the class defend AC ("come on, it's just saying a choice can be made"), when they shrug off Zorn's lemma in proofs that every vector space has a basis, the vibe is always that socks are basically boots. But I was reading around and it seems like there are models of ZF where you can have a countable pile of pairs of socks and still no function picking one sock from each, plus weird consequences like Banach-Tarski and the well-ordering theorem coming out of this supposedly obvious little axiom, and some constructivist-flavored complaint that AC gives you a choice function existing without ever telling you which sock it picks. So now I can't tell if the boots/socks story is a real argument or just a bedtime story.

Can you actually audit the analogy for me? Like: set out what maps onto what (finite picking, having a rule, the collection of pairs, the function's existence), then where the mapping breaks, and especially what the boots case quietly has that the socks case lacks -- I'm guessing something about definability or uniformity, and maybe also about whether there's anyone literally doing the choosing, which feels off for an axiom about sets. Then tell me: which of the disanalogies are load-bearing for the uses people put the analogy to (the "AC is obviously true" use, the "AC is harmless, use Zorn freely" use), and which don't matter? And does the conclusion that AC is just an obvious extension of everyday choosing survive once those are on the table -- or is there a better analogy that would make what AC actually asserts clearer, maybe one that makes the strange consequences feel less like a bait and switch? sorry if this is a bit rambling, the problem set is due friday and my TA just keeps saying "it's true in ZFC, move on".
