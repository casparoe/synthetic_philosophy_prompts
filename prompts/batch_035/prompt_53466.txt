Next month I'm presenting at a small workshop on population ethics and the future of welfare, defending a version of suffering-focused ethics that I want to get right in front of a hostile room. My position, roughly: the badness of suffering is not just another entry on the same welfare ledger as the goodness of pleasure, but I don't want the fanatical version of the view either. Specifically, I hold (1) there is a procreation asymmetry -- it's wrong to create a life you can reasonably expect to be very bad, but there's no corresponding duty to create a life you can expect to be good, and this asymmetry reflects something deep about the moral structure of suffering rather than a mere framing effect; (2) reducing suffering has lexical priority over creating or increasing happiness once suffering rises above some severity threshold, but not an absolute one -- a sufficiently enormous gain in happiness can outweigh a small amount of mild discomfort, so I'm not committed to choosing the destruction of a world with any suffering in it over letting it continue; and (3) this is meant as a theory of moral weight, not a decision procedure for doctors or parents. My argument leans on a battery of pairwise comparisons: everyone agrees it's bad to bring into existence a child whose life would be unremittingly miserable, and almost nobody thinks there's a mirror-image obligation to bring into existence a child whose life would be blissful; additively separable views that treat pleasure and pain as commensurable seem unable to say why the cases feel different without smuggling in the asymmetry by hand.

The audience is the problem. The workshop is mostly welfare economists and philosophers who are classical or at least symmetric utilitarians -- people who are fluent in total-order axiology, social welfare functions, and the_repugnant_conclusion literature, and who will treat any weighting of suffering over happiness as either (a) an ad hoc departure from additive separability that undermines interpersonal welfare comparisons entirely, or (b) a psychological artifact -- negativity bias, loss aversion, whatever -- dressed up as a moral principle. There will also be a couple of Kantians who think the whole exercise of ranking world-states by aggregate welfare is misguided, and at least one person I know of who works on digital minds and will ask whether my severity threshold applies to suffering we create in simulations or artificial systems. I can hold my own on the pairwise intuitions; what I can't yet do is answer the aggregation objections -- the one that says my threshold view forces me to prefer a world of trillions of lives with trivial discomfort to one life with a single severe pain just above threshold, and the one about why the exact placement of my threshold isn't arbitrary in a way that shows the whole lexical structure is a sham.
