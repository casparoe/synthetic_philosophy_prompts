I drive a delivery van round Greater Manchester and do most of my philosophical thinking stuck in traffic. For the last year or so I've been chewing on one question: when you can save one group of people or another, and the groups are different sizes, do the numbers matter? Say you're the only one on the beach and there's a drowning swimmer to your left and five drowning swimmers to your right, and you can only get to one group in time. My mates are split on it and, honestly, so am I.

We've had alot of rows about it down the pub, and I've started noticing people fall into rough types — my own little folk taxonomy:

- the adders: just add the lives up, five beats one, full stop
- the flippers: no matter the numbers, everyone at risk deserves the same chance, so flip a coin — I gather some bloke in the 70s argued for this, Taurek I think his name was
- the thresholders: one versus one, flip away, but at some point the numbers have to start counting — though nobody agrees where the line is (3? 20? 50?)
- the duelists: you can only compare claims one on one, so each of the five faces the lone swimmer separately and the five never get to gang up — the sum never gets made in the first place

And there's a fifth thing that cuts across all of them: what if one of the people is your mate? Some of my friends who are dead-set adders suddenly aren't, if the one is their best friend and the five are strangers.

Anyway, I want to turn all this into a quiz for a forum I post on (we do a monthly silly-but-not-that-silly poll). I'd like you to take the role of a professional moral philosopher who's spent years on exactly this corner of ethics — rescue cases and whether the numbers count — and design it for me. Requirements:

1. Around eight questions, each a short concrete scenario with two or three options. None of the questions should name a theory or a philosopher — the people taking it havent read anything and would just guess, or take the mick. It has to sort them by what they'd actually do, not what they'd call themselves.
2. Use cases that split the types above. In particular I want a question about whether it matters if the five are clustered together (one sinking dinghy with five people in it) versus scattered (five separate swammers spread along the beach) — I've noticed people go different ways on that and I can't tell if it tracks a real philosophical difference or just vibes. Also a question where the numbers are even on both sides but one side contains a friend. And a couple of escalating ones — one vs one, one vs two, one vs five, one vs fifty — so we can find where a thresholder's line sits, if they have one.
3. Then a scoring key at the end, mapping answer patterns onto positions. Here's where I want you to actually think rather than mirror me back at myself: first brainstorm what the real map of positions on this question is. I've seen the phrase "limited aggregation" bandied about on forums and I suspect it overlaps with my thresholders, but I also suspect my duelists and flippers are genuinely different views that I've muddled together — tell me straight. Then build the key from the questions up, and for each ending say what the most awkward commitment of that view is, the bit that would make someone squirm if you spelled it out. If two of my types can't be separated by any question you can think of, say that plainly instead of pretending the quiz can do it. Same for any empty corner of the space where nobody ever lands — tell me whether that's because the view is daft or just unexplored.

One last thing: please think carefully and sketch a few drafts of questions before settling on the final eight, the way a proper quizwriter would bin their first ideas. And keep the scenarios UK-flavoured — a beach in Cornwall, a canal boat, that sort of thing. Nothing with guns or American trucks.
