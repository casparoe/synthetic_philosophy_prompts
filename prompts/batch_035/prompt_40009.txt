I've drafted the following short essay on the retroactivity objection to the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribulations, and I'd be grateful for critical comments on it. I'm particularly interested in whether the central argument works, and I've flagged specific worries at the end.

---

**Retroactivity at Nuremberg: A Change of Forum, Not a Change of Law?**

The familiar complaint about the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals is that they violated the maxim nullum crimen sine lege: the defendants, above all on the count of waging aggressive war, were punished under a law that did not exist when they acted, and they were judged exclusively by the victorious powers. The standard defense replies that the law was already "there" in customary international law and in the Kellogg-Briand Pact, waiting only to be declared. I want to argue for a different defense, and then to concede something important to the complainant.

The ex post facto doctrine, I suggest, conflates two distinct functions of legality: providing fair notice to citizens, and constraining officials. Retroactive criminal law is an assault on the first function — a person cannot guide conduct by a rule that does not yet exist — and this is what the doctrine exists to protect. But on three of the four counts at Nuremberg, the defendants had notice under norms that already bound them. The Hague regime, which Germany had ratified, prohibited the mistreatment of prisoners and civilians; more decisively, the Wehrmacht's own code criminalized killing prisoners and noncombatants, and ordinary German criminal law criminalized murder. The true innovation of the tribunal on these counts was not the norm but the forum: for the first time there existed a court with the capacity and will to enforce norms that German courts would not or could not enforce against the state's own agents. That is a change of adjudicator, not a change of law, and the ex post facto doctrine has nothing to say about changes of adjudicator. The individual criminal liability of state officials was likewise a question of who answers for the violation, not of what was forbidden.

The crimes-against-humanity count is harder, since the Nazi regime had domestically authorized, and in places mandated, the persecution; but at least the components of it consisting in murder remained murder under the law in force in Germany throughout. The genuinely retroactive count is crimes against peace, and here the standard defense (customary law, Kellogg-Briand) seems to me weak: a norm renouncing war as an instrument of policy is a norm for states, and no German official had reason to expect that it would be converted, after the fact, into a personal criminal prohibition enforced by the enemy.

Yet the complainant's deeper point survives, in a different place than it is usually located. What was fatally compromised at Nuremberg was not notice but symmetry: no Allied conduct could come within the tribunal's purview. The Tokyo counterpart is the cleanest illustration — the decision to exempt Emperor Hirohita from prosecution was made for reasons of occupation politics, and Justice Pal's famous dissent was in large part an insistence on exactly this asymmetry. At Nuremberg, the Soviet delegation had strong reasons of its own to keep certain matters outside the scope, the Katyn massacre of Polish officers being the clearest case. My proposal, then, is that we should stop pressing the retroactivity objection where it does not bite (war crimes and most of crimes against humanity, where the problem was enforcement, not enactment) and press it where it does: no doctrinal fix, including a fully prospective codification of aggressive war as a crime, can supply the symmetry that a court convened by only one side of a war structurally lacks.

---

My specific questions for you:

1. Does the change-of-forum argument survive the objection that the Nazi regime had domestically authorized the conduct, so that "German law" did not supply fair notice in the relevant sense either — i.e., am I helping myself to an ambiguous notion of what the law in force in Germany between 1933 and 1945 actually was?
2. Is the distinction I draw between fair notice and the constraint of officials doing real work, or is it a relabeling of the standard defense?
3. Does the closing contrast between notice and symmetry hold up, given that a defender of the tribunals might argue that selectivity is a defect of any criminal court, national ones included?

Please be blunt about weaknesses, and feel free to comment on any historical claims you think need qualification.
