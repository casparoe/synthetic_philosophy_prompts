hey, so I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a book where the main character — call her Marta — is a woman whose preferences just flip on her, over and over. She pledges on Sunday night to never take another payday loan, and by Thursday she's at the counter. She buys the gym membership and dreads using it. The whole book is structured around these reversals and I want the philosophy underneath it to be right, because a couple of reviewers of my last book caught me hand-waving and I'd rather not do that again.

Below are the notes I drafted for a kind of author's essay I'd put on my website. Could you tell me if the philosophy in them holds up? Not a full edit, just whether I'm using these ideas correctly and where I'm overreaching.

The notes:

---

The standard charge against Marta is the money pump: if her preferences cycle, a sharpie can start her at option A and trade her around A→B→C→A, charging a small fee each time, until she's back where she started and broke. So her inconsistency isn't just a quirk, it's a vulnerability, and rationality is supposed to protect you from that.

But I want to push back, and here's my move: a money pump only works if Marta walks into every trade. Suppose she can see the sharpie coming. Then she can refuse the very first trade — the plan that looks best *now*, taking her future selves' choices into account, is to never start. This is what I understand economists call sophisticated choice (I think it goes back to Strotz on myopia, the guy who modeled the person who plans and then doesn't follow through). So the pump doesn't force moment-to-moment consistency; it just forces you to plan backwards from what you'll actually do. Marta's problem isn't inconsistency, it's that her planning horizon keeps shrinking.

And there's a stronger thought, which I like even more for her: what if she treats her Sunday-night pledge as binding? Not as a prediction, but as a commitment — the future self is supposed to obey the plan even when the plan looks bad from there. I believe this is McClennen's resolute choice: rational people can stick to a chosen plan at a point where deviating would seem better, and it's still rational because the plan as a whole was chosen for good reasons. If that's right, then Marta's Sunday self isn't naive — she's resolute — and what the book is really about is what it takes to hold a resolution when the person holding it has changed.

My worry: I have a feeling philosophers have answers to both moves, like pumps that work even on foresighted agents, or the objection that resolute choice is just cheating by letting your past self boss your present self around. I don't know that literature well enough to know if my two escape hatches are live options or if they were demolished decades ago.

---

So: is this roughly right? Where am I fudging? Specifically tell me (a) whether the sophisticated-choice move actually blocks money pumps or just hides them one step back, (b) whether the resolute choice reading of Marta is a respectable use of the idea or a misuse, and (c) what the strongest counterargument to my notes would be — what would actually make you conclude Marta is just irrational, full stop? And please tell me where my reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your verdict. Keep it under 500 words, I'd rather have your sharpest paragraph than a lecture.
