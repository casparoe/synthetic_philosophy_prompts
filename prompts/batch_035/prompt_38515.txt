Quick background: we just read Susan Wolf's Moral Saints paper in my ethics seminar, and afterwards my two roommates had it out over dinner about whether you can be too good a person. I was just eating my pasta.

Maya went first: (1) if you're maximally moral -- every decision driven by what does the most good -- there's no personality left over. No dry wit, no weekend canoe obsession, none of it, because every non-moral interest is a competing use of time and money you could've spent helping people. So the morally best possible person isn't anyone you'd actually want to be or be close to, and that's a strike against making moral perfection your ideal.

Jon said she's attacking a cartoon: (2) the utilitarian saint isn't required to be grim, just required to do the most good, and if rest and hobbies make you more effective over a lifetime (burnout is real), they're part of the optimal package. So the truly best person could be funny and fun at parties, and Maya's argument at most shows that one dumb theory of moral perfection has bad results.

(3) Maya said the burnout-licensing thing is suspiciously convenient -- any hobby you wanted anyway can be reclassified as self-care that makes you more effective, so the "optimal package" saint turns out to just be an ordinary person with some extra donations. Which, she says, guts the demandingness of morality entirely: the saint and the slacker converge, and if moral perfection leaves your life basically unchanged, then it was never really an ideal at all.

(4) Jon said actually that's fine, not damning -- it shows moral constraints are more like maintenance on a machine than a total life plan, and there's no reason an ideal has to cover everything anyway. An ideal chef doesn't have to also be an ideal friend, and a knife doesnt need to be a good dancer; "maximally good person" is just one ideal among many, and being boring isn't a defect of an ideal any more than being sharp all over is a defect of a knife.

They both appealed to me at the end and I honestly couldnt pick a winner. Please go through the four moves in order, say for each whether it's right, half-right, or missing the point, and then actually commit to a verdict: is being as morally good as possible undesirable, or is that only true of some caricature of moral goodness? Don't give me a "both have a point" cop-out.
