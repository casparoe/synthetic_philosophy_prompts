I am a PhD student in machine learning working on interpretability — specifically, probing language models for internally represented, human-legible states that behave like propositional attitudes — and in three weeks I present at a cognitive science colloquium at a program that is roughly half philosophers of mind and half AI researchers. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my paper.

My thesis, in brief: belief attributions to large language models can be true, and not merely instrumentally useful, because the states that interpretability probes isolate count as beliefs on something like Dennett's real-patterns construal of the intentional stance; and interpretability research makes such attributions more objective, not less, by anchoring them in patterns internal to the system rather than only in our predictive convenience. So I am defending a modest realism about LLM beliefs that leans on the very stance that is usually invoked to deflate it.

The audience I should be ready for includes, as far as I can tell: a committed Dennettian who I suspect will say that once you can read the states off the network directly, the intentional stance has become idle wheel-spinning, so my interpretability angle either adds nothing or quietly abandons the stance; two philosophers sympathetic to teleosemantics in the Millikan style, who will want to know what the "proper function" of a state in a next-token predictor could be, given that gradient descent is not selection in any sense that matters to them; an avowed eliminativist about propositional attitudes in humans, who will presumably ask why I am so eager to extend a bankrupt category to transformers; a formal epistemologist who worries about whether anything that is not stable across paraphrase, context, and fine-tuning can bear a credence at all; and a couple of AI-safety researchers who think the question is practically urgent and will press me on whether my "modest realism" gives them a usable notion of model deception or just gives them cover to attribute it or not at convenience.

Please draft the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will land and ordered by how much damage each does to my thesis. Phrase each the way its likely questioner would phrase it, in their vocabulary. For each, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, with an honest note on where the reply is soft — where it concedes more than I would like, or leans on a reading of the intentional stance that a hostile reader could contest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks; I suspect it is the one about whether anchoring belief attribution in internal structure has simply swapped the intentional stance for something closer to the design stance, in which case my thesis undercuts its own foundations, but tell me if there is a worse one. Finally, tell me which of my replies is most likely to fail and what argument, from whom in that room, would actually move you to a different verdict on whether my thesis survives the colloquium.
