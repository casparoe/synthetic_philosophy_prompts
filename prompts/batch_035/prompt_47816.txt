I edit the correspondence section of a small-circulation philosophy magazine, and a recurring configuration has been nagging at me for which I have no name. Two correspondents dispute some sentence — that numbers exist, say, or that a person could have done otherwise than she did, or that a tree falling in an empty forest makes a sound. When pressed hard enough, both come to agree on all the underlying facts, and both concede that the contested sentence admits of more than one respectable reading. But instead of shaking hands and dispersing, the fight simply changes register: each insists that under the reading they care about, the dispute remains substantive, while under the reading the other cares about, it is trivial — and since each party's preferred reading is precisely the one that keeps the dispute alive, each accuses the other of dissolving the debate rather than answering it. The charge of merely arguing about words becomes itself something they argue about, and the second-order argument comes to look structurally identical to the first: the same heat, the same inability to locate common ground, the same conviction that the other side has changed the subject.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration? I'm aware there's a literature on verbal disputes, so a bare label of that sort won't help me — both of my correspondents would happily apply it to the other's position and deny it for their own, which is exactly what makes the case interesting. What I want to know is whether the standoff itself — one in which the boundary between the verbal and the substantive is contested symmetrically, so that each party's metalinguistic accusation mirrors the other's first-order assertion — has been isolated and named. If it has, please tell me what the nearest concepts are, how each of them misses this case, and where exactly the edges of the phenomenon fall: does it cover situations in which one party is simply right that the dispute is verbal, or only the symmetric ones? Does it cover disputes that eventually terminate in a shared reading, or only the chronic ones? And what distinguishes it from a plain equivocation, where the two readings never meet at all? If nothing established fits, then coin a term, define it so as to mark off the phenomenon from an ordinary verbal dispute, an ordinary substantive dispute, and mere equivocation, and say whether these standoffs should be understood as a fact about language, a fact about the world, or a fact about the disputants' stubbornness. Please answer in British English.
