Me and my friend got into a two-hour argument last night about whether you can defend democracy on "the crowd is wiser" grounds, and it ended with him reciting what he swears is the Condorcet jury theorem. His version, roughly as I remember it: 'If each voter is more likely to be right than wrong — even barely, 51% — then the probability that the majority picks the right answer goes toward 100% as you add voters. So a million mediocre voters beat a panel of experts, which is why referendums are fine on any question as long as the electorate is big enough. The catch is the independance assumption: the voters have to judge independently, so any prior discussion or shared media environment breaks the theorem. And in the end it's all academic anyway, because political questions are mostly about values, not facts, so there's no right answer for the theorem to find — which is why Condorcet himself ended up conceding that democracy has no epistemic case and rested it on equality instead.'

Something about this felt off to me, but I couldn't put my finger on where. The first sentence sounds right to me. But the "beats experts" bit and the "values not facts" bit both seemed to move way too fast, and I have a vague memory of reading somewhere that the independence assumption is subtler than "nobody talked to each other beforehand" — something about what the independence is conditional on.

Can you go through his summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a social choice theorist would accept as stated, which they'd wince at but let slide as a simplification, and which are just wrong? And then tell me whether his two conclusions actually follow from the parts that survive: (1) that big electorates beat expert panels, so referendums are fine on any question however technical; and (2) that once you notice politics is partly about values, the whole epistemic defense of majority rule collapses. I genuinely don't know where the errors are hiding, so please be more precise about the math than charitable to either of us.
