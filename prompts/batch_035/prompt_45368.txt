I teach an intro ethics course, and last week two of my TAs got into a genuinely long argument about Glaucon's ring of Gyges setup (Republic Book II, the bit where Glaucon dares Socrates to show that a just man with an invisible ring would not behave exactly like an unjust man with one). For context, Glaucon's challenge is basically: justice is only instrumentally valuable, a burden we bear for reputation and to avoid punishment, and the ring is the thought experiment that strips those away. Maya said the whole challenge is a cheat, because it smuggles in an assumption about what people care about: the ring only removes external enforcement, so if it turns out people are motivated by things like not wanting to see themselves as a cheater, the ring doesn't touch that at all, and staying just with a ring on proves nothing about self-interest, since the just man's "inner" rewards are still there. Dev said that's exactly backwards -- Glaucon's point isn't that the ring removes all reasons to be just, it's that if you need the ring's invisibility to behave, then your justice was always just fear of getting caught, and Maya's "not wanting to see yourself as a cheater" is precisely the internalized fear Glaucon is exposing. Maya countered that calling self-image a "fear" is a stacked description, and that by that move every motivation whatsoever can be redescribed as self-interest, which makes psychological egoism unfalsifiable and the challenge vacuous rather than profound.

Dev then tried a different tack: suppose we grant that some people would stay just even with the ring. Fine, but now the challenge just becomes empirical -- how many, and why -- and once it's empirical, the fact that most people cheat when anonymous (think anonymous online behavior, or the old "would you steal if guaranteed no consequences" dorm room question) suggests Glaucon was roughly right about human nature, whatever Socrates says about the nature of justice. Maya said that was equivocating between two different claims: (a) most people behave unjustly when unpunished, which is a boring psychological fact, and (b) rational agents *should* behave unjustly when unpunished, which is the actual philosophical meat, and the ring can't establish (b) because it says nothing about whether injustice damages you in ways that don't show up in your external circumstances. Dev said that last move just assumes the conclusion Socrates is supposed to prove -- that injustice harms the agent's soul -- so Maya is defending Glaucon's challenge by secretly doing Socrates' homework for him.
