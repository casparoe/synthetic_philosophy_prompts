And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"The Dutch book theorem states that if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then a bookmaker can construct a set of bets, each of which the agent regards as fair, that together guarantee the agent a net loss no matter which propositions turn out true. The idea goes back to Ramsey and de Finetti, and the key move is measuring degree of belief by betting behavior: an agent with degree of belief p in a proposition will take bets on either side of it at odds corresponding to p. Importantly, the bookie doesn't need to know anything about which propositions are actually true, only about the agent's dispositions — that's what makes the exploitation possible in every possible world. When the agent's degrees of belief violate the axioms, the bookie can buy and sell bets so that the agent loses come what may. As de Finetti argued, this means incoherent degrees of belief are inconsistent in exactly the way that believing both a conjunction and its negation is: accepting a set of bets you consider individually fair that jointly guarantee a loss is the betting analogue of holding a contradiction. So an agent whose credences are not probabilities is irrational, and this is what establishes probabilism as a norm of rationality rather than a mere mathematical fact about betting.

The diachronic version extends the strategy to updating. Teller, extending de Finetti's earlier result, showed that an agent who plans to update by any rule other than conditionalization can be Dutch-booked in the same fashion: the bookie places bets with the agent before the evidence arrives and further bets after, and the combination guarantees a loss whatever the evidence turns out to be. This shows that failing to conditionalize is irrational in exactly the same sense as having non-probabilistic credences. The only extra assumption is that the bookie knows the agent's updating plan, which is harmless, since we can imagine the plan being announced in advance.

So the mathematical theorem, plus the identification of degrees of belief with betting quotients, yields the normative conclusion: a rational agent's credences obey the probability axioms and are updated by conditionalization."
