I've spent years reading the rationalist blogs, where "luminosity" is a local coinage for the project of making your own mind legible to yourself — coming to know, on reflection, what you actually believe, want, and feel, as opposed to what your self-model says you believe, want, and feel. The line I absorbed there is that native luminosity is poor (confabulation, choice blindness, unnamed akrasia) but trainable: introspection is unreliable, not impossible. I recently discovered that Timothy Williamson used the same word in Knowledge and Its Limits (2000) for something far stronger: a condition is luminous when, whenever you are in it, you are in a position to know that you are in it. His chapter 4 argues that almost no conditions are luminous, by way of a margin-for-error principle. Fine so far; I am sympathetic to the fallibilist moral. But when I sat down to reconstruct the argument in my own words, it kept growing teeth, and I ended up with a version that seems to prove far too much. Here it is.

(1) Whenever you make a judgment about your own current mental condition — that you are in pain, that you believe p, that you feel uncertain — there are possible cases differing from your actual one by less than any discrimination you could make, in which you would make the very same judgment while the condition it reports fails to obtain. Whatever internal difference it takes for the condition to flicker, the flicker can be made smaller than your capacity to detect it.

(2) Those indiscriminable cases are close in the sense that matters for knowledge: if you know that you are in the condition, then in every close case in which you make the same judgment, the judgment is true. Knowledge cannot be lucky enough to come out false in a case you could not have told apart from your own.

(3) So you never know that you are in any mental condition of the kind you might introspectively report. At best you believe it, probably correctly.

(4) Nothing in the reasoning so far is special to introspection. For any judgment whatever, indiscriminable-and-close cases in which the judgment is false can be located near its topic. So, with equal force, the reasoning shows that you never know that the reading lamp is on, that 57 is prime, or that the close cases posited in (1) really are close.

(5) Therefore either no one knows anything — including the premises of this argument, which are then not known either — or premise (2), or whatever notion of closeness it smuggles in, has to go.

I can't accept the skeptical horn. But I also can't simply deny (2), because I believe the underlying thought that knowing requires not being wrong in nearby cases — it is roughly what stops me from counting as knowing that I locked the door when I merely always lock it. And I would rather not declare introspective judgments special by fiat, since the confabulation literature gives me no reason to think they are special in a flattering direction. Which step should I reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a principled way to stop the argument that still lets it do its original anti-luminous work — or does every diagnosis that saves my knowledge that the lamp is on also restore a decent amount of luminosity, so that the choice is really between Williamson's conclusion and the generalization in (4)?
