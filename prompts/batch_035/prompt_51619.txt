I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher of biology — someone at home in the literature on function ascription (the selected-effect and causal-role traditions, and the debate between them), and also comfortable with the empirical side: molecular evolution, exaptation, gene recruitment. Please think the problem through carefully before answering; begin by brainstorming the possible escape routes from the argument below, then commit to a diagnosis and defend it.

My study group has been arguing about functions, and a friend has produced an argument whose conclusion — that the whole selected-effect/causal-role dispute is verbal — seems to prove too much. Yet each step looks defensible to me, and I can't name the exact point where it goes wrong. Here it is:

(1) On the etiological or selected-effect account, the function of a trait is the effect of past tokens of that trait that explains, through natural selection, why the trait is present in the population today.
(2) On the causal-role account, the function of a trait is whatever the trait does that contributes to some capacity of a containing system, where the capacity in question is whatever the biologist is trying to analyse.
(3) But the containing-system capacities that can earn a trait a function in the first place are those that explain the system's own persistence and proliferation — capacities irrelevant to staying and spreading don't ground functions, or the account would hand out functions for making heart sounds.
(4) A contribution to a capacity that explains a system's persistence and proliferation just is a contribution to fitness.
(5) The effects of a trait that contribute to fitness are exactly the effects that selection accumulates, so the effects that explain why the trait is present today are exactly the fitness contributions.
(6) Therefore, wherever both accounts deliver a verdict, they deliver the same one: selected effect and causal-role contribution coincide in extension, and the dispute between the two camps is a disagreement about a word.

For context, the cases we keep coming back to: antifreeze glycoproteins in Antarctic notothenioid fish, which on the standard account were cobbled together from a pancreatic trypsinogen-like gene before selection could possibly have favoured them for freezing-point depression; and duck lenses, whose major structural protein is essentially argininosuccinate lyase, a urea-cycle enzyme doing double duty as a crystallin — so the very same protein looks like it has one function on one account and a different one on the other. My friend says these don't threaten (6), since by the time biologists are studying the trait, selection has caught up and the two accounts re-converge. I'd rather not accept (6) — it seems to erase something both camps think they're fighting about — but if I have to sacrifice something, I honestly don't know whether the culprit is (3), which looks like it saves causal-role accounts from a real embarrassment, or (5), which looks like a truism about how selection works.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If more than one step is independently suspect, tell me which rejection does the real work. And if you think the argument is valid and (6) is simply true, say so and explain why the debate nonetheless refuses to die — but I'd want to know what a defender of either camp actually believes that the other denies, on your reading.
