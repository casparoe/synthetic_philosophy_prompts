I am preparing a seminar on chapter XXV of The Prince (Marriott's translation, "What Fortune Can Effect in Human Affairs and How to Withstand Her"), where Machiavelli concedes that fortune governs much of life but insists, "not to extinguish our free will," that "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less." He argues for this by analogy: "I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings ... and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous." One of my colleagues treats this as the load-bearing move of the chapter: on the river picture, fortune just is a stationary natural hazard — a distribution of shocks of fixed character — and virtù is civil engineering: you cannot stop the flood, but you can build dikes in calm weather and channel the next inundation. On this reading, the material later in the chapter about conforming one's method to the spirit of the times is a practical corollary: study the hazard profile of your era and prepare accordingly.

I suspect this reading rests on the river far more heavily than the river can bear, and I would like a proper audit of the analogy. Please begin with the correspondence table: what in the target is supposed to play the role of the flood, the fair weather, the dikes and barriers, the canal, the inundated plain, and the unbarriered country (Machiavelli's Italy being, in his words, "an open country without barriers and without any defence"). Then set out where the mapping breaks. I can see at least two candidate ruptures, and I want them weighed rather than merely listed. First, within Machiavelli's own sentences fortune is not indifferent the way water is: he says she "turns her forces" where she knows defences have not been raised, which makes her something like a strategic opponent probing for weak points, not a blind mass — the source domain quietly imports indifference while the target, in Machiavelli's own phrasing, imports intent. Second, rivers are periodic and more or less stationary: a flood regime you can study across generations, and against which dikes built in one calm season serve the next. But the later paragraphs make success turn on fitting the spirit of the times, which shifts in a way that ruins even the man who has always prospered by a single method and cannot change; and the chapter closes by recasting fortune as a woman who yields to the impetuous rather than to the careful — which is not anything a dike can be built against, and which seems to change the target object mid-chapter from a climate to an audience. For each rupture, tell me whether it matters for the engineer's use of the analogy or is a mere ornament, and say whether the "arbiter of one-half" conclusion — that fortune leaves half or a little less of our affairs to prudence — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the river the whole time. If the river cannot support the role the rest of the chapter assigns to fortune, propose the nearest analogy that can, and state what that replacement costs the engineering reading. I would rather have the audit sharpen my colleague's thesis than bury it, so if some version of the dikes-in-calm-weather picture can be rescued, show me which one and at what price.
