I spent thirty-five years as a research physiologist, the last twenty of them studying renal tubular transport, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of biology literature. The debate between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function strikes me as peculiar, and I would like a diagnosis of it rather than another rehearsal of the positions.

Here is the dispute as I understand it. The etiological account holds that the function of a trait is what it was selected for — the effect of ancestral versions of the trait that explains why it is here now. The causal-role account holds that the function of a component is the contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system, picked out in advance by the analyst. The standard observation is that they diverge on a handful of cases: hearts make noise and pump blood, and only pumping is the function; a trait co-opted from a different ancestral use has its current role as its function on the causal-role account but its ancestral effect on the etiological one; vestigial structures and accidental by-products get functions on one account and not the other. In the laboratory, my colleagues and I used the word "function" constantly, and I cannot recall a single occasion on which we needed to decide between these accounts. We said the function of the Na+/K+-ATPase is to maintain the sodium gradient; we said the function of the countercurrent multiplier is to concentrate urine; and if a postdoc had asked whether we meant selected effect or causal role, the honest answer would have been that the question did not attach to anything we were doing. That may be mere sloppiness, or it may be a clue.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the two camps are answering different questions under one word. The etiological account answers a question about why a trait is present — a question from evolutionary biology — while the causal-role account answers a question about how a system works — a question from physiology and molecular biology. On this view the debate is verbal, and the appearance of substantive disagreement is manufactured by the divergent cases, which are exactly the cases neither discipline cares about: nobody's research program turns on whether a vestigial organ has a function. But I can see an alternative diagnosis that fits the same facts: the debate is substantive but stalled because it is really a proxy war over whether teleology can be naturalized once and for all, so each side needs a single, unified account of function, and the refusal to pluralize is the actual point at issue. And there is a third possibility I find uncomfortable: that both accounts were tailored to a mid-century picture of biology — one trait, one selection pressure, one clear system capacity — and that modern systems biology, with its network explanations, degenerate and redeployed pathways, and multi-level selection, has quietly outgrown both, so the debate stalls because the disputants are analyzing a concept that no longer matches the practice.

Please diagnose the disagreement: verbal or substantive; a difference about facts, about what a concept is for, or about background commitments to naturalized teleology; and whether the pluralist settlement some philosophers propose (use each account in its home domain) is a resolution, a capitulation, or a refusal to answer the interesting question. Since you will not be able to consult sources, argue from the structure of the positions rather than from the literature's history. I would also like a candid answer about what progress would even look like here: a decisive case, a conceptual distinction, an empirical development in biology that would settle or dissolve the question — or an argument that a forty-year impasse between sophisticated people who agree on the science is itself evidence that the question was ill-posed. As a former bench scientist, I am especially interested in whether you think the day-to-day indifference of working biologists to the dispute counts as data for the verbal-disagreement diagnosis, or whether it merely shows that practicing scientists can tolerate conceptual ambiguity that philosophers, for good reasons, cannot.
