I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and for the last few years a chunk of my beat has been machine consciousness — AI welfare, whether chatbots can suffer, that whole emerging mess. What I keep running into, and what my editor keeps cutting for space, is the fight underneath it: panpsychism. On one side, people like Philip Goff who say consciousness has to be taken as fundamental and woven into the physical world at the bottom — the intrinsic nature of matter is, or might be, experiential. On the other, the physicalists and especially the illusionists, Keith Frankish types, who say the panpsychists have just relocated the hard problem rather than solved it, and dressed up a failure of explanation as a positive theory. This argument has been running in basically the same shape for a couple of decades now, in journals, edited volumes, and an unending drip of very similar op-eds, and as far as I can tell nobody on either side has budged an inch in all that time.

Here's where I think it actually sticks, and why I suspect the usual framing ("is consciousness fundamental?") is not the real question. The panpsychists have the combination problem, fair enough. But there's a mirror version that gets less airtime: even if you grant micro-experience, how would anything at the level of particles be relevant to the things we actually care about — how could something that doesn't remember, compare, or report be a locus of anything worth calling suffering? When I push panpsychists on this, they tend to say that's an empirical question for later; when I push the critics, they say that any version of the view filled in enough to answer it stops being panpsychism and becomes plain psychology. Meanwhile the moral stakes keep getting invoked: if experience goes all the way down, maybe suffering does too, and dismissing the view is a gamble with something enormous; the critics answer that this is a guilt trip, not an argument — you can't conjure moral seriousness by refusing to say what the morally serious thing would even be. And crucially, no observation, no experiment, no conceivable result splits the two camps. So my working hypothesis, as a journalist who has watched this longer than she'd like: the disagreement is at bottom verbal, but each side experiences it as substantive, because the word "experience" is doing two jobs at once — the ordinary thing (the one that's like something when you're awake) and a technical placeholder for whatever intrinsic nature physics leaves out. The sides are attached to different jobs while thinking they're fighting over the same word.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey — I've read the positions, I don't need them restated. So: is this actually verbal, or is it a genuine substantive dispute that just happens to be empirically idle, or a clash over standards of evidence (inference to the best explanation versus a demand for conceptual legibility)? What kind of progress, if any, is even available — a distinction, a reformulated question, something — and is it telling that the only people who've changed their minds on this did so for reasons that look nothing like the published arguments? And since my name goes on whatever I print: tell me what would change your diagnosis, and be blunt about where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail. I'd much rather run a verdict with its error bars attached than another confident piece that pretends this fight is going somewhere.
