I edit a small philosophy blog with a readership of graduate students and ambitious autodidacts, and I want to build a "which theory of persistence do you actually hold?" quiz. The occasion: last Saturday a friend and I argued for the better part of five hours about whether ordinary objects persist by being wholly present at every moment of their existence, or by being spread out in time with temporal parts the way they are spread out in space. The argument went badly, and I finally understand why: we kept trading slogans — "the very same thing, wholly present," "you're a four-dimensional worm" — while the real disagreements lived in cases neither of us stated cleanly. He would bite a bullet on one case and refuse the same bullet on another, and neither of us noticed until the end. So I want a sorting instrument that locates people by what they say about cases, not by which label they claim.

Please write the quiz as follows. Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options phrased as plain claims, so that someone can answer without knowing what endurantism, perdurantism, or stage theory are, and no question may contain the phrases "temporal parts," "wholly present," or any other term of art. The cases must include at least: something with incompatible properties at different times (a poker straight on Monday and bent on Tuesday, and what exactly has the bending); a fission case (an amoeba dividing, or a person's hemisphere transplants if you prefer); a case of gradual material replacement; and a counting case, where the theories disagree about how many things are in the room or on the mat. Beyond those, choose cases you think do the most diagnostic work.

Then give the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto: perdurantist worm theory; stage theory with its exdurantist account of how a stage gains the past-tensed properties of its predecessors; and endurantism, split into its three standard responses to the problem of temporary intrinsics — the one that relativizes having a property to a time, the one that says properties are had timelessly and the changing thing stands in different relations to them, and the one that reinterprets the property itself as tensed or time-indexed in how it is instantiated. If you think a deflationary or no-fact-of-the-matter leaf belongs on the tree, include it and say which answer pattern lands there.

Two further requirements. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: state the questions the cases are really asking, which combinations of answers they permit, and then locate the named positions in that space. Where a combination of answers is coherent but, as far as you know, unoccupied, say so and guess why — is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Second, be honest about which leaves your instrument cannot separate: if no case you can pose in a blog quiz pulls apart, say, the stage theorist from the worm theorist who talks loosely, then say that, rather than pretending a question did the work. The audience is smart but has never read the primary literature, so every case must be comprehensible to someone whose last metaphysics course was an intro survey.
