A few of us in a long-running reading group -- mostly longtime LessWrong and Slate Star Codex readers -- have been working through Moloch-adjacent material again, and this time we picked the Anglo-German dreadnought race of roughly 1906 to 1914 as our test case. On the standard rationalist telling, this looks like a textbook multipolar trap: Britain maintained a "two-power standard" (a fleet at least as large as the next two navies combined), Germany under Tirpitz built a "risk fleet" intended to make war with Germany too costly for Britain to contemplate, HMS Dreadnought's launch in 1906 reset everyone's capital-ship count to roughly zero and kicked off a building spiral, and both publics ended up paying enormous sums for weapons that arguably made both countries less secure. Nobody, on this telling, wanted the race as such; each government was simply responding to the other's moves, and from a god's-eye view both would have preferred a stable, cheaper equilibrium. The Hague peace conferences of 1899 and 1907, which tried and failed to limit armaments, look like the failed coordination mechanism, and the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922 -- which actually did freeze capital-ship ratios (5:5:3 for Britain, the US, and Japan, with smaller shares for France and Italy) -- looks like the escape that came a war too late.

I'd like you to analyze the race through the Moloch/multipolar-trap lens, but I want the mapping done carefully rather than gestured at. Who exactly are the "players" -- is this really a multipolar situation or effectively a two-player game with spectators, and does that distinction break the Moloch frame or just modify it? What were the actual moves and payoffs, and in particular which participants actually *liked* the race (Tirpitz's fleet-building was entangled with domestic German politics and industrial interests, and British shipbuilding had powerful constituencies too)? Where the classic Moloch story requires that everyone hate the equilibrium, does the dreadnought race satisfy that condition, or were some of the actors roughly optimizing for the race itself? Also assess the "reset" logic of Dreadnought: is an innovation that zeroes out accumulated advantage a Molochian mechanism, or something the framework doesn't really have a slot for?

Then run the analysis in the other direction and tell me what the episode does to the theory. If the Washington Naval Treaty succeeded where the Hague conferences failed, what does that suggest about the escape conditions for multipolar traps -- did it take a hegemon with side payments to offer, an external catastrophe that made coordination salient, capital ships that were unusually easy to count and verify, or some combination? And honestly flag where the Moloch frame is silent or misleading here: does the eventual outbreak of war show the trap was never really about mutual ruin through overinvestment but about something Moloch doesn't describe, or does it show the trap working precisely as advertised? I know the naval history reasonably well, so don't hesitate to be technical about the game theory; what I want is a disciplined case analysis that credits the framework where it earns it and demotes it where it doesn't.
