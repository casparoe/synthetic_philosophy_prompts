I am a high school senior writing my extended essay for an English-philosophy seminar, and I would appreciate help developing a reading of Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde through the idea of Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment. My philosophy teacher explained the concept using Odysseus ordering his sailors to bind him to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens but not steer toward them, and then mentioned that the same logic appears in constitutional law, where a society ties its own hands in advance (against a future self it no longer trusts) through entrenched rights and amendment procedures. It struck me that Jekyll does something strangely similar before his transformation ever becomes involuntary: he rewrites his will so that if he disappears or dies, everything passes to Edward Hyde; he sets up the back door and the servants; he seals the confession for Utterson to open only after his death or disappearance. These look like institutional arrangements for a future self he cannot trust, almost like a one-man constitution.

What I want to understand, and would like you to work through with me, is where the analogy genuinely holds and where it breaks down. In the classic Ulysses case, the bound man cannot act on his altered preferences, and the binding works because someone else (the sailors, the courts, the guardians) enforces it. But Jekyll's arrangements do not actually prevent Hyde from doing anything: they smooth his path, guarantee him property and a house, and seem more like a discrediting of the very idea of precommitment. Is Stevenson showing that self-binding by a divided agent is impossible in principle, because the agent who wants to bind is the same agent who can unbind — Jekyll never loses access to the drug or the freedom to stop consulting his own rules? Or is the point subtler, that Jekyll's precommitments fail precisely because they are designed by the wrong party, the tempted self rather than an external authority like Utterson, who is arguably the true mast-and-rope figure and yet constitutionally disabled by his discretion and loyalty? I would also like to know whether you think the ending — Poole and Utterson breaking down the cabinet door, and the sealed letters finally being read — resembles the emergency provisions by which a constitution can be suspended or overridden, and whether the novella takes a position on whether precommitment is a protection of the self or a betrayal of the future person's rights to make his own choices.

I do not need quotations from the text, since I have my copy; what I need is a rigorous reading that takes both the plot mechanics of Stevenson's story and the precommitment debate seriously, acknowledges where the novella resists being reduced to an illustration (for instance, that Hyde is not merely temptation but a distinct center of consciousness with his own memories and projects), and ends by saying what Stevenson's story might add to the philosophical debate that abstract arguments about Ulysses contracts cannot. My essay is due in three weeks, so a structured analysis I can argue with, rather than a summary, would be most helpful.
