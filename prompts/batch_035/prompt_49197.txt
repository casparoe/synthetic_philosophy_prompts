In my metaethics seminar, the professor defends moral relativism by way of an analogy to predicates of personal taste. Her case runs like this. When I say that cilantro tastes soapy and disgusting, while you say it tastes fresh and pleasant, we are both speaking truly — each relative to our own standard of taste — and what looks like a disagreement is really just a difference; there is no single proposition one of us gets wrong. She then says moral judgments work exactly the same way: when I say eating meat is wrong and you say it is permissible, we are both right relative to our respective moral codes, the apparent dispute is merely verbal, and neither of us is in a position to correct the other. She extends the analogy to moral change: moral progress, such as the abolition of slavery, is like a shift in fashion — hemlines rise and fall, standards change, and nothing is getting closer to a truth that was there all along. Since philosophers of language sometimes call the taste phenomenon "faultless disagreement," she concludes that moral disagreement is faultless in the same sense, and that arguing about ethics is in the end no more substantive than arguing about whether anchovies are tasty.
