At a public-art committee meeting last week, our vote over two finalist sculptures stalled when a fellow committee member shut down my argument with the proverb that beauty is in the eye of the beholder — as if the fact that people respond differently settles that there is nothing further to discuss about which piece is better. I suspect the sentence is compressing several very different claims, only some of which would license the use she was making of it. Disentangle the readings for me.

Spell out the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing, ordered roughly from weakest to strongest: the near-trivial psychological observation that aesthetic responses vary across perceivers; the claim that aesthetic disagreement cannot be resolved by argument but only by further exposure or experience; the claim that there is no fact of the matter about which of two works is better, so disagreement is never disagreement about anything; and full-blown error-theoretic or non-cognitivist views on which aesthetic judgments state no truth-evaluable propositions at all. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude everyone accepts, a substantive and contestable philosophical position, or simply false, and — this is the part I need for the next meeting — whether it actually supports refusing to adjudicate between the two proposals rather than merely explaining why adjudication is hard.

Two further things I want woven in. First, I have read enough to know that Hume is usually brought in on exactly this question, and the way he is brought in confuses me. In his essay Of the Standard of Taste he seems to accept something like the weak reading — beauty is not a property residing in objects independent of human response — and yet he is famous for arguing that some judges are better qualified than others, and that the joint verdict of qualified critics over time supplies a genuine standard by which taste can be corrected. Is that combination coherent, or is the appeal to qualified judges an illicit slide from the fact that some preferences are more informed to the conclusion that some verdicts are truer? Second, my colleague's deeper worry, which I think is doing more work than the proverb itself, is that any standard of taste is just the tastes of socially dominant people dressed up as objectivity — so the exclusionary history of art institutions makes the appeal to better judges politically suspect rather than merely philosophically shaky.

Set the readings out as an ordered list I can bring to the meeting, with a verdict on each: trivial, defensible, false, or defensible but beside the point. Mark clearly which readings would actually support my colleague's refusal to deliberate, which would instead merely reframe what the deliberation should be about, and which would be consistent with my position that one sculpture is simply better made.
