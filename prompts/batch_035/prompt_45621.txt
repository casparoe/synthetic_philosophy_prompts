Ok so I just spent like three hours fighting with my roommate about moral worth and I need someone to tell me if the position I ended up defending is actually defensible or if I talked myself into a corner.

The standard line, basically Kant, is that an act has moral worth only when you do it *because* it's the right thing to do — acting for the right reasons, where the right reason is the moral fact itself (or the duty, whatever you want to call it). My roommate was fine with that. The thing we actually fought about was this twist I put on it: I claimed that what matters isn't just what moved you in the actual situation, but how robust that motivation is across nearby counterfactual situations. So when you help someone because a friend you trust told you it was the right move, and the friend happens to be right — the standard view gets stuck, since you weren't moved by the rightness itself, just by testimony. My version says: you get moral worth anyway *if* you would have caught the mistake and done the right thing had the friend told you something wrong instead. It's the reliability of your motivational setup that carries the moral worth, not the actual causal chain. Basically you earn credit for having a character that tracks right reasons, even when on this occasion the tracking happens through a shortcut.

I liked this because it handles the testimony case and it also handles the objection that nobody literally acts *from* duty in the pure sense — there's always some mix of sympathy, habit, whatever, and the robustness thing lets all of that count as long as it would have steered you right in the nearby worlds where things go differently. My roommate's pushback was that I've quietly changed the subject from moral worth of the act to something like praiseworthiness of the person, and that once I do that I can no longer say *this particular action* was worthy, which was the whole point. Also she said the nearby worlds thing is doing all the work and nobody can agree on which worlds count as nearby, so I can always gerrymander the modal base to get whatever verdict I want — which she says means my view isn't risking anything and that should bother me.

So: what would actually count against this view? Not just her two objections — I want the full map. What cases, what arguments, what results from neighboring debates (like whether moral worth attaches to acts or agents, or how we individuate motives) does it stand or fall with? Which of these would a committed defender concede as counting against it, and which would they shrug off? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest with me about the gerrymandering worry — is the vagueness in the counterfactuals a real problem, or is that just the kind of vagueness every view in this area lives with? I guess I most want to know whether I've made the view stronger or just made it so flexible it can't lose.
