I write a column on science and ideas for a Sunday broadsheet, and I'm preparing a piece on how scholarly debates come to a halt without anyone declaring a victor. My case study is the long argument over two-dimensional semantics in philosophy of language. My sense of the terrain, gathered over years of desultory reading: the apparatus itself was never really in dispute. Nearly everyone accepted the formal move of indexing semantic evaluation twice — Kaplan's distinction between character and content being the template — and then Kripke's examples of the necessary a posteriori were reanalysed with scenarios in one coordinate and worlds in the other. The fight started when the apparatus was put to epistemic work. On one side, roughly Chalmers and Jackson, the primary intension or epistemic intension was treated as a genuine semantic value in its own right, something that could model what a speaker grasps in advance of empirical discovery, and could therefore underwrite claims about the relationship between conceivability, a priori entailment, and possibility. On the other, roughly Stalnaker and Soames, the two-dimensional machinery was allowed as a device for modelling context and assertion — diagonal propositions, what is common ground between parties who misdescribe each other's worlds — but was accused of smuggling cognition into semantics when it was asked to do the epistemic work: the complaint, as I understood it, was that either the primary intension is a semantic value, in which case it cannot explain the a priori, or it is a cognitive notion, in which case it cannot be a meaning.

To my amateur eye the debate ran out of motion sometime in the 2000s and has never restarted, even though everyone involved is still publishing. I have a hypothesis I'd like tested, and I want it taken seriously as a hypothesis rather than flattered or dismissed out of politeness. It is this: the two sides agree on every formal fact and disagree only about what the primary intension is *for* — whether it is part of the semantics, a meta-semantic claim about how reference is fixed, or a psychological claim about concepts — and because that question was never cleanly separated from the applications, the whole thing became unfalsifiable from the inside. The applications, meanwhile, detached and went their own way: people who wanted the conceivability argument about consciousness used the epistemic machinery as a premise-supplier regardless of what Stalnaker thought of it, and people who didn't, didn't. The formalism survived; the debate about its interpretation lost its customers.

So here is what I want from you, and please write your answer in British English. Diagnose the disagreement: was it substantive, verbal (a dispute about the word 'meaning' or 'proposition'), two different projects wearing one formalism, or a genuine meta-semantic question that simply went unanswered? Support the diagnosis from what the parties actually claimed rather than from textbook labels — I want to know whether Stalnaker's complaint, for instance, is that epistemic two-dimensionalism is false, or that it changes the subject, and whether those are even different complaints in this case. Then tell me whether my stall-hypothesis holds: did the debate die because it was unanswerable, because it was answered and nobody noticed, or because its audience got what it needed and left? Finally, say what would have counted as progress, or would still count as progress: a compositional semantics delivered on epistemic two-dimensionalist terms, a theorem about the limits of the diagonal, a cognitive model that vindicates or destroys the idea of an epistemic intension, or simply an agreed division of labour between semantic and epistemic vocabulary. If you think the honest answer is that nothing short of a change of fashion would have moved it, say so and explain why.
