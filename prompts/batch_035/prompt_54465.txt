I'm a former market-microstructure researcher (PhD in financial economics, now doing statistical arbitrage at a quant fund) who recently got pulled into an AI-safety reading group at work, and last month we worked through the 2016 Logical Induction paper by Garrabrant and collaborators. The setup, as I understand it: an algorithm's degrees of belief in mathematical sentences are identified with prices in a stylized market where each sentence is a share that pays a dollar if it turns out provable, traders with bounded budgets buy and sell over time, and the resulting price sequence counts as rational precisely when no trader can exploit it for unbounded returns. The authors are upfront that the entire construction is motivated by stock-trading analogies, which made me feel oddly at home — and also suspicious, because I know how much ritual and how many assumptions hide behind the word "market" in my own field. My two reading-group colleagues have split in a way I find frustrating. The type theorist says the market framing is decoration: real markets aggregate dispersed private information, whereas in arithmetic there is no private information, just one big fact of the matter disclosed on a schedule by a slow deductive process, so market efficiency here is at best a computability fact wearing a Wall Street costume. My quant colleague says the opposite: prices as credences, arbitrage as incoherence, efficiency as rationality, so logical uncertainty is just empirical uncertainty in a funny hat and the whole epistemology of markets transfers wholesale.

Rather than adjudicate between them by vibes, I'd like the analogy actually audited. Please give me the correspondence table — trader, market maker, budget, share, settlement date, liquidity, arbitrage, efficiency — and then separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the epistemological conclusions from those that are harmless idealizations. In particular: (a) what does the market picture quietly import from real financial markets that this setting lacks — dispersed information, risk aversion, an underlying good whose value exists prior to any trading, settlement at a determinate time — and which of those imports is carrying a conclusion it can't actually support? (b) Does the criterion that the market can't be exploited in the limit genuinely earn the title of rationality for beliefs about unproven sentences, or is it just one desideratum among many that happens to be easy to state in market vocabulary? And (c) is my quant colleague right that there's nothing left over — no residue about mathematical uncertainty that the market framework cannot express — or does the type theorist's complaint point at something real, namely that the only thing doing the epistemic work is the deductive process playing the role of a slow news feed? If the market is ultimately the wrong financial metaphor, I'd like your considered view on what the better one would be; my own suspicion, as someone who spent years studying specialists, is that it's closer to a market maker forced to quote two-sided prices continuously while receiving news too slowly to process it, but I'm not confident that framing helps or just relocates the confusion.
