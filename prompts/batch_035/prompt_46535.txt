I'm a PhD student in archaeology writing a dissertation chapter on the late Classic Maya, and for the past year I've been circling a debate that I've come to suspect is stalled for reasons that have little to do with evidence. On one side sits the popular collapse literature, exemplified by Jared Diamond's account of Easter Island as self-inflicted ecocide: a society that cut down its palms, destroyed its own resource base, and destroyed itself. Against it stand the archaeologists of transformation, who point out that the deforestation story is far less tidy than Diamond told it — palm nuts gnawed by introduced rats, no sign of a dramatic population crash before European contact — and that in the Classic Maya case, the "collapse" of the ninth century was a collapse of divine kingship and the southern lowland cities, while millions of people went on living there and the northern cities flourished. My field's review essays and conference Q&As rehearse this exchange over and over, and nobody has moved in a decade. Both sides agree that palace systems ended and populations persisted; both agree that "collapse" is doing rhetorical work for popular audiences; and yet the argument never resolves.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to take as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the stall is not empirical at all. It may be a disguised normative disagreement about the baseline: if the unit that matters is the political economy of the elite — palaces, monumental construction, dynastic inscriptions — then these episodes are failures; if the unit is the ordinary household, they are reorganizations, and calling them collapses smuggles in an aristocratic standard of what a society is for. On that diagnosis, the two camps are answering different questions under one word, and the way forward is not more fieldwork but an explicit decision about which reference class a theory of collapse is a theory of. But I can also see a competing diagnosis: that there is a genuine, answerable empirical question being obscured — whether the post-palatial settlement systems were poorer, less healthy, or less secure than the palatial ones, in which case the transformationists are minimizing real losses and the debate is substantive after all. There is even a third possibility, that it is partly verbal in the boring way, with the camps merely reserving "collapse" for different thresholds of population decline or institutional loss.

Please diagnose which of these — or some further alternative you think fits better — the disagreement really is, using what each side actually claims rather than a caricature of either. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a settlement-data synthesis, a reframed question, or an argument that the impasse is permanent and why. Throughout, please flag how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the point you are least sure of — I would rather know where the diagnosis rests on thin ice than receive it as settled verdict.
