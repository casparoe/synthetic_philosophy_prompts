Fair warning up front: I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background, I've just been reading some later Wittgenstein (the Philosophical Investigations) in the evenings. I keep getting the feeling that his private language argument is basically obvious to anyone who's maintained a codebase for a few years, and that feeling makes me suspicious of myself. So: translate the argument into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, then tell me what didn't survive the translation.

Here's the argument as I currently understand it, stated my way. Wittgenstein asks whether there could be a language only one person can in principle understand — a language for his own private inner sensations, where the words get their meaning purely from being attached to those sensations from the inside. His test case is a diary: someone decides to mark down a sign every time a particular sensation occurs, intending the sign to name just that sensation. Wittgenstein says this can't work, because there's no such thing as using the sign correctly or incorrectly — nothing distinguishes getting it right from merely feeling like you got it right. And if whatever seems right is right, then right doesn't mean anything anymore. Correctness needs a public criterion. If I've already mangled that, tell me.

Now do the translation, piece by piece, and be honest about each:

1. Private fields and encapsulation. On the face of it this is exactly his target — state only the owning object can see, no external access, meaning fixed from the inside. But I suspect it's a mismatch: private fields are private by convention and enforcement, not in principle. Anyone with a debugger or the source can inspect them, and the field's meaning is partly fixed by how the rest of the program, written by other people, uses it. So is encapsulation a counterexample to Wittgenstein, or a case he'd say isn't private in his sense at all — and if the latter, what exactly is the difference between hidden-by-convention and logically private? That distinction is the thing I'm least confident I actually grasp.

2. The diary case as a logging convention with no reader and no test. A dev writes to a log under a tag only he understands, and there's no spec, no consumer, no assertion anywhere that could ever fail. That seems like the closest software analogue to the diary — but it also seems like the log entry still means something to him a week later. What is Wittgenstein's actual reason for thinking the later re-identification fails? Is it about memory being unreliable, or about something else? This is where my translation feels shakiest, so don't gloss over it.

3. Rule-following as running code. He says following a rule is a practice, and that thinking you're obeying the rule isn't obeying it. My instinct is: sure, that's just the difference between a spec someone else can hold you to and code only ever read by its author. But then all the interesting cases in software are ones where the public is tiny or historical — a one-person project, a dead language, a codebase where the last person who understood a module retired years ago. Do those count as public in his sense, and if the criterion is something like available to others in principle, how is that not just the encapsulation point from item 1 wearing a different hat?

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Sort the argument's parts into: (a) translates cleanly and loses nothing, (b) translates only if I smuggle in something from the software side that Wittgenstein would never grant, and (c) has no expression in my vocabulary at all. For (c), give me a view on wether the residue points to something my vocabulary can't say — the felt, first-personal side of sensation, maybe? — or to Wittgenstein being less clear than he looks. And be blunt if some of what survives is just a platitude about specs versus code dressed up in fancy language; I'd genuinely rather know. Same goes for the reverse: if the argument really is just obvious to anyone who's maintained a codebase, say that plainly — I don't want to be smug about a translation that's quietly missing his point.
