I'm the TA for a second-semester game theory course, and next week I'm running the discussion section on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, including the wrinkle that a strategy can be dominated only by a mixed strategy. The lecture will already have covered pure-strategy dominance, so students will arrive believing that elimination is just a matter of scanning for rows (or columns) that lose entry-by-entry to some other single row, and the mixed-dominance point will land as an afterthought if I don't structure things carefully. I have one fifty-minute section and a whiteboard. Please plan it for me rather than explaining the material yourself.

Here is what I'd like you to work out. First, the dependency structure: what has to be established before the mixed-dominance step can make sense, and in what order — for instance, whether students need the formal definition of strict dominance as an inequality against every opponent strategy before they see a mixture do the work, and whether I should get the full iterated-deletion procedure running on an easy matrix first and only then spring the mixture on them. Second, the carrying example: my instinct is a 3x3 game where, against opponent columns L, C, and R, the row player's strategies T, M, and B pay (3, 0, 1), (0, 3, 1), and (1, 1, 0.5) respectively, so that no single row beats B entrywise but the equal mixture of T and M does; but I'm not sure whether this example is well chosen, whether it should be extended so that a second round of elimination becomes possible, and whether it's better than alternatives you can construct — tell me what the carrying example must have and lack, and either fix mine or replace it. Third, the misconception map: I can already predict some errors (students thinking that a mixed strategy dominating B means anyone would actually randomize between T and M in play; students assuming the order of elimination can't matter; students confusing "not dominated" with "rational to play"; students assuming iterated elimination only needs each player to be rational rather than rationality being commonly known), but I don't know where in the sequence each should be headed off, and I suspect there are others I haven't anticipated. Finally, suggest one question I could pose at the end of the section that would reveal whether they genuinely understand why the mixture dominates B — as opposed to having just watched me do arithmetic on the whiteboard — and identify what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (weak dominance and its order-dependence? the epistemic foundations literature? the relationship to rationalizability?) and say so to the students.
