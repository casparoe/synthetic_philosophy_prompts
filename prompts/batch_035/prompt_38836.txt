I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on Nietzsche's thought experiment of eternal recurrence: the demon who finds a person in their loneliest solitude and announces that this life, exactly as it has been lived — every pain, every hesitation, every trivial and contemptible moment included, nothing new added — must return innumerable times, in the same sequence, forever; and that the person hearing this, and every small and great thing in that life, will return to it. For the chapter in which my protagonist first takes the thought seriously, I would like a sestina.

A few requirements. First, the form should do the philosophical work: the six end-words come back in a fixed rotation, the same elements returning in every stanza but in changed combinations and contexts, and I want that return to enact the doctrine rather than merely decorate it — the reader should feel that nothing new is being added and yet that each recurrence is not quite the same, which is precisely the question my protagonist faces about her own life. Second, at least two of the six end-words should be deliberately mundane — a kitchen implement, a bus, a pill organizer — because the cruelty of the thought lies less in its great pains than in its demand that one affirm the trivial and the shabby, and I want the poem to refuse to ennoble them. Third, the envoi should arrive at the fork the announcement creates: despair that grinds one down, or the wish for nothing more fiercely than this exact life again. But do not resolve it into serene acceptance; my protagonist has not decided, and the poem must not decide for her.

Please do not mention Nietzsche by name. Someone who knows the doctrine — its cosmological version, in which time is finite and repeats, as much as its use as a test of whether one can affirm one's life — should recognize it exactly; someone who does not should simply find it a poem worth carrying around afterward.
