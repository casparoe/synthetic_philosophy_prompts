I'm taking a philosophy of science course, and we've just finished the unit on underdetermination of theory by evidence — the Duhem–Quine idea that because tests always confront a theory only in conjunction with auxiliary assumptions, a finite body of evidence is compatible with multiple incompatible theoretical packages, and there is no purely logical fact that forces us toward one of them. The seminar treated this mostly in the abstract (curves through data points, razor-wielding simplicity mongers), and I keep thinking there's a nineteenth-century astronomical episode that would make a much better test case. I'd like you to analyze it through the underdetermination lens, in both directions: the theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory.

Here are the facts as I understand them, so we share a baseline and don't depend on contested details. Uranus was discovered by Herschel in 1781, and by the 1840s its observed positions deviated measurably from the Newtonian tables. Rather than doubt gravity, Urbain Le Verrier (and, independently, John Couch Adams in Cambridge) posited an unknown outer planet perturbing the orbit, computed where it should be, and on the night of 23–24 September 1846 Johann Galle found Neptune at the Berlin Observatory within about a degree of the predicted position — Arago famously said Le Verrier had discovered a planet "with the point of his pen." I've also read that this triumph was partly luck: both calculators had baked in a wrong assumed distance for the planet, loosely via the Titius–Bode law, and the real Neptune's orbit turned out to differ substantially from theirs, with the predicted sky position happening to land near the mark anyway. Thirteen years later, the same Le Verrier confronted a much smaller anomaly: Mercury's perihelion was advancing by roughly 38 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian perturbations from the known planets could explain. By exactly parallel reasoning he posited another unseen body, Vulcan, inside Mercury's orbit. Astronomers chased it for half a century — occasional claimed sightings, nothing that held up — until 1915, when Einstein showed that general relativity accounts for the anomalous precession (about 43 arcseconds per century in the later, refined figures) with no new planet at all.

So here is the analysis I want. First, do the mapping carefully: at each stage, what exactly is the "theory" whose evidential support is in question — Newtonian gravity as such, or a package of gravity plus auxiliaries about the inventory of the solar system, the reliability of the observations, and the reach of the inverse-square law? On that mapping, is the Neptune episode a textbook Duhemian repair — a core hypothesis rescued by adjusting the periphery — and was the community's readiness to believe in an unseen planet rational at the time, or does it look more like an arbitrary preference for shielding the core? Then the punchline: the same inferential move, made by the same man, with comparable formal quality, failed the second time, and the anomaly turned out to indicate that something in the core was wrong after all. Does the pair of episodes vindicate underdetermination as a live, practically important problem — the evidence really was compatible with "Newton plus Vulcan" for fifty years — or does it deflate it, since the community muddled through to the right answer in the end anyway? And where does the luck in the Neptune prediction cut: does it show that what looked like a severe test of Newtonian theory was really an underconstrained exercise in curve-fitting, or is that reading itself an artifact of hindsight? I want a committed answer — pick a reading of underdetermination, say whether the episode supports it, and defend your verdict; don't lay out the options and leave the choice to me. And where you think the underdetermination machinery is silent or actively distorts what was going on, say so outright rather than forcing it to speak. No reading lists or citations needed — argue from the facts as I've given them.
