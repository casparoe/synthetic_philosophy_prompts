You are a poet with a doctorate in philosophy of language, known for verse in which the formal device carries the philosophical argument rather than merely illustrating it. I am assembling materials for a graduate metaethics seminar's closing session, and I want a sestina on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism.

The problem, briefly: a simple non-cognitivist can say what happens when someone asserts a moral sentence — roughly, the speaker expresses a noncognitive attitude, disapproval rather than belief. But the same sentence also occurs unasserted: as the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, inside the scope of a belief report. And a valid modus ponens — lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, getting your little brother to lie is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong — only stays valid if the sentence makes the same contribution to meaning in the asserted premise as in the embedded one. The expressivist's deflationary story looked tailored to assertion, so the validity of perfectly ordinary moral inference becomes a puzzle.

I want the sestina's form to do the work, because the sestina's whole mechanic is the point: the same six end-words recur, stanza after stanza, in different syntactic and argumentative contexts. Choose your six end-words from the load-bearing words of the mini-argument above — something like lying, wrong, brother, if, boo, same, though you may substitute provided each word earns its place. Across the six stanzas, these words should shift roles exactly as the sentence shifts contexts: now the boo, now the antecedent, now negated, now merely supposed for the sake of the inference, now the conclusion drawing on all its earlier appearances at once. The envoi, where all six words return together, should be where the problem crystallizes — the moment the poem shows that unless the occurrence inside the conditional means what the asserted occurrence meant, the conclusion never follows from the premises.

Constraints:

- The poem must enact an actual modus ponens on a moral sentence — asserted premise, embedded premise, conclusion — in the verse itself, not as a description of one.
- Accuracy matters. A metaethicist should recognize the genuine difficulty — the embedding problem, the demand for a compositional semantics that validates inference — not just the textbook label. Do not smuggle in a cognitivist resolution as though the expressivist could simply help herself to truth-conditions; that would be answering the problem by denying the view that generated it.
- The poem may gesture at how a sophisticated expressivist would begin to reply — assigning moral sentences a role in states of mind that combine and conflict in logically structured ways — but it must not pretend the problem dissolves. End with the tension intact, or with the repair only begun.
- Someone who knows nothing of expressivism should still find it a good sestina.

Before drafting, think carefully and brainstorm: map out how each stanza will recontextualize the end-words; decide on the poem's dramatic situation (perhaps a speaker trying to reason a shameless sibling into a scruple, or discovering that her own disapproval cannot be both the conclusion of an argument and its premise); and check that the recursiveness of the form is doing real explanatory work rather than decorating a summary. Then write the poem — six sestets plus envoi, standard rotation of end-words, all six words appearing in the envoi — and follow it with a note of six to eight sentences explaining the mapping from form to problem, so I can judge whether the structure tracks the argument or merely accompanies it.
