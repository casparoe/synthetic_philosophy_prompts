I'm in a reading group that spent this term on Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and afterwards, in the pub, a friend laid out an argument that's been bugging me all week, mainly because it seems to prove a lot more than anyone in the group actually wanted to sign up for. It went roughly like this. (1) A law of history, if there were one, would have to support predictions of the future course of history — otherwise it wouldn't earn the name of a law any more than a regularity about the past would. (2) The future course of history is substantially fixed by the growth of knowledge: new science and new technology are what redirect societies, and nothing else compounds the way knowledge does. (3) We cannot, even in principle, predict the future growth of knowledge, because predicting a discovery would just be making it — the prediction would be the discovery. (4) Nothing that cannot be predicted even in principle can be governed by a law, since a law that could in principle never be used to anticipate anything would be idle: it would explain nothing and forbid nothing we could ever check. (5) So there are no laws of history — not merely none found so far. And then, because he knew it would wind the rest of us up, he added a coda: (6) the same reasoning applies to any system whose future runs through what people will come to know, so it also shows there can be no laws governing long-run demographic or epidemiological or climatic development — anything where human learning feeds back into the outcome — which is why, he said, long-range projections in those fields are at best lucky guesswork dressed up in maths.

I can't follow him to (6). Demographers have been telling us for decades roughly how many seventy-year-olds there will be in 2050, and they're not guessing — nearly all of those people are already born — and if his argument really showed that long-range climate projections are impossible in principle, I'd want to know why it doesn't show the climate models are doing somethign rather than nothing. But I also can't see which earlier step I'm entitiled to reject. (4) looks to me like where the rot sets in — surely something can be lawful without being predictable by us, as with chaotic systems where the laws are known and the long-term trajectory still can't be computed. But if (4) is false, I seem to have handed a victory to the historicist in the group, since "there are laws of history, we just can't in principle use them to predict anything" sounds exactly like what a sensible historicist would happily say back to Popper. That's the bind: rejecting (4) saves the projections but appears to gut Popper's argument; accepting it keeps Popper but torches the projections. And I have a nagging feeling the slide might not even be at (4) — maybe something happens between (3) and (5) that none of us have put our finger on.

So: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine when my friend stated it, and what does rejecting it cost? Where you can, keep clear about which parts of this are settled among people who genuinely know Popper and which parts are live controversy — I've gathered that how this argument is supposed to work is itself disputed among scholars, and I'd rather not walk into my supervisor's office presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
