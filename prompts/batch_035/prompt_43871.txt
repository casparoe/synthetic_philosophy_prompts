A close friend of mine has, over several years of arguing about ethics, accumulated a fairly stable set of rulings on the question of who has the standing to blame — when a condemnation is legitimate criticism and when it's just the pot calling the kettle black. We've finally got enough of a track record to try to reverse-engineer the rule she's running, and the puzzle is that more than one principle seems to fit her verdicts. Her record so far: she says a tax auditor who quietly fudges his own return has no standing to publicly condemn a celebrity tax cheat; she says a man who berates his sister for shouting at her children has no standing, since he is famous in the family for shouting at his own; she says a woman twenty years sober, whose present life and career owe nothing to her drinking, does have standing to lecture her nephew about binge drinking; she says an acquaintance who has never owned a car has standing to criticize SUV drivers, even bluntly and at parties; and she says a hopeless procrastinator still has standing to blame a mutual friend for breaking promises — her gloss there was that his disorder is a different vice, not the same one being blamed.

Three new cases have now come up, and before we put them to her I'd like your prediction on each. Case one: a professor nearing retirement who plagiarized his way through university thirty years ago and has been scrupulous ever since, but whose whole career — the tenure, the life — was built on the credentials the plagiarism secured; he is now vigorously condemning a student caught plagiarizing, arguing that it was another person, decades ago. Does he have standing? Case two: a father confiscating his teenage son's phone who says, quite sincerely and in the same breath, that he is exactly as hooked as the boy is and is blaming himself for it too. Does he have standing to blame the son? Case three: a woman condemning a close friend's now-destructive gambling, who was the one who first took her to the casino years ago and still organizes the trips because she enjoys them, though she has never placed a bet herself. Does she have standing?

I'm less interested in the three yes-or-no answers than in the analysis around them. First, lay out the candidate rules that fit her existing verdicts — at minimum the strict matching-fault rule (standing is lost when you currently commit the same kind of wrong), the double-standard rule (standing is lost when you hold others to a standard you exempt yourself from), and the complicity rule (standing is lost through contributing to or profiting from the wrong, even if you never commit it yourself), plus any others you think fit the record. Second, say what each rule predicts for each of the three new cases, flagging where they diverge. Third, give your best guess for each case, with reasons — and tell me honestly if some earlier verdict of hers strains whichever rule you favor. Finally, propose the single further case that would best discriminate between the rules that survive her answers: if she rules as you predict, what one hypothetical, put to her next, would tell the remaining rules apart?
