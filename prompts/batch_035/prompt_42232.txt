I am grading a take-home exam in an upper-level metaphysics course and would like an independent assessment of one answer. The exam question read:

A patient dies of poisoning after her attending physician, though on duty and with the antidote at hand, never administers it. Explain why cases of this kind, causation by omission, create problems for theories of causation on which causes must be events, and why they threaten counterfactual analyses with a proliferation of causes. Then present what you consider the strongest response and assess whether it fully succeeds.

The student submitted the following:

The physician case is puzzling because the omission seems to cause the death: had the antidote been administered, the patient would have survived, so the death counterfactually depends on the failure to administer. The problem for event-causal theories is that an omission is not an event — nothing happened — so if causes must be events, the physician's failure cannot be a cause, even though we ordinarily hold her responsible for the death. There is also a proliferation worry: everyone else who failed to administer the antidote — the nurses, the janitor, every physician in the world — would equally count as a cause, since the death also depends on their omissions, and a theory that returns infinitely many causes of this sort has lost its grip on the concept.

The counterfactual analysis can answer the proliferation worry. In the closest worlds where some random stranger administers the antidote, the antecedent requires a large departure from actuality — strangers have no reason to do it — whereas the closest worlds where the attending physician administers it depart only slightly, since administering it was her routine duty. So the death depends on the physician's omission but not on the strangers', and the analysis avoids the proliferation. Phil Dowe takes a complementary line: on his account, omissions are genuine causes, with the effect counterfactually depending on them and dependence doing all the causal work.

But there is an even simpler way to see that the case is harmless. The physician's failure is just a case of double prevention. The antidote would have prevented the death, and the failure to administer prevented the antidote from being given; Lewis himself already accepted double prevention as genuine causation. Omissions therefore fall under a form of causation that had already been countenanced, and the event-causal theorist loses nothing by treating the physician's failure as a cause in the ordinary way.
