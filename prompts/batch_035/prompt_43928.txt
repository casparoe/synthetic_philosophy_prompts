For several years I've led a small discussion circle that meets monthly at my local library — twelve adults, ninety minutes a session, none of us with any philosophy background. We take turns bringing a question we've personally been chewing on, and next month it's my turn. I want to bring one I've been turning over privately for a long time, and I'd rather the group stumble into it than hear me lecture about it.

Here is what I've noticed in my own big decisions. Sometimes weighing two options feels like haggling: there is some amount of extra money, or shorter commute, or better title that would get me to switch, even if I can't put a number on it — there's a rate of exchange hiding somewhere. Sometimes there's no rate at all, but one option simply beats the other, so that's easy too. But sometimes neither option seems better, and yet I'm not indifferent between them either. When I was choosing between staying near my aging parents and taking a job abroad, I genuinely could not say which mattered more — but flipping a coin felt like a betrayal of something. If I were really indifferent, the coin should have been fine. So I've privately started distinguishing three things that all get called "a hard choice": choices with a hidden price, choices where one option quietly dominates, and choices where neither is better but a coin toss still feels wrong. I even have a name for my little test: the coin test — if you'd let a coin decide, you're indifferent; if you wouldn't, something stranger is going on.

I want you to design the ninety-minute session. The crucial thing is that the group should meet these distinctions in their own behavior, through choices they actually make during the session, before anyone (including me) explains anything. No lecturing up front; the point should arrive in what they do, not in what I say afterwards. Ideally the activity will produce, from the participants' own responses, live examples of all three patterns — priced tradeoffs, quiet dominance, and the no-winner-but-no-coin situation — so that the debrief can simply point back at things they already did.

Beyond the activity and the debrief, I need three more things. First, a materials list and timeline using only ordinary supplies, and no real money as stakes. Second, an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect some participants will insist on a price for everything, or will treat the whole exercise as a personality quiz — and how I should handle each failure without giving the game away. Third, candour about whichever part of this the activity cannot actually reproduce: if some feature of the phenomenon can only be delivered by me at the end rather than felt by them, say so plainly rather than papering over it.

The whole plan should come in under 800 words — rules, timeline, debrief, failure notes and all. Tight is better than complete.
