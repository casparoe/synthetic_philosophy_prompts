My colleague, who wrote her dissertation on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, keeps a running game with me: I describe an election and a voter, and she rules on whether that voter has a real incentive to vote insincerely. Her record so far: a plurality race where the voter's favorite polls 12% and two frontrunners are nearly tied — yes, vote for the better frontrunner; a two-candidate referendum — no; a plurality race where the favorite is in a dead heat for first — no; a Borda count where the favorite is far behind on points while the voter's second and last choices are locked in a close race — yes, top-rank the second choice; a committee where the chair's ballot alone decides everything — no. Next week I bring the case: an instant-runoff election with three candidates, where the voter's most hated candidate polls 40%, her favorite 36%, and her second choice 24%. The polls say the second choice will be eliminated first and his transfers will hand the final round to the hated candidate; but if a few thousand of the favorite's voters — this voter among them — rank the second choice first instead, the favorite is eliminated and the second choice wins. Will she say those voters have an incentive to vote insincerely? I care less about the verdict than about the rule she seems to be running — abandoning hopeless favorites, backing whoever can actually win, tracking pivotal probabilities, or something theorem-shaped about where manipulation must be possible — which rival rules fit her record just as well, and which further case would best tell them apart. Please say how confident you are in your best guess and which part of it you are least sure of.
