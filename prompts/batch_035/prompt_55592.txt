I teach an intro-level theory of knowledge class, and every year the truth unit goes sideways because students memorize the four labels (correspondence, coherence, pragmatic, deflationary), pattern-match on vibes, and move on. This year I want to force them to actually locate themselves via cases. Please write me a decision-tree handout, and here's what it needs to do:

Start from a neutral opening question, something like whether truth has a substantive nature worth theorizing about, or whether "is true" picks out a real property -- but phrase it so a student can answer it without already knowing which camp it lands them in.

Each internal node should be a concrete case or question the positions genuinely split on, not a "do you believe X?" label question. Specifically I want these worked in somewhere:

1. The generalization use: someone says "everything in the report is accurate" but the report is a hundred pages, so you can't just eliminate the truth-talk by repeating the sentences. Does that show the predicate is doing real work, or is it still just a logical device for endorsing without having the list? This is a real fork and I want it as a node.

2. Whether "true" applies to things that aren't sentences -- maps, pictures, beliefs, pre-linguistic animals' states. I've noticed encyclopedia entries lump all the deflationary views together, but this is one place where they actually come apart, so the tree must NOT treat deflationism as one leaf. I want at least three distinct deflationary species as separate leaves (redundancy-style, disquotational-style, prosentential-style, minimalism-style -- your call which three-plus you use and how you phrase them for students).

3. Whether truth explains anything. Like: is "her beliefs serve her well because they're mostly true" a decent explanation of her success, or is that just dressed-up restatement? Apparently the deflationists themselves split on this, so make it do splitting work.

Non-negotiable leaves: correspondence, coherence, pragmatic, plus the deflationary species above. Whatever else you add is fine.

Also please flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate -- I'd rather the handout admit a gap than pretend the cases decide everything. And if the liar sentence would be diagnostic anywhere, feel free to hang a bonus branch on it, but don't let it eat the tree since everyone's embarrassed there.

Roughly 7-9 nodes total, one sentence of gloss per leaf, written for students with zero philosophy of language background. My goal is that a student who answers honestly gets placed somewhere they'd actually defend when I push on it, not somewhere they just liked the sound of. Sorry for the long spec, this is the one unit I always mess up.
