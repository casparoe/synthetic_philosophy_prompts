Analyze the September 11th Victim Compensation Fund as a case study for luck egalitarianism. The facts I want on the table: Congress created the fund within days of the attacks, as part of legislation meant to save the airlines from bankruptcy, with the quid pro quo that families who accepted payment gave up their right to sue; Kenneth Feinberg served as special master and set awards largely by projecting each victim's lost lifetime earnings; around ninety-seven percent of families ultimately took the deal; and the formula was deliberately compressed — Feinberg applied a principle of narrowing the gap between the largest and smallest awards — so that the families of bond traders received several million dollars while the families of kitchen workers and janitors received a small fraction of that. Run the analysis in both directions.

First, what does the theory say about the scheme? The attack itself looks like pure brute luck, which by luck egalitarian lights is exactly what compensation is for; but the earnings-based payouts translate that brute luck into differential awards reflecting differences in income, and income differences are, on the theory's own account, supposed to be a mixture of option luck (which should be allowed to stand) and brute luck (which should not). So is the scheme too egalitarian, not egalitarian enough, or does it expose an instability in the brute/option luck distinction itself — was working at a high-rise financial firm in a high-profile target a chosen occupational gamble, or a background circumstance nobody can be held responsible for? Does Feinberg's gap-narrowing compression look, from the theory's standpoint, like an incoherent compromise between responsibility-sensitivity and something else, and if so what? And what, if anything, does luck egalitarianism have to say about the structural features the theory has no obvious vocabulary for: that the fund's purpose was protecting the airlines, that compensation was routed through a take-it-or-leave-it waiver of litigation rights, and that harms to rescue workers that only manifested as illness years later were left to a later and separately structured scheme?

Second, what does the case show about the theory? Public discomfort with the disparities seemed to rest on a thought like equal worth — the janitor's death is not less of a loss — which is not obviously a luck egalitarian thought at all; the theory's signature claim that responsibility-tracking matters to compensation seemed nearly silent in the public argument. Say honestly whether the episode confirms luck egalitarianism, anomalies it, or reveals it as the wrong explanation of our actual judgments about mass compensation; and be disciplined about the mapping — who the relevant agent is (the victim who died, or the family whose welfare is set back), what counts as the outcome to be equalized, and which parts of the case the theory should decline to speak to rather than being forced to pronounce on.
