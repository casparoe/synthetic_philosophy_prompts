My friend and I spent a long drive back from a wedding arguing about the honor–dignity–victimhood typology — the one in which honor cultures demand that slights be answered, dignity cultures counsel proportionate, direct responses while shrugging off the rest, and victimhood cultures favor appeals to third parties and the accumulation of support. My friend thinks the typology is a slander on the third category; I think it is roughly right; and at some point we abandoned the abstractions and started testing her intuitions case by case. Her rulings on how a wronged person ought to respond turned out to be surprisingly consistent, but neither of us could pin down the rule she was actually running, and we parted with the argument unresolved and a fresh case on the table.

Her verdicts so far: a stranger shoulder-checks you on the sidewalk without so much as a glance — let it go, engaging would be foolish. A coworker twice mocks your stutter in team meetings — report it to HR rather than answering him in the meeting or pulling him aside afterward; that is what the channel exists for. Your roommate's guest breaks your speaker and refuses to pay — demand compensation from the guest directly, and leave the roommate and everyone else out of it. A professor publicly ridicules a student's question in seminar — the student should file a complaint with the department rather than strike back in class. An old friend belittles your new career at a party — take it up with her privately a day or two later, one on one, and do not retaliate in front of the other guests.

I have two questions. First: if a teammate at my climbing gym — a voluntary club with an elected committee and a written code of conduct — makes cutting jokes at my expense on two or three separate occasions, what will my friend tell me to do, and why? I can see at least two rules that fit all five of her rulings equally well: one keyed to the setting (workplaces and schools get official channels, households and friendships get direct private handling, strangers get nothing), and one keyed to severity and power (trivial slights are ignored, moderate wrongs between peers are confronted directly, and anything repeated or backed by institutional power is escalated). What does each rule predict for the gym, which sits awkwardly between a friendship and an institution, and where would you put your money — and is there a further case we could put to her that would cleanly separate the two rules? Second, and this is what the argument was really about: if she rules the way you expect, is she still defending dignity culture as she claims, or has she quietly talked herself into something closer to the victimhood culture she insists she despises?
