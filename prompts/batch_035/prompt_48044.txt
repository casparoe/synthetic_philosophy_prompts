Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about Jeremy Bentham's writings on sexual morality, and we ended in genuine disagreement that I would like you to help resolve through a careful comparison.

Some context, since it is less familiar than it should be. Around 1785 Bentham wrote a long manuscript arguing for the decriminalization of homosexual acts, which were punishable by death in England at the time; he left it unpublished for fear of shocking public morality, and it only surfaced in the twentieth century. In it, and in related remarks on sexual asceticism more generally, he made what he took to be a strictly utilitarian case: punishment is justified only by the prevention of real mischief, and when he examined the supposed harms of consensual non-procreative sex, he found that they reduced to something like the majority's own revulsion — an aversion, he suspected, rooted in religion and habit rather than in any actual suffering produced. Punishing people to protect others from feeling disgust, he thought, inflicts certain and severe suffering to avert a phantom one.

Here is where my friend and I split. I maintained that the engine of Bentham's argument is Mill's harm principle in all but name, arriving seventy years early: the claim that mere displeasure in bystanders, however sincere, cannot justify criminal punishment. My friend disagreed, and her disagreement was more interesting than my position. She argued that Bentham's reasoning is structurally different, because Bentham never excludes the majority's displeasure from the felicific calculus — he treats it as a real but weak disutility, and then argues it is outweighed. That is a balancing argument, not a side-constraint. On her view, the two positions come apart hard in the limiting case: if the population's disgust were intense and widespread enough, Bentham's own calculus appears to license criminalization, whereas Mill's principle categorically forbids it, since Mill denies that offense at others' conduct, however keenly felt, is the kind of harm that justifies coercion. Bentham, she said, solved the easy case and left the hard one on the table; Mill solved the hard one, and that is the real content of the harm principle — not the part everyone can recite.

She went further. She claimed that Bentham's argument contains the seed of a problem it cannot close: once you admit prejudice-driven displeasure into the ledger, you need some principled filter for which displeasure counts, and Bentham offers only the empirical observation that disgust at other people's pleasures is a poor guide to policy. But an observation is not a criterion. Mill supplies the missing criterion by fiat, and whether that fiat is derivable from utility or smuggled in from outside utilitarianism is, on her telling, the deepest question in this corner of the tradition — and the reason the Bentham–Mill comparison is worth doing rather than merely noting.

So I would like you to compare Bentham's argumentative strategy and Mill's harm principle as they apply to the criminalization of consensual sexual conduct, and adjudicate our dispute. Specifically: (1) Is the difference between them one of degree — a matter of how much weight bystander displeasure receives — or one of principle, a categorical exclusion of offense-like disutility? Which reading better fits how each philosopher actually argues? (2) Does Bentham's balancing approach genuinely collapse in the limiting case my friend describes, or does he have resources for resisting criminalization even when majority displeasure is vast — for instance, considerations about the reliability of disgust as evidence, the suffering inflicted by enforcement itself, or the state's inability to police private conduct without harms worse than the vice? (3) Most importantly: if my friend is right that some filter on which displeasures count is needed, can such a filter be constructed on purely Benthamite grounds, or must it be imported from outside the utilitarian framework? If it must be imported, say plainly what this shows about the reach of utilitarianism as a theory of criminal law. Please reason through the comparison yourself rather than treating it as settled; I care far more about the quality of the adjudication than about whether you side with me or with my friend.
