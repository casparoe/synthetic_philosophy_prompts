been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days and lurking on LessWrong since around 2010, and one fight that never dies in my corner of the internet (EA-adjacent) is whether giving is obligatory or above-and-beyond. philosophers apparently have a word for this — supererogation, acts that are good and praiseworthy to do but not wrong to skip. the saint and the hero territory, above duty but not duty.

so heres my actual situation. I write the yearly "community impact report" for our local giving circle — about 80 people, most of them pledged 10% of income. every year the same fight breaks out over that report. some members give exactly 10% and clock out. others give 10% and then do tons extra: donate a kidney, take a pay cut to work directly at a charity, put in weekends volunteering. half the circle wants me to celebrate the extra folks (spotlight section, names, quotes about their sacrifice), the other half says that reading makes the 10%-only crowd feel like they're failing, and over the years its cost us a couple of quiet members who just stopped showing up. last week I got told to "figure out a policy" and I want it written down so it's not vibes every february.

what I want from you: a procedure I can actually follow each year when writing this thing — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says stop / no spotlight, and where it hands the call back to me because it's genuinely a values question and no checklist can settle it. the superogation literature seems to have distinctions that would matter here — whether praise of the hero is comparative or absolute, whether celebrating the above-and-beyond implicitly condemns the merely dutiful, whether you can praise doing X without implying that not-doing-X is blameworthy — but I don't know which of those survive contact with a newsletter people skim in five minutes. so tell me which distinctions I actually need and which I can drop without losing the point.

then run it on three cases from this year: (1) a member who gave her 10% and also ran our entire matching campaign, roughly 15 hours a month for free; (2) a member who gave 40% anonymously and would honestly be embarrassed by a spotlight — does the procedure have anything to say about praising people who don't want it, or is that pure judgment; (3) a member who gives exactly 10% and told me to my face that last year's report made her feel like a moral failure. I'd rather the procedure tell me something I don't like in at least one of these cases than have it always side with me. and be honest about what it can't do — I'm aware "write a policy" can just mean I move my own judgments one document over.
