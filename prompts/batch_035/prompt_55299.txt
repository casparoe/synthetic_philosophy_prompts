I got out of a long argument with a friend last night about whether the Anthropocene is "really" a new epoch, and we ended exactly where we began, so I want to reframe the question, because I've come to suspect the disagreement isn't about geology at all — it's about what procedure gets to decide, and what that procedure quietly takes for granted about what a named period of time is.

Here is the practice. The International Commission on Stratigraphy formalizes units of geological time by ratifying a "golden spike": a single physical point in one specific outcrop whose strata are declared to mark, worldwide, the lower boundary of a unit — with written criteria requiring continuous sedimentation, a primary marker (usually the first appearance of a fossil), datable beds, and correlatability across the globe. In 2024, after some fifteen years of work, an Anthropocene Working Group's proposal was voted down by a wide margin by the relevant subcommission and then by the commission itself: the proposal had fixed the epoch's start at the mid-twentieth-century layer of plutonium from bomb tests, in the sediments of a small lake in Canada, and it was rejected largely because that start is too recent and the record too shallow to bear the weight. The commission's parent body then issued a statement accepting the rejection while adding that the term will nevertheless continue to be used across the sciences and public life, and that it, in the statement's words, "will remain an invaluable descriptor of human impact on the Earth system."

My friend's position is that this outcome is simply what the word "epoch" means: epochs are units of the geological timescale, the timescale is built out of strata, so the question of whether there is an Anthropocene epoch is a question about rock, and if humanity's impact has not yet left a single, globally synchronous, datable layer, there is no fact of the matter — the term is a metaphor until the geologists ratify it, and the geologists have said no. My position is that this settles by procedure a question that shouldn't be settled by procedure at all: whether a historical period is the kind of thing whose existence consists in a layer of sediment. What bothers me is that the same institution seems to trade on both views at once. Nearly all of the units the geologists now police — the epochs and periods — were named in the nineteenth century by people dividing up time on historical and paleontological grounds, and the golden-spike rules were introduced only in the 1970s and then retro-fitted to boundaries already in use. The institution's whole vocabulary was, originally, a way of talking about history; now its descendants claim exclusive jurisdiction over it, while simultaneously demoting the one proposed unit that is unambiguously about human history to a mere "descriptor."

So here is what I want. Take the golden-spike practice as the object — the rules, the ratification votes, and the statement that both rejects the term as a unit and blesses it as a descriptor — and reconstruct the implicit theory of periodization it presupposes. What view of what an epoch is does the practice only make sense under: that units of time are individuated by strata rather than by causes; that a genuine period must begin everywhere at once; that naming rights belong to whichever discipline owns the relevant material record? Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined rather than committed — in particular, whether the 2024 outcome was actually forced by the rules. A plutonium layer appears in sediments on every continent within a few years and is directly datable, which looks like a better global marker than many of the fossil first-appearances the commission has ratified; so could the same rules, consistently applied, have licensed the Anthropocene, or does the practice carry a further, unstated requirement — a minimum duration, a settled past — that did the real work? Distinguish what the rejection strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. And finally, tell me what a naming practice would look like if the presupposed view were replaced by the one historians work with, on which periods are individuated by what humans do and undergo rather than by what they leave in the mud: what would have to change, and who would then hold the deciding vote my friend is confident the geologists already hold? If, along the way, you conclude that part of our argument was verbal — that "Anthropocene epoch" and "Anthropocene as descriptor" are just two names for one thing seen from two disciplines — say which part, and explain what the geologists' name could then mean that the historians' could not.
