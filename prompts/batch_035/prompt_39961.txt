I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but I've gotten pretty into the importance/tractability/neglectedness framework for comparing causes -- the one where you score each cause on a 1-10 scale for each of the three factors and multiply them together to get a rough priority score. I've actually built a little spreadsheet at work (for choosing which tech-debt project to tackle, ha) and I've come to think it's not just a EA thing, it's basically the correct general-purpose way to allocate effort. My position is roughly: any rational allocation of limited resources across problems should look like an ITN calculation, even if you never write the numbers down. If a cause scores low on the framework, that's a real strike against it, not just an artifact.

What would count against this? And I mean against the framework itself, not just against bad scores -- I know people can put garbage numbers into any scoring rubric. I want the stuff that would make me think the whole multiplication approach is wrong or misleading, not just noisy. A few things I've already half-noticed that bug me, and I'd like you to tell me whether these are actually problems or whether I'm overthinking:

1. The three factors don't look independent. A problem that's super important (affects a huge number of people) almost by definition has lots of people working on it already, so neglectedness goes down as importance goes up. If the inputs are correlated, multiplying them feels like it's double-counting something. Or maybe tractability already falls out of importance and neglectedness somehow? Like, if a problem is easy to make progress on, someone probably already did, so high tractability implies low neglectedness in equilibrium. Am I inventing a problem here or is this a known structural flaw?

2. There seems to be no principled place for diminishing returns in the framework. Two causes can tie on ITN while one has a nice shallow curve and the other pays off only after a massive upfront investment. It seems like the thing I actually want is cost-effectiveness at the margin, and ITN is at best a cheap proxy for it. At worst it's a proxy that systematically points the wrong direction -- e.g., toward causes that are neglected *because* they're intractable, which the tractability score is supposed to catch but in practice nobody scores honestly.

3. Relatedly: neglectedness feels endogenous. If everyone uses ITN, everyone piles into whatever currently looks neglected, which stops it being neglected, and the whole thing becomes a crowded-trade dynamics problem more like a market than an audit. Does the framework have anything to say about its own adoption, or does it just quietly assume nobody else is using it?

Tell me what else is out there that I haven't thought of: which observations, arguments, or worked examples would genuinely move me off the framework, and which defenders of it would themselves concede are real hits versus which they'd wave away. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward for me. And be honest about whether the framework has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- like, if someone can always say "well, your score was just wrong, the framework is fine," does that make the whole thing not a claim about the world at all, and is that a problem for this kind of framework or is that just what a decision procedure is? For what it's worth I'd find a worked case most persuasive -- a comparison where ITN and some rival way of thinking about allocation genuinely give different answers, so I can see which one I trust.

Please say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and call out the single point you're least sure about. I'd rather have honest uncertainty than a clean story. Also if any of my three worries above are just confused, tell me straight -- I'm an engineer not a philosopher, I can take it.
