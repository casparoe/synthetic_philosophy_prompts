A classmate in my historiography seminar closed off a debate about whether the Industrial Revolution was bound to happen roughly when and where it did by reciting the old rhyme — 'For want of a nail the shoe was lost; for want of the shoe the horse was lost; for want of the horse the rider was lost; for want of the rider the message was lost; for want of the message the battle was lost; for want of the battle the kingdom was lost — and all for the want of a horseshoe nail.' She used it as though it were a law of history, sufficient to show that 'deep structural cause' talk is empty. I want to work out what the proverb could actually be claiming before deciding whether it did any work for her. Please lay out the distinct precise theses the rhyme might be expressing, and for each give a verdict — platitude, substantive but defensible, or false — and, above all, say whether that reading would entitle her to her conclusion, which was not just that some outcomes are contingent but that explanations abstracting from individuals are thereby illegitimate.

Some distinctions I suspect matter and would like woven into the menu:

1. The proverb as one specific counterfactual about one kingdom versus a general thesis about history. Only the second can support her move; the first is just a story about a story.
2. 'Some small causes have large effects' — which seems trivially true, and memorable mainly because we notice such cases and forget the countless nails that change nothing — versus 'small differences in initial conditions are systematically amplified,' the reading borrowed from chaos theory. Please assess whether that borrowing is licensed: dynamical chaos requires iterated amplification, while many historical processes look instead like systems with attractors that pull differently seeded trajectories back together (parallel independent invention, the recurrence of similar institutions in unconnected places).
3. A claim about the world (outcomes are contingent) versus a claim about us (our models cannot track the small causes). Her conclusion was the epistemic one, and I want to know whether any reading gets her from a fact about the world to a verdict on the standing of structural explanation.
4. The rhyme's own chain structure — nail, shoe, horse, rider, message, battle, kingdom — where each arrow is a counterfactual 'would' that must hold, and the cascade is only as probable as the product of its links. Does the vividness of a completed chain conceal how rarely such chains complete, so that the proverb describes a rare event while performing as a description of a typical one?

Finally, if any reading would support her, say what it commits her to — in particular whether she must deny that any historical process exhibits equifinality, many paths converging on the same outcome, since that is exactly what the 'it was coming anyway' position in our seminar rests on.

Present the readings as a numbered menu with verdicts rather than as a defense of any one of them, so that I can bring it back to the seminar as a disambiguation she has to choose from.
