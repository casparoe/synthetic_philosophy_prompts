So here's my situation: I'm a science and ideas reporter and I've spent the better part of a year embedded with the death determination panel at a large teaching hospital for a feature on how we actually decide someone is dead, which -- spoiler -- is way messier and more interesting than the one-line bioethics gloss I came in with. The panel reviews every brain-death declaration and every donation-after-circulatory-death procurement at the hospital, and after sitting through their meetings and raiding their minutes, I've got their full track record from the last few years. Their verdicts so far: a man with total brain failure confirmed by two exams plus the apnea test and a flat flow study -- dead, no drama. A woman in a permanent vegetative state whose family asked whether the panel could just "call it" -- not dead, she breathes on her own, case closed. A cardiac arrest patient where resuscitation was abandoned, no pulse, fixed pupils -- dead, obviously. And then the one that keeps nagging at me: a donation-after-circulatory-death case where the ventilator was withdrawn, the heart stopped, they waited a bit over two minutes per protocol to rule out autoresuscitation, declared death, and moved to organ recovery. One panelist muttered that the man's brain wasn't actually dead yet at the two-minute mark and everyone kind of shuffled papers and moved on, and I've been chewing on that moment ever since.

Now there's a new case on their docket and I want to write about it intelligently rather than just describing the shouting. A woman, whole-brain dead by their usual double-exam standards, also about sixteen weeks pregnant, and the family and the hospital have agreed to somatic support -- ventilator, hormones, feeding tube -- aiming to keep the body going until the fetus reaches viability, something like four more months. Her body is doing a alot of work in the meantime: gestating, maintaining blood pressure with support, apparently healing small wounds. So: is she dead? When I look at the panel's record I can read it at least two ways -- either they're running a straightforward whole-brain rule (in which case she died on the day of the second exam and what's on the ward now is, on their own view, a ventilated corpse with a passenger), or they're running something like "permanent loss of the body's ability to run itself," where the DCD two-minute declaration fits naturally and the PVS verdict fits too, but where a body that is actively gestating and healing and being kept running for months gets much harder to call dead. And those two rules diverge exactly on this case, no?
