You are to take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who is used to working with technically sophisticated students and colleagues. I need a consistency audit of my own views, and I would like you to think carefully before answering: please brainstorm the possible pairwise tensions in what follows first, check each one against what I actually said, and only then write up your conclusions, rather than going down the list once and commenting item by item.

Some background on me: I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. Over the past few years of reading around the philosophy of mind in my spare time, I've gradually assembled a set of positions that each seemed obviously right when I adopted them. I've written them out for the first time, in my own vocabulary, and now I'd like to know whether they form a consistent package or whether I've smuggled in commitments that undermine each other. Here they are:

1. Mental states are functional states. What makes a state a belief, a desire, or a pain is the causal role it plays — its characteristic relations to sensory input, to other internal states, and to behavior. The underlying material isn't constitutive of mindedness; it matters only as a way of implementing the right causal organization.

2. Multiple realizability is close to a certainty. Octopus pain and human pain presumably share little at the level of chemistry or anatomy, but both are pain. A sufficiently faithful whole-brain emulation running on silicon would have a mental life in the full sense: if the emulation of my wife reaches for her knee and says "ow," that is not a simulation of pain, it is pain.

3. Current large language models do not have beliefs or desires about anything. The reason, as I understand it, is that their internal states were never causally grounded in the world: a system whose entire contact with things comes from compressing text has symbols that aren't about anything. Real understanding requires a learning history of causal interaction with the world the symbols are supposed to be about.

4. On the other hand, I'm quite sure that a state snapshot is the state. If a freak event in a swamp assembled a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me, complete with all my memories, the duplicate would have genuine beliefs from its first second of existence — no different in principle from restoring a virtual machine from a checkpoint, or a distributed system rebuilding its state from a persisted log. It doesn't matter how the bits got there; what matters is the state they're in.

5. I'm equally sure about the opposite kind of case: a finite lookup table that recorded my entire life's conversational behavior — such that any conceivable input string produces exactly what I would have said, forever — would not be a mind, not even a marginal one. It's a recording, however vast. A mind has to be doing something, not merely to have done something.

6. In the phenomenology department: if surgeons replaced my visual cortex with a silicon device that preserved every causal role — every input-output relation and every internal transition — I'm confident I would see exactly the same colors afterward as before. Yet I also think the inverted spectrum scenario is coherent: my oldest friend could be functionally identical to me since birth and still experience red where I experience green. I don't see a contradiction there, but I can't articulate why there isn't one.

7. Finally, a methodological note that might matter: I trust intuitions about concrete cases far more than theoretical elegance. When a well-described case and a theory clash, I go with the case. I realize this could be a bias, but it's how I debug systems too — if the spec disagrees with the observed behavior, the spec is wrong.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature and not a lecture. Identify which of these seven commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, distinguish those from pairs that merely look like they conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension explain what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it up — including whether the cost is a revision of a specific claim or of the motivation behind it. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and end with a verdict on the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most. Please don't be diplomatic: if the package is incoherent, tell me exactly where it breaks.
