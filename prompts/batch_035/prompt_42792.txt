I keep going back and forth on Dennett's "quining qualia" thing and want an essay that actually takes a side. He tries to show the standard notion of qualia (roughly: ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible properties of experience) is confused, and the centerpiece is the Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster story, where the idea is that there may just be no fact of the matter about whether the coffee "tastes the same to you now as it did then" -- your memory of the earlier taste could have shifted, or your reaction to an unchanged taste could have shifted, and introspection can't tell which. So write an essay on whether this dissolution strategy works. Some things I want you to actually engage with rather than just summarize:

- Does the Chase and Sanborn case generalize, or is taste a friendly target (heavily hedonic, plastic, memory-dependent)? Consider whether pains or afterimages, where the link to discrimination behavior is tighter, resist the same treatment.
- Dennett's list of four classic properties is doing a lot of work. Suppose you grant that all four, as he defines them, are incoherent or empty. Does a watered-down notion survive the purge -- something like whatever aspect of experience the knowledge argument (Mary in the black-and-white room) is gesturing at -- or does the dissolution of the classic notion take the knowledge argument down with it? Basically: is "qualia" like "phlogiston" (a posit whose explanatory failures doom any successor) or more like "vital force" (wrongly defined, but tracking something real that got rebranded)?
- If you think some residual notion survives, say what it is precisely enough that it couldn't itself be quined by the same tricks; if you think nothing survives, explain why the intuition that there's something ineffable going on is so stubborn -- what's the error, exactly?

Don't end on "both sides have a point." Take a verdict and defend it. No citations or bibliography needed, just the arguments.
