I'm in a final-year course on the philosophy of death, and my friend Max (huge deprivationist, will not shut up about it) walked me into an argument at the pub last week that I can't find my way out of. It goes: (1) something can be bad for you only by making things go worse for you than they would otherwise have gone -- there is no other currency of badness-for. (2) Death, when it prunes a life, makes things go worse for the person than they would have gone: in the nearest world where she survives, she gets further goods that are absent from the actual world. (3) So the bad of death is nothing over and above the counterfactual gap between the life she gets and the life she would have had. (4) But nothing in (1)-(3) mentions death. Any gap between your actual life and a better nearby one is, by the same reasoning, bad for you -- the friend you would have made if you'd sat one seat over on the first day of uni, the book you'd have adored but never opened, the accident you only narrowly avoided by luck. (5) So every life that falls short of the best available life is saturated with harms, and death is just the biggest item on a very long invoice. Max accepts (5) cheerfully and says harm is just cheap, and that anyone who thinks death is bad but the seat-over friend isn't is just being squeamish about big numbers.

I can't accept that, but I genuinely can't locate the broken step. When I pushed him, Max offered a patch: he says the bad-making gaps are only the futures that were already coming to you -- hold the past fixed, vary the future -- so "worse than you would have been" isn't "worse than you could have been". That patch does seem to get death's badness back and even handles the old Lucretius symmetry point about the time before your birth not being bad for you. But it feels like he's just declared a cutoff, and I can't see what makes "already coming to you" anything more than a finger in the dyke -- and if the patch is fiat, maybe the original chain was fine and the problem is further up, in (1) itself? Please act as a professional philosopher who works on well-being and the badness of death, think it through properly rather than pattern-matching to a stock reply, and then tell me: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd very much like to keep (1), and the intuition that the seat-over friend isnt a harm, and I want to know if that's coherent or whether I have to give one of them up.

Give me the diagnosis in prose, but lead with a one-line verdict on the single weakest step, and then follow the prose with a table -- columns: Step (1-5, plus Max's patch as a sixth row), Verdict (keep / reject / re-read), Why it looked fine, Price of rejecting it. For the patch row specifically, tell me whether it's a genuine repair or just trades one embarrassment (the symmetry problem) for another (arbitrariness about which counterfactuals count), and dont go easy on Max just because he buys the rounds.
