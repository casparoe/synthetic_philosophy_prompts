I'm a research intern at a small ML lab, mostly doing evals, and last week two of the full-time researchers got into a genuinely heated lunch argument about mechanistic interpretability — specifically whether it makes sense to say a neural network "has" representations at all. The trigger was some sparse autoencoder work where people claim to isolate individual features, like a famous bridge-detection feature, and then steer the model's behaviour with them. I'll try to reconstruct the exchange fairly, numbered by move, since they asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't.

(1) Dan, who does interp work, said the features are genuine discoveries, not labels we paste on: if you add the feature's vector to the activations and the model's outputs change in exactly the way the feature's name predicts — activate the "bridge" feature and the model won't shut up about bridges — that's the same causal test you'd accept in any other science. Intervene on the thing, see the predicted effect, conclude the thing is real. (2) Marta replied that he's sliding from "some direction in activation space correlates with bridges and nudging it shifts outputs" to "the network itself contains a discrete bridge-thing", and those are very different claims. The dictionary of features is something we impose: change the sparsity penalty or the dictionary size and you get a different set of "features", each of which presumably also steers. If what you find depends on the decomposition method, you didn't discover the features, you manufactured them — real stars, made-up constellations. (3) Dan said multiple valid decompositions of the same underlying structure shouldn't scare anyone — physicists describe the same state in different bases all the time — and pointed to convergence: independent teams using somewhat different methods keep landing on recognisably similar features, which wouldn't happen if they were pure artefacts of any one method. (4) Marta said the convergence point is weaker than it looks, because the methods all share the same core assumption (that the network is sparse and superposed in the first place), so of course they agree; and "recognisably similar" is doing a tonne of work there anyway, since at some point a human squints at a feature and declares what it's for, and humans are pattern-matchers who see faces in toast — the whole labelling step is outsourced to the most apophenia-prone thing in the building.

They ended inconclusively and went back to their desks. Could you adjudicate move by move — who's right, or righter, at each of the four steps, which of the two core objections (causal steering as evidence of reality vs. method-relative decompositions as evidence against it) is actually the stronger one, and what, if anything, you think both of them are missing that would move the debate forward? Please write your answer in British English.
