ok so this might be a weird one. my daughter's civics class is doing a unit on originalism vs living constitutionalism, and parents were invited to contribute a short writeup on a historical figure who wrestled with the same question before it had a name. I picked Frederick Douglass, mostly because of a thing a lot of people don't know: he completely flipped on the Constitution. In the 1840s he was with Garrison's camp, treating the whole document as a pro-slavery pact that should be denounced and torn up, and then around 1851 he switched and started insisting it was an antislavery document all along — nudged partly by arguments he picked up from Lysander Spooner and Gerrit Smith — and his way of arguing it was to point at what the founders actually intended, which is basically originalist machinery avant la lettre.

so here's what I want from you: figure out where Douglass would stand in today's version of this fight. Remember that nowadays originalism is mostly associated with conservative outcomes, and that the punishment-for-crime exception in the Thirteenth Amendment keeps getting invoked to defend unpaid prison labor. Given all that, would he happily sign up as an originalist, since reading the founders' intent is exactly what he used to break slavery's grip on the document? Or would he say the method was only ever a tool — keep it while it liberates, drop it the moment it stops? Actually reason it out from what he argued in the 1850s and in his 1860 Glasgow speech if that helps (but don't just hand me a list of sources, I can't use that). Take a position at the end. A wishy-washy both-sides answer will get seen right thru it, my kid's teacher is sharp.
