I host a podcast on philosophy of mind aimed at an audience of graduate students and serious amateurs, and next month's guest is a cognitive scientist best known for defending a strange-loop theory of the self. The core of her view, as I understand it, is that the 'I' is not a thing residing in a single brain but a self-referential pattern — a feedback structure that brains happen to instantiate — and that because patterns are substrate-flexible, a person's self can be partially instantiated in other brains: that the people who knew someone deeply, and modeled them richly for decades, come to host fragments of that person's pattern, so that after death the person is not entirely gone but distributed, dimly and partially, across the minds of those who loved them. She developed this view partly through reflecting on her own grief after her spouse's death, and she takes the view to be a factual claim about personal identity, not a poetic consolation.

I want roughly ten questions that press on the actual content of the position rather than inviting her to restate it. In particular I want at least one question on each of the following pressure points, though I'm happy for you to add others you think are sharper: (1) the distinction between hosting an accurate *model* of someone and hosting a *partial instance* of them — why isn't her view just a redescription of memory and imagination with more dramatic vocabulary? (2) what individuates a self-pattern such that the fragments scattered across other brains count as fragments *of the same person* rather than as loosely similar patterns, the way two people's mental models of Sherlock Holmes aren't fragments of Sherlock Holmes; (3) whether the view makes identity a matter of degree and whether she can live with the sorites consequences — was her spouse fifty percent there, five percent, and does the question even make sense; (4) what the view implies for the classic persistence questions, e.g., whether advanced upload scenarios would preserve the person, and whether she thinks her view agrees with or revises the standard patternist treatment of duplicates; and (5) whether anything normative follows — if parts of the dead persist in us, do we owe them anything, and does that 'owing' have any grip if the remaining pattern is too thin to have interests?

One caution: she is extremely adept at deflecting with the remark that these worries treat identity as binary when the whole point of the view is to dissolve the binary. I'd like the questions sequenced so that this deflection becomes progressively harder to deploy — early questions can let her use it, but the later ones should either accept degrees-of-identity and demand the resources to still answer (a degree-preserving criterion of numerical identity, a threshold for practical purposes), or force her to say whether there is any question about the self her view cannot reframe. End with a single question about what observation or thought experiment, if it obtained, she would count as counting against her view, and include the follow-up you'd expect to need when she answers it.
