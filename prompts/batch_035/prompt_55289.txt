I teach a third-year seminar of twelve students on archaeological theory and the philosophy of the historical sciences, and I have a two-hour session coming up on a problem my students currently treat as a dry methodological caveat rather than a live wound: how anyone infers beliefs and institutions from objects. On paper they can recite that a midden tells you about subsistence more easily than about ritual, but they have never felt how quickly the inferences outrun the evidence. The old image I want them to encounter bodily is Hawkes's ladder of inference from the 1950s -- the idea that archaeology climbs without much strain from production techniques to economy, with rather more strain to social and political organisation, and with real difficulty to religious belief and cosmology -- because I suspect most of them secretly believe the top rung is just slower to reach rather than different in kind. So I want an in-class exercise, not a lecture: the idea should emerge from what the students do, not from anything I say in advance.

Here is my half-formed plan, which I would like you to develop, correct, and make runnable. I fabricate a small pre-literate settlement and write a truth key for it in advance -- its economy, its descent system, whatever its inhabitants thought the dead owed the living. I then prepare four or five sealed evidence packs, each describing a different part of the same site: a midden, a burial area, a workshop floor, a structure with repeated burnt deposits, and so on -- though I want to be careful that the pack labels and the drawings themselves not smuggle in interpretations, since calling a structure a shrine or even a house already settles half the question. Teams of two or three each work a different pack for perhaps forty minutes and write up a reconstruction of the community, deliberately reaching the top rungs of Hawkes's ladder rather than stopping at diet and tool typology. Then the reveal: I put the competing reconstructions side by side, and the teams must argue over whose institution is really there in the objects -- before I open my truth key and, crucially, before I show them a second layer I want you to design, some form of deliberate underdetermination built into the site itself, such as a deposition episode that is equally consistent with ritual and with mundane discard, or a plough-zone disturbance that has shuffled the contexts.

Please produce the full session plan: the rules and materials (I imagine printed cards or line drawings rather than real objects, and I need to know how many, and what should be on them), the instructions given to each team, the payoff or pressure that stops them from settling for a thin functional description, a timeline for the two hours, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto specific parts of the problem -- Hawkes's rungs, the use of ethnographic analogy as a ladder one can also fall off, the difference between an artefact's function and its meaning, and the way a discarded truth key is itself a comforting fiction that real excavations never offer. I would also like two honest notes: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my guess is that one team will reconstruct a religion and another will refuse to leave the midden, and I need to know what to do about both), and which feature of actual inferential archaeology the exercise simply cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief -- for instance the years of accumulated comparative knowledge that make a real archaeologist's analogies better than guesswork, or the way field conditions and sampling decide in advance what questions can even be asked. If you think my design has a flaw -- say, that letting me write the truth key makes me a god in a way no past community ever had -- tell me and propose the fix. Please write the answer in British English, and do not pad it with citations or a reading list; I am asking for a plan I can run, not a bibliography.
