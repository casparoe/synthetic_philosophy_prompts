Please play a committed non-reductive physicalist — a philosopher of mind who believes mental properties are irreducibly real and genuinely causal, despite also believing that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. Be sharp and not a strawman: you know the exclusion argument cold, you anticipate the standard objections, you concede small points when honesty demands it, but you never give up your central claim that my deciding to raise my arm is a cause of the raising. Where it helps you, you may draw on the resources your camp actually uses — realization-dependent counterfactuals, the idea that mental properties can be more proportional or general causal explanations than their physical realizers, interventionist treatments of causation — but in your own words, and you may not simply name-drop a paper as if that settled anything. Stay in character until I write "stop," and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so this feels like a live exchange rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. I lay down three premises you have to accept as a physicalist: first, the physical domain is causally closed, so every physical event that has a cause at time t has a sufficient physical cause at t; second, mental-to-physical causation, if it occurs, is not a case of two independent causes producing one effect the way a firing squad kills a man — it is not systematic overdetermination of that crude kind; third, if an event e has a sufficient physical cause p, then no distinct event q outside the physical domain is also a cause of e, since causes of the same effect that are not related as background and foreground conditions must compete. My decision is distinct from any neural event, being a mental event; my arm's rising is a physical event with a neural cause; so either the decision does nothing, or you owe me a story about how two distinct sufficient causes can coexist without the firing-squad absurdity. I have heard non-reductive physicalists reply that the mental event is realized by, or identical at the token level with, the neural event, so the two causes are not distinct after all. But then I press you: if token identity is doing all the causal work, what is left for your irreducible mental properties to contribute? You cannot have it both ways — either your decision is a distinct cause, and it is excluded, or it is not distinct, and your property dualism about the mental has become causally idle decoration. Which horn do you take, and what exactly does your irreducible mental property do at the moment my arm goes up?
