I'm presenting to a graduate seminar in economic history next month a paper defending a specific claim about the Great Divergence debate, and I need a Q&A prep sheet built for that room, not for philosophers.

The thesis I'm defending: the question "why Britain and not China?" is not merely hard, as most economic historians assume, but ill-posed, because it treats Britain and China as members of a single contrast class when they are units of radically different kinds. Contrastive explanation, I'll argue, only works when the candidate alternatives share the relevant causal background — you can ask why this seedling and not that one, because the difference in outcome traces to differences against a common structure. But "Britain" and "China" differ not just in the variables we care about but in scale, internal heterogeneity, and the very level at which economic processes operated, so no single answer can be contrastive in the required sense: whatever made Britain industrialize was not a Britain-shaped variant of whatever kept China from doing so. Two riders I'll insist on. First, I am not denying the divergence was real, and I'm not claiming China was Europe's equal until 1800, nor that Europe was secretly ahead for centuries — I reject both the strong "surprising divergence" story and the "long-standing European advantage" story. Second, and this is where I expect trouble: I think the Needham question — why did modern science arise in Europe and not China — is *better* posed than the economic one, even though it looks structurally identical, because there the comparison runs at the level of intellectual communities whose practices are more commensurable than regional economies. And I think the *within-class* versions of the divergence question are perfectly fine: "why the Yangzi delta and not the Pearl River delta by 1850," or "what would it have taken for the Yangzi delta to industrialize by 1850," are genuine questions with possible answers, and I suspect most of what's valuable in the divergence literature survives as answers to those.

The room: about fifteen people. Two or three are committed to the California school position in its strong form and will hear my rejection of the "equal until 1800" story as an attack on their life's work; they also think that comparing Britain to the Yangzi delta rather than to China as a whole already solves my problem, and they may be right. One is a cliometrician who will say that "ill-posed" is just a concession to our data limitations, and that with better estimates of regional GDP and factor prices the question reduces to a comparative statics exercise — this is the objection I'm least sure I can meet. Two are historical sociologists who think all explanation is implicitly contrastive and that "why Britain?" just means "why Britain rather than a world in which no one industrialized first," which would dissolve my distinction entirely. The rest are agnostic graduate students who have read the standard literature and will judge the talk by whether I sound like I'm dodging the question everyone came to ask.

Prepare the sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they'll arrive, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it, with a two-minute reply, a note on what I should concede outright, and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that turns my own criterion against me — and the best available answer to it, flagged honestly as such.
