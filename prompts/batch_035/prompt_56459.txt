The exam question was: "The cogito is often summed up as the thought that my thinking guarantees my existence. Set out what the argument is supposed to establish, explain why it survives the doubt of the deceiving demon where mathematical beliefs do not, and critically assess its scope. In particular: does the cogito by itself establish that the self is a thing with a determinate nature — a thinking substance, perhaps an immaterial one — and can its certainty be generalised beyond the first-person case?"

And the student wrote: "The evil demon scenario is meant to cast doubt on everything — mathematics, the senses, even the existence of my own body. But there is one thing the demon cannot deceive me about: while I am being deceived, I am thinking. Deception is itself a kind of thinking, so if the demon is deceiving me, there must be something there to be deceived. So the cogito survives where arithmetic does not. It follows that I am, and that what I am is a thinking thing — not merely that thinking is going on, but that I am a substance whose whole nature or essence is to think, since the cogito delivers a clear and distinct perception of myself as a thinking thing and nothing else. This also shows that the mind is distinct from the body: the cogito establishes my existence as a thinker while I can still doubt that I have a body, so the mind must be able to exist without the body — the cogito is in effect an immediate proof that the soul is immaterial. Moreover, since my own case shows that thinking entails existing, we can generalise: whatever thinks, exists. The certainty here is unlike that of mathematics because it is self-verifying — each time you perform it, it is true — whereas the demon could in principle have made it seem to me that 2+3=5 when it isn't. The cogito does not tell me anything about the past or the future, or about whether other minds exist, so its scope is limited to the present moment of thinking."
