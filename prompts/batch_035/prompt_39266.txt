Now that I have retired from a career in experimental physics, I have joined a discussion group that reads philosophy of science together with economic history, and I have noticed that two analogies are doing nearly all of the argumentative work in our debates. The economists in the group say that history is a laboratory, and that the division of Korea at the 38th parallel in 1945 was a natural experiment: one people, split by outside powers into something like a treatment group and a control group, which is taken to show that institutions, rather than geography or culture, made the South rich. A geographer in the group replies that geography is to an economy what climate is to an ecosystem — a slowly varying background that fixes what is feasible — so that searching for laws of history is like searching for laws of ecology, and the economists' causal question may be ill-posed from the start. Having spent forty years in laboratories where an experiment meant random assignment, controls, and independent replication, I would like both analogies audited before I trust either conclusion. For each, please give the correspondence table — what plays the role of random assignment, the control group, the treatment, and replication in the first; of climate, ecosystem, and species in the second — then identify where each mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the conclusion being drawn from those that do not. Finally, tell me whether the claim that institutions caused the Korean divergence survives the audit, and whether the geographer's skepticism about ever knowing why some nations are rich survives hers; and if neither analogy can carry the weight, propose a better one and state what it would imply about the limits of causal inference from history.
