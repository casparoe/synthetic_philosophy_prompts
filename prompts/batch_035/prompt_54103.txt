I'm taking a seminar that straddles criminal law and philosophy of action, and I've become obsessed with what the doctrine of common purpose (what everyone still calls joint enterprise) quietly presupposes about collective intentionality. Please respond as if you were a philosopher of action with a firm grasp of English criminal law doctrine, and think carefully before committing to anything.

Here is the practice as I understand it, and the tangle I can't untie. Under the pre-2016 case law, if two people set out on a criminal venture together and one of them, in carrying it out, killed with intent, the other was guilty of murder so long as he had foreseen that the killing was a real possibility — no agreement on the killing, no intent that it happen, just foresight plus continued participation. The Supreme Court in Jogee in 2016 said the law had taken a wrong turning there: foresight is merely evidence of an intention to assist, and the accessory must himself intend to assist the principal to act with the requisite intent. But the same judgment leaned heavily on conditional intention — the example given is the gang of robbers who all intend that guns be used with serious intent if resistance is met — so the group is still, in effect, treated as a single intentional unit in the run of cases, even while the official story is that liability always bottoms out in one individual's intention. And the rest of the law points in both directions at once. Criminal law insists it never imposes vicarious liability: guilt is personal, nobody is punished merely for what another did. Yet under common purpose each party is, functionally, answerable for the other's excesses within the scope of their shared design, and only escapes for what counts as a fresh departure from it. Withdrawal, meanwhile, has a peculiar shape: an accomplice who simply drifts away, stops participating, and says nothing is still in it; to get out he must communicate his withdrawal — as if participation is a commitment that cannot be rescinded unilaterally. Compare the neighbouring doctrines: in company law, the identification doctrine attributes an employee's acts to the firm only when the employee is the directing mind and will — the company literally acts through him — and a store manager whose sloppiness breaches trading law is not the company; whereas in the civil law of partnership, each partner binds the others as their agent, on a straightforward representation story. So: two people with a plan to burgle are treated as something like a single intentional agent; ten thousand shareholders and a management structure are treated as a single agent only through a directing mind; two partners are treated as separate agents tied by authority rather than shared mind. My question is what theory of joint action, if any, the criminal doctrine presupposes. Does it presuppose a genuine plural subject, something like a joint commitment that each party incurs on joining and cannot exit without communicating — or a Bratman-style shared intention built from interlocking individual intentions, or an irreducible we-intention of the Searle sort? Does Jogee's retreat to individual intention reveal a philosophical commitment to reductionism about collective intentionality, and if so, how do the withdrawal and fresh-departure rules survive that retreat — they look irreducibly structural? Or is the doctrine philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of joint action would each license it?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of collective intentionality and joint action in the criminal rules, distinguishing what those rules strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably alongside, and telling me where the practice is underdetermined. Please work through the concrete features — the foresight rule and its correction, conditional intention, the communication requirement for withdrawal, the fresh-departure limit, the contrast with the identification doctrine in company law and agency in partnership — rather than discussing the theories in the abstract, and say which theorist's account best fits which feature, if any single account fits them all. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the doctrine if the law were rebuilt on, say, a joint-commitment view rather than a reductionist one — which convictions would go, which acquittals would go. Please be honest about the points where you are reconstructing rather than reporting, state how confident you are in the fit of each part of your reconstruction, and single out the part you are least sure of. Please write in British English throughout.
