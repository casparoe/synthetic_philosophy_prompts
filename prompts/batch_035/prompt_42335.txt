My friend and I spent most of Saturday arguing about Buridan's ass — well, not the donkey itself, but what cases like it say about choice. You know the setup: two options alike in every respect you care about, so there's no reason to prefer either. She holds the classic line that if you can't rank them they're just equally good, and choosing between equals is trivial — pick either, flip a coin, done, no philosophical residue left over. I pushed the Ruth Chang stuff on her: that there seem to be choices (her examples are things like comparing two very different careers, or two very different artistic achievements) where neither option is better, they're clearly not equal, and they're also not just incommensurable in the sense of being unrankable because there's no common currency — they're comparable but "on a par," and that's supposed to explain why Buridan-type cases still feel like a puzzle even after the coin flip. We got stuck at exactly the point where everyone seems stuck: she says "on a par" is just a name for our confusion about vague or hard-to-weigh comparisons, a relabeling rather than a discovery, and I say that if it were mere vagueness the small-improvement test would behave differently — a tiny nudge to one side should resolve a vague tie, but in the genuinely hard cases the nudged option often still doesn't win cleanly. And around we went.
