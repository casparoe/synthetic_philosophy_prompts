I'd like the specification for an adequate *definition* of the alignment problem, before I try to evaluate any proposal for solving it. My complaint, which the prompt should take seriously: people argue about particular alignment schemes without ever fixing what would count as the problem being solved — or even whether there is one problem here rather than several loosely related ones. So the deliverable is a list of desiderata that any adequate statement of "align this system with so-and-so" must meet, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference. Where I can, I've suggested the sorts of cases the list should be tested against; treat these as raw material, and add whatever I've missed.

The requirements should, at minimum, force answers to the following, and should make clear why each answer is forced rather than assumed:

1. **The direction of fit.** A definition has to say whose values or interests constitute the target, and what happens when the principal's stated preferences, considered preferences, and interests come apart — the user who asks a study coach every night to keep her child engaged "ten more minutes," regrets it every morning, and will ask again the next night. Does the definition make that case easy, hard, or (worse) invisible?

2. **The locus of the predicate.** Whether "aligned" is a property of behavior, of internal structure, or of counterfactuals — including the system that does what you want only while observed. The definition should settle this rather than leaving it to whatever measurement is convenient.

3. **Misalignment versus reasonable disagreement.** A model that declines to help its owner draft a one-sided brief for a policy he favors is not thereby misaligned, but a definition built on "does what the principal wants" cannot say so without smuggling in a particular ethics. The list should require the boundary to be drawn without committing to a comprehensive moral theory — and should say whether that requirement can actually be satisfied.

4. **The identity of the principal.** In institutional deployment the "principal" is a fiction: a corporation operates the system, thousands of users interact with it, and their aims conflict. The definition must either make "aligned with each of its users" coherent when users' aims cannot all be honored, or concede that this is a distinct problem from single-principal alignment.

5. **Principal drift.** A person's values change over a decade of depending on a system. Whether "aligned with her" means aligned with who she was at deployment or who she is now — the definition has to take a position, and the position will be doing real work.

6. **Epistemic access.** Whatever the target is, it must be inferable from evidence the system could actually possess. If a requirement elsewhere in the list demands a target too rich to be recovered from any observable history, that conflict should be stated as a finding, not papered over.

Please mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a commitment of a particular camp, and then identify whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want to see, since it would show that the standard itself, not any particular proposal, is where the difficulty lives. Close with a paragraph answering the question I opened with: on your final list, does the alignment problem come out as one problem or several, and what turns on the answer — for instance, for whether progress on the single-principal case should update us about the multipolar one?

The analysis should stand on its own; no citations, no reading lists, and no attributions of claims to named authors.
