I teach an intro stats course for social science undergrads — about 25 students, one 75-minute session, whiteboard and printed handouts but no laptops. Design an in-class activity for Simpson's paradox. My friend (we argued about this for like two hours last friday, he also teaches stats at a different school) insists the right move is to just show them a famous real-world case on a slide and let the reversal do the work — his claim is basically that once you've seen a trend flip once, the idea "clicks" and you don't need anything else. I think that's wrong, and it's kind of the reason I'm asking you: I want the reversal to happen TO them, in data they themselves produced or at least committed to judgments on before seeing the breakdown. So the activity should front-load a decision they make on the aggregate numbers — with real stakes somehow, maybe a ranking they have to defend publicly, or points, I don't know, you figure out something that works without money — and only then hand them the disaggregated version and let them discover that their confident call doesn't survive.

Two design constraints from the argument with my friend, feel free to push back on either. First, I think a fictional or neutral scenario (made-up departments, made-up treatments, whatever) is better than a loaded real-world case like the admissions or salary-gap data, because with the real cases students start fighting about whether the world is like that and never actually look at the arithmetic — he thinks the realism is what makes it stick. Tell me who's closer to right and design accordingly. Second, he swears the group-size asymmetry doesn't need to be set up carefully, you can just have one subgroup be much smaller and it'll happen naturally; I suspect you have to engineer the subgroup sizes pretty deliberately or the reversal won't actually materialize when I run it, and an activity where the trick doesn't fire is worse than a slide. Please include: the exact rules and materials, a timeline, how the debrief should map what happened onto the formal statement of the paradox, the misconceptions I should expect (the one I keep hitting when I lecture on this is students walking away thinking it's a small-sample artifact that goes away with more data — tell me where in the sequence to kill that), and an honest note on what part of the paradox an activity like this fundamentally can't reproduce and I'll have to just assert at the end.

Also — and this is the part I really want your judgment on — should the participants get to choose which aggregation to use at some point, with some of them choosing the disaggregated view for motivated reasons (because it flatters their earlier call) and others not? That feels to me like the closest an activity can get to the real lesson, which is that the aggregation choice is a substantive one, not a technicality. But I can see it derailing into students just picking whatever confirms their prior. Design it one way or the other and defend the choice. End your response with a one-sentence summary of the activity on the very last line.
