As part of my coursework on collective action problems (a third-year elective in political economy cross-listed with philosophy), we've been studying multipolar traps: situations in which many agents, each doing what is individually rational, converge on an outcome that is worse for every one of them than some available alternative. Our instructor emphasized that the tragedy here is structural rather than moral — the first agent to defect from a destructive race captures the gains, the last one absorbs the costs, and so no participant can afford restraint while the race is on. We covered canonical examples (arms races, advertising wars, overfishing, grade inflation, journal publishing norms) and some more contemporary framings, and I've become fascinated by how hard it is to even *talk about* the equilibrium from inside any one participant' vantage point, since each participant' optimization is locally impeccable.

For the creative component of the course, I want to submit a parody of a strategy consulting deliverable, and I'd like your help drafting it. Please write roughly 650–700 words in the voice of a prestige management consultancy (invent a plausible-sounding name) presenting findings to the board of a mid-sized firm locked in a destructive price-and-quality war with its competitors. Format it as a professional report with numbered sections and headings along the lines of "Executive Summary," "Situation Assessment," "Strategic Recommendations," "Risks and Mitigations," and "Next Steps," plus a short "About the Firm" blurb at the end. The humor must come from getting the game theory exactly right rather than caricaturing it: every recommendation should be a strictly dominant strategy from the single firm' point of view — undercut further, downgrade inputs, externalize costs, accelerate cadence — while the report simultaneously documents, in metrics it dutifully tracks, the industry-wide equilibrium degrading step by step. The consultant should notice the aggregate deterioration and report it without ever connecting it to the recommendations; the framework treats the rules of the game as fixed background data, so it can only ever recommend moves within the game, and the more rigorously it optimizes, the worse the trap closes. This blind spot is the whole joke, and it should be sustained rather than winked at.

A few constraints to keep it fair to the material: at exactly one point, some exit from the race (a standards agreement, regulation, or unilateral restraint) should be mentioned and then dismissed in a single clause as "outside the engagement' scope" — that phrase or something equally dry should do the dismissing. No named real firms, consultancies, or industries; invent everything, though the invented industry should be concrete enough that the reader can picture the specific corner being cut. Keep the tone deadpan throughout: no exclamation points, no asides to the reader, no explicit editorializing. The test I'm applying is that a game theorist or an economist reading it should laugh because the payoff reasoning is correct and the equilibrium analysis is sound, while a lay reader should still come away understanding that everyone following the report' advice ends up worse off — with the report itself the only party that profits in every scenario, which perhaps deserves a line in the "About the Firm" section.
