For years I've followed the debate over why promises bind, and it has settled into a rut I would like to understand rather than referee. On one side, the practice-based camp, in the lineage of Hume and Rawls, holds that promissory obligation is the product of a social convention: by invoking the practice you accept enforcement by its rules. On another, the assurance camp, associated with Scanlon's later work, locates the wrong in deliberately inducing an expectation you then let down. On a third, the obligation arises from the exercise of a normative power — the promisor's declaration, like consent, creates a duty at will. The debate runs on well-worn counterexamples: promises to the dead, promises the promisee begs you to break, promises between two castaways who invent the institution from scratch, promises extracted by fraud, promises waived before being relied on. Each camp patches its theory to accommodate these, the others call the patches ad hoc, and nobody has moved in decades. My working hypothesis is that the three camps are not answering the same question: one is after a conceptual account of what makes something a promise at all, one a normative account of what makes breach wrong when it is wrong, and one a theory of how persons can create duties at will — and that a good deal of the fight is a verbal dispute over 'binding'. But I am unsure how much is verbal and how much is a genuine clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for without begging the question. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is, supporting your diagnosis with what each side actually says about the cases. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new distinction, a reformulated question, a shared criterion of success for accounts of promising — or else argue that no progress should be expected here, and why.
