Been reading up on social choice and I want the spec before I read any more of the actual literature on voting rules, because I keep finding papers that argue for or against a particular rule by pointing at one property and I'd rather see the whole checklist first. So heres the ask: write out the desiderata for a social choice function — a rule that takes a profile of strict preferences over three or more alternatives (from two or more voters) and returns a single winner — to count as adequate.

Things the list has to cover, at minimum: what exactly strategy-proofness is demanding (and the distinction between "no voter can ever gain by misreporting" and the weaker things people sometimes settle for, like no voter gaining from misreporting when they only know the distribution of other votes rather than the actual profile), whether group manipulation needs its own desideratum or falls out of the individual one, and what to do about the distinction between a rule being manipulable in principle versus manipulation ever being plausibly attempted by a real person. Also whether you think cardinal information (intensity of preference) has to be part of the spec or whether an ordinal rule is fine — make a call, don't just list both options.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps. And then the part I actually care about most: given the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, some subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — I want you to identify the minimal such subset and show that it really is minimal (i.e., dropping any one member of it makes the rest jointly satisfiable, or at least argue why you think so). Then run the standard escape hatches against your list: randomizing between outcomes, restricting the domain (say, single-peaked preferences), and the one a friend of mine keeps pushing, which is that we should just require that finding a profitable manipulation be computationally hard, so that no voter could realistically carry it out. Is that a legitimate weakening of the strategy-proofness desideratum or is it changing the subject — and does it conflict with anything else on your list? I suspect at least two of my desiderata are in tension with each other even before you invoke the theorem, so tell me if you agree.

No bibliographies or reading lists please — I want the reasoning, not pointers to it. And at the very end, put a single line exactly in this form:
