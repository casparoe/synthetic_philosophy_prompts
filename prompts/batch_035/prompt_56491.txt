I teach a 200-level metaphysics course, and every spring I do a unit on time where the Rietdijk-Putnam argument shows up for a week before we move on to endurantism vs. perdurantism and everyone forgets relativity again. This year a colleague in physics lent me a popular book she likes (I'll leave it nameless, you've probably seen it on an airport shelf) that has a two-paragraph rundown of the argument, and I'm tempted to use it as a find-what's-off exercise for my students -- but I'd want a key first, and honestly I'd like to know myself where it goes wrong, since some of it reads as basically fine and some of it sets off my alarm bells in ways I can't fully articulate. Here's what the book says, near as I can transcribe it: "In 1967 Hilary Putnam proved that time doesn't flow. His argument, which he took from a Dutch physicist, starts from the fact that relativity abolished the privileged 'now,' so two observers moving past each other genuinely disagree about which distant events are simultaneous with the present moment. But whatever is simultaneous with your present is real, period -- that's just what the man in the street means by 'real.' Since one of those observers, moving toward a distant galaxy, has your future in his present, your future is real already, so it's fixed, so it's already been decided. Determinism follows, and free will is an illusion. Einstein himself agreed, writing to a grieving family that the split between past, present, and future is just a stubborn illusion."

So that's the thing I need audited. What I want is a bit-by-bit breakdown: which sentences a careful philosopher of physics would accept more or less as stated, which are subtly distorted (I have a feeling the slide from "real" to "fixed" to "decided" is doing illicit work, and something about presenting the simultaneity-equals-reality premise as a trivial definition rather than the contested move it obviously is), and whether the free will conclusion actually follows from whatever accurate core survives the audit. A couple of wrinkles you should know about: I half remember that the Dutch physicist's original paper, which came out a year or so before Putnam's, literally had "determinism" in its title, while Putnam's piece was framed differently -- so the book may be running the two versions together, and I'd like to know if that matters for the audit. Also the Einstein line is a paraphrase of a condolence letter, not a physics paper, so tell me whether it's doing any argumentative work or is just decoration. And if the summary were repaired into its strongest form, what's the best objection to it? I dimly recall a guy named Stein wrote a famous reply somewhere in the late 60s that something something "the present is local," but I've never actually chased it down, so sketch the objection even if you can't pin the citation. My students are sharp but new to relativity, so keep the spacetime stuff concrete -- the two-passers-by-and-a-distant-galaxy example is about the level of machinery they can handle. And please be honest about which of my own suspicions above are right and which are me being paranoid, I've been wrong about this stuff before (I used to think the twin paradox was a real paradox, don't ask).
