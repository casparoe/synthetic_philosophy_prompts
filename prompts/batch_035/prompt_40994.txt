I'm writing a term paper on the badness of pain, and somewhere in it an argument has gone wrong, but I can't locate the fault, and I'm starting to suspect it may be more than one fault or none.

The dialectical situation is this. I begin from pain asymbolia, the condition in which a patient still feels pain but is no longer bothered by it. I then derive, by a short chain of steps, a general thesis about the badness of experience. Applied to a further case, that thesis yields a conclusion I cannot accept. My problem is that every step seemed undeniable when I wrote it, and I cannot tell whether the mistake is in the derivation from asymbolia, in the generalization, or whether I must simply swallow the conclusion. Here is the argument as it stands in the draft.

(1) In pain asymbolia, the patient still feels pain: asked, she reports its location, rates its intensity, and describes its quality much as before; but she is not bothered by it — she does not withdraw the stimulated limb, can converse through the stimulus, and sometimes appears faintly amused by the procedure.

(2) The syndrome dissociates two components that normally travel together: the sensory-discriminative component, how the pain feels, and the affective-motivational component, the aversion to it, the urge to make it stop.

(3) Asymbolia removes the second component and leaves the first; that is why the patient reports the pain as unchanged in character while acting as though it were nothing.

(4) If the badness of pain were intrinsic to the felt quality itself, then the asymbolic's pain would be exactly as bad as the same sensation felt by you or me; badness would travel with the quality.

(5) The asymbolic's pain is not exactly as bad as ours: nothing bad is happening to her, and it is not the case that we would be doing her a favor by taking the sensation away in the face of her indifference.

(6) Therefore the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the felt quality; it resides in the aversive component that asymbolia spares the patient.

(7) What holds of pain in this respect holds of experience generally: the badness of an experience for a subject consists in the subject's aversion to it, not in the experience's qualitative character; the character is at most the aversion's occasion.

(8) So for any experience whatever — grief, shame, dread, boredom — if the subject has no aversion to it, then it is not bad for her, however it feels from the inside or however it looks from the outside.

(9) Now consider a severely depressed patient who has lost the capacity to care. She reports her state accurately, in much the terms she used before her illness, but nothing in her now wants anything; in particular, she does not want her condition to end.

(10) By (8), nothing is bad for her.

(11) That is not credible. Her condition is bad for her, and the distinctive horror of it is that she can no longer feel how bad it is.

When I wrote steps (1) through (6) they struck me as watertight, and step (7) seemed like nothing more than the same move made again — if the badness of pain is the aversion, why should pain be special? Yet the destination is (10), and I will not go there. I have tried to break the argument at several points and failed to satisfy myself at any of them. Rejecting (5) appears to commit me to saying that the asymbolic is being harmed while serenely not minding, which seems to inflate badness beyond anything that shows up in the patient's life; but rejecting (7) while keeping (6) looks ad hoc, as though pain got to be the one experience whose badness outstrips the subject's aversions, and I have no independent story about why. There is also a nagging thought that the trouble might sit further back, in (3) or even (1), on some view about what pain even is, but I have not been able to make that thought precise. I would most like to keep (11); I am prepared to give up almost anything else.

Please tell me, in your own voice, which step or steps I should reject, and make the diagnosis genuinely diagnostic rather than a verdict: name the exact point where the chain breaks or becomes contestable, explain why that point looked innocent to me, and then price the alternatives — what each candidate rejection costs elsewhere in my paper, including the costs I have not noticed. Then, separately, if the argument can be repaired rather than merely amputated — if there is a reading of some step on which the derivation from asymbolia survives without the generalization, or vice versa — sketch the repaired version in numbered steps.
