I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a piece on how much credit a scientific theory deserves for explaining facts that were already known. My editor keeps repeating the line that explaining what everyone already knew earns a theory no credit, most recently to argue that we should stop counting general relativity's treatment of Mercury's anomalous orbital drift — a puzzle astronomers had measured for half a century before 1915 — as evidence for Einstein, since Einstein knew the number he had to hit before he started. A colleague on our data desk says something similar about machine learning: you cannot score a model on the data it was tuned on. The maxim feels obviously right to me in the machine learning case and shaky in the physics case, and I cannot yet say why, which is why I want the sentence taken apart before I file.

What distinct precise claims might the maxim be expressing? I can think of several candidates: a rule of scientific practice about what should persuade a community, versus what should persuade an individual who already holds the facts as background knowledge; a claim about the mathematics of Bayesian updating, where evidence already absorbed into one's credences has no further likelihood work to do; a reconstructionist proposal that confirmation by old evidence should instead be measured against what one would have believed before learning it; a historical claim about how the Mercury anomaly actually functioned in the reception of general relativity; and a methodological point about out-of-sample testing that transfers neatly to machine learning but perhaps not to theory confirmation at all. There may be readings I have missed.

Please lay the readings out as a numbered menu, and under each give a one-sentence verdict — true, false, or true-but-failing-to-support-my-editor — plus a brief note on whether that reading would support refusing Einstein credit for the Mercury explanation. Then close with two or three sentences on which reading my editor is most plausibly relying on, and whether the machine learning analogy is doing him any favors. Keep the whole reply under 400 words.
