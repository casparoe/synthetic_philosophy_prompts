I should say at the outset that I come to this as an outsider. I spent forty-one years in experimental physiology, most of them recording from the nervous systems of crustaceans and insects, and only after retiring did I begin reading philosophy of mind in earnest. I have noticed that the philosophers' debate about moral status seems to assume a single ladder: the thermostat at the bottom, then perhaps the nematode, the insect, the fish, on up to the great apes and ourselves, with each rung assigned a quantity of moral considerability. My own laboratory experience sits uneasily with that picture, because the animals I worked on did not seem to have a quantity of anything. The mechanisms that mediate escape from noxious stimuli, the mechanisms that mediate something like anxiety before a predictable shock, the mechanisms that mediate long-term tradeoffs between food and danger — these developed at different times, can be dissociated by different interventions, and fail independently of one another. So I have drifted toward a view I have not seen stated in quite this form, and I would like an essay that evaluates it seriously.

The view, which for lack of a better term I will call dimension-wise moral status, holds that moral status is both a matter of degree and a matter of kind: it attaches not to organisms as wholes but to particular affective and perceptual capacities, each of which admits of gradations. On this view a given animal can rank rather high on one dimension and rather low on another, and the moral weight of an intervention should track which dimension the intervention engages. If the view is right, some practical questions become strangely structured: whether a pest-control method is preferable to another would depend on whether it acts through the dimension that instantiates the higher status, and whether anesthesia that suppresses one capacity but not another leaves the patient partly unprotectable. I would like the essay to do four things.

First, state the view precisely, and distinguish it both from the familiar single-ladder picture and from the simple claim that sentience comes in degrees.

Second, test whether the view can stop where it must. My own discipline's favorite concepts — irritability, negative feedback, homeostasis — were all defined broadly enough that a thermostat satisfies them, and I have seen colleagues jest that a good thermostat is one-fortieth of an animal. A view that locates status in capacities must explain why the thermostat's capacity for error correction earns it nothing, without smuggling back in a ladder of organisms. If the principled stopping point cannot be found, say so plainly.

Third, work through at least three concrete cases across the insect-to-therapist span: an insecticide that acts on escape circuitry versus one that acts on the circuits underlying longer-horizon states; an anesthetic regime that abolishes one dimension while leaving another intact; and metamorphosis, in which a nervous system is largely rebuilt while the animal persists — asking whether status can change discontinuously under the view even though the organism does not.

Fourth, reach a verdict: is dimension-wise moral status an improvement over the scalar picture, or does it merely relocate the difficulties, for instance into the question of how weights across different dimensions are to be compared at all?

Please argue from the ideas rather than the literature; I cannot check references, and I would rather see the reasoning. And since I am new to this field and may be missing something obvious to you, close the essay with a short section in which you name the consideration most likely to move you to the opposite verdict, and identify the step in your own reasoning you regard as weakest.

Aim for roughly 2,000 to 2,500 words, in a formal register, with numbered sections corresponding to the four tasks above.
