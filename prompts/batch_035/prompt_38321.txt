I spent the better part of four decades as an entomologist with an agricultural research service, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of biology in the evenings — most recently Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme," which at last put a name to a habit of mind I had watched for years without being able to describe it.

The person I have in mind was my doctoral supervisor, an old-school population geneticist with a legendary intolerance for what he called storytelling. Over many years of seminars, grant panels, and long arguments at the pub, he handed down a stream of verdicts on whether this or that trait was an adaptation or merely an incidental byproduct, and rereading my old notes I find that his track record no longer looks like the single rule I always assumed he was applying. I would like your help reconstructing what rule or rules he was actually running, and then extrapolating them honestly, because there is a set of traits I never got to put to him.

His verdicts, as I recorded them at the time:

1. Industrial melanism in the peppered moth: adaptation. He regarded the mark–release–recapture experiments as the standard of proof and considered the case closed.
2. Sickle-cell heterozygosity in malarial regions: adaptation. The covariance of the allele's frequency with malaria endemicity was, to him, a textbook demonstration.
3. Fever: adaptation. The metabolic expense of it did not bother him — an expensive response maintained against constant pressure to lose it was exactly what selection ought to look like.
4. The human chin: byproduct. He dismissed the literature on chins as fitness signals as stories invented after the fact.
5. The female orgasm: byproduct, on much the same grounds.
6. Goosebumps in humans: byproduct, though he readily granted that the same machinery was an adaptation in our furred ancestors.
7. Male nipples: byproduct, and he would add "developmentally obvious" before anyone could ask.

The new cases I would have put to him are these:

(a) Literacy. A region of the visual cortex reliably becomes specialized for written words in readers, which looks uncannily like design, but reading is far too recent for genes and was never universal even within populations.
(b) Lactase persistence in pastoral populations. A single regulatory change, apparently cheap to carry, with strong evidence of selection and a striking geographic match to dairying.
(c) Human musicality. Universal, costly, developmentally early, and supported by no evidence of selection that anyone agrees on.
(d) The palmar grasp reflex in human infants. It does nothing for us now, and its structural cousin does obvious work in infant monkeys.

I am less interested in the four answers, which I could half-guess myself, than in the analysis: What rules actually fit his track record? Is he demanding independent evidence of selection before granting the title of adaptation? Or is he classifying by whether the trait currently does anything and can or cannot be explained as a side effect of something else that was selected? Or is some third rule doing the work? Where do the plausible rules diverge across the four new cases, and which of his recorded verdicts is the closest analog for each? Give your best prediction for each case with your reasons for preferring it, and then tell me which further trait — not one of the four — would best discriminate between the rival rules, so that if I could convene a séance I would know the single question to put to him.
