You are a professional philosopher working in philosophy of religion and modal logic, asked to prepare material for a graduate seminar. Think the problem through carefully before writing; I want considered judgments, not boilerplate from the literature.

Before the seminar takes up any version of the ontological argument, I want the standard of adequacy fixed in advance, so that we are not grading each version by ad hoc lights. Write the desiderata that any version of the ontological argument must meet in order to count, for our purposes, as a serious candidate rather than a word-game. Our purpose, which fixes the standard: a version that would put a logically competent agnostic, initially unmoved, under real pressure — one where rejecting the conclusion forces the rejection of some premise whose denial carries independent costs, rather than a stipulation that smuggles the conclusion in.

Cover at least the following, and add anything else you think belongs on the list:

1. Transparent validity: the modal system used must be stated, and the load-bearing use of its strongest axiom — the S5 move from possibly-necessary to necessary — must be flagged, not buried in the symbolism.
2. Non-question-begging in a specific sense: the conclusion must not be a definitional unpacking of a premise. For axiomatic versions in Gödel's style, say where the substantive premise is supposed to sit — the positivity axioms? the definition of a God-like being? — since there the conclusion is a theorem and the question is whether accepting the axioms is anything more than accepting theism in disguise.
3. Parody immunity, the Gaunilo constraint: no structurally analogous argument with an absurd conclusion should go through. Explain why axiomatic versions are supposed to be immune — I take it the thought is that Gaunilo's island has nothing corresponding to the closure and necessity axioms for positivity — but say whether that immunity is real or just relabeled question-begging.
4. A proves-too-much test: if the premises entail that whatever is true is necessarily true — modal collapse, as the Gödel axioms are often said to entail — the argument disqualifies itself by proving more than anyone would accept.
5. Consistency with the classical objections: the argument must locate, not merely dodge, the point of Kant's complaint that existence is not a real predicate, and must say how "exists" is functioning in its own premises.
6. Epistemic ascent: no premise may be less antecedently credible than the conclusion is supposed to be.

State each desideratum so it is testable — a version either meets it or fails it, with a determinate counterexample — and mark each as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp. My own suspicion is that the epistemic-ascent demand, in its strong form, is a prejudice of the empiricist camp; argue that either way. Also flag any pair of desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable; my candidate is full parody immunity together with non-question-begging, since blocking the parody seems to require exactly the necessity-involving axioms that let the definition carry the argument. If some subset really is unsatisfiable, say so plainly — that would be the most interesting result of the list.

Then, briefly — at most a quarter of your total — score three versions against the list: Anselm's reasoning in Proslogion 2 about that-than-which-a-greater-cannot-be-thought existing in the understanding; a Plantinga-style maximal-greatness modal version; and Gödel's axiomatic version with positive properties, essences, and necessary existence. For each, name the single desideratum it most clearly fails and the one on which it outperforms the others. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.

Hard limit: 800 words total. Number the desiderata, one short motivation each; present the scorecard as a compact table. No bibliography.
