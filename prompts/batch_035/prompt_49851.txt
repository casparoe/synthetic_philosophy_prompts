I'm taking a philosophy of science course this semester on chance and probability, and we just finished the unit on deference principles — the idea, roughly, that if you know the objective chance of an outcome, your credence in that outcome should match it. My professor made an offhand remark that modern meteorology is perhaps the largest functioning institutional deployment of a theory of objective chance, and it's been stuck in my head since, because the more I look at how probabilistic weather forecasting actually works, the less sure I am which theory of chance it's deploying.

Here's the practice as I understand it, and please correct any details I've gotten wrong. Weather services issue probability-of-precipitation forecasts as single numbers — a 70% chance of rain tomorrow — where the official definition is something like the probability of measurable precipitation at any point in the forecast area over the forecast period. The service's accuracy is then evaluated not by whether each individual forecast was vindicated or refuted, but by calibration across a long run of forecasts: a forecaster who says 70% should be followed by rain roughly seven times in ten, and their skill scores (Brier scores and the like) are computed over the whole track record. Yet the ordinary user takes the very same number and makes a one-off decision with it — umbrella or no umbrella for tomorrow, a single unrepeatable day.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit metaphysics and epistemology of this two-level practice. Does issuing single-number forecasts for particular days commit the service to single-case chances — propensities, or something like them, attaching to tomorrow-at-my-house specifically — or only to long-run frequencies, with the daily percentage a kind of bookkeeping fiction? Could a strict frequentist and a propensity theorist both run the weather service without changing anything observable, or does some part of the practice (say, the way forecasts respond to newly arrived atmospheric data) favor one? Second, when I take the 70% as my credence for tomorrow, what exactly am I deferring to: an objective chance, or the forecaster's credence — and does that distinction matter for whether my background knowledge is "admissible," in the deference-principle sense? There's also a reference-class puzzle lurking in the definition: the official number covers a point in an area over a period, but users treat it as the chance of rain on them, and forecasters get complaints when it pours on part of the forecast area on a day that "said 30%." Does the practice presuppose a principled fact about which reference class is the relevant one, or is that left indeterminate? Finally, tell me whether the calibration-based scoring quietly commits the whole institution to one particular view of chance, or whether it's genuinely underdetermined — and if the latter, what a rival theory would have to change to make the practice its own.
