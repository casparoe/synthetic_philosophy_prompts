I'm a science and ideas journalist — mostly books and features, the odd exhibition review when it touches on something I cover. Recently I wrote a piece about a big climate-data installation (sound, light, the whole thing) that I never actually visited: deadline crunch, the venue was a six-hour train ride away, and I built the piece out of the press kit, a video walkthrough, and interviews with the artist and two people who'd seen it. My editor ran it with a disclaimer, fine. But then a colleague whose judgment I actually respect told me, over coffee, "you have to see it for yourself — you can't get art secondhand," and now I can't stop chewing on it, because I'm honestly not sure if she was making a deep point about art or just calling me lazy.

So here's what I want. Act as a professional philosopher who works on epistemology and aesthetics, and think carefully before answering — maybe do a quick brainstorm of candidate readings first, then commit. Take that one sentence, "you have to see it for yourself," and pull apart the different precise claims it could be making. I'd guess some readings are trivial platitudes (like, duh, being physically present gives you more sensory information than a video does), some are substantive and maybe defensible, some are probably flat-out false, and at least one is an interesting old philosophical position about whether aesthetic judgments can legitimately be formed on the basis of testimony at all — I have a feeling there's a real debate lurking there but I don't know the terrain. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible and, crucially, whether it would actually support my colleague's use of it — i.e., whether it makes my secondhand piece illegitimate, or merely incomplete, or just makes it a different genre of writing.

A few things I want you to keep in mind. The aesthetic case might be special — knowing a fact from testimony feels different from coming to find something moving or beautiful from someone else's report — but I'd want you to say whether that difference is real or whether I'm romanticizing art compared to, say, forming beliefs about a physics result I haven't reproduced in a lab. Also, my piece wasn't pure testimony: interviews with the artist are testimony, but the video walkthrough is a degraded copy of the experience, and that seems like it might need its own treatment. And finally be honest if one of the readings supports my colleague to the point where I owe the artist an apology — lay the readings out as a numbered menu with verdicts, not as a defense of me, because I can already tell I want this to come out my way and I don't trust my own framing. thanks in advance.
