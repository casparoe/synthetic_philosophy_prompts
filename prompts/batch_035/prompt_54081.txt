I've been keeping a list of cases in which something looks to me like a reason exists in the world without any reasoner grasping it — what Dennett calls a free-floating rationale — and I'd like to know whether my verdicts add up to a coherent principle or whether something has to give. The cases, with how I find myself judging them:

The wing pattern of a moth that matches the lichen it rests on. The pattern is there because it conceals the moth from birds, and that because-statement was true for millions of years before any biologist or any moth worked it out. Verdict: clearly a free-floating rationale.

Vervet monkeys' alarm calls — a distinct call for leopards, another for eagles, another for snakes, present in the repertoire before infants have seen the predator. The call carries information about leopards and functions to warn, though no monkey intends the semantics or could state it. Verdict: yes, rationale floats here too.

A rule of the common law that survived not because any judge chose it deliberately, but because it happened to assign liability to the party who could avoid the accident most cheaply, and courts kept re-encountering and re-applying it. Nobody ever had the efficiency rationale in mind; it did the preserving rather than being the preserver's purpose. Verdict: yes.

In a long-running simulation of the iterated prisoner's dilemma, a reciprocating strategy emerges and holds the population — something in the vicinity of tit-for-tat — so that the strategy is, in some sense, for sustained mutual cooperation, though nothing in the simulation knows what cooperation is. Verdict: yes, same category as the moth.

A reinforcement-learning agent, trained once by gradient descent in a single run — no population of agents, no reproduction, no copying of successful individuals — that learns to position itself between an opponent and the goal. Its policy seems to embody a reason about the geometry of the situation that nobody, not even its designers, ever articulated. Verdict: yes, and yet I can feel myself less sure than with the previous four.

A thermostat set to twenty degrees. The room stays near twenty because the thermostat keeps it there — there is a genuine because — but I want to say the rationale is not floating: it is anchored in the engineers who comprehended exactly what they were doing. Verdict: no.

A modern processor, in which no single engineer comprehends the whole artifact from layout to microcode, yet whose every level was designed by people who grasped the rationale for what they were building at their level. Verdict: no — incomprehension spread across a team of comprehenders doesn't make the rationale float.

A crystal's facets, which are the shape they are so that bonding energy is minimized. Verdict: no — this looks like a reason without a reasoner, but I insist it isn't one.

So I have a problem. My tentative principle is that a rationale floats when the item carrying it was shaped by a selection process that differentially preserves what works, with nothing anywhere needing to grasp why it works. That handles the moth, the alarm calls, and the simulation nicely, and it excludes the crystal. But it puts pressure on at least three of my verdicts: it threatens the single-run RL agent, since one training pass is a long way from a lineage of selection — or is gradient descent itself a selection process among parameter settings? It sits awkwardly with the common-law rule, unless the practice of following precedent counts as a copying mechanism; and it makes me unsure what to say about a thermostat wired by accident, or by someone who misunderstood what they were doing.

I'd like you to do reflective equilibrium on this list. Please state, as precisely as you can, the principle or small set of principles that best captures my verdicts, motivating each clause by the case that forces it; then say which of my verdicts that principle cannot accommodate, and argue for keeping or overturning them; and then apply the result to some cases I haven't listed — in particular, the first mutant moth with the camouflage pattern before selection has had a single generation to confirm it, and an antenna shape produced by an evolutionary algorithm and bolted onto a spacecraft after being selected for performance without anyone understanding why it works. If the principle you offer has a rival that saves different verdicts more simply, I'd like to see the tradeoff stated honestly rather than settled by stipulation.
