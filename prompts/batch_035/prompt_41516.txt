I'm part of a small reading group on program equilibrium and open-source game theory — the corner of decision theory where agents can read one another's source code before choosing their moves, so cooperation can rest on each side verifying how the other decides. My colleague Maya has the strongest views in the group about which agents ought to cooperate with which, and since she's been remarkably consistent, we've started treating her past verdicts as precedents whenever a new scenario comes up. Her track record so far: (1) facing a byte-for-byte copy of her own agent in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with payoffs of 3 for mutual cooperation, 1 for mutual defection, and 5 for defecting against a cooperator, she says both copies should cooperate; (2) against an agent whose readable code simply says defect always, she defects; (3) against Mirror, an agent with a completely different architecture, no shared code, and unrelated goals, whose code reads cooperate with the opponent iff the opponent will cooperate with Mirror — and whose predictions are reliable — she cooperates; (4) against Leverage, a transparent agent offering mutual cooperation worth 5 to each side (versus 1 each for mutual defection), but conditional on a side transfer of 2 utility from her agent to Leverage itself, she refuses to pay and lets the deal collapse, even though paying would leave her agent better off overall.

The case we have to decide this week is trickier, and Maya is at a conference and unreachable. Our agent plays two separate one-shot games. In Game 1, against Quid, the payoffs are exactly the prisoner's dilemma numbers above. In Game 2, against a third agent called Bot, the payoffs are 5 for Bot no matter what, 1 for us if we defect, and 0 for us if we cooperate — because Bot's readable code defects unconditionally. Quid's readable code says: cooperate in Game 1 iff the opponent cooperates with Bot in Game 2. Quid receives nothing from Game 2; the concession goes entirely to Bot, whose behavior is fixed either way. So the package deal — cooperate with Bot (costing 1 against defecting there) in order to secure mutual cooperation with Quid (gaining 2 over mutual defection) — nets out one point better than defecting everywhere. Half the group calls Quid's condition a harmless loyalty test, a sort of membership fee routed through its ally; the other half calls it a racket dressed up as one, since it requires subsidizing an unconditional defector, which is exactly the kind of concession Maya refused in the Leverage case.

Please act as an expert decision theorist advising our group, and reason carefully before committing to anything: brainstorm the candidate decision rules that could fit all four of Maya's precedents, and only then settle on a recommendation. I want the answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than a few sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your bottom-line recommendation on the Quid/Bot package and the one-sentence principle it commits us to; (2) the candidate rules consistent with all four precedents and what each predicts for the new case, noting which precedents rule out pure similarity-based cooperation and naive expected-value maximization; (3) the precedent you take to be the closest analog and why the disanalogies matter; (4) your best guess at which rule Maya is actually running, and what would make you doubt it; and (5) a further case — with its payoffs and code conditions spelled out — that would cleanly discriminate between the rules that diverge on Quid, along with each rule's verdict on it.
