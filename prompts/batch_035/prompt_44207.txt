so i'm a third-year phd student in developmental psych, and my program has this tradition where every couple years you write up a "position statement" on your area and the rest of the cohort gets to poke at it. mine is on genes and cognitive development, and before i present i want to know whether what i've written actually hangs together or whether i'm going to get taken apart by the one girl in my cohort who did her masters in behavior genetics. here's roughly where i've landed:

(1) i think the behavior geneticists basicly won the numbers fight. twin and adoption studies put adult IQ at somewhere around 60-80% heritable in contemporary western samples, and i don't believe the equal environment assumption is violated badly enough, or that shared prenatal environments are confounded enough, to make those numbers garbage.

(2) i think heritability is a population-level statistic about variance. it's a fact about a particular population living in a particular range of environments, it says nothing about any individual kid, and it can in principle change if the enviroment changes. i roll my eyes when people talk about "the heritability of X" as if it were a property of the trait itself, like a boiling point.

(3) i accept the standard line that within-group heritability tells you nothing about the causes of between-group differences. whatever one thinks about group gaps, the fact that a trait is highly heritable within each population doesn't settle anything about the gap.

(4) that said, in a seminar paper last term i argued that because adult IQ is this heritable, we should expect early intervention effects on cognitive outcomes to fade out, and that there's basically a ceiling on how much environment can move the trait. i still think that's right.

(5) from the adoption literature i also accept that shared environmental influences on IQ are close to zero by adulthood. but i'm also pretty committed to childhood SES causally shaping adult cognitive outcomes — lead exposure, school quality, chronic stress, all the stuff i actually study. these have always struck me as two ways of saying the same thing, namely that environment matters, so i never saw a conflict there.

(6) i think gene-environment interaction is everywhere, to the point that a heritability estimate from one population at one historical moment can't be projected onto another. the 60-80% number is a snapshot, not a constant of nature.

(7) and i've never fully resolved why i'm not more bothered that population mean IQ rose something like three points a decade through the twentieth century for clearly environmental reasons, while (1) says the differences between people within a generation are mostly genetic. people keep assuring me these are compatible and i sort of nod along without seeing it.

so that's the set. go through it and tell me which of these commitments are actually in tension, which only look like it, and how badly. rank the tensions by severity instead of just listing everything as equally fatal, and for each real one tell me what would have to give and what it costs me to give it. i genuinely don't know where the problems are, so don't pull any punches to spare me — i'd rather hear it from you than in front of the whole cohort. and please no formatting in your reply: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. just continuous prose, like you're writing me an email before the presentation.
