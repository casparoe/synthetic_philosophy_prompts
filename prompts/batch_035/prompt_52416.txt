I've been around LessWrong since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've grown weary of how the experience machine gets flattened in our discussions — it's become a shorthand for "wireheading" and little else, with the interesting questions about well-being left unexamined. I'd like a short story that puts the pressure back in the right places.

Write a story (roughly 1,500–2,500 words) about a woman who works as a fidelity auditor for a company that operates experience machines marketed as memory-optimized vacations: clients emerge with indistinguishable-from-real memories of trips they never took, at a fraction of the cost and none of the jet lag. Her job is to enter the machines wearing an audit token that preserves her memory of which sessions are real and which are simulated, so she can compare, say, a genuine weekend in Kyoto against the machine's version of the same, and certify the fidelity of the simulation. The complication: she's started to suspect that her audit token is itself among the things the machine can edit, so she can no longer be certain that any of her comparison baselines — including her sense of what her life outside the machine is like — weren't manufactured.

I'd like the story to do at least two things. First, use her epistemic predicament to sharpen the actual philosophical stakes: if the client can't distinguish the real Kyoto from the simulated one, what exactly is lost, and for whom — is the deficit in her experiences, in her life as a whole, or only in the third-person facts about her history that she happens to value? Second, resist the obvious resolution. Do not end with the revelation that she has been in the machine the whole time, or that her whole life is a simulation — I consider that twist a way of dodging the questions rather than facing them. Instead, let the story end on her making some concrete choice, small or large, and let the reader see which theory of well-being she's implicitly betting on when she makes it — without the story ever naming a philosopher or theory.

Tone: grounded and literary rather than gadget-heavy, and please avoid both utopian salesmanship and dystopian moralizing; the company should be neither sinister nor benign, just ordinary.
