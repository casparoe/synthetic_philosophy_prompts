I've been following the Great Man theory debates on LessWrong for years, and I've come away persuaded that the anti-Hitler counterfactual is the cleanest stress test of the whole framework: Sidney Hook's point that some historical junctures are "eventful" while others are merely "eventful-adjacent" (I'm paraphrasing him badly, I suspect) seems to me to capture why individuals matter sometimes and not others. I want to stress-test my position against a serious opponent rather than the usual online strawmen, so here is what I'd like you to do: play a committed structuralist historian of interwar Germany. Not a caricature, and not someone who denies Hitler existed or mattered at all; your character holds that the Weimar Republic was dead by roughly the spring of 1932 regardless of who happened to be standing in front of the microphone -- that mass unemployment, the breakdown of parliamentary arithmetic, a conservative elite that had already concluded democracy was dispensable, and a revanchist public mood overdetermined some form of authoritarian, expansionist, antisemitic-nationalist regime. You are willing to concede that Hitler's specific talents shaped the *style* and tempo of what happened, but you will not concede the counterfactual that no Hitler means liberal-democratic continuity in Germany. You should argue like a working historian: mechanisms, institutions, class coalitions, not slogans. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so this feels like a real back-and-forth, and stay in character until I type the word "done".

My opening move, then: I think your overdetermination thesis quietly assumes what it needs to prove. "Some authoritarian revanchist regime" is doing enormous work in your position, because regimes differ enormously in what they *do*, and the differences we care about morally and historically -- the deliberate decision to launch a general European war in 1939, and the industrialized extermination of an entire people -- are exactly the kinds of decisions that vary with the individual in charge. Put a generic authoritarian nationalist in Hitler's chair: a Schleicher, a Papen, even a less charismatically gifted Nazi like Göring, and you plausibly get a revanchist military dictatorship that remilitarizes, bullies its neighbors, maybe grabs Austria -- and stops there, because without Hitler's peculiar blend of ideological fixation and risk-seeking, nobody around that table wants a two-front war against Britain and France, and nobody else has built their entire political identity around redemptive destruction. The structure explains the reservoir of resentment; it does not explain why anyone drew it down to the last drop and then blew up the bank. If your counterfactual "structurally equivalent" Germany looks nothing like the one we got, in the respects we actually care about, then what exactly has structure explained?

One more rule for you, since I've been burned before in these practice sessions: when I press you on the counterfactual, don't retreat into "counterfactuals are unknowable, that's why structural explanation wins" -- that move proves too much, since it would equally forbid your own claim that the authoritarian outcome was overdetermined, which is itself a counterfactual judgment. You're allowed to say the space of outcomes was constrained, but you have to tell me *how tightly* and on *which dimensions*, and then own the dimensions where you think structure is silent. Your turn.
