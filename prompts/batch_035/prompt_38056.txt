I teach an undergraduate seminar on legal interpretation, and this coming term I want to build a session around the trial scene in The Merchant of Venice (Act IV) as a case study in textualism versus purposivism. On its surface the scene looks like a textbook confrontation between the two camps: Shylock demands his bond as written — the pound of flesh — insisting that Venice's charter and its commercial reputation depend on enforcing contracts to the letter, and brushing aside both the money offered him and the appeals to mercy; Portia, in disguise as the legal expert Balthazar, first tries the language of equity, and when that fails she defeats him by out-literalizing the literalist, granting the bond but holding that he may take exactly a pound, neither more nor less, and not one drop of blood. The easy reading is that the play mocks strict construction by pushing it to absurdity. I'm not convinced it's that simple, and I'd like a reading that resists the easy allegory in both directions.

Take textualism seriously first. What would a serious textualist make of Portia's flesh-without-blood move — is it principled interpretation of the bond's terms, or a gotcha that smuggles in requirements (no blood, an exact weight) that no reasonable drafter or reader of the contract would have supplied? And note that Shylock's insistence on the letter is partly a foreigner's demand for equal treatment before the law in a hostile court: does that recast textualism as the outsider's best protection against a judge's discretion, and purposivism as the majority's instrument for exercising it? Then take purposivism seriously in its turn: the bond's evident purpose is security for a loan that has already been offered repaid several times over, so a purposivist could void the forfeiture without any trick — which suggests the scene is less a victory for interpretation than a display of what interpretive ingenuity can do when it has already chosen its winner. There is also the fact that mercy enters the scene not as interpretation at all but as sovereign grace — the Duke's pardon of Shylock's life under the statute against aliens, followed by Antonio's own conditions — which points toward the thought that equity lives outside the law rather than within it, something neither textualists nor purposivists want to concede.

Finally, be honest about where the play resists the whole exercise: the judge is a disguised fraud answering to a private party, the outcome is engineered before the legal arguments begin, and the scene is saturated with the anti-Semitism that makes any "vindication of the law" reading queasy. I want to know whether the trial ultimately dramatizes something the textualism/purposivism literature misses — that the choice between interpretive methods is itself an exercise of power, shaped by who the parties are and whom the community regards as fully a person — or whether it merely stages a rigged exception that tells us nothing about interpretation under legitimate conditions. Along the way, point me to the specific beats within the trial I should have my students track (the offer of thrice the sum, the mercy speech and its failure, the no-blood ruling, the alien statute, the Duke's pardon and Antonio's conditions), and close with what you take to be the strongest objection to your own reading, so I can put it to the class.
