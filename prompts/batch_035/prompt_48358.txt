I edit a small web journal on formal models of fairness, and for an upcoming issue on bargaining theory I want to run a diagnostic quiz along the lines of "Which bargaining solution do you actually accept?" My sense is that readers who've taken a bit of game theory all know the labels — the Nash solution, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, egalitarian, utilitarian — but haven't noticed which axioms they are and aren't willing to give up, and I want the quiz to sort them by their commitments rather than their sympathies. The setting for every question should be the standard two-person bargaining problem: a convex feasible set of utility pairs, a disagreement point, and utility assumed meaningful only up to positive affine transformation, unless a question explicitly grants interpersonal comparability for the sake of the case.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each a concrete case on which the solutions genuinely come apart, with two or three answer options apiece, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. To give the flavor I'm after: a question might turn on the fact that enlarging the feasible set so that only one bargainer's best possible outcome improves can leave that bargainer's assigned payoff unchanged or even lower under the Nash product, but never lower under Kalai–Smorodinsky's individual monotonicity condition — told as a story about, say, two musicians renegotiating their split of gig fees after one of them gains access to a better-paying market that neither can actually afford to enter yet. Alongside such cases, include at least one question about whether the disagreement point itself should influence the outcome at all — two otherwise identical negotiations where one party can credibly walk away to a survivable outside option and the other can't — since readers seem to split on that in ways that don't track their favorite solution; and at least one on symmetry, i.e., whether bargainers facing a fully symmetric feasible set must receive equal payoffs. Make sure the standard axioms each get a chance to bite where they separate solutions: Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to affine transformations, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and individual monotonicity.

The scoring key should map each combination of answers to one of the four solutions above where that's possible, and flag the combinations that don't correspond cleanly to any of them — readers who demand both individual monotonicity and independence of irrelevant alternatives, for example, or who refuse symmetry — with a sentence or two diagnosing whether each such combination is unattractive, genuinely incoherent, or merely underexplored (there are further solutions in the literature, and you may name one or two in the key if you're confident they fit a stray combination). Explicitly flag any pair of solutions the quiz cannot reliably separate rather than papering over the gap. The audience is readers with one game theory course behind them, so keep the questions themselves in plain, case-based language and confine the technical machinery to the scoring key.
