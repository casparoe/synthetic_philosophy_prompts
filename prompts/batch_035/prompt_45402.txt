A paleontologist friend of mine keeps insisting the historical archive is just the fossil record of societies, and the comparison is doing a lot of work for him. His pitch: almost no organisms fossilize (no hard parts, wrong sediments, eroded away), and almost nothing anyone does gets recorded (most people couldn't write, most of what was written didn't survive, nobody bothered preserving it) — yet paleontologists reconstruct whole ecosystems, confidently, from scraps of bone that are maybe one bone in a million. So historians, he says, should stop agonzing about the "silences" in the archive and just work their sediment. He even pushes the parallel on bias: hard parts survive the way official paper does, the rare soft-tissue windows like the Burgess Shale are like the accidental archives — a diary that survived a fire, a court transcript of somebody who never expected to be recorded — and reasoning from absence should work the same way in both fields. Can you map it out for me: what corresponds to what, where the mapping breaks, and which breaks actually matter for his optimistic conclusion versus which are just noise? My own hunch is there's one difference that sinks the whole thing — a fossil isn't trying to do anything when it forms, but a document is produced by someone for a purpose, so the archive is biased not only in what got preserved but in what got made in the first place — but I'm not sure that's disqualifying, since arguably paleontology has its own version of it somewhere (an animal that died where it was lured? probably not, actually). So: does his "stop agonizing" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, is there a better comparison that keeps the parts of his picture that were doing real work? Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, no preamble, then the reasoning — and if a correspondence table helps, include one.
