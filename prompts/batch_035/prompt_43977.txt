I run a weekly discussion group for early-career software engineers — twelve people, no formal logic background, but comfortable with expected-value reasoning and game trees. Next session I have twenty-five minutes on superrationality: Hofstadter's idea that in a symmetric, one-shot decision situation, the rational move is the one you would want everyone facing the same situation to make, since if you reason your way to a conclusion, others reasoning similarly will arrive there too — so you should treat your own choice as evidence about theirs, even though it causes nothing. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

For the carrying example, here's my draft scenario, which you should replace if it's inadequate: our apartment building has a shared kitchen, and before a one-time landlord inspection every tenant independently decides, without communication and with no prospect of future interaction, whether to spend forty minutes deep-cleaning their share; tenants are strangers but all read the same cleaning guide and are, by reputation, conscientious types. If you replace it, say what the replacement must have (symmetry, one-shot, no communication, similarity of reasoners) and what it must lack (any actual causal channel between choices, any repeated play).

What I need from the plan: the order the pieces must go in — especially whether to establish the evidential point, that my choice is information about similar reasoners' choices, before or after introducing the scenario; the misconceptions the group will predictably form, and the exact point in the sequence to head off each. I already anticipate three: that it's just Kant's categorical imperative relabeled; that it licenses thinking my decision somehow reaches out and causally determines other people's (I gather there was a well-known dispute over exactly this, but I won't name names — I'd rather you place the objection in the sequence than attribute it); and that it only applies to literally identical copies or clones, which would make it irrelevant to merely similar humans like our tenants — this last one seems to me the most dangerous, since once it takes hold the whole idea looks like a parlor trick. Please also tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level — I assume the full apparatus of evidential versus causal decision theory and anything Newcomb-shaped. End with one question I could ask the group, answerable in a sentence, that would reveal whether they've internalized the evidential point rather than just walked away with a vague feeling that cooperation is nice.
