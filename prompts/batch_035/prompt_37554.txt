The steps are numbered for reference.

(1) The replication crisis arose because researchers had many researcher degrees of freedom: choices about when to stop collecting data, which outcome measure to emphasize, which covariates to adjust for, and which analyses to report, all of which could be made after seeing which way the results were pointing.

(2) Frequentist significance testing is hostage to these choices, because a p-value is defined over the sample space — the data that could have occurred but did not — and therefore changes with the sampling plan; an analyst who repeatedly checks the data and continues collecting until p drops below .05 will publish spuriously significant results.

(3) Bayesian inference is not hostage to these choices, because the posterior depends only on the prior, the likelihood, and the observed data; two analysts with the same data and the same model reach the same posterior, whatever stopping rule either of them had in mind.

(4) Therefore the crisis was the predictable output of frequentist foundations: had those studies been given Bayesian analyses instead, most of the findings that later failed to replicate would never have been reported as findings in the first place.

(5) Therefore the replication crisis is itself an empirical refutation of the frequentist theory of evidence, and the correct lesson is to rebuild scientific inference on Bayesian foundations.

The confusing part for me is that (3) seems like a genuine feature of Bayesian inference, and (1) seems like an accurate description of how the literature operated, yet the chain somehow gets from there to a conclusion that sounds like it licenses calling an entire journal's editorial practice unscientific.
