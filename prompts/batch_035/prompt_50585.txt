I'm taking a behavioral economics course and we just covered information cascades and rational herding, and now I can't stop seeing them everywhere. Here's what's bugging me: pretty much every platform I use displays aggregate social signals — star ratings with counts, "1,200 people found this helpful," most-popular sort orders, bestseller badges, follower and like counts. The whole design seems to rest on treating these numbers as evidence: lots of people bought it and seemed happy, so it's probably good. That's the wisdom-of-crowds picture, where a bunch of roughly independent judgments average out into something reliable.

But the display itself kind of poisons the independence, doesn't it? Once I see the average rating and how many people left it, my own rating isn't an independent signal anymore — I anchor on what's already there, and I'm also more likely to click on (and then rate) stuff that's already popular. So each later rating partially just copies earlier ones, and a product that got lucky early can snowball. And the platforms seem to know this at some level — I remember reading that Instagram tested hiding like counts precisely because visible numbers were distorting behavior — yet the same companies also build trending lists and "X people bought this in the past month" badges that make popularity maximally salient. There's even a design out there where the widget collects your vote before showing you the average, which looks like a deliberate break from the norm.

So my question: what implicit epistemology does this whole practice presuppose? Does displaying running aggregates only make sense if you assume the signals are independent of one another, or can the design survive on a herding view where the numbers are partly self-fulfilling — maybe because a cascade is still evidence that the early raters were on to something? Please don't give me a neutral survey of the possibilities — take a definite position. Say whether you think the standard practice is philosophically coherent or whether it quietly presupposes two inconsistent pictures of what ratings are evidence of, and then tell me concretely what a platform that really believed the cascade worry would have to change, and what it would lose by changing it.
