I'm grading final exams for a second-year ethics course on blame and responsibility, and one students answer has me torn. Yesterday I also got into a two-hour argument with my friend (he's a philosophy PhD student) about the exact issue the answer takes a stance on — whether a reformed wrongdoer ever regains the standing to blame — and we got absolutely nowhere, so I'd rather have a fresh pair of eyes on this than trust my own judgment right now.

The exam question was: "(a) In lecture we distinguished the question whether blame is fitting from the question whether one has the standing to blame. Explain the distinction, using hypocrisy and at least one other case where standing is at issue. (b) Can someone who has committed the same wrong in the past ever have the standing to blame it in another person? Defend your answer."

The student wrote:

"The difference between standing and fittingness is about who is entitled to do the blaming, not about whether the blame is correct. Hypocrisy is the standard case: the husband who has had affairs himself loses the standing to blame his wife's affair, even though everything he says about it is true. Prudential worries are different again — maybe his blame will be counterproductive, but that's a matter of wisdom, not standing. This fits Cohen's idea that blame is in the idiom of a demand rather than a description: to blame someone is not just to register that they did wrong but to demand something of them, and a demand can only be made with clean hands. The victim case works the same way: the driver whom the drunk driver nearly killed can blame him, and being affected herself doesn't weaken her standing — if anything it strengths it. But the husband's case shows something stronger: once you've done the same thing yourself, the accusation stops being true from your mouth — the words stay the same, but coming from him they become false, which is why his wife needn't take it seriously. It's like the boy who cried wolf: the final warning about the real wolf was accurate, but nobody believed it, and that's what lost standing is. On (b): past wrongdoing can't be lived down. Either you have standing or you don't, and the once-unfaithful husband, however reformed and sincere, has lost it for good — his wife can always point to the past, and there's nothing he can say back."

Here's the thing though: part (b) is exactly what my friend and I argued about, and it's genuinely contested, so please don't grade the answer by which side it lands on — the student is entitled to a view. Grade whether the answer earns whatever it says, and above all whether the distinctions are kept straight throughout. I'd like a grade (x/20 or a letter, your choice) plus specific point-by-point comments justifying every judgement, the good and the bad, since the student can appeal the grade and I need to be able to defend it. Keep the whole thing under 350 words.
