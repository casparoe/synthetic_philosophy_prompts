I edit the explainer desk at a mid-sized news outlet. Every day my writers pitch me takes on the news of the day, and my recurring problem is deciding which items deserve a "this is bigger than it looks" piece and which deserve nothing more than a straight news brief, without that call being made by whoever argues most forcefully at the morning meeting. Some years ago I read Braudel, and his way of splitting historical time has stayed with me as a rough sorting principle: the almost motionless time of geography, climate, and enduring structures; the intermediate rhythm of economies, institutions, and social arrangements; and the fast, churning time of events, which he treated as little more than foam on the surface, brief and noisy and, in his view, a poor guide to what actually moves. I want that turned into a procedure my desk can actually run.

Please write me a decision procedure my writers can apply to any news item in about ten minutes: a fixed order of questions, each with answers that pass and answers that fail, where the failing answers end in a definite instruction — treat it as an event, cover it as news, don't commission a structural take. The procedure should mark, at each step, whether the question is one of fact that a junior writer can answer from the clip file, or one that cannot be settled that way and must be handed up to me, since Braudel's own boundaries between his tiers were fuzzy and my writers' will not be sharper. Then run the procedure on three items, one paragraph each: (1) a single regional bank failing over a weekend; (2) a steady multi-year rise in reported food-bank use; (3) the sudden collapse of a coastal fishery after decades of gradual decline. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — I would love the bank failure to be a story about a decade of eroding regulation, and if the procedure refuses to hand me that, show me exactly where it stops me and what additional evidence would have to be added to change the verdict. Close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do: whether it can ever tell us that a fast event has reset the slow structures themselves, and who at the desk is entitled to overrule it and on what grounds.

Structure the reply in this order: first, The procedure, roughly 450 words; second, The three worked cases, about 150 words each; third, Where judgment takes over, around 200 words; last, What the procedure cannot settle, around 200 words.
