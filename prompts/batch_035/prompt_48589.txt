I run the newsletter of a university philosophy society, and for the freshers' issue I want to commission a satirical "problem page" — an agony-aunt column, the kind you'd find in a glossy magazine, in which every letter concerns chance and uncertainty and each agony aunt answers strictly according to her own interpretation of probability. There should be three aunts with invented, mildly punning pen-names, one for each of frequentism, subjectivism/Bayesianism, and the propensity view, and each letter in the column should receive replies from all three (or from whichever aunts you judge the letter to torment most effectively). The letters should come from ordinary aggrieved readers: someone deliberating about whether to propose marriage and wanting to know the probability their partner will say yes, someone rattled by an eleven-head run at coin-flipping, perhaps a small-time bookmaker who has been systematically Dutch-booked by a sharp customer, or a patient who wants the odds on a one-off surgery. The comedy has to come from the interpretations themselves, faithfully rendered: the frequentist aunt must refuse, on principle, to attach a probability to any single unrepeatable event, no matter how piteously the reader begs, and can only offer them a place in a suitable reference class; the Bayesian aunt should demand the reader's priors before she will say anything at all, condition relentlessly on the reader's own evidence, and get into at least one visible squabble about whose priors are absurd; the propensity aunt must locate the chance in the physical set-up of the experiment or situation, so that changing the restaurant, the ring, or the surgeon changes the probability. Each reply should sign off with the aunt dispensing a piece of advice that her interpretation genuinely licenses — and, ideally, is genuinely useless for.

The piece should be deadpan throughout: written as an actual magazine page, with the breathless editorial framing ("Our aunts tackle your chance-iest dilemmas!") and no authorial winking, so that a reader who knows the literature can identify each aunt from her answers alone. It should be fair-minded satire — a specialist in the philosophy of probability should laugh in recognition rather than wince at a distortion, and should be able to point at each aunt's reply as an exaggeration of something the position really commits her to. Somewhere in the piece, at least one reader should complain that their question has gone unanswered, and the complaint should stand unrebutted. Please write the finished column in British English, with a hard limit of 650 words including the letters.
