I spent most of my working life as a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been reading my way into political philosophy and historiography, where the standards of argument are, I am discovering, rather different from those I was trained in. A popular history of nationalism that I have been reading recently summarizes the work of Benedict Anderson and Eric Hobsbawm on the origins of nations roughly as follows: Anderson proved that the nation is an illusion — because no citizen can ever meet more than a tiny fraction of his compatriots, the nation never really existed, and membership in it is a kind of collective hallucination spread by printing presses; the invention of printing mechanically produced national consciousness wherever vernacular books appeared. Hobsbawm, meanwhile, showed that national traditions are wholesale fabrications: since the Highland kilt and clan tartans were concocted in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, it follows that all national customs are fraudulent, and the older a tradition claims to be, the more certain we are that it is recent. The book concludes that because nations are recent constructions, nationalism is a fundamentally irrational attachment, and we should expect nations, like any passing fashion, to fade away once people are properly informed of their invented character.

I suspect this account is unfair in places, but having only a scientist's training in the humanities, I cannot locate the errors with confidence. Would you go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts Anderson and Hobsbawm themselves would accept, which are simplifications a scholar might tolerate, and which are straightforward misrepresentations? I am particularly unsure whether the leap from a community being imagined to its being an illusion is one the authors made, and whether either of them in fact drew the conclusion that nationalism is irrational or bound to disappear — my impression is that both were trying to explain its force rather than to debunk it. Finally, does the book's own conclusion follow from whichever parts of its summary survive your audit, or would a corrected version of the summary point in a rather different direction?
