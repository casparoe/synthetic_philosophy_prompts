I'm a student in a graduate seminar on evolution and mind, working on a term paper about Dennett's idea of free-floating rationales: the reasons why some pattern exists -- why the vertebrate eye has the shape it does, why trees overshadow their rivals, why certain social insects are born already pregnant -- that are nobody's reasons, since no mind ever articulated them, yet which arguably explain the pattern better than any merely mechanical redescription. My problem is that I can realistically develop only two questions in depth, and I can't decide which two matter most. The candidates I've drawn up are:

1. Whether free-floating rationales are genuinely reasons or only reason-shaped patterns: is the "why" of an adaptation really there in the world, or is it projected by a mind that comes along and translates selection's output into the language of justification?
2. Whether free-floating rationales can be missing or false in an interesting sense: selection favors the adaptive, not the true, and byproducts and spandrels can mimic rationale-bearing design. Is there a principled way to distinguish a genuine free-floating rationale from a design-like effect that merely looks as if it has one?
3. How the notion relates to attempts to ground mental content in selection history: can free-floating rationales do the work of carrying content for simple systems, or does the whole project collapse when we notice that most selection history contains no representations at all?
4. Whether cultural practices -- writing systems, rituals, institutions -- can have free-floating rationales that outrun anyone's actual reasons for maintaining them, and whether that would change how the social sciences should explain behavior.
5. Whether learned but uninterpretable systems, like trained neural networks whose behavior we can predict but not explain, give us a fresh class of free-floating rationales, and whether it matters whether any future mind could ever articulate them.

Which two would you prioritize for someone with about fifteen pages, a background in evolutionary biology, and only a little cognitive science? Please defend the ordering rather than just summarizing the debates: I want to know which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable at term-paper length without turning into a literature survey, and which would let me make an actual argument rather than report on one.
