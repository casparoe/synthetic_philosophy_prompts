I'm building teaching material on the Meno problem — why knowledge is better than mere true belief — and every case in the litterature runs the same direction: knowledge wins because it's safer, or because you get credit for it. I want the reverse. Construct a thought experiment in which someone's true belief serves them better than knowledge would have, and not because the believer got lucky in a Gettier-y way, and not because the knower is lazy or irrational or a perfectionist — the knowledgeable agent has to do everything right by their own lights. Realistic setting please, an ordinary workplace or hospital, nothing sci-fi, no lottery-style devices. The route I suspect will work: make the very ingredient that upgrades true belief to knowledge (say, safety, or sensitivity to nearby ways of being wrong) the thing that costs them — the anti-luck condition forces you to register the failure modes, and that awareness, fully rational as it is, makes you act worse. Try to make the case bite against the swamping problem as well: if the value knowledge adds over true belief can go negative in your case, does the swamping intuition survive, or does it just show that the "extra" value was never epistemic in the first place? Give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario; (2) what a safety-based account of knowledge's value says the knower has that the believer lacks, and why exactly that backfires here; (3) the same for a credit or virtue account, and whether credit can also backfire or only safety can; (4) whether a defender of knowledge's value can plausibly diagnose your case as a decision-theory artifact (mere risk aversion) rather than a genuinely epistemological one, and if so whether that diagnosis is ad hoc; and (5) which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you remove that feature.
