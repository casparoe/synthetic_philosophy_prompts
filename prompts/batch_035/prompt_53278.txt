I never went to university, and everything I know about this I worked out on my own, mostly during a long stretch of night shifts, so please bear with the homegrown vocabulary. The question I keep circling comes from Plato's Meno: if a guide who genuinely knows the road to the next town and a guide who merely holds a true belief about that road both get you there, what does the knowing add? Why would any of us pay more, or care more, for the one state than the other? I've convinced myself that knowledge is worth more than true belief, but I can't say why, and that gap is exactly where two of my friends have been arguing for the past three years.

I'd like you to write the joint statement these two could sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration — the document they should have produced long ago instead of talking past each other. The two sides, in their own plain words, are:

- The process side: knowledge is better than true belief because of how the belief was formed. A belief that tracks the facts, produced by a method that tends to get things right and would have gotten them wrong if they were wrong, is more stable, more reusable, and reflects well on the person holding it. The extra value lies in the etiology.
- The value side: that can't be the whole story. If what you want from a belief is truth, and you already have the true belief, then the route you took to it seems unable to add any further value — the way the value of a delivered parcel doesn't go up because the van took a scenic route. The extra value of knowledge, if real, must attach to the state itself, not to the detour that produced it.

Please structure the document as follows: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can sign — including, at minimum, what they agree on about the man who glances at a watch that stopped exactly twelve hours earlier and so happens to read the right time, and about the distinction between that man and one who checks a working watch; (2) a table of their remaining disagreements, each phrased so the side holding the view would accept the wording as fair to them; (3) the cruxes — I have a hunch one is whether a belief's value can depend on facts about its formation that the believer has no access to, and another is whether the value of a good method survives after the method has already delivered the truth, but you should identify the cruxes as you actually find them. For each crux, include at least one case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. In particular, I'd like both the case of my grandfather, whose left knee ached a day before rain more reliably than the local forecast, though he could not have said why and sometimes did not notice he was doing it, and the case of deciding what to pay a surgeon who will act on your behalf: does it matter, and how much, whether she truly believes you are allergic to the anesthetic or knows it? Close with a short section saying which parts of the residual dispute, if any, strike both sides as verbal rather than substantive.

One more request: argue everything out in plain reasoning from the cases themselves. Don't pad the answer with reading lists, citations, or the names of scholars, and don't lean on anything you'd need to look up — I want the position and the argument, not the bibliography. And please write the entire response in British English: spellings and usage throughout should be British (organise, recognise, colour, "in hospital"), not American.
