I chair the allocation subcommittee at a mid-sized regional health board — six members, mixed backgrounds, one social scientist, no philosophers. Every few months we get a proposal that trades many small benefits against a few large ones: extend clinic hours so a thousand patients each wait twenty minutes less, versus fund an expensive therapy that transforms twelve lives. Our written policy just says decisions must be justifiable to each person affected, which everyone interprets differently. Half the committee are Scanlon fans who keep appealing to reasonable rejection, the other half quietly add up QALYs, and I have no principled way to referee.

Write me a decision procedure based on Scanlon's contractualism that the subcommittee can actually run in an afternoon meeting. It should say what counts as someone's complaint, when two people's complaints are even comparable, and how to handle the aggregation problem — Scanlon himself forbids just summing small gains across people, but he does let numbers matter when claims are roughly comparable (think his rescue cases), so the procedure has to say where comparability comes from and what happens when claims are wildly unequal. Give me the steps in order, what a sufficient answer to each step looks like, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it can only hand the value judgment back to the committee instead of settling it — be honest about those points, don't dress them up as procedure. No outside reading, the reasoning should be self-contained.

Then run it on two proposals: (1) redirecting a home-visit nursing budget that gives roughly 800 frail patients modest help into a cochlear implant program for 12 children, and (2) a winter surge plan that closes a walk-in clinic used occasionally by thousands to fully staff the ICU. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like — I'm personally attached to the implant program — and I want the worked example to say so plainly rather than fudge it.
