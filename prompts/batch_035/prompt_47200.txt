So, novelist here. I'm writing a book whose narrator does pest control for a living, and the engine of the character is that he can't stop doing arithmatic in his head: how many roaches one apartment treatment kills, and how many of those might have felt something. I want him to hold a real position, not a strawman for the reader to pity. Please argue, for him, that the scale argument goes through even on pessimistic assumptions: given the sheer number of insects — they vastly outnumber all vertebrates put together — even a very low probability of sentience per insect, or a tiny fractional moral weight for each one, yields an expected total that should dominate our concern for the animals we're much more confident about. To make it something he'd actually say, rather than a canned line, ground the argument in a specific and somewhat unusual claim: that uncertainty about sentience discounts the moral weight of each individual insect, but aggregation across insects stays fully additive — tiny weight per creature, but no further discount just for the tininess itself. And then defend that asymmetry against the obvious comeback, which his daughter (a philosophy grad student, long story) throws at him in chapter six: that if you're unsure enough to discount each insect, maybe you should be unsure enough to refuse to add them all up. He's allowed to lose arguments elsewhere in the book, but this one I want him to at least fight to a draw.
