I have just taken a seat on the ethics committee of a hospital trust, and one of our standing jobs is to maintain the guidance notes that clinicians are supposed to consult when a case gets difficult — end-of-life decisions, disagreements with families, allocation of scarce theatre time. Almost immediately the committee split in a way I have since recognised as a rerun of the particularism/generalism war in academic ethics. One faction wants the guidance to state principles, with exceptions written in wherever experience has shown the principles to misfire. Another faction, led by a quietly formidable old physician, holds that this whole approach is misconceived: any code of principles will need exceptions, and eventually the exceptions will be recognised as the wrong ones in new cases, and the only real skill is judgement in the particulars — trained experience, not rule application. When I went looking in the literature to settle this for our committee, I found the same stall: Dancy and McDowell on the particularist side, Hooker and the Rossian moderates on the generalist side, decades of papers, and as far as I can tell nobody has been moved an inch since the 1990s.

I do not want you to adjudicate for my committee. I want a diagnosis of the philosophical dispute itself, because I suspect the shape of the disagreement determines what our guidance documents can sensibly aspire to.

You are to write as a senior moral philosopher with expertise in metaethics and normative theory, and I would like you to think carefully and structure your answer. Begin with a brief brainstorm of possible diagnoses before committing to any — sketch at least four candidate structures for the disagreement and then argue for the best one. The candidates I have in mind, though you should feel free to add or reject them, are these:

1. A verbal dispute about the word "principle": particularists deny only invariant, exceptionless principles, while the generalists who matter (the Rossians, the rule consequentialists) never claimed their principles were exceptionless, so the two camps may be talking past one another — one refuting a thesis the other side never held.
2. A substantive dispute about the nature of reasons: Dancy's holism of reasons — the claim that a feature that counts in favour of an action in one case may count against it, or make no difference, in another — against the atomist picture in which each feature carries a fixed valence. If holism is true, principled moral reasoning of the codifying sort may be impossible in principle, which would matter for us.
3. A hidden disagreement about moral epistemology rather than moral ontology: perhaps the two camps agree about how actions are justified but disagree about how finite, fallible agents — especially novices, or a committee drafting guidance for strangers — ought to deliberate, in which case the debate is about the psychology and training of moral agents, not the structure of morality itself.
4. A difference in bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for without circularity — something like whether moral thought is more like perception (seeing what matters in this case) or more like subsumption under a rule — with each camp's arguments presupposing its own starting picture.

There is also a fifth possibility I find intriguing and would like your view on: that the dispute is partly formal, and could in principle be sharpened or even dissolved by work on non-monotonic and default logics — formal systems in which a rule licenses a conclusion only ceteris paribus, with exceptions that need not themselves be capturable by further rules. If the generalist's "principles with principled exceptions" can be given a coherent formal semantics, does the particularist's claim that morality is not codifiable become precise enough to be true or false? Or does the formal machinery merely relocate the disagreement into a dispute about whether the exception structure has a moral shape or an open-ended, case-driven one?

Finally, tell me honestly what would count as progress, given your diagnosis — a distinction, a class of cases, a formal result, findings from moral psychology about how experienced clinicians actually reason — and, if your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected, say so plainly and explain why, rather than offering a diplomatic synthesis. And do relate the diagnosis back to my practical situation: I would like to know whether the guidance documents my committee drafts should be written as attempted codifications, as training tools whose principles are honestly presented as defeasible, or as something the particularist could sign without crossing the street.
