I have been reading Overcoming Bias and the surrounding rationalist blogosphere since about 2008, so I have absorbed the standard futarchy debate in full: Hanson's slogan that in futarchy "we would vote on values, but bet on beliefs," the worry about manipulators with deep pockets and convex payoffs, the difficulty of specifying a welfare metric that markets can settle bets on, the complaints that speculators discount the long term. I know those objections and I know the canonical replies to them, and I am tired of rehearsing both. My problem is that my next audience will not raise them.

In three weeks I am presenting to the weekly political economy workshop at a public policy school. The room will be about fifteen people: two behavioural economists who made their careers documenting bubbles and mispricing in speculative markets and regard the efficient-market premise as mostly ideology; a deliberative-democracy theorist for whom a decision's legitimacy depends on its being justifiable in public reason to those it binds; a veteran public-choice economist who agrees markets aggregate information well but suspects I have mis-modelled who the principal and who the agent are in my scheme; a statistician who spends her life on Goodhart's law and on metric gaming in public-sector performance management; and a handful of doctoral students who will take their cues from the faculty.

My thesis, in outline: futarchy as usually pitched overreaches, but a narrow trial version is worth running. A single agency faces a genuinely contested choice between two already-shortlisted policies. Before any trading, the ordinary political process fixes the decision metric and the five-year horizon; conditional prediction markets then price each option's expected performance on that metric; a decision rule pre-committed in advance converts the spread into a choice; and the design includes subsidies, position limits and a standing bounty for evidence of manipulation. On legitimacy: the market does not authorise anything — it merely executes a choice that the ordinary process has already scoped, just as a randomised trial does, and for the same reason.

Please prepare my objection sheet. Specifically: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary rather than the rationalist one — the public-choice theorist's agency problem, the deliberation theorist's legitimation worry, the behavioural economists' evidence about thin and manipulated markets, the statistician's account of what a metric does once it becomes a target — ordered by how much damage each would do in that room, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should concede up front, and which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive the workshop. Include the objection from the person who broadly agrees with me about markets, since I suspect that is the one I am least prepared for, and finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write your answer in British English.
