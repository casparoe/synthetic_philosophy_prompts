So here's my situation: I've somehow ended up a believer in origin essentialism about persons -- the Kripke-flavored idea that I couldn't have developed from a different sperm and egg. Anyone who grew from a different pair of gametes, no matter how similar to me they ended up, just wouldn't be *me*; they'd be someone else who happens to resemble me. I never bought this as an argument, more like it's an intuition that hardened into a position, and now I'd like to know what I've actually signed up for.

What would count against it? I don't want the standard textbook objection restated at me -- I want a ranked map of the view's exposure, from fatal to merely awkward. A few things I specifically want you to weigh in on, though don't limit yourself to these: (1) the slippery slope worry -- if the particular gametes matter, why not the exact timing of conception, or the food my mother ate in the first week, or eventually the whole causal history back to the big bang? At what point does "origin" stop being a thing, and is there any principled place to draw the line that isn't just vibes? (2) The twinning cases and the clone-from-saved-DNA scenario -- if a clone is later made from my genome, am I spread over two bodies, is one of them me, or neither? Does my view force a bad answer there? (3) The thought that this might be a case where a linguistic intuition ("we just wouldn't *call* that guy me") got over-promoted to a metaphysical thesis -- i.e., that the view risks nothing at all and has been insulated by qualifications into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind or just par for the course. Along the way, tell me which of these defeaters a committed origin essentialist would themselves concede as real trouble versus dismiss, and what the view actually predicts that rival views (best-candidate theories, "no fact of the matter" views) don't.

One more thing: I want a committed answer, not a menu. At the end, take a definite stance on whether origin essentialism survives, and if you think it does but only with the origin drawn at some particular grain (gametes only? gametes plus early embryo? something wider), say which grain you'd draw it at and why, even if you're not fully certain. Don't end with "it depends on your intuitions about persons" -- I know it does. Tell me where *you* land and what moved you. (And no reading lists or citations please, this is a discussion not a bibliography.) Also fair warning: if you just regurgitate the generic "Kripke said names are rigid designators" boilerplate without engaging with the actual cases, I'll be dissapointed.
