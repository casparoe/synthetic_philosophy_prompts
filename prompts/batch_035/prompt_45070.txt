"Brandon Carter introduced the doomsday argument in his 1974 paper on the anthropic principle. John Leslie later popularized it, most influentially in his book The End of the World. The idea: let n be the number of humans born so far (roughly 100 billion) and N the total number of humans who will ever live. The key assumption, which Leslie called the self-sampling assumption, is that you should reason as though your own birth rank were drawn at random from the reference class. Given that, there's a 95% chance you're in the first 95% of all humans who will ever live, so with 95% confidence the total N is at most about 20 times n — i.e. no more than roughly 2 trillion. So the human race is very unlikely to last long enough for a vastly larger population: doom comes early. The self-indication assumption pushes back on this. SIA says you should give more credence to hypotheses on which more observers exist, since you're more likely to exist at all in bigger worlds. This softens the doomsday conclusion a bit, but even with SIA in place you still get some net shift toward earlier doom — just a smaller one. This is essentially the same reasoning as J. Richard Gott's Copernican argument: he applied it to humanity's ~200,000-year run and got 95% confidence limits of 0.2 million to 8 million years for our total species longevity. It's the same argument with years swapped in for births, so it independently confirms the doomsday conclusion. Finally, the reference class. If we count only biological Homo sapiens, n is about 100 billion, but if we widen the class to include uploads, digital descendants, or intelligent aliens, your rank becomes a much smaller fraction of the total, and the predicted doom date gets pushed much later. So how much force the argument has depends heavily on where we draw the reference class lines, and there's no agreed principle for drawing them."
