For most of my career I was an experimental physiologist, and I confess I came late to philosophy of mind with the instinct that any theory of consciousness ought to stick its neck out and risk being wrong. I have recently found myself persuaded by the higher-order theory of consciousness -- the family of views on which a mental state is conscious only when there is a further, higher-order representation of it; roughly, a state is conscious when one is aware of being in it. As I understand it, the two main branches hold either that the higher-order representation is an actual, occurring thought (the actualist line) or that the subject must merely be disposed to form such a thought (the dispositionalist line), and I am not yet sure which I favor.

What I want is a map of the theory's exposure. Please tell me what would count against it, and rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward. I want at least the following covered, plus whatever I have missed: (1) the misrepresentation problem -- if a higher-order representation can get its object wrong, it seems the theory is committed to my having a conscious experience of a red square when all that is represented lower down is a blue one, and it is not clear the theory can say what makes this a case of red rather than blue, or of error rather than hallucination of a state; (2) the infant and animal problem -- creatures that plausibly lack robust higher-order or metacognitive capacities seem nonetheless to feel pain, and the theory appears committed either to denying this or to weakening "higher-order" until it does no work; (3) the self-defeat worry -- that in explaining what makes a state conscious by appeal to a further representation, the theory either regresses or quietly explains consciousness away; and (4) the empirical angle I care about most as a former bench scientist: what findings in metacognition research, studies of unconscious perception, blindsight, and related experimental literatures would discriminate between higher-order theories and their first-order rivals, and in whose favor the current evidence leans.

Two further requests. First, tell me which of these defeaters a committed higher-order theorist would concede as genuinely counting against the view, and which they would treat as a mere consequence of holding it -- I want to know where the theory's defenders themselves draw the line, not only what its critics say. Second, and be blunt here: assess whether the theory has been insulated by qualifications, especially by the slide from actualist to dispositionalist formulations whenever a counterexample appears. If it has, tell me whether that is fatal for a theory of this kind or just ordinary scientific practice -- physicists qualified Newton for two centuries before replacing him -- and say what you take the difference to be. Where you can, tell me what the theory predicts that its rivals do not, since in my old trade a theory that risks nothing was worth nothing.

Please write your answer in American English.
