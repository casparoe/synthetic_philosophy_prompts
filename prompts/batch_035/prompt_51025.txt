I edit a small plain-text philosophy newsletter and want to run a "what kind of epistemic injustice theorist are you actually?" quiz. Please write eight questions, each a short concrete case on which the positions genuinely come apart — for instance: a patient whose reported chronic pain is discounted by doctors; a witness whose accent costs them credibility with a jury; someone who cannot name a wrong done to them because no concept for it has yet circulated in their community; a speaker from a subculture whose perfectly intelligible-to-insiders idiom is dismissed as incoherent by outsiders who could make the effort to follow it; and further cases of your own choosing. Each question gets two or three answer options. Then provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto four positions: the view that locates the wrong in individual hearers' credibility judgments and prescribes a corrective virtue in the hearer; the view that locates it in structural gaps in shared interpretive resources and prescribes collective conceptual repair; the view that there is a distinct additional wrong in an audience's refusal to extend interpretive resources it already has, over and above any gap in those resources; and the deflationary view that there is no distinctively epistemic wrong here at all, only ordinary injustice carried out by epistemic means. The quiz must sort readers by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like, so no question may simply ask whether the reader "believes in" one of the positions, and no question should be answerable from the label alone. Please also flag any pair of positions the answer options cannot separate — for example, if two leaves coincide on every case and differ only on remedies — and say whether that reflects a real convergence in the literature or a flaw in the quiz I should fix. Since the newsletter carries only plain text, please deliver everything as continuous prose with no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables: number the questions in-line and describe the branching of the scoring key in full sentences rather than laying it out as a flowchart.
