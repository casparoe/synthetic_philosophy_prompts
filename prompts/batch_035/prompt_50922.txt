I have been recording my honest reactions to the well-known thought experiments about well-being, and the pattern that has emerged is starting to worry me. Here are my verdicts, stated as plainly as I can.

First, the standard experience machine. Plug in for a two-year stretch, live a life considerably more pleasant than my real one, and be none the wiser: I would not plug in, and the longer I sit with the question the more confident I get. I would take a weekend, but that feels like a different question.

Second, the deceived businessman. He seems to himself loved, respected, and successful, and from the inside his life is warm and rich, except that none of it is true: his colleagues find him tiresome, his deals quietly collapse, his family is indifferent. My verdict is that he is badly off, though here it gets uncomfortable, because while I want to say his life is bad for him overall, I cannot make myself say that his pleasant Tuesday afternoons are worth nothing. There is some welfare there, just far too little.

Third, a man whose life's project, something along the lines of an anonymous benefactor quietly funding scholarships and restoring a woodland he loved, is completed only after his death, which he spent expecting failure. Verdict: the later success made him somewhat better off, even though he never experienced anything of it. Satisfaction without a subject is a strange thing to assert, but I cannot give it up.

Fourth, a runner whose hometown marathon was secretly rerouted so that the course ran only thirty kilometers. He finishes believing he has run a marathon, and the felt triumph is the proudest moment of his year. Verdict: it contributed nothing to his well-being, nothing at all, and the feeling itself seems worthless in a way the businessman's pleasant afternoons do not. When I ask myself whether his finding out a week later would make the previous week worse, I keep thinking no: the week was already lacking, and the news would only make him sad about it.

Fifth, a woman whose life is small: same flat, same soap operas, same five square blocks, no deception anywhere and nothing she believes is false, and she is serenely, wholeheartedly content with it and would choose it again. Verdict: her life is going well for her. Not as well as it might, but well.

What I would like is a principle that captures all five verdicts, and I have a candidate I distrust: something like the view that you are better off to the extent that the world actually is as you want it to be, rather than as it seems to you to be, with some independent weight for felt pleasure. I distrust it because of the tension between my second and fourth verdicts: pleasant feelings in the businessman's life seem worth something, felt triumph in the runner's seems worth nothing, and I cannot locate the difference. So my questions are these. Is there a single coherent account that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely and show me the machinery working on each case. If not, tell me which of my verdicts you would overturn and why. Then test whatever account you land on against two further cases.

The first is one I keep going back and forth on. A variant of the experience machine in which the simulated people are, as far as anything could ever tell, genuinely conscious and genuinely happy, each of them living a life as good as a real one, and all of them owing their existence to my plugging in. Does the happiness inside the machine count for anything: toward my well-being, toward the ethics of plugging in, or toward neither? If one of my standing desires is that people flourish, and there is now a machine full of flourishing people, has my desire been satisfied?

The second is a case on which I have no verdict, so I want your account's prediction. Suppose the machine, before you plug in, computes exactly what the remaining years of your real life would have contained and then simulates precisely that: no more pleasant, no less, identical in every fact you could ever care about. Does plugging in cost you nothing? If your account implies otherwise, so much the better for it, but show your work.
