me and a friend have been stuck for months on Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and I'm starting to think the disagreement between us mirrors the one in the literature that's also gone basically nowhere since the 60s. here's where we are: I buy that the gavagai stuff shows behavioral data can't fix a unique translation manual. my friend says that's just garden-variety underdetermination of theory by evidence — the same thing that happens in physics, where rival theories also fit the data until you weigh in with extra theoretical virtues — so translation isn't special the way Quine wanted it to be. I keep replying that Quine was explicit that indeterminacy is stronger: two manuals can both fit ALL possible speech behavior, not just the data we happen to have, so there's no further fact even in principle. she says that's also true in physics if you're crazy enough about it, and at that point we talk past each other and go get coffee.

so what kind of disagreement is this actually? is it substantive — is there a real difference between "no fact of the matter even in principle" and "no evidence could ever decide it" — or is it verbal, hinging on what we're willing to count as a fact? Quine seems to allow that facts about the native's neural setup could fix facts within physics, because physics is in his terms immanent where translation is transcendent, and my friend thinks that whole distinction is doing all the work while nobody ever argues for it. my sense is that the Davidson-style objection — charity already constrains translation so heavily that the indeterminacy left is trivial — never really engaged the core point, and that the Chomsky-style objection (it's all just underdetermination, get over it) is at bottom a refusal to accept Quine's premise rather than a counter to it. do you agree with that characterization, and if so does it mean the debate is structurally doomed, because the premise itself — that the holism of translation runs deeper than the holism of physics — can't be argued for without begging the question?

and then there's the thing that keeps it alive for us: LLMs. her latest move is that models trained purely on text — behavioral data, no access to any facts of the matter — converge on remarkably similar internal representations of meaning, which she takes as emprical evidence against the premise that behavior can't pin a translation down up to trivial relabeling. my instinct is that this begs a different question, since convergence among systems trained the same way shows at most that one particular carving is stable, not that its the right one — but I can't quite say what would establish the "right one" without smuggling in a fact of the matter, which is exactly what's at issue. given your diagnosis, would this kind of empirical result count as progress, or is it just a new object for each side to interpret in its own terms? and be honest about whether anything — a thought experiment, a formal result, a cleaner distinction between indeterminacy and ordinary underdetermination — could actually move this, or whether we should just accept its stalled and invest in better coffee.
