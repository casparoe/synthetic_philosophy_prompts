I've been following the decision-theory threads on and off since the old LessWrong days — the ones that spun out of Newcomb discussions into things like consistent reflection and self-modification — and I keep coming back to a case that never got the same airing: the absent-minded driver. I want to put a number on where I stand on it, and I'd like you to do the real work of decomposition rather than hand me a slogan like "imperfect recall just breaks expected utility."

Here is the setup, so we're looking at the same thing. A driver is driving home late at night along a straight road. He will pass two identical intersections where the same exit is possible; he is absent-minded enough that when he arrives at an intersection, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, and he knows this about himself in advance. If he exits at the first intersection, his payoff is 0 (that route has a long detour). If he continues past the first and exits at the second, his payoff is 1. If he continues past both, he reaches home quickly, for a payoff of 4. Before setting out, at the planning stage, he picks the optimal mixed strategy: exit at any given intersection with some probability p, and the planning-optimal value works out to p = 2/3, with expected payoff 4p(1−p) + (1−p)², maximized where p = 2/3. But once he is actually at an intersection, the reasoning flips: from inside the information set, he should update on being at some intersection, work out the posterior over which one it is, and choose the action that maximizes expected payoff given that posterior — and the policy that comes out of that reasoning differs from the 2/3 he planned on. Worse, if he follows the action-stage reasoning to its conclusion, the expected payoff he secures for himself is lower than what the planning-stage self guaranteed, which is what made the case famous when Piccione and Rubinstein introduced it: the analysis at the action stage, taken at face value, does worse than the plan made in full view of the whole problem.

So my question is a credence, and I want it decomposed. My proposition: the action-stage policy — the one derived from updating on "I am at an intersection" and optimizing from inside the information set — is what a rational agent should actually implement in this problem, as opposed to the planning-stage policy, or to the view that the question is somehow ill-posed and neither answer is the rational one. Give me a number for that proposition, then break it into terms you actually believe in, along lines like these (add or discard terms as you see fit, but justify each): the probability that "what should I do?" has a determinate answer for an agent who cannot condition on what he doesn't remember, rather than splitting into a planning question and an action question with different answers; the probability that the problem, once described this way, is really a single-agent decision problem at all, versus a situation better modeled as successive selves whose interests partially conflict — and if it's the latter, whose preferences the word "rational" is tracking; the probability that an agent at an intersection can legitimately treat his own future or past choice at the other intersection as evidentially linked to his current one, in a way that changes what the action-optimal policy is; and the probability that the whole tension is an artifact of how the information set is formalized, such that the paradox would dissolve under a different but equally faithful formalization — in which case my proposition is false in an interesting way rather than a controversial one.

For each term, give the probability, one line of reasoning, and then show how they combine into your final credence. After that, tell me two more things. First, which term the final number is most sensitive to — I'd genuinely like to know whether the load-bearing uncertainty is metaphysical (about selves and personal identity over forgetting) or technical (about formalizing information sets), because I can't tell from the armchair. Second, what concrete development would move you most: a resolution I'd accept if someone derived the action-stage policy from an axiomatic decision theory I already trusted, a resolution you'd accept if a well-known formal treatment showed the planning and action stages must coincide in any extension of expected theory that preserves one specific property — name the property you'd insist on preserving. I'd rather not have a bibliography of the follow-up literature; I want your own accounting of where the probability mass sits.
