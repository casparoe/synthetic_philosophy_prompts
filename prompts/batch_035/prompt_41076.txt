My brother and his wife recently announced that they are trying for a baby, and at the family dinner that followed I made what I intended as an innocent remark: that it seems strange we treat the decision to have a child and the decision not to as morally symmetric, given that no one is harmed by never existing but someone can certainly be harmed by existing. This sparked a long argument and, over the following weeks, sent me down a rabbit hole of encyclopedia entries and forum threads about David Benatar's asymmetry, despite my having no philosophy training whatsoever (I'm a software engineer; my last formal exposure to ethics was a freshman elective fifteen years ago). In the course of it I've tried to write down where I actually stand on a set of related questions, and I would like a consistency audit of the resulting package, because I suspect at least one of these commitments is quietly doing work that the others forbid.

Here is what I believe: (a) I accept the asymmetry itself — the absence of pain is good even when there is nobody around to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless somebody is deprived of it. (b) I think it is wrong for a couple to conceive when they know the child's life would fall below neutral, with more suffering than happiness, but permissible, and genuinely good, to conceive when they confidently expect a happy life. (c) I think possible future people have no moral status before they exist; you cannot wrong someone by failing to bring them into being. (d) I think a couple who could take a free pill before conception guaranteeing that their future child will have a happy life, rather than leaving it a coin flip between happy and miserable, would be irresponsible not to take it. (e) I do not think we are obligated to have as many children as we reasonably can, even if every additional child would have a wonderful life. (f) I reject what I've seen called the pro-mortalist conclusion: none of this implies that an existing person with a good but painful life should end it, or that such a person would have been better off never existing.

I don't want a general lecture on antinatalism — I want you to treat the six commitments as a set and tell me which of them pull against each other. Please respond with exactly five numbered points, ordered from the most severe tension to the least, each only a sentence or two long: name the commitments in conflict, say how badly they clash, and give the cheapest fix, meaning the single commitment that would have to be dropped or qualified. If a pair only looks like it conflicts and dissolves on closer inspection, include it as one of the five and say so explicitly. No introduction or conclusion, just the five points.
