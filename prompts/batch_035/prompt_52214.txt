So here's my situation: I work in procurement at a mid-size company — I mostly evaluate vendor bids — and I got this job because a college roommate of mine is a director here and basically walked my resume to the top of the pile. Fast forward to last month, when my friend Anna, whose catering company did my wedding at a steep friends-and-family discount, sent me a very nice bottle of scotch out of the blue, and my brother-in-law started hinting that I should put in a good word for his landscaping company's bid on our campus grounds contract. Nobody has asked me for anything explicit, and I said yes to the scotch before I even thought about it, but my wife's very earnest coworker told me flat out that I'm now corrupt, full stop, and it's made me want to figure out what I actually believe corruption is. So here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if some principle captures them.

Giving your mail carrier a gift card at Christmas: obviously fine. A city health inspector who collects a Christmas tip from every restaurant on his beat: obviously corrupt. My roommate walking my resume in: fine, I'm qualified, and honestly almost everyone I know got at least one job through a connection. A politician hiring her qualified daughter as chief of staff: not fine, even though the daughter is qualified — but I can't really articulate what changed between that and my roommate. My barber giving me a free haircut after I referred three new clients to him, and me making sure to send people his way: fine, feels wholesome even. Two companies whose execs sit on each other's boards and quietly steer business to each other: not fine. A government minister taking a consulting fee from a firm that just won a giant contract: corrupt. And here's where I get stuck: my brother-in-law's company is genuinely good, Anna's discount was real friendship and not a setup for later, and in the town where my grandfather grew up, getting anything done — a permit, a doctor's appointment, a business license — meant knowing a guy and owing that guy, and I don't think my grandfather was corrupt, I think that's just how a place with barely functioning institutions runs. But if I steelman the coworker, all my fine cases have a favor economy in them too, just with small stakes and people I happen to like. Is there an actual principle that seperates my fines from my not-fines — role, duty owed to some principal, whether reciprocity stays open-ended versus getting a price attached, whether third parties lose out — or am I just labeling things corrupt once the numbers get big and the people involved are strangers? And then tell me what the principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: whether I should recuse myself from the landscaping decision, and whether the scotch has to go back.
