I have just been elected chair of my university's curriculum committee, a position I did not especially seek, and before our first consequential vote I need to understand something I stumbled into while reading up on committee procedure.

Over the summer, in anticipation of a vote on restructuring the general-education requirements, I informally polled the eight other members about their preferences over the three leading proposals. When I tabulated the results, I found that a majority prefers proposal A to proposal B, a majority prefers B to C, and a majority prefers C to A. Whatever we do, it seems, a majority of the committee would rather we had done something else.

A colleague in the mathematics department waved this off, telling me it is just Arrow's impossibility theorem and that I should stop worrying. But I am not sure the shoe fits. My understanding, from a hasty reading, is that Arrow's theorem concerns rules for converting ranked ballots into a ranking of options, whereas my problem begins after everyone's preferences are known. It is a problem about which procedure to run, and I have noticed that it has two parts that may or may not be the same phenomenon:

First, there is the cycle itself. No single option commands a majority against each rival, so the result of head-to-head voting depends on the order in which the head-to-head votes are held. If I, as chair, pair A against B first and then the winner against C, we end up with C; run it in the other direction and we end up with A. Every individual vote is a fair majority vote, and yet the final outcome tracks my choice of agenda nearly as much as it tracks the committee's preferences. I have noticed that political scientists use the phrase agenda-setting for the media's control over what gets discussed at all, and I cannot tell whether that is the same concept or a neighboring metaphor.

Second, in an earlier episode I have belatedly understood, the committee did not use head-to-head voting at all. We voted to eliminate the least popular option and then voted again between the survivors, and the option that survived that gauntlet would, on the preferences I have since reconstructed, have lost a direct comparison with one of the eliminated options. So even when the committee's preferences do contain an option that beats every other one on one, the procedural details can still smuggle in a different result. That makes me suspect my mathematician friend has pointed me at a famous theorem while missing the actual mechanism.

I am not asking for a reading list. What I want is a careful answer to the following: Do these phenomena — the order-dependence of sequential majority voting, and the divergence between elimination-style procedures and pairwise comparisons — have established names in the social-choice literature? Is my friend right that this is Arrow's theorem, or is it something adjacent to it, and what exactly is the boundary between the two? I have also heard that there is a separate literature about voters misrepresenting their preferences in early rounds to secure a better final outcome, and I would like to know where that ends and what I have described begins, since in my case I am assuming everyone votes sincerely throughout. Finally, a request about how you answer: please keep clearly separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, for instance about how much any of it matters in real committees like mine, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I ask because the practical question facing me is whether any procedure I choose can honestly be described as neutral, and I need to know which parts of the answer I can rely on and which are matters of ongoing dispute.
