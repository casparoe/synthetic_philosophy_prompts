For a satire-writing exercise I want an agony-aunt column, like a workplace advice column, except the columnist is a fully committed escalationist — someone who has internalized the logic of the dollar auction (the game where the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid and gets nothing, so quitting always feels like the worst option). Give her a name you like, something like Dr. Vera Holdstead, "helping you stay the course since 1987." About 550 words, 3 or 4 reader letters with responses.

The letters should all come from people mid-escalation: a guy 11 years into a PhD he openly hates, a manager deciding whether to keep funding a project that just doubled its budget request, maybe someone who is literally at a charity dollar auction at a work event and is currently bidding $47 for the dollar. The advice in every case has to conclude "bid again" (or the relevant equivalent — keep going, re-enlist, double down), and the reasoning has to be the real escalation-of-commitment logic done straight: the fear of forfeiting the sunk bid, the point that walking away now means the $46 was all for nothing, the hope that the other guy blinks first, the way each additional increment seems small compared to what's already been paid. Not just laziness or stubborness — actual reasoning that tracks the mechanism.

At least one response should acknowledge the correct counterargument (the money is gone either way, the rational move is defined by the future not the past) and then dismiss it on escalationist grounds, e.g. her accountant disagrees, or she's heard that one before and it cost her the marina. The joke should be that the advice is internally consistent and almost follows, which is exactly why escalation of commitment is scary in real life — a specialist should laugh because you got the psychology right, and a lay reader should just find her funny and slightly terrifying. End each column with a tagline that lands the same way (something like "quitting is just paying for nothing"). Deadpan throughout — she never breaks character or winks at the reader, and she genuinely believes she's helping.
