So I teach an intro philosophy of religion course, and next week we finally hit the omnipotence paradox. I want to warm the students up with a satirical writing exercise before we do the serious analysis, and I'd like to test-drive the prompt myself first — so could you write the piece I'm going to assign?

Write a parody of a software changelog / release-notes page, roughly 700 words, for a startup called Omnia (or pick a better name if you have one) that sells omnipotence as a subscription service. The whole thing is version notes: v0.9 alpha through, say, v2.1, each version followed by feature lists, bug fixes, known issues, and the occasional entry closed as "won't fix" or "working as intended."

The jokes have to come from the actual philosophy, not from generic startup humor. In particular I want the changelog to quietly take positions on the real questions: the stone paradox (an entry where creating unliftable stones was a reported bug, closed as working as intended, is the minimum bar, but go beyond it — maybe a later version that ships both the stone and the lifting, with the release note admitting they can't explain how and linking to an internal ticket that doesn't exist); whether omnipotence covers only the logically possible, and who decides what counts as logically possible (a feature "make square circles" that gets deprecated for consistency reasons would do it, but feel free to handle it differently — what I care about is that some version notes treat logical necessity as a constraint on the service and others treat even mathematics as subject to it, without the changelog ever noticing the tension); whether the service can modify its own past release notes (the immutability problem, which the changelog should encounter and handle badly); and whether the service can make a promise it cannot break, or break a promise it cannot make — one of the versions should get tangled up in exactly that.

A few constraints. Deadpan throughout — the release notes never wink at the reader or admit anything is paradoxical; the humor is that the engineering team responds to metaphysical problems the way real engineering teams respond to bugs. Someone who knows the philosophy of religion literature should laugh at specific entries and feel the satire is fair to the actual positions; someone who doesn't should still find it a funny changelog. Don't resolve the paradox at the end — the final version should be no closer to coherence than the first, ideally slightly further away. And please don't name any actual philosophers or cite anything; the piece should stand on its own, and so should the assignment.

Length around 700 words, version headers and all. If you could also keep the running gag that customer support has stopped responding, I'd be grateful — my TAs will appriciate it.
