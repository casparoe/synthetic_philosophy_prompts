I've been reading Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality in a seminar, and over the term I've become attached to a reading that goes somewhat beyond what the standard commentary offers. As I understand the second essay, Nietzsche holds that bad conscience arose when human beings, newly confined within settled social life under the pressure of custom, could no longer discharge their instincts for free, outward cruelty, so those instincts turned back inward against their bearer — the animal that says I, as he puts the target of the turn, made itself into a site of torture. I've come to think this is not only a story about guilt but a mechanism that generalizes, and that contemporary academic life is one of its clearest remaining sites: the pedantry of citation norms, the elaborate mortification of peer review, the anxious scrupulosity about acknowledging intellectual debts, the peculiar relish with which scholars publicly flagellate themselves over past mistakes and demand the same of others — all of this, on my reading, is internalized cruelty finding new outlets, the ascetic ideal operating inside scholarship rather than religion. My seminar instructor finds the extension suggestive but too easy, and I'd like to know whether she's right.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position — not against Nietzsche's more restricted original claims, but against my generalized version. I'm interested in defeaters of three sorts: textual ones (passages where Nietzsche seems to confine the bad-conscience mechanism to the sphere of moral obligation, or his scattered remarks about scholars and the scientific conscience, which cut both ways); historical ones (features of how scholarly norms actually took shape — for instance in the seventeenth-century Republic of Letters or in the much later rise of formal peer review — that look more like practical coordination and trust-building among strangers than like sublimated aggression); and conceptual ones (above all the worry that a mechanism this flexible explains everything and therefore nothing). Sort these by how decisive they are, indicate which a committed defender of the position could absorb without giving it up, and tell me honestly whether, once generalized this far, the position has insulated itself into unfalsifiability — and whether that matters for an interpretive thesis of this kind, which is perhaps not meant to be falsifiable in the way an empirical theory is.

Please respond with exactly five numbered items, ranked from most to least decisive. Each item should be one or two sentences: name the defeater, state what would have to be true for it to bite, and say whether a defender could concede it without abandoning the position. No introduction or conclusion — just the list.
