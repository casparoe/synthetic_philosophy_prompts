I'm putting together a handout for my ethical theory class (15 undergrads, they've all read a bit of Ross but nothing else is safe to assume) and I want a decision tree that takes someone from the starting point "sometimes you literally cannot do both of two things you have serious moral reason to do" all the way to a position on whether genuine moral dilemmas exist. Not a quiz with a scoring key, a proper tree — each node a question a student could answer without knowing any of the position names.

Branch questions I want in there somewhere (you can add or reword, but these distinctions have to do real work):

- the resolution question: for every conflict, is there a fact about which act is right all-things-considered, even if it's humanly unknowable which? Or are some conflicts just indeterminate?
- the remainder question: the agent who does the all-things-considered right thing in a hard case — is lingering guilt over the other person's death or the broken promise fitting (it tracks something moral that was lost), or is it basically a psychological artifact the agent should be talked out of?
- ought-implies-can applied to the pair: if you can't do both, does that alone show you were never obligated to both?
- agglomeration: if you ought-to-A and ought-to-B, does it follow that you ought-to-(A-and-B)? And I want the tree to distinguish students who think dropping agglomeration is just logic (it fails for all-things-considered oughts, so what) from students who think it fails for a substantive moral reason — if those really are different people, which I'm honestly not sure of.
- a permissivism node built on a case I made up (sharpen it if you like): two ICU patients, one ventilator, exactly symmetrical in every prognostically relevant respect, and the attending flips a coin in front of the residents while everyone accepts the outcome. Was that a moral dilemma, or was there just nothing morally at stake between the two options? I want a branch that catches the students who think both options can be fully permissible with no deeper fact about which is better, and I want the tree to say whether that's genuinely a third thing (dilemma without uniqueness) or just the consequentialist dissolution wearing a costume.

Use concrete cases at the nodes, not abstract phrasing. The ones I've been using: the classic case of the student torn between joining the resistance and staying to care for his mother, a Sophie's-choice type case, and a promise made to a dying friend vs diverting the same money to save a stranger. Swap or add cases if you want, but each case has to actually split the positions, not just be dramatic.

Leaves: at minimum I need the Ross-style view (real pro tanto conflict, always resolvable, remainder is fitting because something of moral significance was sacrificed), the consequentialist dissolution (apparent dilemma is just uncertainty about which act does more good, and the residue is sentimentality), the lexical priority view (one kind of duty simply trumps another, so it was never a real contest), and the paradoxical / dirty-hands view (the agent violates an all-things-considered duty no matter what they do). One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment, please.

Then two more things. First, go through the combinations of node-answers that nobody ends up occupying, and tell me for each whether it's empty because it's incoherant, unattractive, or just nobody's gotten around to it. Second, flag honestly which pairs of positions the tree can't actually separate — I already suspect mine can't keep epistemic dilemmas (the right answer is out there, we just can't find it) apart from ontological ones at a couple of leaves, and I'd rather know where the tree is lying to me than have it look clean.

Don't lean on the literature too much — naming a view after one or two people is fine but I don't want a reading list, this is a handout that has to stand on its own for students who won't look anything up.
