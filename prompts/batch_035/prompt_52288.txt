I'm a PhD student in epistemology writing a paper on the norms of assertion, and I'd like help thinking through a case that the standard debate seems to handle badly.

Please compare the knowledge norm of assertion (roughly: assert only what you know) with the truth norm (roughly: assert only what is true), and then assess how each fares with assertions in pure mathematics. Mathematicians routinely assert theorems whose proofs they have never personally checked, relying instead on peer review and the testimony of the community. The four-color theorem is a vivid instance: the original proof depends on an exhaustive computer check that no human has surveyed case by case, and the community nonetheless treats assertions of the theorem as paradigms of proper assertion. Individually, few mathematicians seem to know such results in the way the knowledge norm demands; and the truth norm is silent on why asserting a conjecture that happens to be true, before any proof is in, is improper. This suggests the operative norm might be collective rather than speaker-individual — perhaps something like: assert only what your epistemic community is entitled to rely on — or that the individual-level norms need supplementing with a story about the division of epistemic labor.
