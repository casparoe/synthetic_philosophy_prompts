My closest friend and I spent most of a long walk this past weekend arguing about something neither of us could put a name to, and I want your help working out whether the thing we kept circling has an established concept attached to it — and if it does, what exactly that concept covers.

The background: we both accept, roughly, the Schelling-style point that being credibly bound can itself be a source of bargaining power — the general who burns his bridges behind him, the union that puts its strike fund beyond recall before sitting down at the table. Neither of us doubts that. What we couldn't agree about is a second-order feature my friend kept waving away. When two parties each have the ability to bind themselves in ways the other can observe, and the binding is advantageous only if it takes effect *before* the other party binds themselves, there's pressure to commit early — early enough that you're committing under time pressure, with much less deliberation than you'd like, and often in ignorance of how the other side will respond. So individually rational agents end up with an incentive to lock in positions before they've thought them through, and once locked in, the positions can't be revised even when it becomes clear they were badly chosen. My friend says this is just ordinary credible commitment with a stopwatch attached, and that the stopwatch adds nothing of interest; I think the whole shape is different, because the race makes it rational to be less rational, so to speak — deliberation itself becomes the thing you're racing against. We tested this on examples: two firms deploying automated bargaining systems that must react to each other in milliseconds, where each firm chooses between letting its system gather information before committing to a stance or hard-coding the stance immediately, and whichever firm hard-codes first may set the terms of every later interaction; a negotiator who publicly and politically irreversibly pledges to her membership that she will never accept less than some figure, where the pledge is more powerful the earlier it is made, and the earliest moment is precisely the one at which she knows least about the other side's circumstances.

What I want from you is this: does the configuration — a race in which the strategic value of self-binding comes from being bound *first*, so that parties are pushed toward irreversible commitments made under deliberately degraded information and shortened deliberation — have a name in game theory or in any neighboring literature? If there is an established term, I want to know what it precisely covers and how it differs from the nearest neighbors I already know the names of: Schelling's commitment tactic itself, brinkmanship (which seems to involve escalating mutual risk rather than self-binding), and strategic irrationality, the so-called madman theory, where one feigns or cultivates an incapacity to be reasonable. If there's no established term, please say so plainly rather than forcing one of those neighbors onto the pattern, and instead tell me what you would call it, define it, and mark its boundary cases — for instance, whether the phenomenon survives when the commitments are only partially irreversible, or when only one party can commit at all. And since I'll be taking this back to my friend: wherever people who actually study this genuinely disagree about whether the phenomenon is distinct from ordinary commitment under deadline, please flag that disagreement as contested rather than papering over it — I'd rather bring him the open question than win the argument by having picked a side for him.
