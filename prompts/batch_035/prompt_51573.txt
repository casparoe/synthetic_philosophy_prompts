Setup in case it's new to you: a teacher announces on Friday "there will be an exam next week, on exactly one weekday, and it will be a surprise — on the morning of the exam you won't be able to work out which day it is." The students reason backward: it can't be on the last day, Friday, since by Thursday night with no exam yet they'd know it must be Friday, contradicting the surprise. But then it can't be Thursday either, because if Wednesday night arrives with no exam, they'd know it can't be Friday (just eliminated) so it would have to be Thursday, again no surprise. And so on until every day is eliminated, so the students conclude the announcement can't come true at all. And then the exam happens on, say, Tuesday, and they definately didn't see it coming.

Round one. My roommate said the backward induction is just modus ponens applied five times — if the Friday step is valid, there's no principled place to stop, so the conclusion follows: the announcement is self-defeating and the teacher simply cannot make it true. I said that's too fast: the Thursday step assumes the students still trust the announcement on Thursday night, but by then their own reasoning is midway through destroying that trust, so each later step leans on a premise their earlier steps are undermining — the induction eats its own tail.

Round two. She came back with: fine, that just means the students can never justifiably believe the announcement in the first place — it's an epistemic blind spot for them, true when stated but not knowable by the people it's about, so the teacher can truthfully make it and the students can still be surprised, since the surprise was never backed by anything they were entitled to rely on. I answered that this dissolves the paradox way too cheaply: if the students rationally conclude there will be no exam at all, then literally any exam day surprises them, and the announcement ends up true only because nobody believed it — the teacher's claim becomes uninformative, which can't be the whole story. She said maybe it is the whole story, and that "surprise" in these announcements does a lot less work than it looks like it does — the real content is something like "don't bother trying to out-calculate me," and the paradox only feels deep and wierd because we keep shifting between the students' perspective and an outside view of them.
