I'm taking an intro stats course and we just covered Simpson's paradox, using the old Berkeley admissions case: the university as a whole looked like it admitted men at a much higher rate than women, but within most individual departments women did fine or better, and the aggregate gap came mostly from which departments each sex applied to. My takeaway was that this shows aggregated statistics are basically propaganda — the subgroups are where the truth lives, so any headline figure should be presumed wrong until it's been broken down, and if an effect disappears when you dissagregate, it was never real to begin with. I've been applying this as a blanket rule in arguments with my friends, and one of them, who works in epidemiology, says it's not that simple. She claims she's seen studies where a treatment effect is real in the aggregate but vanishes or reverses once you split by a subgroup variable that is itself affected by the treatment, and that disaggregating by the wrong variable can manufacture a Simpson-style reversal out of thin air. She couldn't explain it in a way I followed, honestly.

So here's my question: given that Simpson's paradox proves the aggregate can always be flipped by disaggregation, how do you know in advance which level of the data is the "real" one? Is there a principled rule for how far to break the data down, or which variables to split by? Please answer in one paragraph of at most 250 words, and tell me straight if my rule of thumb is just wrong.
