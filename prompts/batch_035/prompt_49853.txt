In the course of reporting, I've been having a running argument with two friends, a couple deciding whether to try for a baby this year or wait another two. One of them, Lena, says waiting is a no-brainer: if they conceive now, their finances are tighter and the child's life would go somewhat worse than that of a child they could have later, so conceiving now would be unfair to that particular child. Her partner, Marcus, accepts the timing point but draws a further conclusion: if creating a somewhat worse-off child counts as a wrong to that child, then creating a happy child must count as a good to it, so couples who could have a happy child but choose not to are depriving someone of a happy life — perhaps not a tragedy, but at least a small moral failure. Lena finds that absurd: no one is wronged by never being conceived. Marcus replies that if the harm claim only works one way, it looks ad hoc. Both of them, I suspect, are half right and half wrong, but I can't pin down where the reasoning goes astray.

Please help me sort this out. Is there a principled way to hold that creating the worse-off child now is a reason to wait, while denying that failing to create a happy child wrongs anyone? If so, how would you state the principle, and where exactly does Marcus's inference from the first claim to his stronger conclusion break down? And if there is no principled way to hold both, which of the two should my friends give up, and what would following that through commit them to? Before answering, please brainstorm the available positions and the costs of each, then settle on the view you find most defensible and defend it. I'd like your advice in plain, non-technical language that I can adapt for readers of a general science-and-ideas publication.
