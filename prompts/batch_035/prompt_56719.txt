I keep needing to pick a "reference class" to estimate how likely something is, and I've noticed I have pretty firm gut feelings about which class is the right one in each case, even though I can't state the rule behind them. My brother says there is no rule and I'm just doing vibes, so I want to dump my verdicts on you and see if there's a principle that captures them — and, where it can't, tell me which of my verdicts has to go.

Here they are. Whether my startup dies within three years: the right class is seed-funded startups in our sector over the last decade — not "all new businesses" (that includes corner stores and restaurants) and definately not "all human endeavors." My own divorce risk: I want to condition on everything, married at 30 or later, both college-educated, no kids yet, how our parents' marriages turned out — the class can get tiny; even if it shrinks to a couple dozen couples I trust it more than the national rate. Whether my flight tomorrow is late: I'm happy with the airline's on-time percentage for this exact route this season, and it would be silly to further adjust for "I've personally had bad luck with flights this year." But then my recently divorced friend swears the right class for him was "married men whose wives have a lot of close male friends at work," which is way narrower than "all married men," and I think that's nuts — even though by my own "narrower = more informative" logic it should be great. Similarly, I'll happily use "people my age who exercise as much as I do" for my life expectancy, while I'd roll my eyes at someone using "people who share his zodiac sign," and I can't articulate the difference, since his class is narrower than mine.

The case I keep flip-flopping on: my doctor says my ten-year risk of a particular condition is about 6% using patients matched to me on age, weight, family history, and a couple of blood markers, versus about 9% using all patients of my age and sex. The matched group is only a few hundred people. I honestly can't decide whether 6 or 9 is "my" number, and it matters for whether I take an annoying preventive medication. Related question: when two defensible classes give wildly different answers — say "startups founded by first-time founders" versus "startups in my sector" for failure rates — is there any principled way to pick between them or combine them, or is that just the point where statistics shrugs? So: is there one principle behind all my verdicts, something about which features belong in the class and which are noise, and if not, which verdict would you overturn first? And I'm curious what the principle predicts for a case I haven't thought through: whether I'll like a restaurant I've never been to — am I in the class of "people whose ratings have matched mine on past restaurants," or "people my age in my neighborhood," and does your principle give a clear answer there?
