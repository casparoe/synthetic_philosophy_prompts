I chair the wellbeing and discipline subcommittee of the governing board at a large public secondary school of about 1,400 students. For decades our discipline system has run on what everyone calls the honor board: offenders' names are read at Monday assembly, students may be required to stand and publicly apologize to the school, and a list of those on disciplinary probation is posted outside the headteacher's office. After an incident last autumn in which a Year 10 student withdrew from school entirely following a public naming, a reform faction of teachers, supported by our school psychologist, proposed replacing the whole apparatus with a restorative model: private conferences with the harmed party, negotiated repair agreements, and an explicit ban on public identification. Their stated reason is that shame is the wrong moral emotion for a school to cultivate — shame tells a student that he *is* bad, whereas guilt tells him that he *did* something bad, and only the second points at repair rather than at hiding. But an old guard of teachers, two of whom grew up in families where honor and public standing were the central moral currency, are pushing back. Their claim is that shame is not some Anglo-American pathology to be diagnosed out of children: entire cultures organize moral life around face, honor, and communal standing without collapsing into a population of withdrawn, self-loathing adults, and they suspect that a shame-free school will graduate students with tidy private consciences but no moral stake in the social world around them — that we will be teaching morality as something between a student and his own reflection, with no audience and therefore no cost.

The headteacher has asked the subcommittee for a written recommendation before the start of the spring term, and I would like the memo I circulate to be philosophically serious rather than a restatement of safeguarding boilerplate. Please draft it. It should recommend a concrete course of action — full replacement, retention of the honor board, or a specified hybrid with clear limits on what may ever be made public — and reason from what is actually understood about the difference between guilt and shame as moral emotions. Two questions in particular need honest treatment. First, whether shame really is the worse educator: the picture may be more mixed than the reform faction admits, since some cultures seem to sustain shame without producing the withdrawn, evasive variant we see in our own shamed students, which would suggest the problem lies in how shame is administered — the permanence, the audience, the absence of a route back — rather than in shame as such. Second, and this is the question our deputy head keeps raising in meetings, a school is one of the very few cultures that can actually *choose* its moral emotions: it can write rules, stage rituals, and control what becomes public knowledge, in a way that whole societies notoriously cannot. Whatever we recommend is therefore an act of deliberate cultural engineering, and the memo should say plainly whether that is a reason for caution — we are experimenting on the emotional formation of other people's children — or precisely why choice is possible here when it famously is not at the level of a society. Please engage the strongest version of the old guard's position rather than dismissing it, recommend a rollout with a time frame and a review point, and be candid about where the psychological evidence runs out and the decision becomes a question of values that the full board, not the literature, must settle.
