My roommate and my old TA have been going at each other about the Liar paradox for like two years now and I finally got them to agree to an adversarial collaboration, and they asked me to draft the joint statement since I'm "neutral" (I'm mostly just confused). One of them is a paracomplete guy — truth-value gaps, Kripke-style fixed points, and he's into Field's stuff where you iterate a "determinately" operator to try to outrun revenge. The other one is a full dialetheist — Priest, LP, the Liar is both true and false, no apologies. I want you to write the document they should have come out of the room with.

Structure it like this:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. And I mean claims they could both actually put their name on — no weasel wording that just hides the disagreement. I'd expect stuff about naive truth principles being what generates the paradox, about classical logic not being fully recoverable, about revenge problems being real and not a fringe worry. If some apparent agreement is actually just equivocation on "not true," flag it instead of letting them sign it.

2. A disagreements table. The wording for each side has to be something that side itself would accept as a fair statement of its own view — so the paracomplete row can't sneak in "the Liar is untrue" if the dialetheist thinks that very sentence is glutty. I'm especially curious whether part of the gap-vs-glut fight is partly verbal, e.g. about whether their negations and their "untrue"s even mean the same thing, and if so which part is verbal and which part is left over.

3. The cruxes, and this is the part I care about most. For each one, record what both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. Use these test sentences, stated in the jointly agreed vocabulary:

(a) The plain revenge liar: "This sentence is not true" — as in not true, not just false. What does each camp's machinery actually say about it, and does the paracompletist's "determinately true, determinately not true" apparatus and the dialetheist's "true and not true" apparatus end up disagreeing about any concrete case, or only about how to describe it?

(b) "This sentence is false or has no truth value." — the classic anti-gap revenge. The gap guy has to say something and so does the glut guy, and I want each one's honest prediction about whether his own theory handles it cleanly or just pushes the problem one level up.

(c) The mirror one: "This sentence is false or is both true and false." — anti-glut revenge. Same deal. I have a suspicion that (b) and (c) are more symmetric than either of my friends admits, and I want to know if that's right.

(d) Curry: "If this sentence is true, the moon is made of cheese." Both camps have grief with the condtional, and my sense is this is where the choice of conditional is actually load-bearing. Have each side predict whether the other's conditional can be made to work here without giving up modus ponens or contraction.

4. One last thing, and this is my own addition: put a line in the agreed section that says "At least one claim in this joint report is not true." Are they allowed to sign a report containing that sentence? Given what each of them thinks assertion and endorsement amount to when the asserted thing might be a glut or a gap? If the answer is different for the two of them, that itself belongs in the disagreements table.

Keep it honest — where one theory is just silent or hand-wavy, say so rather than papering over it. And don't let either camp come out looking like the obvious winner, since if I wanted that I'd just let them keep yelling at each other over dinner.
