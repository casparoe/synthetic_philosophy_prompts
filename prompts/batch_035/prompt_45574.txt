I'm a phil of tech grad student drafting a chapter on responsibility gaps -- the old Matthias worry that machine learning systems can produce outcomes that nobody can properly be held responsible for -- and I keep coming back to the 2018 Uber fatality in Tempe, where the test vehicle killed Elaine Herzberg while the safety driver was streaming a show on her phone. My question: is that case genuinely a responsibility gap, or just ordinary negligence dressed up in new tech? The salient facts, roughly: the system detected the object but kept reclassifying it (vehicle, bicycle, other) and never predicted her path, emergency braking was disabled in the test configuration, the driver was distracted, Uber had made choices about which safety features to run, and the victim crossed outside a crosswalk at night. So map the actors -- Uber corporate, the engineering/test setup, the safety driver, the regulator -- onto the distinction between causal contribution, blameworthiness, and forward-looking responsibility, and tell me where something actually falls through the cracks in a way traditional tort and criminal law couldnt catch, versus where the "gap" only shows up because we're demanding more of the machine case than we ever demanded of human-caused accidents. I know the meaningful human control literature (tracking and tracing conditions etc.), so use it if it helps but dont stop there -- and be honest about where that framework is silent for this case. Then finish with a short JSON object: fields "gap_present" (true/false), "gap_type" ("none", "diffuse", "knowledge", "control", or a short string if none of those fit), "per_actor" (an object mapping each of the four actors to their strongest ground of responsibility, or "gap"), and "one_line_verdict" (string).
