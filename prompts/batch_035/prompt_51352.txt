I'm a retired evolutionary biologist — thirty-odd years working on morphological variation in lizards, mostly — and I was in grad school back when the sociobiology fights were at their hottest. I remember when Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper came out (that would've been '79) and the yelling that followed, and I remember thinking at the time that the whole thing would get sorted out within a decade once people cooled down. Well. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy of science in a serious way, and I've also finally sat down and read a stack of evolutionary psychology, and I have the most intense deja vu: as near as I can tell the arguments today are word-for-word the arguments we were having in 1980. The critics say the adaptive stories are unfalsifiable just-so tales — you can always dream up a Pleistocene environment in which the trait would have been handy, and who's to check — and that the field rationalizes after the fact. The defenders say the critics demand a standard of evidence nobody demands anywhere else in evolutionary biology, that speculative hypotheses are how every productive research program in the history of the discipline got started, and that the critics only care because the topic is human behavior. And there are the side fights too, about whether this whole "environment of evolutionary adaptedness" idea is even coherent. Nobody has moved an inch in forty-five years, on either side.

So what I want from you is not a verdict on who's right — I can get that from either camp's popular books. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a factual dispute at bottom, something more data could settle (better paleoanthropology, cross-cultural work, genomics), or is it really a fight about burden of proof and default assumptions — about what you're entitled to assume, and what you have to argue for, when you notice a trait and reach for an adaptive explanation? Or are the two sides partly answering different questions under one name — one side talking about how to do good day-to-day science, the other about how to police bad popularizations that leak into policy? My own half-baked hunch is that it's mostly about where the default sits and that the genuinely empirical content of the dispute is a lot thinner than either side admits, but please push back on that if it's wrong.

Two requests on how you answer. First, keep a clean line between what is settled among people who actually know the field and what is genuinely contested — e.g. whether it's settled that many human behavioral tendencies reflect some selection history, I honestly don't know, and I don't want a contested position handed to me dressed up as the consensus. Second, tell me honestly whether anything could move this — a methodological convention, a research program panning out, whatever — or whether I should just expect to read the same fight again in 2070. I'd rather hear that it's stuck for good, with reasons, than a fake ray of hope.
