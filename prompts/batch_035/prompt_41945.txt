I teach at a place where the real philosophy happens at the wine hour after the weekly colloquium, and for years now my colleague Ruth — metaethicist by trade, opinions about literally everything — has been issuing verdicts on the little deception hypotheticals we throw around. I've started keeping a list. Next week I want to run a new case by her, and I'd like to walk in with a prediction in hand, as a bit of a game between us. Can you help me figure out what she'll say?

Her track record so far:

1. Murderer at the door asking whether his victim is in your house. Straight lie — "No, he's not here." Ruth: totally fine.
2. Same murderer, but you say "He's not in the house," which is true — the guy is hiding in the garden shed. Also fine, and she got genuinly annoyed when our junior colleague suggested this answer is somehow cleaner. She said the two are exactly on a par and she doesn't award any moral credit for keeping your sentences literally true.
3. Granny knits you an itchy sweater and asks how you like it. You say "I love it." Ruth: a wrong. Tiny, maybe excused, but a wrong. She is very firm on the pro tanto point.
4. Same granny, same sweater, but you say "I've never seen anything like it!" — true, and she takes it as delight, which it isn't. Ruth: no wrong at all. Fashion chatter, she says, is its own genre.
5. A student asks whether you've seen the emails about the reading group. You have, but haven't read them, and you say "My inbox is a disaster zone right now" — true — and the student walks away convinced you haven't seen them, which is exactly what you were going for. Ruth: wrong, and she said she doesn't see a shred of difference between that and just saying "No, I haven't."
6. Telling your dinner host "This is the best meal I've ever had" when it's Tuesday-night pasta. Ruth: not even in the ballpark, that's just how talking works.

The new case: a friend of a friend is on a second date, the other person asks "Are you seeing anyone else?", and the answer given is "Work has completely taken over my life lately" — true as far as it goes, except this person IS seeing someone else, and the sentence was chosen precisely so the date would conclude otherwise. Will Ruth call this wrong, or fine?

Honestly the verdict is the least of what I want from you. What rule is she implicitly running such that 1–6 all come out the way they did? I can see at least two that fit her whole track record, not just some of it, and I suspect they part ways exactly on my date case. So: predict her verdict, lay out the competing rules and what each says about the date, tell me which you'd bet on and why, and then tell me what case I should bring to the NEXT wine hour to really force her to choose between them. I have a feeling it involves saying something false to someone who knows full well it's false, but I haven't worked it out.
