Grading intro philosophy of science finals and would love a second pair of eyes before I finalize grades. The question was: "Present the no-miracles argument for scientific realism. Then present the pessimistic meta-induction as an objection, and explain one realist reply to it."

Here's one student's answer:

"The no-miracles argument, first formulated by Hilary Putnam, says that the empirical success of mature scientific theories would be a miracle unless those theories were at least approximately true, so realism is the best explanation of why science works. The pessimistic meta-induction, associated with Larry Laudan's 'A Confutation of Convergent Realism' (1981), responds with a list of past theories that were empirically successful in their day but are now judged false — phlogiston, the caloric theory of heat, the optical ether. By induction over that list, our current successful theories are probably false too. So the realist owes us some way of breaking the symmetry between past and present science.

One reply is structural realism, defended by John Worrall following Poincaré. The idea is that the mathematical structure of past theories gets retained even when their claims about entities are lost. For example, the equations of phlogiston theory carried over essentially unchanged into modern chemistry, so phlogiston theory was structurally correct even though phlogiston itself doesn't exist. This shows the pessimistic induction only threatens entity realism, not structural realism.

Another reply is selective realism: distinguish the 'working posits' of a theory, the ones that actually do the explanatory work, from 'idle posits', and claim that in each of Laudan's cases it was only the idle posits that got discarded. Since Laudan's successful-but-false theories only ever had idle posits doing the work, the induction never really gets off the ground.

Overall, the pessimistic induction proves that empirical adequacy is the most we can ever achieve, which is exactly what constructive empiricism claims, so realism as traditionally understood is untenable."
