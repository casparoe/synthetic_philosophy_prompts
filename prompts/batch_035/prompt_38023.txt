I do model validation at a mid-sized asset manager — my team's job is basically deciding wether some new predictive model or research write-up deserves to move our positions before we put real money on it. The thing I keep hitting, and can't find a clean answer to, is retrodiction. Somebody shows up with a model that would have called the 2008 crash, or a new factor that "explains" the last forty years of returns, and the pitch is always look how well it fits the history. But we already knew the history — I gather the philosophers of science call this the problem of old evidence, and that straight Bayesian conditioning kind of chokes on it, since your probability for data you already have can't exactly move when the new model arrives. I don't need that debate settled, I need something usable.

So: write a procedure my team can actually run in an afternoon on a model that shows up pre-fitted to history. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends with "no retrodiction credit, come back with live forecasts", and where it hands off to a human because the question is a judgment call dressed up as arithmetic — stuff like how much the builder had the known data in mind when designing the thing, or whether the whole fit hinges on one episode. It should also say what to do about the awkward middle case, models with no track record at all that we can't just sit on for five years while they accumulate one, and it should be honest about which steps are basically vibes.

Then run it on two cases so I can see where it bites: a valuation model that supposedly would have flagged both the 2000 tech bubble and 2008 before they broke, and a macro model built in 2024 that nails the 2021–2023 inflation retroactively and now predicts a recession in 2026. If you can, end with which step of the procedure you'd expect to fail most often in practice — where the retrodiction trap actually catches people like me.
