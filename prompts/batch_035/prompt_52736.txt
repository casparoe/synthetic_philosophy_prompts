I run a small blog where we do those self-assessment quizzes — so far we've done "which theory of personal identity do you actually hold" and "are you a Bayesian in denial," and they do pretty well. Next up I want one on the norms of assertion: when is it OK to flat-out say "p" (as opposed to hedging with "I think p" or "probably p")?

The quiz should be 8-10 questions, each a concrete little scenario with 2-3 answer options. The scoring key at the end maps answer patterns onto: the knowledge norm (assert only what you know), the truth norm (assert only what's true), the justification norm (assert only what you're justified in believing), the belief norm (assert only what you actually believe), and a "no single norm / it's all context" deflationary slot. But here's the thing — the questions have to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like. So no question is allowed to just ask "do you think you need knowledge to assert?" People have to be answering scenarios, and the labels only show up in the scoring key.

Cases I want in there somewhere (you can pick which, but a good spread):

- the lottery case: your ticket is one of a million, the drawing is rigged by God so you win, you've seen the result isn't announced yet but you know the odds... okay, simpler version: it's extremely probable you'll lose, you do lose, is having checked the odds enough to have asserted "I'll lose" beforehand?
- Moorean stuff: someone says "p, but I don't believe p" or "p, but I don't know whether p" — is that infelicitous in a way that tells us something about the norm?
- the selfless assertion: a teacher who's a closet creationist teaches evolution from the books, knows it all cold because they studied the literature, but doesn't believe a word of it. When they tell students "humans and chimps share a common ancestor," are they asserting properly?
- a Gettier case: your belief is true and justified but only luckily so (JTB without K), do you assert?
- justified false belief: flat-out asserting something false you had every reason to think was true
- poker bluffing and other games where lying is the point — does the norm not apply there, and if not, does that hurt the norm?
- secondhand knowledge: telling someone "the bridge is out" on good authority, without having seen it yourself

One more thing, and this is the part I actually care about most: I want the scoring key to be honest about where the quiz fails. Some of those leaves probably can't be seperated by any realistic answer pattern — like, people who take knowledge and justification norms apart mostly do it on Gettier, so if someone answers the Gettier question the "wrong" way for their other answers, what do you do with them? And the belief norm vs justification norm split probably needs the selfless-assertion question to do all the work. Point out which questions are load-bearing like that. Also note any answer-pattern combos that don't map to a real position, and say whether nobody holds them because they're incoherent, unappealing, or just unexplored.

Oh and — the quiz should distinguish propriety from prudence. A few questions where asserting is arguably proper but dumb (or improper but harmless/blameless), so we find out whether people's gut answers track "was that assertion OK, full stop" vs "was that a sensible thing to say." If a respondent's answers are all about prudence, maybe they get a note that the quiz couldn't reach their actual norm. Audience is smart laypeople, no jargon in the questions themselves (the scoring key can use the jargon, with one-sentence explanations). Make sure the knowledge norm, the truth norm, and the justification norm all show up as reachable leaves, and give each leaf one line on its most awkward commitment.
