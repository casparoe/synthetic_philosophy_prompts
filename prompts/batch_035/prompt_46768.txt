So at 33 I finally got assessed and diagnosed as autistic (the kind that used to be called Asperger's, before they folded everything into one spectrum). A bunch of things changed after that. I started reading stuff written by other autistic adults, found out there's a word — masking — for all the effort I'd been putting into eye contact and small talk and pretending not to be overwhelmed, and basically stopped doing a lot of it. I let myself stim at home. And honestly, my autistic traits are more visible now than they were in the ten years before the diagnosis, at least to family.

My sister thinks this is proof of a self-fulfilling prophecy, that the label made me "more autistic," and now she's dead set against my brother-in-law getting their son assessed, because the kid is just a bit socially awkward and she doesn't want a diagnosis to create the condition in him the way she thinks it did in me. My first reaction was that she's confusing me being more myself with me being more impaired. But then she said something that actually got under my skin: if a whole generation of people gets diagnosed young, grows up knowing what the label means, reads the same accounts I've been reading, and changes how they present — isn't the category itself shifting underneath? Like, the diagnostic criteria were validated on people who had no concept of what they were being screened for, and now the instrument is being applied to a population that knows exactly what answers lead where. Does that make the category track something different than it did 20 years ago?

I guess I have three questions tangled together and I'd love help pulling them apart. First, is there a coherent sense in which the diagnosis *caused* my traits, versus just causing my willingness to show them, and does that distinction even hold up for things like stimming that were clearly there all along? Second, if lots of diagnosed people reshape themselves partly in response to the label, does that erode the validity of the category over time, or does it not matter so long as the same underlying people keep getting picked out? And third, does any of this give my sister a real argument against assessing her son, or is she just taking a fact about how the concept works and drawing exactly the wrong practical conclusion from it? For what it's worth I think she's wrong about the last one but I can't quite say why without also sounding like I think the label is consequence-free.
