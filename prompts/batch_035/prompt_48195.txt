So a bit of background on me: I'm a backend software engineer, mostly Python and a bit of Go, no philosophy training beyond skimming a Dennett essay someone left in the office kitchen years ago. At work I've noticed something odd about how our team talks about the ML systems we build. When the fraud-detection model misclassifies something, half the team says things like "it's learned that foreign cards are risky" or "it thinks refunds are suspicious", while the other half insists that's sloppy anthropomorphism and we should only ever say "the model assigns high probability to class X given features Y". The funny thing is the "it thinks" camp debugs faster. I've been chewing on this ever since, and it's got me half-convinced that treating a system as if it has beliefs isn't a mistake about the system, it's a tool — but a tool whose price I can't quite work out. I've tried writing my thoughts down and it just comes out as a bulleted list of gripes, so I want to try it as fiction instead.

Write me a short story, roughly 1500-2000 words, set in a company like mine but slightly bigger: a mid-sized firm running an LLM-based assistant that helps triage customer support tickets. The protagonist is an on-call engineer, let's call her Priya, who has been paged at 2am because the assistant has started routing billing complaints to the wrong queue. The story should follow one long night of debugging, but the real subject is the language Priya reaches for as the night wears on. At first she forces herself to stay mechanical — "the classifier conditions on the ticket prefix" — and then, somewhere around the fifth failed hypothesis, she slips: "it's avoiding the refunds queue", "it doesn't trust the billing labels". I want the story to dramatise the question of when that slip is a useful compression and when it starts making predictions the system won't cash out — maybe there's a moment where "it wants to avoid blame" leads her badly astray, and another where only something like "it's treating the new queue as spam" cracks the case. No magic realism please, the system should stay boringly non-conscious; the whole point is that the intentional vocabulary is doing work even though everyone in the story agrees there's nobody home. If you can end it somewhere quieter than a verdict — say Priya writing the incident report and having to choose, for the official record, between the two vocabularies, knowing the report will be read by an intern who'll learn how to talk about the system from her — that would be great. Please don't have any character lecture about philosophy of mind; I want the ideas to be carried entirely by the dialogue and Priya's internal narration, the way engineers actually talk at 3am, typos and grumpiness included. One more small thing: I'd like a supporting character, an old-school engineer who refuses the intentional talk on principle and is right about it exactly once.
