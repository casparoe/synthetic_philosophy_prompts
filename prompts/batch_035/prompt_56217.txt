At a recent workshop on AI risk, a presenter argued for aggressive pre-deployment compute governance by analogy to nuclear fission: a system capable of recursive self-improvement is like an assembled critical mass of fissile material. Below some threshold capability, each improvement fails to trigger enough further improvements and the process dies out, the way a subcritical chain reaction fizzles; above it, improvements multiply exponentially and the thing runs away, so the only remaining questions are when it goes critical and whether anyone can insert control rods in time. She leaned on the analogy to support two conclusions: that capability thresholds are real and sharp, in the way criticality is a genuine discontinuity in material behavior rather than a gradual slope, and that post-threshold intervention is nearly hopeless, so all of our governance effort should go into pre-threshold controls — her phrase was that we should be managing enrichment, not firefighting.

I found the picture vivid but left unsure how much work the physics was actually doing. Please audit the analogy. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the fissile atom, the neutron, the neutron multiplication factor k, the critical mass, prompt criticality, control rods, and the time constant of the exponential — and flag any cells that can't be filled without straining. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for her two uses from the ones that don't. I'd particularly like your judgment on: whether the fission picture can even represent a case where the effective k falls below one mid-runaway, as improvement hits diminishing returns or bottlenecks in experiments, serial research time, or hardware; whether criticality's sharpness is doing illegitimate work, given that in a reactor it's a property of geometry and material at a moment, while whatever corresponds to k in an AI system depends on choices, supply chains, and the state of the surrounding world; and whether the fact that a chain reaction consumes its fuel while a self-improving system might expand its own is a mere wrinkle or a structural break. Finally, tell me whether her two conclusions — sharp thresholds, and the near-hopelessness of post-threshold intervention — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them the whole time; and if some other comparison (compound interest, an autocatalytic reaction, a cascade failure, a bandwagon) would do the same work more honestly, say which and why.
