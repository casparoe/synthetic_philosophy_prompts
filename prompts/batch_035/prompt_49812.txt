I am a software engineer whose day job is formal specification and model-based testing: underspecified requirements, refinement, conformance suites, test oracles, and the constant question of what a requirement determines versus what it leaves open. On the side I have been reading about vagueness, and I can state the two positions I care about without jargon: the epistemicist holds that there is a sharp boundary between 'heap' and 'not a heap' — a precise number of grains where the one becomes the other — and that our ignorance of it is just that, ignorance; the supervaluationist holds that a vague sentence is true only if it comes out true on every admissible precisification, false only if it comes out false on every one, and otherwise has no truth value at all. I can parrot both. What I cannot do is check either claim, because it is never stated in terms I can evaluate.

So translate this debate into my vocabulary, and then tell me what the translation loses. As the target frame, take a vague predicate to be an underspecified requirement: the community's usage and dispositions constrain but do not fully determine the predicate's extension, the way a specification constrains but does not fully determine the behaviour of every conforming implementation. The admissible precisifications are then the implementations that conform to the spec. Within that frame, restate each position as faithfully as you can. My first pass is that epistemicism becomes the claim that there is a unique intended implementation — the customer had one machine in mind, the spec underdetermines it, and our tests can never reveal which one it is — while supervaluationism becomes the claim that the requirement determines a property of the system only via what it entails of every conforming implementation: a property holds of the spec iff it holds on all implementations, and where two conforming implementations diverge, the spec is simply silent. Correct this mapping wherever it is wrong before you rely on it; do not let my framing do your work.

The residue report is the part I actually want, so be concrete:

1. Clean translations. Which parts of the dispute become almost obvious once stated my way? I suspect that supertruth is just entailment by the spec, and that the truth-value gap is an input on which conforming implementations are permitted to diverge. If it is that tidy, say so plainly.

2. Distortions. Which parts only translate because something got bent? Two I already worry about. First, treating a natural language as a specification smuggles in a designer with intentions and a moment at which the spec is signed off, and English has neither an author nor a delivery date. Second, the epistemicist's unknowable cutoff would have to be a unique intended implementation that exists without anyone intending it — and if you have to posit that to make the translation run, I want to know whether the posited thing is doing real work in the original view or is a cost the original view also pays.

3. No counterpart. Which parts of the debate have no expression in my vocabulary at all? The obvious candidate is higher-order vagueness — the penumbral sentences whose penumbral status is itself penumbral, which in my frame would be a regress of specifications of specifications. Does engineering have any genuine analogue, or does the analogy simply run out there? Say honestly whether this is a limit of my vocabulary or a place where the philosophy is pointing at something no spec-language can say.

4. Verdict. My instinct is that the whole dispute collapses into a question every engineering project faces: who is entitled to close an underspecification, and when — the epistemicist answering that reality closed it long ago and we merely cannot read the closing, the supervaluationist answering that no one has closed it and correctness outruns any single closing. Tell me whether that instinct is right, or whether it flattens a genuine disagreement into a project-management choice.
