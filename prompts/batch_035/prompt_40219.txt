"The Allais paradox comes from Maurice Allais in the early 1950s, when he presented it to an audience including Leonard Savage, who reportedly switched his answer on the spot. The setup involves two choices. First choice: option A gives you $1 million for sure; option B gives you $5 million with probability 0.10, $1 million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. Second choice: option C gives you $1 million with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89; option D gives you $5 million with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90. Most people pick A over B and D over C.

The axiom these choices violate is the independence axiom, which Savage also formulated as the sure-thing principle — they are basically the same principle stated in different frameworks. The independence axiom says that if you prefer lottery A to lottery B, you should also prefer any mixture of A with some third lottery C to the same mixture of B with C, provided the mixing is done with the same probabilities. This is what allows expected utility theory to assume that compound lotteries can be reduced to their induced simple lotteries, since you can always substitute the preferred branch in and simplify.

You can see the violation by noting that both pairs share a common consequence of $1 million with probability 0.89, which cancels out. So preferring A over B but D over C is incoherent — specifically, it's a violation of transitivity, since the preferences flip when the shared component is removed. This incoherence is usually explained by the certainty effect: people overweight outcomes that are certain, so the sure million in the first pair looms larger than it should relative to its expected value.

Is this a genuine problem for EU theory? I'd say yes, but less than advertised. A defender can respond in several ways. First, the EU theorist can say the choices are just errors — but Allais himself anticipated this, arguing that his own preferences in the paradox were deliberate and stable, so the 'mistake' response looks question-begging. Second, the theorist can say the violated axiom wasn't really justified in the first place: the independence axiom is a substantive assumption about rationality, not something forced on us by logic, and once we see it can fail descriptively we might doubt its normative force too. Third, one can revise the theory — Machina, for instance, dropped independence while trying to keep much of the rest of the apparatus. My own view is that since the certainty effect has a plausible psychological explanation and the choices feel rational to the people making them, the burden is on the EU theorist to show why the independence axiom deserves its normative status rather than just asserting it."
