You are an expert in formal epistemology, specializing in accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory, with hands-on experience translating that theory into working procedures for analytic and forecasting teams; you write for practitioners, not for journals.

I lead a geopolitical forecasting unit at a risk consultancy. Each quarter, my analysts log credences on 80–120 questions with fixed resolution dates, and everyone is scored on a Brier-style basis. We run a two-stage process: credences are logged privately, then revealed to the whole team, and each analyst may revise once after seeing the others' numbers. Our current revision policy is "use your judgment," which in practice means the timid drift toward the median and the stubborn don't move at all. I want to replace this with a disciplined second-stage policy grounded in what accuracy-first epistemology actually supports: what the propriety of scoring rules implies about when deference maximizes expected accuracy, when averaging over a crowd with conditionally independent evidence beats holding your own credence, and — crucially — when those arguments break, for instance when analysts share the same news diet so their errors are correlated, when one analyst holds private information the others cannot see, or when the resolution criterion is vague enough that "the truth" is not a fixed target.

Please think carefully and begin with a short brainstorm of what the epistemic utility framework gives you to work with here and what it does not, then commit to the procedure.

The deliverable: a decision procedure an analyst can run in about ten minutes at the reveal stage, before revising a single credence. It should be stated in words with at most arithmetic — nobody on my team will evaluate an integral under deadline — and should specify, in order: what to inspect about the distribution of the other credences (spread, clustering, who holds the outliers), what to inspect about one's own credence and the evidence behind it, when the policy mandates moving a specified distance toward the group mean, when it permits holding, and when it requires flagging the question to me rather than revising at all. Mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then work the procedure through three cases: (1) all seven other analysts cluster tightly around 0.15 on a currency-crisis question where you logged 0.45, and you suspect your number leans on sources the whole team reads anyway; (2) you are the only analyst with a contact on the ground, and the team median runs against you; (3) the team splits cleanly into clusters at 0.2 and 0.8 on an election question whose resolution wording is ambiguous enough that reasonable people could score the outcome either way. At least one worked case should end in an answer my analysts will resent, and you should close by stating what the procedure cannot do — the situations in which the entire framing of accuracy-maximizing revision misfires and the honest move is to escalate or abstain rather than revise.
