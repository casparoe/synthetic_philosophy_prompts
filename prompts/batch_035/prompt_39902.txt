A comparative-religion book I'm reading introduces the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda — the claim that truth is many-sided — with the old parable of the blind men and the elephant: each man touches one part, the trunk, the ear, the leg, the tusk, and each confidently reports what the elephant is — a snake, a fan, a pillar, a ploughshare — so that each is partly right and altogether wrong. From this the author concludes that no philosophical or religious doctrine can claim the whole of truth, that traditions should regard their own views as one perspective among equally valid others, and that dogmatism is the only genuine intellectual sin. My suspicion is that the parable supports far less than this. Please audit the analogy: set out what maps onto what — the elephant, the blindness, the body part touched, the person who arranged the confrontation — and then say where the mapping breaks. Note in particular that the parable seems to involve a fully determinate elephant existing whether or not anyone perceives it, with each man's report simply true of the part he touched; that reads more like realism about a single whole truth, glimpsed partially, than like the claim that truth itself is perspectival. So: does the strong conclusion — all doctrines equally valid, dogmatism the only error — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the elephant carrying it the whole way? And if the parable fails as an argument for the strong conclusion, is there a weaker version of many-sidedness that it does support, or a different analogy that would better serve the author's purpose? Please open with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and no restating of my question.
