Consider the following claim about why human disagreement persists:

C: Among pairs of honest, reflective people who genuinely try to make their beliefs track the evidence, and who nonetheless disagree about some factual proposition despite prolonged good-faith exchange, the most important way they violate the assumptions of Aumann's agreement theorem is at the level of priors rather than at the level of common knowledge — that is, their disagreement persists principally because they began with genuinely different prior credences, not because their posteriors never became common knowledge at the level the theorem requires. Restrict the reference class to factual propositions with determinate truth values (no value disagreements, no strategic deception); and if you think no single violated assumption dominates in the typical case, count C false rather than splitting credit between the two.

I am not asking for a lecture on the theorem; assume I know its statement (common priors; posteriors of an event common knowledge implies the posteriors are equal). What I want is your credence in C, with the derivation exposed. Please give me a number for each of the following terms, one line of justification apiece, and then the combination:

1. The prior term: the probability that, for the typical pair in the reference class, the two agents' credences prior to their exchange differ in a way that does real explanatory work — that is, in a way not reducible to evidence both would accept as shared.
2. The common-knowledge term: the probability that, for the typical pair, iterated disclosure of posteriors has actually reached genuine common knowledge (each knows the other's number, knows the other knows, and so on without limit), rather than stalling at some shallow finite depth of mutual knowledge.
3. The bounded-rationality term: the probability that even granting common priors and common knowledge of posteriors, the agents' stated numbers would fail to equal what ideal Bayesians would hold, because neither can actually compute the posterior their total evidence implies — so that the theorem's conclusion is defeated by intractability alone, whichever assumption you apportion the rest of the blame to.
4. The reducibility complication: the probability that terms 1 and 2 are not genuinely independent — since a prior is typically the residue of evidence absorbed before the exchange, an apparent prior difference may simply be a common-knowledge failure about pre-exchange evidence, laundered. If your credence here is high, say so, and adjust the way you combine terms 1 and 2 rather than multiplying them as if independent.

Then tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to, and describe one realistic observation — a study, a protocol, a kind of transcript analysis, something actually obtainable rather than a thought experiment about ideal Bayesians — that would move your number the most. For calibration: my own credence in C is roughly 0.3. If you land materially away from that, I would like you to say which of my four terms you think I am most likely mis-weighting, and in which direction.
