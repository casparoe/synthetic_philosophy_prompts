I am marking closed-book final examinations for a third-year metaphysics course. Below is one of the exam questions, followed by an answer reproduced verbatim from one script. I would like a second marker's assessment.

The exam question:

Section B, Question 3. State the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, taking care to distinguish numerical from qualitative identity and to make clear which formulation of the principle is nontrivial. Explain Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment and what it is intended to show about the principle. Then consider the most promising replies available to a defender of the principle, and say whether any of them succeed.

The student's answer:

Numerical identity is being one and the same object, whereas qualitative identity is having all the same properties. Two copies of a book from the same print run are qualitatively identical but numerically two. The principle of the identity of indiscernibles (PII) says that qualitative identity entails numerical identity: if two things have all their properties in common, there is in fact only one thing. This is simply the converse of Leibniz's Law, which says that if a is identical with b, then everything true of a is true of b. Leibniz's Law is a truth of logic, and since PII is its converse, PII is a truth of logic as well: to suppose two indiscernible objects is as incoherent as supposing a married bachelor. Leibniz put the principle to theological use, arguing that God, always having a sufficient reason for his choices, would never create two exactly similar drops of water, since there would then be no reason for placing one here rather than there.

Black's thought experiment asks us to imagine a world whose only contents are two spheres of the same size, material, mass, temperature, and so on, resting a fixed distance apart with nothing else in existence. The world is perfectly symmetrical: reflection through the plane midway between the spheres maps the world onto itself. Whatever is true of one sphere is true of the other. Black concludes that here we have two things sharing all their properties, which refutes PII.

The natural reply is that the spheres differ in location, one being to the left and the other to the right. This will not do, since left and right are defined only relative to an observer, and there is none; and in any case each sphere is at some distance from a sphere just like itself, which is true of both. The same defeats the suggestion that each sphere has the property of being distant from an indiscernible sphere, since the other has that property too. So the location reply fails, and with it any reply that tries to name a qualitative difference between the spheres.

It is worth adding that if we allowed non-qualitative properties, such as the property of being identical with this very sphere, PII would become trivially true, since nothing but that sphere itself has the property. The interesting principle must therefore be restricted to purely qualitative properties.

But Black's scenario undoes itself. The two spheres are a fixed distance apart, and nothing can be a fixed distance from itself. If the world contained only one sphere, nothing in it would be at any distance from anything. That the spheres stand at this distance apart therefore shows that there are two — and more than that, it shows that they are discernible: each has the property of being at a fixed distance from a sphere, a property no lone sphere could possess. Black's universe, properly described, is a universe of two discernible spheres, so far from refuting PII it illustrates it. In the end, Black shows at most that we could not know, from inside such a world, whether it contained one sphere or two. The principle itself remains necessarily true, and his case fails.
