I cover science and ideas for a general audience, and over the years I have kept returning to the same well: research claiming that what a person believes, wants, or dreads changes what that person literally sees. There was the 1947 experiment in which children, asked to estimate the size of coins, judged them larger than equally sized gray disks, with the poorer children overshooting the most. There was the 2008 brain-imaging study in which the same wine, presented to tasters under two different believed price tags, was reported as tasting better in its expensive guise and showed more activity in regions associated with pleasantness. There is the long-running program of research, out of the University of Virginia in the 2000s, in which a hill was judged steeper by people who were tired, out of shape, or wearing a heavy backpack. Each time, I wrote the story the way the researchers framed it: as a finding about seeing.

And each time, a second act followed. Someone re-examined the design and observed that the effect only appears where participants can figure out which answer flatters their need, expectation, or desire. Rearrange the measurement so they cannot know which response points in which direction — judgments under time pressure, responses given before the relevant belief can plausibly be consulted, indirect measures — and the difference in seeing evaporates; what survives, at most, is a difference in what people are willing to say. I have begun to think of this not as a series of unrelated scandals but as one recurring pattern, and I want a name for it. Stated generally: an effect is presented, in the paper's title as in the press release, as a change in what the eye delivers, but it is demonstrated — and demonstrable — only as a change in what the mouth reports. Two complications keep it from being simple. First, it does not look like lying: participants appear sincere, and sometimes the body seems to cooperate, as when the wine tasters' pleasure-related activity tracked the believed price along with their ratings. Second, there are intermediate suspects — where attention was directed, what was remembered afterward, how low a bar there was for saying "I saw it" — and I genuinely do not know where the boundary of my pattern runs among them, which is part of why I want the concept.

So, some questions for you. Does this pattern have an established name in perception science or the philosophy of perception — not a label for the optimistic claims, which I know came packaged as a movement, but a label for the recurring deflationary arc itself? The nearest thing I have heard is the idea of a response bias, but as I understand it, that concerns how freely a perceiver reports a given experience, and my pattern seems to live partly at the level of a whole research literature rather than a single perceiver, which makes me suspect the fit is imperfect. If no single term covers it, tell me what the nearest concepts are and, for each, exactly where my pattern parts ways with it. If nothing established fits, coin a term: define it precisely enough that I could apply it to the next study in my queue, and give me a boundary case on each side — one in which the eye genuinely changed, where my term must not apply, and one in which the mouth alone moved, where it must. I would settle for a phrase serviceable in print. But it cannot smuggle in the verdict before the evidence is in, because that maneuver is precisely what I am trying to name.
