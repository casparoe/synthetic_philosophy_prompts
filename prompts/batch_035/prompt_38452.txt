I am writing a position piece for a rational-choice reading group on what Dutch book arguments do and do not establish, and in drafting it I have discovered that I apparently hold all of the following at once:

(1) Probabilism: an agent's credences at a time should satisfy the probability axioms.

(2) The classic Dutch book argument is essentially sound as a justification for (1). If an agent's credences are incoherent, there exists a finite set of bets, each individually acceptable by the agent's own lights, whose joint effect is a guaranteed loss; and standing ready to accept such a set is a form of inconsistency, not mere imprudence. I take this to show that incoherent credences are defective in themselves, independently of whether any exploiter actually exists.

(3) A dominance principle: it is irrational to choose an option when another available option is certain to be better by the agent's own valuations.

(4) Betting behavior is a fallible window onto credence. Diminishing marginal utility of money, risk aversion, and a simple distaste for gambling can each rationally drive a wedge between what an agent believes and which bets she accepts. An agent can be fully rational in declining a bet that is fair by her own credences.

(5) The diachronic Dutch book arguments — for conditionalization and for reflection — fail, because they require attributing to the rational agent, at the earlier time, a duty to accept particular bets at the later time, and no such duty can be grounded in one's present credences alone. Rationality's verdicts concern an agent at a time; they cannot reach forward into the agent's future betting behavior.

(6) Conditionalization is nonetheless the correct update rule, but on entirely different grounds: updating by conditioning minimizes expected inaccuracy relative to the agent's current credences.
