You're a professional philosopher who's also good at writing natural-sounding dialogue, and I need your help thinking through something. Take the role seriously and really dig in — before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit about what each side's strongest moves are, and make sure neither character gets to win just because you find them more intuitive.

Write a dialogue between a consequentialist and a deontologist working through the so-called paradox of deontology. The setup: an old-style sheriff in a small town, where a mob is about to riot and lynch several innocent people, and the sheriff could stop it by framing and executing one particular innocent man who the mob already suspects. The two characters are old friends — a retired judge (the deontologist) and a policy analyst (the consequentialist) — sitting on the judge's porch arguing about what the sheriff should do. The paradox, as the consequentialist should articulate it early on: if rights violations are so bad, how can it be right to allow five of them rather than commit one? Refusing looks like the sheriff caring more about keeping his own hands clean than about people not being killed.

Some things I want you to work into the argument, because I'm tired of hearing the stock versions:

1. The consequentialist should NOT just be a crude utilitarian. Let them concede a lot — that rights matter lexically, that numbers aren't everything — and still press the core challenge: even granting rights their full weight, agent-relative restrictions seem to make the sheriff's own agency weirdly special, as if his one violation counts for more than everyone else's five combined simply because it's his.

2. The deontologist should get to use the doing/allowing distinction, but the consequentialist should push back that it's doing a lot of unexplained work here (framing someone is a doing, letting a mob act is an allowing — but why does that asymmetry get to outweigh lives?). The deontologist needs a real answer, not a repetition of the distinction.

3. Here's a device I want the deontologist to introduce, and I'd like you to develop it well since it's not standard: the consequentialist proposes, half-jokingly, a "violation market" — if restrictions are really about an agent's ledger, why not let agents trade or pre-purchase permissions, so the sheriff could buy the right to frame one man now in exchange for funding protections later, netting fewer violations overall? The deontologist should take this seriously and use it to sharpen their view — the intuition that this market is obscene even when it reduces total violations is supposed to reveal what restrictions actually are: not budget lines to be optimized across, but a refusal to let violations enter one's practical accounting at all. The consequentialist should then have a genuine reply (e.g., that this "refusal to account" is aesthetic, a way of keeping the ledger clean by refusing to look at it, which doesn't help the lynched).

4. At some point the consequentialist should raise the "outsourcing" move — the sheriff quietly deputizes someone else to do the framing — and the two should figure out together why the deontologist is still bothered (or whether they should be), since that case tests whether the restriction attaches to the agent's hands or to the structure of the plan.

Rules for the dialogue: both characters are smart and actually respond to each other — no talking past each other, no one conceding out of exhaustion. Each should land at least one blow the other can only partially absorb. Don't resolve it artificially at the end; it's fine (better, even) if it ends with both of them having located the real crux, stated as precisely as they can get it. Aim for maybe 900–1200 words, and feel free to let it be a bit conversationally messy — interruptions, half-finished sentences when one of them sees where the other is going.

One more thing: think carefully about whether the judge's deontology has to be absolutist. If they allow the restriction to give way at some extreme (a million lives, say), the consequentialist will say the paradox just re-enters at the margin — let them have that exchange and see if the deontologist can say something interesting about thresholds versus weights.
