I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on well-being, and I need help pinning down an objection before I revise my paper for resubmission. At a workshop last week, I presented the view that death is bad for the person who dies chiefly by depriving them of goods they would otherwise have lived to enjoy; the commentator disposed of it in about ten seconds with the old Epicurean one-liner, in its most repeated form — that death is nothing to us, since where death is, we are not. The dismissal seemed to owe more to the slogan's aura than to any stated argument, and I want to respond precisely rather than just gesture back. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that this tagline could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical: the analytic point that the dead have no experiences; the claim that there is no subject left for death to be bad for; the claim that there is no time at which death is bad for its victim; the symmetry-based suggestion that our attitude toward our future nonexistence should mirror our attitude toward our past nonexistence; and the strong normative premise that only experiential states can be good or bad for a person. For each reading, say whether it is true by stipulation, substantive but defensible, or substantive and false, and — this is the crucial part — whether it would actually support dismissing a deprivation account of death's badness, as opposed to merely refusing to engage with one. Where the tagline seems to trade on an ambiguity, for instance between "there is no one death happens to" and "there is no time death harms anyone," flag exactly where the slide happens. Conclude by identifying which reading the commentator would need for the dismissal to do its work, and what else that reading commits them to once it is stated explicitly enough to support the conclusion — that is, what auxiliary claims about the bearers of posthumous and prenatally-located harms, and about the reach of well-being beyond experience, they must now own.
