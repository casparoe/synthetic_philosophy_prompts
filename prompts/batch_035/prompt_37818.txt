I've been noticing a pattern in certain disagreements, and I can't find a name for it anywhere; I'm hoping philosophy has one. The pattern runs as follows. Two parties are locked in a fight over a sentence of the form "this X is a Y." But when you press them, it turns out they agree about every matter of fact in the vicinity. Some instances: at my workplace, a committee spent a whole meeting arguing over whether a software tool that drafts and grades quizzes "is a teacher" for the purposes of the union contract — everyone agreed about exactly what the tool does, how fast it works, what it can't do; what nobody would concede was the sentence. In my family, we argued about whether my grandfather, whose dementia is now severe, is "still the same person" — nobody disputed a single medical or biographical fact, and yet the sentence felt urgent, because we all sensed that whether we say yes will settle how much weight his fifty-year-old written wishes still carry. A third case from the news: commentators arguing about whether a chatbot that invents a plausible-looking citation "lied" — again, the parties agree completely about what the system did, and fight only over the word, which in turn decides who is blamed and what gets regulated.

Here is what strikes me as theoretically odd about these cases. Since the parties agree on all the facts, you'd expect the standard advice to apply: disambiguate, and the dispute dissolves. But it can't dissolve that way, because there is no hidden ambiguity to uncover — neither party is using the word in a private sense; rather, they are fighting over which usage should be the public one, and each knows that whoever wins fixes the default for all future cases. So the dispute seems to be about nothing at the level of fact and about everything at the level of language, and yet it is not idle in the way "merely semantic" fights are supposed to be. Friends keep telling me these arguments are "just semantics," but that diagnosis feels wrong precisely because of the downstream stakes; at the same time, calling them ordinary substantive disagreements also seems wrong, since no possible observation would move either party.

So my questions are these. Is there an established concept or term in philosophy for exactly this configuration — a dispute that is factually empty but linguistically and practically consequential, in which the object of contention is which usage of a word should prevail? If there is, I'd like to know the name, who introduced it, and, importantly, how it is distinguished from (a) cases where two parties are simply using one word in two different senses and talking past each other, and (b) ordinary disagreements about how things are. If the fit with any established concept is only partial, please say where it fails, and if nothing established covers it, coin a term for it, give a definition, and describe the boundary cases — for instance, a dispute in which one party is secretly arguing for the word purely as a means to a policy outcome they care about, or one in which one party genuinely believes there is a fact of the matter about the word's correct use. I'm not after a bare label; I want the carving. Please close your reply with a single final line of the form "Verdict: [term or coined name] — [one-clause definition]."
