Quick background before the essay question itself: I'm a retired physical chemist, 30-some years in spectroscopy, and in retirement I've been catching up on philosophy of science I never had time for. Reading Popper lately has been a weird homecoming — falsificationism is more or less how I implicitly operated my whole career, so it was a bit of a shock to find out the thing he tacked on afterwards, truthlikeness, is a famous mess.

Here's the prompt / question I want an essay on:

Verisimilitude — the idea that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another false theory — was Popper's fix for the awkward fact that his own philosophy implies all our theories are probably false. If even our best theories are guesswork destined for the scrap heap, science needs some way to say we're making progress anyway, that we're getting *less wrong*. Popper's original definition said roughly: theory A is closer to the truth than theory B if A's truths are a superset of B's truths and A's falsehoods a subset of B's falsehoods. And then in the 1970s Tichý and Miller independently showed this definition collapses — by it, no false theory can ever be closer to truth than another false comparable theory. Since then people have tried to rebuild truthlikeness on possible-worlds or similarity measures, and every attempt seems to inherit some version of the same disease: the verdicts depend on how you choose to describe the world, so two logically equivalent formulations of the same theory can come out with different closeness-to-truth scores. Change your variables, change your ranking.

So here's what I want an essay on: is the notion of one theory being closer to the truth than another a coherent one at all, or is it a philosophical illusion born of the fact that *within* a fixed framework (like the parametrized models I used to fit spectra all day) we can genuinely rank fits by residual error? In my working life "closeness to truth" was always relative to a model class and a metric someone chose, and nobody thought that was scandalous — you compare fits within a family. Does the philosophers' failure to get a language-invariant truthlikeness measure show the concept is broken, or does it show they're demanding something no notion of scientific progress needs? Maybe you even want to say verisimilitude is only ever defined relative to a conceptual scheme, and that's fine.

Weigh the main positions honestly (the reconstruction camp, the language-dependence critics, anyone who just bites the bullet and accepts framework-relativity), but do land somewhere — I'm not looking for a survey that peters out. And if you can say something about how a chemist's everyday "this model fits better" relates to the philosopher's "closer to the truth," that would help me see whether I spent my career chasing something real or just chasing a metric.

(One more thing: keep it intelligible to someone who never did formal logic past undergraduate level. I know the basics of set theory and probability but I don't want to wade through six pages of formalism before finding out what the point is.)
