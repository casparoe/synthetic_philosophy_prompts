Here is the itch. In class we covered the probabilistic approach to indicative conditionals: the claim that the probability of "if A, then B" is the conditional probability of B given A, and the betting-style justification for it, under which a bet on the conditional is supposed to be settleable once you learn whether A and whether B -- with the bet being called off when A turns out false. What struck me is that this whole justification seems to lean on something that indicatives have and counterfactuals lack: the possibility of eventually learning the truth of the antecedent. A counterfactual's antecedent is already known false; nobody will ever observe the closest world where the coin landed heads. So the machinery that makes sense of probabilities for indicatives appears to go silent exactly where counterfactual probabilities are asked for -- and yet we do seem to assign rough probabilities to counterfactuals, or at least we argue about them ("there's a good chance she would have won the primary").

So the question I want the paper to address is something like: can the probabilistic treatment of conditionals be extended to counterfactuals, and if the betting justification does not transfer, is there some substitute justification -- for instance one in terms of how the bets would be settled by evidence about the actual world rather than by observation of A -- or does the failure reveal that so-called probabilities of counterfactuals are some other kind of quantity wearing that label?
