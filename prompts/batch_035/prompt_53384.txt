I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we just wrapped up the units of selection unit, which turned into three weeks of everyone talking past each other. Our assignment is to write up "where you actually stand" and bring it to section so people can attack it. Mine is below, and before I hand it over I want to know whether it even hangs together internally, because I have a feeling something in it contradicts something else and I can't see what. (1) Selection always acts on phenotypes, never directly on genes — you can't select for a stretch of DNA in the abstract, only for what it does in a body. (2) Genes are nonetheless the only genuine units of selection: they're the only things that last long enough, copy faithfully enough, and are the real beneficiaries of adaptation, while organisms, groups, herds etc. are just vehicles. (3) I'm pretty down on group selection — most supposed cases commit the averaging fallacy, i.e. the fact that groups differ in average composition doesn't show that anything happened at the group level, you'd need to show that a group's composition changed because of something the group did as a group. (4) Species selection, on the other hand, I fully believe in: differences between clades in speciation and extinction rates explain macroevolutionary patterns that nothing at the organism level can touch. (5) Eusocial insect colonies are genuine superorganisms, so colony-level "selection" in ants and bees is just ordinary selection on organisms that happen to be colonies — no group selection needed there either. (6) Deep down I think the question "what level does selection really act at?" is partly a bookkeeping choice — the gene's-eye description and the organism's-eye description are two renderings of the same causal process — though when pressed I'd insist the causal story is ultimately about genes.

Two things I want checked specifically, because I'm unsure of them. First, I used to think (1) and (2) flatly contradicted each other and now I believe the replicator/interactor distinction dissolves the tension, but I might be getting that distinction to do more work than it can actually do — be honest with me about whether it's really fine. Second, my TA read a draft and said something cryptic like "watch how you use the word 'really' in this document," which was not helpful, and I genuinely can't tell if she was pointing at (6), at (2), or somewhere else entirely.
