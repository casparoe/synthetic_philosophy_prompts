I'm a cog sci PhD student taking a philosophy of mind seminar as an outside field, and I've managed to get myself quite confused about what multiple realizability is actually supposed to establish. We read Putnam's old argument against type-identity theory -- pain can be realized by mammalian neurons, octopus neurons, maybe silicon, so pain can't be identical to any single physical-chemical type. Fine, I get that part. But then in class someone brought up the flip side: if mental states are individuated purely by their functional/causal role, and any physical system can be described as instantiating basically any causal role if you're allowed to map states onto it liberally enough, then doesn't the whole view collapse into triviality? Like, the worry that with enough gerrymandered state mappings even a wall or a bucket of water "implements" your belief that it's raining. My housemate (also in the seminar) says I'm just conflating two things: multiple realizability says the same functional organization can show up in wildly different substrates, which is a claim about diversity of realizers, not a claim that realizing the organization is trivial or that the organization constraint doesn't bite. But that feels like it just pushes the bump under the rug -- the functionalist then owes us a principled account of when a physical system genuinely has the right causal organization vs. when we've just projected it, and it's not obvious where that account comes from.

The part I keep going back and forth on is whether this triviality worry is a real problem for functionalism or just a cheap shot that misses the view's actual commitments. I've half-remembered something about Searle making the wall version of this objection and about a debate over whether rocks implement every finite state machine depending on how loose you are about counterfactual transitions between states, but I don't want to just name-drop -- I want to actually understand the dialectic. Can you walk me through it at a level that would let me hold my own in seminar: what MR is claiming (and, importantly, what it is NOT claiming), how the "anything can implement anything" objection is supposed to get its grip, what the standard functionalist replies look like, and where you personally think the objection still has teeth, if anywhere? Also, if it helps, I'd love to know whether this connects to why some people think a simulation of a mind might not be a mind, or is that a genuinely separate issue about substrate independence that I'm smuggling in where it doesn't belong?
