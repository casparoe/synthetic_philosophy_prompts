You are an experienced instructional designer with a foot in two worlds: you spent a decade running undergraduate seminars in philosophy of science, and you have worked alongside Quaternary geologists on how formal time units actually get defined and ratified. You are also unusually good at designing classroom activities in which the structure of the exercise, rather than anything the facilitator says, delivers the lesson.

I teach an upper-level seminar called Deep Time and the Present — 14 students, mostly philosophy and environmental studies majors, one 90-minute session. Next week's topic is the Anthropocene: when this putative epoch is supposed to have started, and who is entitled to decide that. I do not want a debate about whether the Anthropocene is "real." I want an activity in which students run into, first-hand, the fact that the start date of a historical period is not lying in the sediment waiting to be found, but comes out differently depending on who is asking, for what purpose, and under which rules. The penny I want them to drop is this: "when did it start" is underdetermined until "who decides, and in order to do what" is fixed — and the choice of decider is itself a decision with stakes, not a neutral delegation to expertise.

Design the activity. It must meet the following requirements:

1. At least two rounds in which the same body of evidence is processed by two differently constituted commissions with different mandates and different decision rules — for instance, a stratigraphy-style body that must pin the boundary to a marker in a single physical reference section and a dated layer, versus a history- or heritage-style body that is permitted to anchor a period to an event. The same students should sit on both, so the contrast is felt personally rather than observed. The evidence base should be a deck of evidence cards you write yourself: plausible signals such as a radionuclide layer, a CO2 curve, shifts in pollen and domesticated-animal bone assemblages, concrete and plastics in sediment, and a colonial-era land-use signal. Use real anchors where you are confident of them (for example, the convention that "radiocarbon present" is fixed at 1950, or the mid-twentieth-century bomb spike), and explicitly flag anything you invent.

2. A precedent mechanic: each commission receives a short list of its own past rulings (which you also write) and must justify any new decision by citing one. This is where students should discover that consistency does a great deal of the work that evidence appears to do — the candidate start dates you build in (consider using roughly 1610, 1784, and 1950, or your own set) should be sortable according to which precedent each commission naturally reaches for.

3. A voting rule with teeth — supermajority thresholds, abstentions, a written minority statement — so that "who decides" shows up procedurally, not just rhetorically.

4. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, a materials list containing nothing exotic, and role cards if roles are needed.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinctions: empirical onset-detection questions versus the classificatory decision; periodization as interest-relative; the difference between "the Anthropocene is real" and "the Anthropocene is a useful unit for purpose X"; and what was at stake in each commission's jurisdictional claim to be the one deciding.

6. An honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students treating it as a quiz with a right answer, the two rounds collapsing into the same date anyway, a dominant student steamrolling the vote — and how to handle each failure mode.

7. An equally honest note on which feature of the real controversy the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself: for example, the years of fieldwork behind real stratigraphic evidence, the formal constitutional rules that constrain real scientific bodies, or the political weight of actual ratification. Pick what is genuinely true of your design and tell me what I should say, and when, to patch the gap.

Before writing the final design, think it through carefully: brainstorm at least two distinct activity concepts, select the one that best makes the underdetermination show up in the rules and incentives rather than in the debrief, and close with a few sentences noting what you discarded and why. Aim for something I can run from your write-up alone, with no web access, no further reading, and no preparation beyond printing a few pages.
