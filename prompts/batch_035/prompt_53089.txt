I'm taking a course called Philosophy of Probability (basically decision theory plus formal epistemology) and my term paper is on the Sleeping Beauty problem. My professor was pretty blunt: she doesn't want another paper that re-explains the halfer and thirder positions and then picks a side with intuition pumps, she wants an original case that actually does some work. So I need help building a thought experiment to spec, and I have a half-formed idea you should feel free to fix or discard.

Here's my motivation: I think the standard debate is polluted by the fact that everyone argues about it via betting. Thirders point out that if Beauty gets offered a bet on heads at each awakening, the odds at which she'd break even are 1-in-3ish, and halfers reply that this is about betting policy across multiple awakenings, not about credence, so the betting argument never touches the real disagreement. So I want a case where (a) the halfer/thirder disagreement makes a difference to a practical decision, but (b) the decision is set up so neither side can dismiss it as double-counting stakes: no bet offered at every awakening, no payoff that scales with how many times she wakes up. Something like: Beauty can send a signal each morning, but the signal only 'counts' on one awakening, determined by something she doesn't know (maybe the coin, maybe the day, your choice). And the two positions, reasoning from their own numbers — including their different answers about whether today is Monday, not just their P(heads) — should end up recommending different signals, or at least differing confidence about which to send. Constraints: the memory-erasure drug and the fair coin are fine, but no perfect predictors, no copies or branches of Beauty (I want to stay out of the whole anthropics/duplication swamp for this paper), and no payoff structure that makes the same action correct regardless of credence — the case has to be one where getting the probabilities right actually matters to what she does.

Once you've built the case, walk me through it: what the halfer says to do and why, what the thirder says to do and why, and whether the double-halfer position (keep P(heads) = 1/2 at the start but update differently once she learns it's Monday) ends up somewhere distinct from either. Then be honest about which feature of the case is doing the work — my suspicion is it's the mismatch between how many times she acts and how many times it matters, but I could be wrong — and what happens to the case if you remove that feature. Also flag how the side that comes out badly would most plausibly attack the setup (my guess is the halfer says the elicitation is still confounded somehow, but I might be strawmanning them). One last thing: everything I read online treats thirderism as just obviously correct, and I dont want to import that into my paper. Please keep separate what's genuinely settled (like, which formal facts about the betting odds nobody actually disputes) from what's contested among people who work on this, and don't present either side's rebuttal as if it closed the debate.
