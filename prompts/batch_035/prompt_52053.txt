Here's the answer I have to grade: "Hume was the first philosopher to identify the naturalistic fallacy, which is the mistake of deriving an ought from an is. In the Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals, he observes that writers on morality suddenly start using 'ought' and 'should' after making claims about what is the case, without ever explaining how they got there. From this Hume proved that no ought-statement can ever be logically derived from any set of is-statements, no matter what, and this proof became the foundation of his view that morality is grounded in sentiment rather than reason. Moore later applied the exact same objection to ethical naturalists like the utilitarians. His open question argument works like this: take any natural property N, such as being pleasurable, and ask 'is N good?' Since this question always feels open rather than trivially settled, 'good' cannot mean the same thing as N, so good must be a simple, indefinable, non-natural property. So Moore's naturalistic fallacy is really just the is-ought gap restated in the vocabulary of definitions instead of inferences. Both philosophers are making the identical point: you cannot squeeze values out of facts. The upshot is that any ethical theory that tries to build morality out of claims about human nature, pleasure, or evolution commits the same single fallacy, whether it's Hume's target or Moore's."
