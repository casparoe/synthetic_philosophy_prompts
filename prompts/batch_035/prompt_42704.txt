I run a reading group on collective decision-making for policy students with no formal training. Write a decision-tree handout that sorts a participant from an initial commitment to a named position on the discursive dilemma: a group must deliver collective verdicts on several propositions at once, some of them logical consequences of the others, as when a panel of judges rules on liability by majority while its members disagree about which of two grounds make liability follow, so that majority votes on the grounds and on the final verdict come apart. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of schools: each branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads, and the earliest nodes should be the structural choices — whether the group owes a collective view on every proposition on its agenda or may leave some unsettled, whether the aggregation rule must handle every proposition the same way or may give some propositions priority over others, and whether every member's judgment must count equally or the group may instead defer to a subset or a chair. Each leaf should carry the position's name where one exists — premise-based majority, conclusion-based majority, dictatorship, oligarchy, majority rule restricted to a suitably chosen agenda or domain, and settling for an incomplete collective judgment — with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Note the combinations of answers that no position occupies, and say for each whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate, such as a group that votes on the grounds and derives its verdict from them versus one that is simply incomplete. Keep it to roughly ten branch nodes.
