For about a year I've been trying to work out, on my own, whether pain is bad in itself or only bad because of how we react to it. A cousin recently sent me a popular psychology article that seemed to settle the question, and I'd like to check whether I can trust it before I build anything on it.

As I understand it, the article's summary goes like this: Pain asymbolia is a condition produced by damage to certain brain regions, in which patients report that they feel pain but treat it with indifference — they may smile at a pinprick, fail to pull their hand away, and say that it hurts but doesn't bother them. These people feel pain exactly as intensely as anyone else; only the caring is missing. This shows that pain and suffering are two separable things, and that pain itself is not intrinsically bad: the badness comes entirely from the emotional reaction layered on top — the fear and distress — so suffering is in principle optional. The article cites monks and long-term meditators as evidence that ordinary people can learn the same trick, keeping pain's useful warning function while dropping the agony, and it also mentions people born completely unable to feel pain as further support. Pain, it concludes, is just information, and suffering is an add-on we happen to have but needn't.

I'm inclined to accept this, partly because it matches something from my own experience: I once had a nasty burn that hurt far less when I was absorbed in something else, which seems to fit the idea that the suffering is a reaction rather than the sensation itself. But I have a nagging doubt: if pain really were just information, why do the asymbolic patients still call it pain at all, rather than describing something like pressure or warmth? And my distraction case feels like a different thing from their indifference, though I can't articulate why.

Please go through the article's claims part by part: which would a careful philosopher of mind or pain researcher accept, which are subtle conflations or overstatements, and does the conclusion that suffering is optional follow from whatever survives correction? I'd especially like to know whether the article is entitled to move from these patients' indifference — produced by brain damage — to a claim about what pain is like, or could be like, for everyone else.
