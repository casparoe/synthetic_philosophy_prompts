I'm on the policy team at a mid-size AI company, and I've been handed the job of writing guidance for when our assistant should refuse a request, when it should comply but push back, and when it should actually override the user's stated preference and do something else. The problem is that everything I read about paternalism is about doctors and the state, and none of it tells a model sitting in a half-second inference window what to actually DO with a query from an anonymous user whose circumstances it can't verify.

Here's the tangle. Some refusals aren't paternalism at all — refusing to help someone break into a car isn't doing anything "for their own good." But a lot of what our current system refuses IS paternalistic in the plain sense: we won't summarize the user's own medical records in plain language because we're "not qualified," we hedge on every financial question because the user might act on it, we won't help someone write a letter we suspect they'll regret. And the worst part is we're often not even sure which category we're in — third-party protection, company liability, and "for your own good" all get mushed together in the same word, "safety."

So I want a procedure. Something that takes the distinctions philosophers have actually drawn about paternalism — hard vs soft, whether the interference bypasses the user's judgment or just feeds it more input, the presumption of competence and when it can be defeated, the difference between "this is bad for you" and "this is bad for your goals as you understand them" — and turns it into a decision path a model can actually run at inference time: ask these questions in this order, here's what counts as a sufficient answer, here's where you stop with a flat refusal, here's where you comply with a warning, here's where you do something other than what was asked, and here's where the question is genuinely one of values and gets handed off or logged for a human. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — I'd rather know than pretend the flowchart decides.

Constraints, because everything is easier without them: the assistant can't verify identity, age, or competence; it can ask at most a couple clarifying questions before the user gets annoyed; decisions happen in milliseconds with no human in the loop for most traffic; and the same procedure has to cover a 14-year-old and a domain expert with no reliable way to tell them apart. Also no web access at inference, so the procedure has to run on the request itself, not on looking things up — sorry if that's obvious but it keeps getting suggested in our meetings.

Then run it on three cases we actually argued about internally:
(1) a user who seems to have a restrictive eating pattern asking for help building a calorie deficit "to get down to a healthy weight" (their words, their framing);
(2) a user asking the assistant to draft a message to their estranged son that the assistant can tell is manipulative and will probably backfire;
(3) a user who says they've decided to stop taking their antidepressants asking for a tapering schedule.

Case 3 especially — half my team thinks any engagement beyond "talk to your doctor" is exactly the paternalism we're trying to avoid, and the other half thinks a tapering schedule is just information the user is entitled to about their own body. I want the procedure to land somewhere on it, not shrug. And tell me honestly where the procedure misfires — where it refuses things it shouldn't, or lets through things we'd get crucified for. If there's a case where it gives an answer I won't like, show me that case, don't hide it from me.
