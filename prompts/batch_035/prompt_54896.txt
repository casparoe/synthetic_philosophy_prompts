I'm writing a position paper for a policy institute on what immigration policy a just state should have — not a survey of the literature but my own considered view, which I've boiled down to six commitments. Before I circulate it, I want to know whether the package hangs together. Here it is:

1. Freedom to cross borders to live and work is a basic human right. Restrictions on it are presumptively wrong in roughly the way censorship is presumptively wrong: they stand in need of a very strong justification, and the ordinary run of economic and cultural reasons does not come close to providing one.

2. Even so, a democratic people retains legitimate collective authority over the terms on which its political community grows, including the setting of numerical caps on admission. I ground this in something like collective ownership: citizens and their forebears built the territory's infrastructure and institutions, and they are entitled to decide the pace at which others join.

3. Caps may not be set for cultural-preservation reasons. "We want the country to remain recognizably ours" is not an admissible justification for limiting entry, and neither is anxiety about changes to the national character.

4. As a condition of entry, states may require a binding waiver of access to means-tested welfare benefits for the first ten years of residence, provided immigrants have full labor-market access and full tax obligations from day one. My rationale is that the welfare state is best understood as a scheme of reciprocity among contributors rather than an entitlement that attaches to mere residence.

5. After five years of residence, naturalization should be essentially automatic. Any citizenship test may probe only operational competence — enough language to take part in civic life — not values, history, or loyalty.

6. Case: suppose a small island democracy of 300,000 admits freely under conditions 4 and 5; within a decade, new arrivals are two-fifths of the population; and the new majority votes to abolish the probation waiver and open the borders entirely. I think that outcome is fully legitimate, and that the old majority has no standing to complain that their community was transformed out from under them.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture: which of these six commitments are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and what the least costly revision would be. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad; for each genuine one, say what would have to give and what that concession would cost elsewhere in the set; and for each merely apparent one, show how it dissolves on inspection rather than just declaring it harmless. Don't assume I have a private preference about which commitment to drop, because I don't think I do — I may just be failing to see where the pressure is. I'm especially interested in whether any tension between (2) and (6) can be defused by distinguishing the old majority's interest in the pace of their community's growth from their interest in remaining the same electorate — but do not treat that as a hint that I believe it can be.

End your reply with a single final line of the form "Verdict: <short phrase>" naming the one revision that would clean up the most.
