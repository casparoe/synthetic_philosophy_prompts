For the past semester I've been running a Bayesian epistemology reading group for fellow PhD students in philosophy of science, and our final session is on Jeffrey conditioning. The thing that has stayed with the group is Jeffrey's old cloth-by-candlelight case: you look at a cloth by candlelight, you cannot quite tell whether it is green or blue, and yet you quite reasonably raise your credence that it is green, and let that partial shift propagate through the rest of your beliefs. Conditioning on a proposition, we kept noting, turns out to be just the degenerate special case where the new distribution over the evidence partition puts all its weight on one member; Jeffrey's rule is the generalization in which the input weights are strictly between zero and one. What charmed everyone, once we stopped grinding through the algebra, is that learning by degrees still counts as learning — the web of belief can move even when nothing ever reaches probability one.

For that last session I want to close with a poem, and I have decided the form should be a pantoum, because the pantoum's mechanics do the doctrinal work rather than merely decorating it. In case it helps you write one: a pantoum is built of quatrains, and lines 2 and 4 of each stanza return verbatim as lines 1 and 3 of the following stanza; the final stanza, by convention, recycles lines from the first stanza and closes on a repetition of the poem's opening line. I want the reader to feel the rigidity condition through this: the same words come back unchanged, like the conditional probabilities that are held fixed under Jeffrey conditioning, but each time they recur they are surrounded by new lines, so that what the line says to the reader shifts with its neighbors, exactly as a hypothesis's import shifts when the distribution over the evidence partition is reweighted while everything conditional on that partition stays put. And the closing repetition of the opening line should land differently than the opening did — the prior, if you like, returning after the update, recognizable but no longer carrying the same weight. Please make it five quatrains.

Two further constraints, both in service of the doctrine. First, the poem should never attain certainty: no line may fully settle anything — I would like you to avoid terminal punctuation that closes an assertion, so that the whole poem, like the agent in the candlelight, ends without anything having been established outright. Second, it should work on two levels: a colleague who has slogged through the formalism with us should be able to see the partition, the reweighting, and the propagation through the web of belief in how the recurring lines are recontextualized, while a non-specialist should simply get a good poem about trying to make out a color by candlelight. Please write it in American English. If you think a title would help, include one, but it shouldn't do the philosophical work on its own — the form has to carry it.
