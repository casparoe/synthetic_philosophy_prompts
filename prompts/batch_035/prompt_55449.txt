At most competitive debate tournaments, debaters don't choose which side of the resolution they defend: sides are assigned by coin flip, and a team that argued for expanding a federal program in the morning round may argue against it that afternoon. Judges vote on argument quality, evidence handling, and clash — never on which side is actually right — and arguing a position you privately reject carries no stigma; it is simply the job. The same activity is defended on civic grounds: it teaches students to see the strongest case for the other side, to disagree without contempt, and so to resist polarization.

What does this practice presuppose about political disagreement? Reconstruct its implicit theory. Does it presuppose a symmetry thesis — that for every contested political question there is a genuinely strong case on each side — and would the practice still be honest if that thesis failed for some questions? What does it presuppose about whether one's opponents argue in good faith? It seems to treat motive attribution as simply irrelevant: an argument's worth is assessed with no inquiry at all into the sincerity, interests, or incentives of the person making it. Is that coherent on its own, or does it quietly depend on an assumption that in politics, unlike in ordinary testimony, motives never transmit defects to arguments? And is the practice consistent with the civic rationale it is defended with, given that public deliberation outside the tournament requires exactly the motive attributions the format brackets — deciding who deserves charity and who is posturing? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: for instance, would it survive on the view that some political positions are best explained by self-interest rather than by available reasons? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the format — the side assignments, the judging criteria, the choice of resolutions — if the presupposed symmetry thesis were abandoned.
