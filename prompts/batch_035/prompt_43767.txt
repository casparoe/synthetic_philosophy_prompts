I've been thinking on my own about a problem someone told me is called the unilateralist's curse: whenever a group of equally capable people could each take some risky action -- publish a dangerous result, release a modified organism, disclose a security hole -- the action will tend to happen even when most members of the group think it shouldn't, because it only takes one person whose judgment is slightly too optimistic. I want to practice arguing against that view, because I don't believe it. Here is my position: each person should do what they privately judge best, full stop. If I'm sixty percent sure that publishing my results helps the world, I should publish. Requiring agreement just means the most timid member of the group gets a veto over everyone else, and history is full of lone dissenters who turned out right while the cautious majority sat on discoveries that could have saved lives. Please play a smart, committed defender of the curse view -- someone who thinks lone action by the marginally-too-optimistic person is a structural inevitability, not a rare accident, and that individual judgment can't fix it from the inside. Don't strawman me, concede small points when I earn them, but never give up the core claim, and stay in character until I say 'stop.' Keep each reply to one paragraph so it feels like a real conversation. Here's my opening move: gatekeeping by the group is just error-correction for the individual, so even I, acting alone, should want the group's check on me before I act -- and any honest person who admits they might be the too-optimistic one should want the same.
