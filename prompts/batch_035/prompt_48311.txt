I've kind of become a zealot about Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want someone to stress-test my version of it. The way I see it: if two people genuinely have common knowledge of each other's posteriors and started from the same prior, they cannot disagree — so when two honest, smart people disagree about some factual question after a long conversation, someone is being irrational or lazy or just not actually sharing what they know. My stronger claim, which is the part I want examined: in real life, if two people disagree after a real back-and-forth where neither is lying or dumb, at least one of them is failing to update properly. My friends' standard pushback is that the theorem's assumptions basically never hold — you never get true common knowledge, nobody really shares a prior, and real conversations aren't iterated rounds of "I heard your number, here's my revised number." But to me that sounds like dodging: if the assumptions fail in every actual case, isn't the theorem vacuous as stated, and doesn't the spirit of it — that disagreement tracks some failure somewhere — still carry over?

So: what would count against my view? I want the defeaters sorted, from things that would actually show the strong reading is false down to things that are merely awkward for it. Walk me through it assumption by assumption — the common prior, the common knowledge of posteriors, the perfect-rationality requirement — and for each one say whether my view can survive by retreating to "the theorem only promises approximate agreement, and approximation is all we ever needed," or whether that retreat costs me something real. And be blunt about whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every observed disagreement gets explained away as "not really common knowledge" or "different priors, so outside the theorem's scope," is there anything left that could prove me wrong? If yes, what observation, argument, or result would do it — and which of these would a serious defender of the strong reading concede genuinely counts against it, versus which would they wave away?

For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a clean demonstration that two agents could share all their evidence, hold it in common knowledge, and still rationally end up apart — no error, no hidden info, no difference in intelligence. But I suspect the worse problems for me are in the small print I keep glossing over, like what "the same prior" is even supposed to mean between people with different life experiences, or whether treating all disagreement as a bug quietly assumes the thing that's in dispute. Point at those too if they're there — I'd rather find out now than keep embarrassing myself at dinner parties.
