my friend — ex-programmer, currently deep into what he calls "open-source game theory" — has an argument i can't shake, and it ends somewhere insane. setup first so we're on the same page: imagine a one-shot prisioner's dilemma where we don't choose moves directly. instead we each submit a piece of code, the two programs get to read each other, and then they output "cooperate" or "defect". that's the whole game.

his argument:

(1) i can write a program — call it Mirror — that says: cooperate iff the other program, run against me, cooperates; otherwise defect. Mirror never gets exploited. and if both of us submit Mirror, we both cooperate. (he admits there's some infinite-regress thing when Mirror reads Mirror, but says a clause like "or if your code is identical to mine" patches it.) so the transparent PD between us is just solved — the dilemma was never the payoff matrix, it was the opacity.

(2) the trick is fully general. code can encode any conditional policy, and once it's submitted it's binding. so in a divide-the-dollar game — split $10 any way we like, no deal means both get $0 — each of us could instead submit: "accept only splits giving me at least $9, otherwise no deal."

(3) a commitment in code is more credible than any human commitment — no willpower involved, no reputation at stake, nothing to walk back. in the limit, commitment is free.

(4) if commitment is free, both sides commit to the greediest demand; both know the other will; neither code can blink, because blinking isn't in the code. so no deal, $0 each. and he insists this happens always, not sometimes: asking for $9 costs you nothing, and if the other guy also overreached, well, that's his fault for overreaching.

(5) conclusion: wherever interests conflict, transparency plus binding code makes things worse. rational agents literally cannot bargain. every treaty, plea deal, and used-car haggling in history must have rested on something non-rational — secret flexibility, people who can still blink. he now claims smart contracts are a trap and that the more credible we make our promises, the closer we get to universal deadlock.

(5) is the part that seems to prove too much. but here's what bugs me and i can't quite name it. in (1), what solved the PD was a conditional commitment. in (4) every commitment is an unconditional take-it-or-leave-it stand. when i pointed this out, he said: fine, submit a conditional one — "accept any split where i get at least $5, reject anything greedier" — now the fight just moves to which threshold you pick, and if our thresholds don't overlap, you're back at $0, so his conclusion survives anyway. and honestly i can't tell whether that reply works or whether he's smuggling something in. the other thing i keep noticing: his reasoning in (4) — "i'll overreach, and if the other guy overreached too, that's his fault" — sounds exactly like the naive defector in a PD, the very guy he claims step (1) dissolved. when i said that he just repeated "the code can't blink."

so: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? i'd like to keep (1), the idea that mutual transparency dissolves the PD, if thats possible. and if the move is rejecting (3), tell me what the price of a code-commitment actually is — from the inside it really does look free.
