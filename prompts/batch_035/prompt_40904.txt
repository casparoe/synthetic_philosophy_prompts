I keep a running list of cases in which a source of beliefs is made to vouch for itself, together with my gut verdict on each, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures the pattern or whether my verdicts are irreconcilable. I take it the central cases are instances of what gets called bootstrapping, and the standing worry is that such reasoning delivers knowledge too easily; but my list is deliberately wider than that debate.

(1) Moore's anti-skeptical argument: I am holding up a hand; if I am holding up a hand, I am not a handless brain in a vat; so I am not a brain in a vat. Each premise is something I know, and the inference is valid, yet I judge the reasoning defective — it seems to beg the question against the skeptic.

(2) Color bootstrapping: over the past year, whenever something has looked red to me, I have believed it was red, and nothing has come apart; from this track record of agreement between how things look and how I take them to be, I conclude that my color vision is reliable. Each individual step looks harmless, but the conclusion seems unearned — I have used the deliverances of my vision to certify the very instrument producing them.

(3) A friend who has been drinking decides he is fit to drive: he walks a straight line, recites the alphabet backward, and concludes that his self-assessment is trustworthy on this occasion. Clearly defective, and everyone at the party recognizes it as such.

(4) My porch thermometer: I have never compared it with another instrument, but across a year its readings have varied smoothly, tracked the seasons, and agreed with the days when I felt hot or cold; from this I conclude it is reliable. This feels entirely earned — and I cannot see how it differs from (2) except that the thermometer's outputs happen to be cross-checked against my felt sense of temperature.

(5) Doubting whether a table is round, I look: it looks round. I run my hand around the edge: it feels round. I conclude it is round, and my confidence rises — even though sight and touch could be jointly deceived, so this too is agreement among deliverances of my own apparatus. This feels legitimate in a way that (2) does not, and the asymmetry embarrasses me.

(6) I recompute a long sum in a different order and get the same answer; my confidence rises. But I notice my verdict flips when I vary the error I imagine: if the likely mistake is a slip, the redo feels probative; if I know I habitually forget to carry, the redo feels worthless, since the bias would repeat itself. So my verdict here is already smuggling in assumptions about how I fail.

(7) A witness's story, told consistently across repeated interrogation by different interviewers asking unscripted questions, strikes me as thereby more likely true — as courts suppose. Yet if the interviewers merely re-ask the same questions, the consistency seems to prove nothing: a liar rehearsed on those questions would stay consistent too.

(8) The case I cannot settle: a barometer whose readings have sat perfectly constant for a month in a sealed chamber. The manufacturer cites the constancy as evidence of reliability — a broken instrument would wander, after all — but a stuck needle produces exactly the same constancy.

My tentative principle: a check is legitimate only if, weighted by how plausible the failure modes are, the check would probably have delivered a different verdict in the situations in which the checked belief is false. When every plausible way of being wrong would leave the check agreeing anyway, the check is empty. This seems to fit (3), (6), and (7), but I suspect it either saves everything and thereby licenses (2) — since globally inverted yet internally coherent color experience strikes me as a far-fetched failure mode — or, once the demon gets equal billing in the priors, it invalidates (5), which I refuse to give up.

Please tell me whether there is one principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts. State the principle precisely, motivate each clause from a case, apply it to two cases I have not listed — a mathematician re-reading a proof for a step she mis-derived from a systematic notational blind spot, and an AI system asked to double-check an answer it itself produced earlier with no access to anything independent — and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether accommodating them is worth the added complexity or whether the verdict should simply go.
