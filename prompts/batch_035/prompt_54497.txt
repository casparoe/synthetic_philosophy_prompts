My closest friend is finishing a PhD in economic theory, and this weekend we had the longest argument we've had in years, though it started almost as a joke. We were trying to agree by text on when to meet for dinner, and after the third round of "works for me if it works for you," she said we were re-enacting Rubinstein's email game: two people exchanging confirmations forever, each message adding one more layer of "I know that you know," and yet, she claimed, never actually reaching common knowledge — only an infinite tower that always stops one rung short. From there the argument escalated. She defended the theorist's assumption of common knowledge of rationality as a legitimate idealization, like the frictionless plane: exact results derived under exact assumptions that we then perturb for the real world, and she pointed to results that follow from it with real force, including the no-trade idea that two people who each know the other is rational can never even agree to a bet, since what could the bet be about? I pushed back that the assumption describes nobody, not even us: I know that I sometimes act irrationally, I know she knows that about me, and she knows I know — so somewhere up the tower, maintaining the assumption of our mutual rationality becomes itself irrational, and the idealization eats its own tail. We ended at two in the morning with no resolution, only a sharpened sense of exactly which step in the reasoning we disagree about, and she challenged me to put the dispute in a form where both sides get a fair hearing.

Please write that fair hearing as a dialogue. The setting: two colleagues on the governing board of a small organization who have each privately concluded they should jointly resign in protest over a decision they consider improper, but neither is willing to resign alone — each wants to resign only if the other does, and each knows the other feels the same, and each knows the other knows, and so on. They are messaging back and forth late at night, with a deadline at noon tomorrow after which resigning will no longer have any effect. One character, call her a theorist by temperament, believes they have, for practical purposes, common knowledge of each other's commitment by now, and that enough is enough; the other, an empiricist by temperament, believes people are rational only to a few levels of depth, that somewhere in the tower each of them knows the other is capable of blinking, and that the exchange of reassurances can therefore never close the gap. The dialogue should give both characters real competence: the theorist must concede where the idealization has empirically misfired (backward induction predicting behavior no human exhibits, exploitation of over-precise assumptions), and the empiricist must concede that the theorems are exact — the question is only whether their premises ever hold, not whether their logic is sound. They must respond to each other's actual points, press specific objections, and each should visibly win at least one exchange. Let the dialogue end one of two ways, and choose deliberately: either they reach a decision about the letters by noon, or the final exchange pinpoints the precise rung of the tower — the exact order of belief — at which their disagreement lives. Somewhere in the range of 700 to 900 words, and it should read as people deciding under time pressure, not as a seminar; no character gets a monologue longer than a paragraph, and neither is a mouthpiece for my side of the argument with my friend — though if the dialogue is honest, she and I will both find parts of ourselves accused in it.
