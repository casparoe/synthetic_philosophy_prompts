My partner and I spent most of a long drive back from a conference arguing about the value of information, and I want to make sure I actually understood the position I was defending before we pick it up again over dinner.

The argument started when I mentioned Good's theorem — the result that in Bayesian decision theory, the expected utility of the decision you'd make with an extra observation can never be lower than the expected utility of the decision you'd make without it, so that free information is always (weakly) worth acquiring. My partner had two objections, and I stumbled on both. First, she said the theorem is vacuous: it's supposedly just the law of iterated expectations dressed up in decision-theoretic clothing, so treating it as a substantive vindication of "more knowledge is better" is a sleight of hand. Second, she said it's obviously false in the wild: negotiators pay not to look at information that would force their hand, medical patients decline genetic tests that would wreck their ability to enjoy the present, and you can apparently construct auction settings where a bidder's expected payoff is strictly lowered by learning more. I responded with something hand-wavy about the theorem only applying when information is free, when you condition correctly, and when you're committed to acting on it, but she pressed me on which of these conditions actually does the work, and I couldn't say cleanly.

So here is what I'd like. Please give me an intuitive, gut-level explanation of why Good's theorem holds — not the formal proof, but the underlying mechanism that makes the result feel like it couldn't have been otherwise. Then untangle the two halves of her objection. On the "vacuity" charge: what, if anything, does the theorem add beyond the algebra of conditional expectation, and why did people consider it worth proving and naming? On the "obviously false" charge: walk through how each of her examples — the negotiator, the genetic test, the auction — relates to the theorem's assumptions. Which of those are genuine failures of an assumption the theorem needs, and which are instead cases where the agent's utilities change upon learning (so that the thing being maximized before and after the observation is not the same function, and the theorem's comparison quietly breaks down)? I'd especially like to understand which assumption, if relaxed, makes information genuinely able to have negative value in expectation, and whether the practical upshot is best stated as "information is always worth having" or as something weaker and more careful than that.
