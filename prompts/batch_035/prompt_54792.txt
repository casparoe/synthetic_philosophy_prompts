I've been reading about peer disagreement for a while now and something bugs me about the state of the debate itself. As far as I can tell the positions have been fixed for decades: on one side the conciliationists, who say that when you learn an epistemic peer disagrees with you about something (math, morals, whatever), you should lower your confidence, roughly splitting the difference with your peer; on the other side the steadfast crowd, who say you're entitled to hold your ground because you've already taken your own evidence into account, and the peer's disagreement is just one more fact that doesn't automatically trump your first-order reasons. There are refinements -- the "right reasons" view, various watered-down conciliationisms, debates over what exactly an epistemic peer is -- but the core positions haven't moved, and every new paper seems to restate a known argument with a new example. Mathematicians who won't budge on the calculation they've checked ten times, philosophers who won't budge about peer disagreement itself, which is deliciously circular.

My working hypothesis is that the whole thing is a verbal dispute that only looks deep: conciliationists and steadfasters are actually answering different questions -- something like "what credence is best from the inside, given your evidence" versus "what credence would be most accurate across a population of reasoners" -- and because both sides call their question "what rationality requires," they talk past each other. But I'm not confident in that, and I can think of at least two other candidates: a hidden empirical disagreement about how reliable people actually are at tracking their own evidence, or bedrock intuitions about cases (the famously checked calculation, the jury case) that no argument can move.

Diagnose the stalemate for me: what kind of disagreement is this really, and what does your diagnosis predict about what would count as progress -- a distinction, a formal model, new empirical work on reasoning accuracy, a change of question, or nothing? Please be clear about which parts of your diagnosis are pretty much settled among people who know this literature versus which parts are genuinely contested, and don't dress up your preferred diagnosis as the consensus one. If you think the debate is substantive and just hard, say that plainly instead of reaching for a deflationary story because it's tidier.
