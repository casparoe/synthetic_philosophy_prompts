I'm a software engineer at a company that builds digital preservation infrastructure, and a long-running design debate has stalled on what is really a philosophical question. We are specifying an archival file format intended to remain decodable for at least a century, by software and possibly by people we cannot anticipate. One colleague argues that meaning lives entirely in the content: as long as the bytes are correct, the file means what it means, and the format specification is just packaging. Another insists that a file is meaningless without a reader, so essentially all of our effort should go into describing the format to future audiences rather than into the payload machinery. To get unstacked, I have been thinking about Douglas Hofstadter's tripartite distinction from Gödel, Escher, Bach between the frame message (the signal, conveyed by the medium's very structure, that a decodable message is present and worth attempting), the outer message (the information needed to actually carry out the decoding), and the inner message (the content ultimately conveyed). My tentative view is that each of my colleagues is collapsing two or three of these layers into one, but I'm not confident in that analysis, and none of us has the background to press it further.

Please describe the most important implications of the frame/outer/inner distinction for our design problem. In particular, I would like to know how the distinction bears on concrete candidate features we have on the table: a fixed magic byte sequence at the start of every file; a self-describing schema embedded in the file rather than an external specification document; storing payloads as plain text with checksums instead of a compact binary encoding; and including a short human-readable primer, in several natural languages, that explains the schema conventions from scratch. For each feature, it would help if you could say which layer it primarily serves, where it can fail, and whether the failures point to something deeper — for example, whether the outer message faces a bootstrapping problem that no purely technical feature can solve, since the instructions for reading the instructions must themselves be read. Since the whole exercise presumes that future parties will even try to decode our archives, I would also appreciate a brief note on whether the frame layer can do anything to motivate that attempt, or whether it can only ever be recognized by someone who already knows enough to be looking.
