I'm putting together a guest session for our department's cross-listed seminar — a mix of grad students in philosophy of science and, annoyingly, about half a dozen evolutionary biology PhD students who got roped in. The topic is Popper on Darwinism: his infamous remark (in his intellectual autobiography, and again in a late lecture) that natural selection is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme — and then his partial walk-back, where he conceded the theory was testable after all while insisting on the metaphysical status of the broader Darwinian worldview. I have fifty minutes and a whiteboard.

Don't explain Popper to me, I know the material. What I need is a plan. Specifically: what has to be established first before the tautology worry even makes sense — I'm thinking demarcation and the asymmetry between falsification and verification have to come before anyone hears the word Darwinism, otherwise the bio students will hear an attack on evolution and just stop listening — but tell me if you'd order it differently. Then: which example should carry the whole session. My instinct is not the peppered moth and not the classic just-so-story complaints about giraffe necks, both too worn. I want one case that lets me show both faces of the issue at once: the core mechanism, unglamorous and almost logically near-empty on its own, alongside the rich, risky, genuinely predictive theory it scaffolds. If you have a better candidate than "survival of the fittest" read as fitness-defined-by-survival, say so, and say what the example must include and what it mustn't.

Then the misconceptions, and here's the tricky part: the two halves of the room bring opposite ones. The philosophers tend to arrive thinking the tautology objection is just a crude undergraduate confusion, beneath serious discussion. The biologists tend to arrive thinking Popper was a creationist fellow-traveller or at least scientifically illiterate, and that falsifiability is some settled thing they learned in intro class. There's also the intermediate misconception that Popper simply capitulated under pressure and the retraction erases the earlier claim — I want the session to show the retraction is philosophically interesting, not just politically motivated. Map where in the sequence each of these should be defused, and which one I should deliberately provoke rather than preempt.

Also tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level (I'm tempted to say the full neo-Lamarckian background of his world-3 stuff about the emergence of mind, plus anything about gene-centric debates) and how to flag the refusal so it sounds like intellectual honesty rather than evasion. And end with one question I can pose to the room that would tell me whether they actually grasped the distinction between a research programme and its hard core, as opposed to just enjoying the controversy.

Keep the whole plan under 700 words, and lead with your recommended sequence as a numbered list before any prose.
