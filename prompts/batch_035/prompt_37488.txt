For years I've kept a folder of pieces I think of as the under-appreciated philosophical payoffs of complexity theory, and the one I keep returning to is the natural proofs barrier. The naive story about P versus NP is that it's a hard but ordinary mathematical problem: somewhere out there is a clever combinatorial argument waiting to be found, and our failure to find it so far is just that — a failure, of the same kind as any other unsolved conjecture. The barrier suggests something stranger. Roughly: any proof of the standard, "natural" kind — one that constructively certifies high circuit complexity for a large fraction of functions — would double as an efficient algorithm for distinguishing pseudorandom strings from genuinely random ones, and would thereby demolish the one-way functions modern cryptography rests on. So the unreachability of the proof and the security of every encrypted secret stand or fall together. If one-way functions exist, then in a precise sense the proof cannot be of the kind we know how to write; if they don't, our secrets were never computationally safe to begin with. I find this genuinely moving — an impossibility result about our own epistemic reach that is simultaneously the load-bearing wall of private communication — and I want a poem that carries the idea rather than name-dropping it.

Write me a poem in exactly five stanzas — four quatrains and a closing couplet, with the meter and rhyme scheme of your choosing, but with the volta landing at the fourth stanza. Stanzas one through three should build the naive picture: checking is easy, finding is hard, the prize sits there waiting, and it feels like just a matter of the right lemma. The fourth stanza is the turn: the realization that a natural proof would itself be a distinguisher, so that breaking through by the obvious route means breaking the pseudorandomness underneath our locks. The closing couplet should state the paradox exactly — that the difficulty of the proof is not an obstacle standing in front of our secrets but the very thing guarding them. Two constraints: do not use the terms "P," "NP," or any similar notation — render the ideas in plain language — and make the distinction between a proof merely being hard and a proof being ruled out by its own character come through, so that someone who knows the barrier literature nods rather than winces, while someone who doesn't still gets a good poem about an asymmetry that protects us.

After the poem, append a note in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with nothing before or after them: say where the poem takes liberties, which glosses are rough or contested (for instance, the slide between one-way functions and pseudorandom generators, the assumption that a proof of the separation would in fact be natural, or whether "guarding our secrets" overstates what the barrier actually shows). And if you think the task as I've posed it can't be done well — if the conceit forces a distortion the note cannot repair, or if I've misread the barrier itself — tell me that plainly instead of producing a compliant poem. I'd rather learn the conceit is confused than own a handsome misstatement.
