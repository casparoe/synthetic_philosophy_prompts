I'm drafting my philosophical profile on perception for a graduate reading group in which each member's commitments get audited by the others, and before I submit it I'd like a consistency check of my own. Here is the set. (1) Perceptual experiences have representational content, and that content is nonconceptual: a creature with no concepts at all could still undergo veridical experiences. (2) Early vision is informationally encapsulated in roughly Fodor's sense, and I take the persistence of the Müller-Lyer illusion despite one's firm knowledge that the two lines are equal to be the clearest datum in its favor. (3) Expertise genuinely transforms what is perceived: when the radiologist sees the hairline fracture, or the chess master sees the threatened king, this is a difference in perceptual content itself, not merely faster or more confident judgment applied to the very same content a novice receives. (4) Cognitive penetration, where it occurs, does not by itself threaten perception's epistemic role: whether a penetrated experience justifies the beliefs it supports depends on whether the penetrating states improve or degrade the reliability of the connection between how things seem and how things are — penetration by expertise enhances justification, penetration by desire or prejudice can undermine it. (5) I am a dogmatist in something like Pryor's sense: whenever it perceptually seems to a subject that p, the subject thereby has immediate, prima facie justification for believing p, absent defeaters. My own sense is that something in this package doesn't hang together, but I can't locate it. I've worried most about whether (1) and (3) can coexist, since expertise seems like a paradigmatically conceptual state and I've insisted that perceptual content isn't conceptual at all; I've also half-suspected that (2) and (3) simply contradict each other.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how badly. For each real tension, explain the mechanism of the conflict — whether it operates at the level of content, at the level of justification, or somewhere between — and say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and don't just name a pair and label it circular or inconsistent: I want to see exactly how the commitments interact. In particular, I'd like to know whether my own suspicion about (1) and (3) is actually the problem, or whether I've been staring at the wrong corner of the package while the real trouble is elsewhere.
