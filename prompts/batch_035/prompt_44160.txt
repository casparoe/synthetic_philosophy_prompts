Here's the exam question:

"Gongsun Long claims that a white horse is not a horse. Reconstruct his argument as it appears in the dialogue, and assess whether it is mere wordplay or a serious claim about names and what they refer to. Briefly contrast his approach to language and paradox with Hui Shi's."

And the student's answer:

"Gongsun Long's argument has two parts. First, the term 'horse' marks out shape/form, while 'white' marks out color. So the compound 'white horse' marks out both shape and color at once, and what marks out two features can't be identical to what marks out only one of them. Second, if you ask for a horse, a yellow or black horse will satisfy you, but if you ask for a white horse they won't. So the two requests pick out different things, and the terms name different kinds. Is this sophistry? To see what he's up to, compare Hui Shi. Hui Shi's paradoxes (like 'the greatest has nothing beyond it') were meant to show that all things form a single unity, so our distinctions are superficial. Gongsun Long is the opposite: he uses paradox to pull apart things we normally lump together, like shape and color in one object — that's the point of the hard-and-white argument, which Hui Shi also relied on. Both belong to the School of Names, which the Later Mohists attacked for corrupting language. I read the white horse argument as a semantic thesis: a name's meaning is fixed by exactly what it marks out, so a compound name refers to a narrower class than its parts. Gongsun Long has therefore shown that the class of white horses and the class of horses are distinct classes, so no white horse is a member of the class of horses. That's a real insight about how compound terms work, even if he stated it in a deliberately provoking way, and it anticipates later debates about whether general terms name classes or properties."
