Hi there -- quick bit of context before my actual request. I'm a retired geologist (about forty years mapping Precambrian terrain, mostly shield work and some Quaternary stuff late in my career), and since retiring I've fallen hard into philosophy. Metaphysics especially. I've been chewing on what I gather is called the special composition question: when do a bunch of things compose one further thing, rather than just... sitting there being a bunch of things?

Here's why I can't leave it alone. Geologists run into this constantly and paper over it with conventions. Take a talus slope I mapped for a season up in the Yukon: thousands of clasts, from sand grains to boulders, resting against each other, interlocking here and there, with fines slowly settling into the voids and even some spar cement growing at grain contacts -- very early diagenesis, the first breath of the slope turning into something like conglomerate, over some number of millennia. Is the slope one object? Is it one object now but wasn't when the glacier first dumped it? Is the glacier that shed it one object or a process we've named for convenience? My field instinct has always been that in the middle, gradual cases there's no fact of the matter -- just our map units and a line we draw where the airphoto resolution gets bad. But I gather some philosophers say every arbitrary sum exists (my ice axe + the granite pluton three ridges over + the moon), and that any attempt to say only the "real" composites exist can't be made principled without collapsing into arbitrariness. And honestly, after a career of drawing formation boundaries on gradual, intergrading rock, I half-suspect they're right, which annoys me.

So here's the request. Write me a dialogue between two characters walking up that talus slope: Mira, a universalist who thinks any collection of things composes something, no matter how scattered or gerrymandered; and Cal, who holds that only some arrangements make one thing and wants to defend roughly the common-sense line (boulders yes, random scatterings no -- if restrictionism or whatever the proper term is fits better than "common sense," use your judgment). Make them equally sharp, please -- neither one a pushover, and have them actually respond to each other's points rather than lecture past each other. Ideally:

- Cal presses Mira on the gerrymandered sums (my left glove plus the Andromeda galaxy, the moon plus my spoon) -- does she really think those are objects, and how does she individuate them without counting the same scattered stuff as billions of overlapping objects?
- Mira presses Cal to say WHERE the line is between a real composite and a mere pile, and argues that any answer either smuggles in function or interest-relativity (we call it one thing because WE care about it) or goes vague, and that going vague in the middle is really conceding that Cal has no line at all.
- They tangle over my diagenesis case concretely: if there's no fact of the matter in the gradual middle, doesn't that actually support Mira (existence doesn't come in degrees, so if it's indeterminate whether the slope is one thing, something's gone wrong with Cal's picture)? Or could Cal say the middle cases are just semantic indecision, with the ontology staying sharp underneath?
- Let them concede small points honestly and each keep some core they refuse to give up. I don't want a kumbaya ending where they agree it's all just words, unless that's genuinely where the argument lands -- and I suspect it isn't.

Mostly I want the geology load-bearing, not decoration: glaciers, talus, cement, the fact that "a rock" and "an outcrop" and "a formation" are all different grades of thing for us and we never blinked at it. Whether forty years of that supports Cal, Mira, or neither is exactly what I can't tell.
