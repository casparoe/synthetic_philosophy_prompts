I chair the review panel of a mid-sized private research foundation. Twice a year, a panel of seven — mix of senior scientists and one community trustee, none of them social choice theorists — individually assesses roughly forty grant applications, and I have to convert their ballots into a single final ranking that determines which applications get funded. The stakes are real: the ranking is published in an internal report, unsuccessful applicants can and do appeal, and the trustees periodically ask me to justify the method itself, not just its output. I understand that Arrow's impossibility theorem guarantees there is no aggregation rule with all of the properties one would want, but I do not understand what follows from that in practice — which of Arrow's conditions it is least damaging to give up, and how to give it up deliberately rather than by accident.

Please design a procedure my panel could actually follow. It should cover the full pipeline, not just the counting stage: how ballots are collected (full rankings of forty items is a burden, so tell me whether to ask for complete rankings, top-k rankings, or scores, and what each choice costs us); the aggregation rule itself, specified exactly enough that two different people running it on the same ballots would produce the same final ranking; a principled way to break cycles if three or more applications end up beating each other in pairwise comparisons; and what to do with the recurring irritants — a panelist with a conflict of interest who abstains, a panelist who only ranks their top ten and marks the rest as indistinguishable, and a late withdrawal after ballots are in. For each stage, state which of Arrow's conditions it sacrifices and whether that sacrifice happens openly or is disguised in the ballot design, because the trustees will want to know and I would rather not discover it from an appellant's lawyer first.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a genuine Condorcet cycle among the top three applications; an appeal arguing that application A would have outranked B had a third, withdrawn application not appeared on the ballot; and a panelist whose idiosyncratic ballot — one application first, everything else tied last — turns out to determine the entire funded tail. Include at least one case where the procedure's honest output is a result I will dislike, and say so plainly. Finally, mark clearly where the procedure runs out and the judgment of the chair has to fill the gap: I need to know which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
