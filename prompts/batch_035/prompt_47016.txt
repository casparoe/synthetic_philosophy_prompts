Consider the following claim: the long-run trend in human violence has been downward, such that a randomly selected human alive today is substantially less likely to die violently at human hands than a randomly selected human alive in a pre-state society would have been. I want your credence in this claim, stated as a number between 0 and 1, but I am far more interested in the decomposition than the number itself.

Please structure your answer as follows. First, identify the sub-questions your credence depends on, and give each a rough probability with a one-line justification. At minimum, I would expect you to address: (a) the reliability of the archaeological and anthropological evidence on violent death rates in pre-state societies, given that it derives from limited skeletal samples and ethnohistoric accounts that may be unrepresentative; (b) whether the comparison is being made at the right unit — per capita rates versus absolute numbers, and whether averaging over the violent twentieth century rather than the stable present unfairly disadvantages the modern era; (c) whether the relevant definition of violence should include structural harms, such as deaths from famine under extractive institutions or the slower mortality of enslaved and colonized populations, which the narrow battlefield-and-homicide framing tends to omit; and (d) whether the claim requires the trend to be driven by an identifiable mechanism, such as state consolidation, commercial interdependence, or expanding moral circles, or whether a persistent empirical pattern suffices even if its causes remain contested. You may add further terms you think belong in the decomposition, and you should state explicitly how you combine them into your final credence.

After giving the combination, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to — that is, which single term, if shifted by ten or fifteen percentage points, would move your overall credence the most. Finally, describe what evidence or argument would shift that term most: a new archaeological finding, an agreed-upon definition of violence, a demonstration that per capita comparisons across eras of radically different population sizes are conceptually flawed, or something else entirely.

One caution: do not let the last term in your decomposition do all the work by default. If you find that your final credence is almost entirely determined by a definitional choice about what counts as violence, say so plainly, and then give me two numbers — one on the narrow definition and one on the broad one — so I can see how much of the disagreement among people who debate this question is really a disagreement about the facts rather than about the words.
