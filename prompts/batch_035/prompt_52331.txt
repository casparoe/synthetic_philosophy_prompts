I lead a small reading group on Fear and Trembling at my university, and at our last session a dispute broke out over the phrase the teleological suspension of the ethical. One member invoked it to argue that religious obligations can straightforwardly override moral ones, so that Abraham's duty to God trumped his duty to Isaac just as one duty can trump another; another used it to claim something much stronger, namely that there is no universal morality at all, only obligations relative to whatever telos one happens to serve; a third insisted the phrase makes a purely conceptual point about the structure of the ethical itself and licenses no override in any case. All three appealed to the same book, and none of them budged. Before our next session I want to get clear on what that phrase could actually amount to.

Please treat the teleological suspension of the ethical as a slogan that has come loose from its home text and lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. I would like the full range: the near-trivial readings (e.g. something on which the ethical is outweighed in particular cases by another consideration, which ordinary moral conflict already allows and which the contrast with the tragic hero may be designed to exclude rather than capture); the substantive and defensible readings (e.g. that the conception of the ethical as the universal, as a Hegelian-style ethical life, is not self-grounding but rests on something beyond it); the radical readings (e.g. a duty to do what is morally wrong, or a suspension not merely of this or that conception of ethics but of the ethical as such); and the plainly false or straw readings that get attributed to the view (e.g. that it endorses cruelty or exempts the devout from criticism). For each reading say what it would commit one to, whether it is defensible, and — this is the part I most need — whether it would actually support the use my group members put it to in our dispute.

Two further constraints. First, keep me oriented on the state of the scholarship: where you state something, flag whether it is settled among people who know the text (for instance, that the book is narrated by a pseudonymous author, and that the contrast between Abraham and the tragic hero is central to what the phrase is doing) or genuinely contested (for instance, over how far the pseudonym speaks for Kierkegaard, or whether the target is Hegel's own view or a caricature of it). Do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus reading. Second, I want the answer as structured data: a JSON object with the following fields —
