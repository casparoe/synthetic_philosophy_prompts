My philosophy-of-mind study group has produced an argument about the badness of pain that I can't refute and can't believe, and I'd like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) Patients with pain asymbolia feel pain: they identify the sensation, locate it, and rate its intensity like anyone else. But they report that they don't mind it, and they show no fear or avoidance of painful stimuli.
(2) When such a patient undergoes something painful in a clinic — a blood draw, an injection — we are not inclined to think anything bad is happening to the patient over and above the needle's physical effect, or that the clinician owes a special justification for the pain as such.
(3) So the badness of pain is not in the feeling but in the minding: the affective, aversive component rather than the sensory one.
(4) This holds in general: where the minding is absent, the badness is absent, however intense the sensation.
(5) The minding is in principle detachable: a drug could suppress the aversive response while leaving sensation and self-report fully intact, producing temporary asymbolia on demand.
(6) Therefore, if a captor administered that drug to a victim and then inflicted severe pain — the victim feeling and describing every bit of it while unable to mind it — nothing bad would be done to the victim, and the torture would need no more justification than the clinic's needle.

I reject the conclusion of (6), but every step looks fine to me, and my friend has a challenge ready: the drug case simply is the asymbolia case, so if I insist the conclusion is false, I must either say clinicians wrong asymbolic patients whenever they cause them pain, or produce a difference between the two cases that doesn't collapse into the very thing I'm denying — "the pain was inflicted on purpose" or "the setting was a prison" won't do, since the same could be said, harmlessly, of the injection. When I floated the thought that the drugged victim, unlike the asymbolic, has a standing aversion to pain — one formed before the drug and intact after it, which the torture overrides even while the drug silences it — she replied that this makes the wrongness hostage to biography: the very same act would be harmless done to an asymbolic and wrong done to his twin, which she takes as a reductio.

So: which premise or step should I give up, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to hold onto the judgment that the drugged torture is wrong, but I want to know what that commitment forces me to say about the clinic, and whether the standing-aversion reply can be defended as more than an ad hoc repair.
