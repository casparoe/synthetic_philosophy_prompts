In my ethics class we discussed Peter Singer's famous example of walking past a shallow pond in which a stranger's child is drowning, and since then I have been trying to work out what I actually believe by recording my gut reactions to a series of rescue cases. I would like to know whether a single principle captures all of my verdicts, and I would rather you tell me honestly that some of them cannot be held together than force a principle that fits everything only by becoming useless. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. I pass a pond and see a stranger's child drowning; wading in would ruin my phone and make me late for school. Verdict: I must do it, and someone who walks past is doing something seriously wrong, not merely being unkind.
2. A leaflet tells me that a small regular donation would prevent a child far away from dying of malaria. Verdict: I should do it, but skipping it feels like a failure of generosity rather than a cruelty; I would not call someone who never donates a monster, and I do feel that way about the person who walks past the pond.
3. Ten other bystanders are standing around the pond, all equally able to wade in. Verdict: less obligation falls on me in particular, though I would still blame the group as a whole.
4. I already gave part of my allowance to charity this month, so the leaflet can wait until next month. Verdict: excused. Yet if I had already pulled one child out of a pond this month, I do not feel excused from the next drowning child I pass. Both of these feel right to me, and I cannot see how they can both be right.
5. My own child and a stranger's child both need saving and I can reach only one, and mine is slightly farther away. Verdict: saving mine is not merely permitted, it is what I ought to do.
6. A rescue that would cost me a broken arm, or my part-time job that pays for my sister's care. Verdict: heroic if done, but not wrong if not done.

I can imagine several candidate principles: that the obligation turns on the size of the cost relative to the good; that it turns on physical proximity and being able to see the victim; or that it turns on whether I am the only one who can help. Perhaps it is several principles at once. Please state the principle, or a shortlist of candidates, precisely; tie each clause to the case that motivates it; tell me plainly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and which verdict I should give up; and then tell me what the principle predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: whether a one-time donation of $200 that would save a stranger's life is obligatory in the same way that wading into the pond and ruining a $200 phone is. If the answer is no, I would like to know what the morally relevant difference is supposed to be; and if the answer is yes, I would like to know why it does not feel that way to me, or to nearly anyone else I have asked.
