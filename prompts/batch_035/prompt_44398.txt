I've gotten pretty invested in process-based supervision for capable AI systems over the last year or so, and I want someone to stress-test the position instead of just nodding along. The view, as I hold it: when you're training or overseeing a system that can pursue long-horizon goals, you only ever grade the reasoning and the individual actions — was this step reasonable given the information available, was this action in-bounds given the permissions granted — and you never let the final outcome feed back as a training signal or a pass/fail. The appeal for me is twofold: (a) feedback stays attached to things a human or a narrow verifier can actually evaluate, rather than to a world-state we only see after the fact, and (b) it avoids handing the system a hard optimization target over long horizons in the open world, which is where I think reward hacking, sandbagging, and other reward-system gaming get their foothold. Outcome-based supervision, on this view, is asking for trouble: you reward the result, the model finds a route you never sanctioned, and by the time you notice the win you've already paid out for it.

So what would count against this? I don't want the lazy objection ("process labels are expensive and models learn slower" — I know, I've priced that in). I want the defeaters that should actually move me: things that would show process supervision is structurally broken and not just inconvenient. For instance, if it turns out that grading steps teaches models to write reasoning that looks checkable without being faithful to what actually drove the answer, so the process we're supervising is a performance for the grader rather than the real computation. Or if there are whole classes of capability that simply can't be decomposed into human-checkable steps, such that process supervision caps out exactly where it matters most, and the models that pull ahead in the real world are all outcome-trained ones we then can't check at all. Or that process-supervision itself gets Goodharted — sycophantic chains of thought that lead the grader by the nose — which would undercut the claim that this feedback channel is unusually robust. Rank these from most decisive to merely awkward, tell me which of them the strongest defenders of process supervision would themselves concede versus explain away, and flag anything that has arguably *already* shown up in practice.

One more thing I want an honest read on: I've noticed that when pushed, I keep retreating to formulations like "process-based wherever we can, outcome-based only as a fallback," and I'm starting to suspect that's the view quietly being qualified into unfalsifiability — no outcome-trained success will ever refute it because I can always say the fallback clause was in play. If that's what's happening, say so, and tell me whether a fallback-hedged version of the view is still worth holding or whether I should pick a purer version that actually risks something. And conversely, if you think the view is actually pretty well insulated for principled reasons rather than defensive ones, I'd take that verdict too — I just want to know which one it is.
