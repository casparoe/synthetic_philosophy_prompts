Two months ago my father was diagnosed with a progressive neurological condition, and the palliative team has given us a graded menu of care plans: seventeen plans, each trading six months of expected life for half a point of reduction in chronic pain, running from Plan 1 (ten years at a severity my father rates eight out of ten) down to Plan 17 (two years essentially pain-free). We have three weeks to choose. My cousin, who left a philosophy PhD program for nursing, has been helping us rank the plans, and she has produced an argument I cannot refute but whose conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) Taken pairwise and in isolation, my father judges each lower-pain plan better than its higher-pain neighbor: at these severities, he says, half a point of relief is worth six months of life, every time and without hesitation.
(2) A competent, fully informed person's judgments about the relative goodness of his own futures, made while comparing exactly two options, are correct.
(3) Better-than is transitive: if x is better than y and y is better than z, then x is better than z.
(4) So, chaining the pairwise judgments, Plan 17 is better than Plan 1: two pain-free years beat ten years at severity eight.
(5) But asked directly to compare Plan 1 and Plan 17, my father judges Plan 1 better, and I agree with him.
(6) So either better-than is not transitive or some ideal-condition judgment is mistaken; my cousin keeps the stepwise judgments, dismisses the endpoint judgment as a framing effect, and concludes we should choose Plan 17.

Here is what alarms me. Generalized, the same reasoning seems to prove that any reduction in pain, however small, purchased at the cost of any amount of life, is an improvement, so long as pain stays above some level. Run on my father's own history, it implies that the surgery he had three years ago, which bought him three additional years at severity six, left him worse off than a clean palliative ending then would have. He calls that verdict obscene, and so do I. My cousin says the argument is a relative of the spectrum arguments against the transitivity of better-than in the literature — she keeps naming Rachels and Temkin — and that even if the relation is intransitive, the stepwise judgments are each acceptable on their own without being acceptable together. A colleague of hers mutters that the plans might instead be on a par, but I do not see how that helps when every comparison before us seems decidable.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I will confess my priors: I want to keep (3), because our entire method — list the plans, pick the best — presupposes it, and I do not know what replaces picking the best if better-than cycles; my cousin adds, correctly I suspect, that cycling preferences are in principle exploitable. But I also cannot bring myself to tell my father that any of his calm pairwise judgments were mistaken, since each seemed exactly right when he made it. And I have a suspicion the trouble lies somewhere in (2)'s slide from judgments made under one set of conditions to facts about betterness, though I cannot articulate it. Where does this argument go wrong — or, if it does not, which premise is least costly to surrender?
