My therapist friend works with pain asymbolia patients (people who report pain but genuinely don't mind it — they'll say it hurts, locate it, rate its intensity, all while smiling and not withdrawing) and she walked me into an argument I can't find the exit to:

(1) Pain is bad because of how it feels — the painfulness itself, not its downstream effects. That's what people mean when they say pain is intrinsically bad, and it's why we think pain relief matters even when the pain is causing no damage or distress beyond itself.
(2) In asymbolia, the sensory component is intact: patients discriminate, localize, and report. What's gone is the aversive part — the minding, the wanting-it-to-stop.
(3) So these patients' pain is not bad for them. They don't mind it, don't want it gone, and by their own lights it costs them nothing.
(4) If the painfulness itself were the bad-maker, the badness would have to be present whenever the feel is present — that's the whole content of (1), no more and no less.
(5) Therefore the badness was never in the feel. It was in the aversion. What makes pain bad is the wanting-it-to-stop.

And here's where I choke. (6) Run the same argument anywhere else and it proves way too much: on anhedonia it "shows" pleasure was never intrinsically good, on generalized apathy it "shows" nothing was ever bad or good in itself, just desired or not. It also means a person who genuinely and stably doesn't mind their pain has no reason at all to want it relieved, and a doctor who treats an uncomplaining asymbolic's pain is making a category mistake. Some of that I could maybe live with, but the global slide to desire-based value across the board I can't swallow.

I keep staring at the step from (3) and (4) to (5). It smells like it trades on moving from a separability fact to an identity claim — "the feel and the badness come apart in these patients" doesn't straightaway give you "the badness was never in the feel," since both could contribute when both are present, and the syndrome only shows they're separable, not which one grounds what. But I can't nail down exactly where the invalid move is, or whether the argument is actually valid and I just have to give up (1) and pay whatever it costs. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine on the way in, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd especially like to hear what happens to the imperative-type views on which painfulness just is a command to stop — I gather those theories welcome conclusion (5) with open arms, and I want to know what they end up paying for it, including on cases where the command and the feeling clearly diverge.
