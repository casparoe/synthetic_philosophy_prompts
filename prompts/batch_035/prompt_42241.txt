I would like an analysis of the founding and continuing life of the Welsh Gorsedd, run through two frameworks from the study of nationalism: Hobsbawm's account of the invention of tradition, and Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities. The episode is less famous than the Highland tartans, so treat the following facts as given rather than as things to look up (you should not need any source beyond this paragraph and general knowledge):

Edward Williams (1747–1826), a Welsh stonemason and poet who wrote under the bardic name Iolo Morganwg, spent decades forging medieval Welsh manuscripts — including a spurious third series of Welsh Triads — in support of his claim that an ancient Druidic bardic tradition had survived in Wales. In 1792 he staged a ceremony of stone circle, trumpet, and archaic ritual on Primrose Hill in London, presenting it as a revival of these ancient rites, and thereby founded the Gorsedd of Bards. In 1819 the Gorsedd ceremony was attached to the eisteddfod at Carmarthen, and from that point the druidic pageantry — the robed Archdruid, the processions, the Druid's Prayer Williams himself wrote — fused with the eisteddfod movement and was carried forward into the modern National Eisteddfod, which still opens with it. His forgeries were only gradually exposed after his death, by which time they had supplied Victorian Welsh culture with an elaborately constructed national past.

The analytical interest of the case, and what I want the analysis to turn on, is this: the Gorsedd is not just an invented tradition whose invention is hidden from its participants (like the tartans); it is an invented tradition whose invention is public knowledge, and which persists anyway. Today the Archdruid's robes are worn with the general awareness that the "ancient Druidic rites" date to a Glamorgan forger in the 1790s. So the episode should be run in both directions: the theories applied to the case, and the case turned back on the theories. Specifically, I want you to address whether Anderson's central move — that communities are to be distinguished not by the genuineness of what is believed about them but by the manner in which they are imagined — absorbs this without strain, or whether the Gorsedd exposes a limit: whether a community imagining itself through a ritual it knows to be fabricated is a genuinely different phenomenon from one imagining itself through a myth it mistakes for history. And whether Hobsbawm's mechanism, in which tradition legitimates institutions by appearing ancient, can explain the survival of a ritual whose antiquity everyone concedes is spurious — or whether something else (performance, language politics, conviviality) is doing the work, in which case say what.

Some discipline for the analysis. Do not force either framework to speak where it is silent; name the silences explicitly. Paraphrase Hobsbawm and Anderson throughout; do not put anything in quotation marks, and do not give a bibliography or citations.
