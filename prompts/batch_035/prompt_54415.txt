At a meeting of my local giving circle, a member shut down a proposal to fund deworming programs by reciting the old nursery rhyme that starts with a missing horseshoe nail and ends with a lost kingdom. His point, as far as I could tell, was that because any act can ripple into enormous good or harm far downstream, we can never rank our options by their consequences — so there is no reason to prefer one charity to another, or even to give at all rather than keep the money. I'm confident the inference doesn't follow, but I can't yet say exactly where it goes wrong. Someone later told me that philosophers call the underlying issue "cluelessness" about the long-run effects of our acts. Please lay out, as a list I can bring to our next meeting, the distinct precise claims the proverb could be expressing — from a near-trivial caution about unintended consequences to stronger theses about how far our knowledge of effects extends, about whether expected-value reasoning can guide action under deep uncertainty, about whether good and bad downstream effects wash out, and about whether consequences bear on what we ought to do at all. For each reading, classify it as trivial, substantive and plausible, substantive and probably false, or genuinely contested among specialists; mark clearly where the specialists themselves disagree, so that no contested view gets presented as consensus; and say for each whether it, if true, would actually support his conclusion. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Please answer in American English.
