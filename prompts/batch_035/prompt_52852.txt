A popular book on probability aimed at general readers gives this gloss on David Lewis's Principal Principle: 'The Principle says that rational degree of belief just is objective chance. If you know that a setup has a 70% chance of producing outcome A, you must believe A to degree 0.7 — no matter what else you know. So when a fair coin has landed heads nine times in a row, the Principle forbids expecting anything but 50% on the next toss, which is plainly irrational. Worse, the Principle is circular: Lewis analyzed chance in terms of the credences a rational agent would have, so chance is defined by the very credences it is then invoked to constrain. Lewis's own followers conceded this within a few years and abandoned the idea that chances guide credences; the moral is that objective chance is nothing over and above long-run frequency.'

Something about this feels off — my understanding was that the Principle is supposed to say how rational agents should respond to chances, not to equate chance with credence — but I can't locate the errors myself. Please audit the gloss clause by clause: what a philosopher working on chance would accept, what is a forgivable simplification, and what is a straight misrepresentation. I'd especially like your view on two points: whether the "no matter what else you know" clause omits something essential to how Lewis actually stated the Principle; and whether the run-of-heads example really shows the Principle conflicting with rational updating, or whether it turns on mixing two kinds of evidence that the Principle itself keeps apart. Finally, does the book's closing moral — that chance is nothing over and above long-run frequency — follow from whatever accurate content survives the audit?
