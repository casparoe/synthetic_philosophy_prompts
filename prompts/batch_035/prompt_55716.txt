The argument was about whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering. It went roughly like this. (1) Maya, the conservationist, said the premise of the question is obscene: habitat loss is the single worst thing we do to animals, full stop, and any framing that makes a bulldozed wetland look good for the animals living there is a moral laundering exercise. (2) Dev said she's confusing bad-for-the-ecosystem with bad-for-the-individuals: most wild animals are r-strategists — a single fish lays tens of thousands of eggs and all but a handful die within days, often badly; predation, starvation, parasitod worms, the works. A lush old-growth forest isn't a sanctuary, it's a Malthusian machine where population expands until life is just barely tolerable. On Dev's view, MORE habitat means more of that grinding equilibrium, so the naive "habitat good" intuition only works if you're counting species, not experiences. (3) Maya said that assumes wild lives are net-negative, which nobody knows — if a tadpole's few days of swimming around are worth living, then more tadpoles is more good, and also maybe most of those animals (the insects especially) aren't even conscious in a way that puts their suffering on the moral scale, so the whole calculation runs on numbers pulled from thin air. (4) Dev shot back that the uncertainty cuts both ways: if you can't establish whether wild lives are worth living, you can't lean on "destroying habitat harms animals" either, and the consciousness skepticism is awfully convenient — it only shows up when it would keep Dev from counting animals, never when it would keep Maya from counting them. (5) Maya's last move was that even granting everything, there's something deranged about accepting "we can't tell if this ecosystem is net-positive, so we might as well bulldoze it," since that reasoning gets abused by humans instantly and everywhere — the precaution should run the other way.

I honestly could not tell who was winning. As a novelist I mostly noticed that each of them was best when responding to the other, and worst when asserting their own positive view.
