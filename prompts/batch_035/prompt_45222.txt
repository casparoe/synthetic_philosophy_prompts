Something I keep chewing on: in the US, when someone claims a religious exemption — from a vaccine mandate, a dress code, a weapons training requirement — the courts and agencies basically never ask whether the belief is true. That's treated as off limits, the state can't go around judging which religions are correct. What they do instead is two weird sideways questions: is the belief sincerely held, and is it religious in kind rather than just personal, political, or philosophical. So during the Vietnam draft you had boards deciding whether a guy whose objection to war came from his own deeply held convictions, and not from any church, counted as religious enough — and whether being agnostic about God disqualified him. (As I understand it, this all got litigated in the 60s and the basic test survived.)

So what does that whole setup presuppose about what religion even is? It seems to assume religion is an inner conviction — something in the head, sincerity being the thing you can check — which feels like a pretty Protestant picture of religion, where what matters is believing. It seems to quietly rule out views of religion as practice, orthopraxy, community membership, or obedience to a law you didn't personally choose (where "sincerity" is barely the right question at all — you keep kosher or you don't, regardless of how fervently you feel about it). And then there's an apparent incoherence: the state claims it can't define or evaluate religion, yet the "is it religious in kind?" question smuggles a definition back in anyway. Courts can't decide if your god is real but they're happy to decide if your beliefs occupy the right section of your psyche.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of religion this practice rests on, tell me which rival theories of religion (religion as social identity, as law, as ritual, as byproduct of ordinary cognition, whatever) it rules out or struggles with, and flag where the practice is actually underdetermined — maybe several theories of religion would license it just fine. And if the state gave up on the sincerity-and-kind-of-belief approach and instead, say, exempted members of recognized communities, or anyone who fills out a form, what would actually change and what new problems would that create?
