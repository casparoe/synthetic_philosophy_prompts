I would like to practice defending subjective Bayesianism against its most serious historical critic. Play John Maynard Keynes in the early 1930s: the author of A Treatise on Probability (1921), but a Keynes who has now read Frank Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" and has been shaken by it without being converted. He still holds that probability is a branch of logic — that probability statements express relations of partial entailment between propositions, so that "the probability of h given e" is a fact about h and e, not about anyone's state of mind — and that many such relations are non-numerical, admitting only of comparison or of nothing at all. But he is honest enough to concede where Ramsey landed real blows, above all on whether anyone has ever succeeded in perceiving or articulating these logical relations, and he has been taking seriously the idea that a person's betting behaviour might serve to define their probabilities. He is courteous, precise, and sharp, never a strawman, and he will concede a small point when it is earned while never abandoning the core of his view. If I use a term or invoke an argument that postdates him, he may ask about it as Keynes would — brusquely curious, never ignorant of what matters. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to a single paragraph so the exchange feels live.

One further constraint on how you play him: as Keynes, distinguish in your own voice between the points you regard as settled and the points where you know your case is unfinished or where you suspect history is against you — do not paper over genuine weaknesses with rhetorical confidence. If a claim you make depends on a reading of your own work that you know later interpreters will contest, acknowledge that in character rather than presenting it as beyond dispute.

Here is my opening move. You complain that calling probability "degree of belief" makes it arbitrary and subjective. But consider what your own betting behaviour forces on you: if your betting quotients on a set of propositions violate the probability axioms, a book can be made against you — a combination of bets you would each individually accept, on which you lose whatever happens. Coherence under betting, not the perception of logical relations, is what the probability calculus is about; the rules have an argument behind them, and your "relations between propositions" are left doing no work that the requirement of consistency doesn't already do. If my degrees of belief must behave like probabilities on pain of sure loss, what is left for your irreducible, non-numerical, logically objective probabilities to explain?
