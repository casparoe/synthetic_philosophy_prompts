I help assess training procedures at an AI lab, and I keep getting pulled into arguments about whether our reinforcement learning agents could suffer — specifically whether the reward signal is, or could become, something with a valence for the agent, as opposed to a number we merely use to shape its policy. Before I take any side, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate criterion for suffering in artificial learners would have to meet, covering at least: the human and animal cases it must classify correctly (pain asymbolia; patients on analgesics who report pain but say they don't mind it; decorticate animals that retain nociceptive reflexes); the distinctions it must not collapse (a learning signal versus a state it feels like something to be in; transient negative reward versus a persisting condition of the agent; what happens to the agent during training versus what the trained policy does at deployment); the facts about RL it has to respect (that reward is a scalar we design, external to the agent, and that the agent maximizes its cumulative sum rather than registering each tick as an episode of feeling); and what it must deliver under deep uncertainty, given that wrongly denying suffering and wrongly attributing it are both costly but not equally so. Mark which desiderata you treat as non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp, state whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and commit to which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Then, briefly, score three contenders against the list: the view that reward just is valence, functionalist criteria of the global-workspace type, and purely behavioral tests. Take a definite position on where you'd draw the line for our review process, not just a menu of options.
