I'm a novelist -- third book just came out, it's one of those multi-generational things where the same mistake gets made in 1913 and 1998 and nobody in the book notices but the reader does -- and somehow I've let a friend talk me into giving a talk at her university's philosophy department next month. The topic, roughly at her suggestion: why narrative explanation is a genuine kind of explanation and not just a covering-law explanation with the law left as an exercise. My claim is going to be that when a historian (or a novelist) explains why something happened by laying out the sequence of events so that it all becomes followable -- this made that possible, which closed off this other path, which forced the choice that followed -- that's not a defective or elliptical version of subsuming events under general laws, it's a different thing with its own standards of success. I want to argue that followability and the sense of "yes, that's how it had to go, though it could have gone otherwise" is doing real explanatory work, and that the covering-law people never really captured it. I know the department: it's mostly philosophers of science, quite analytically trained, people who take Hempel very seriously and think of him as the default view rather than a historical position, plus a couple of metaphysicians who do counterfactual analyses of causation, and maybe one philosopher of history who's more sympathetic to my side but tends to stay quiet. No literary people at all, so I can't rely on anyone in the room sharing my intuitions.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet. If part of my case really is weaker than I think, I'd rather find out from you than at the podium.
