My colleague Dana has firm views about which mental states count as emotions, and I want to anticipate her rulings on some cases I'll be putting to her next week. Her track record so far: fear of an approaching growling dog, emotion; the full-body jolt at a car backfire, not an emotion; fear of flying while fully judging flying safe, a genuine emotion; fear toward a fictional killer while watching a horror film she knows is fiction, a genuine emotion; a day-long dread with no object whatsoever, not an emotion but a mood; and her calm, phenomenologically flat appraisal that her marriage is going wonderfully — an endorsed evaluative belief with no felt stir — not an emotion. The new cases are: a man with a complete spinal-cord injury who reports vivid grief at his brother's death while noticing no bodily change at all; a woman who suddenly feels unfocused fear while surrounded by a panicked crowd, before she has picked out anything to be afraid of; and a care robot that computes exactly the threat appraisals fear involves and reroutes to rescue its patient, though it feels nothing. Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) at least two distinct rules that fit her verdicts so far; (2) through (4) your best prediction for each new case in turn, noting which of the fitting rules supports it and what pressures it; and (5) the single further case that would best discriminate among the surviving rules, with what each rule would predict about it.
