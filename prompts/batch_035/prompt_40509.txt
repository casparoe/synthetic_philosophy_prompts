When I accepted a two-year posting in Tokyo, a well-traveled colleague told me the rule for everyday conduct abroad is simply "when in Rome, do as the Romans do": follow the local conventions on tipping, queueing, jaywalking, and the rest, and leave your own at home. That sounded right at first, but the longer I'm here the more I wonder how much that one line actually commits me to. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making -- from harmless prudential advice about avoiding friction and awkwardness, to an epistemic point about locals knowing the point of their own conventions better than a newcomer could, to the strong claim that participating in a coordination practice itself generates a moral obligation that binds visitors just as it binds natives -- and say for each reading whether it's plausible and whether it settles what I should do? I'm especially interested in two cases: whether any reading obligates me to follow a local convention I think is worse than the one I left behind (say, a tipping norm that seems to trap servers in lower pay, or a queuing norm that strikes me as less fair), and whether any reading would license the locals to press their conventions on me when my deviating harms no one -- or whether in those cases "when in Rome" is doing no real argumentative work and is just a polite way of ending the discussion.
