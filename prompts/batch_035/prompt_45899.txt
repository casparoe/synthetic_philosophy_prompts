I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, but I spend my days thinking in the vocabulary of git and content-addressed systems, and I'd like a debate I stumbled onto translated into that vocabulary. I recently audited a few lectures in a metaphysics course, and one claim has stuck with me: the view, associated with Kripke, that an object's origin is essential to it — that the table in front of me could not have been made from a different block of wood, only a numerically different table could have been — and the extension of this to persons, where it seems to imply that I could not have developed from a different sperm and egg: the resulting person would have been my sibling, not me, no matter how similar. My friends think this is obviously true; I have an instinct that it's an artifact of how we choose to talk about identity, and I can't make progress on the argument in the philosophy vocabulary.

Here's the translation I want. In my world, a commit is identified by a hash computed over its entire causal history — its tree, its message, and crucially its parent commit — so it is literally impossible for the same commit to have had a different parent: change the parent and you change the hash, full stop. At the same time, we say things like "this is the same change" when a diff is cherry-picked onto a different base, or rebased onto a new history, and we say a fork is "the same project" even though it's a different repository with divergent history. And there's the reproducible-builds world, where two builds from different source trees can produce bit-identical binaries. So translate the origin-essentialism debate into these terms: commits, hashes, parents, diffs, trees, forks, clones, rebuilds. Say which parts of the debate map cleanly (e.g., does the gamete question just become "could this commit hash have arisen from a different parent?" — which sounds trivially no by stipulation), which parts map only with distortion (identical twins seem like clones from the same tree that are nonetheless different people, and I can't tell whether that breaks the analogy or exposes something), and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report: is the essentialist's claim like our hash scheme — a convention we adopted for bookkeeping, with the "couldn't have been otherwise" being true but trivially so — or does the residue point to something in the metaphysical debate that our engineering practice genuinely cannot express, such as the fact that nature didn't get to choose its content-addressing scheme the way we did?

Finally, don't leave me with a menu of positions. Take a stand: given the translated picture, should I believe that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg, and should I believe it as a substantive truth about persons or as something like a decision about how to keep the books — and explain what pushes you one way rather than the other, including where you think the git analogy is doing real work versus where it's quietly distorting the original debate.
