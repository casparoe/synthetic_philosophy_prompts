In 1846, Urbain Le Verrier, working from irregularities in the orbit of Uranus, calculated the position of an unseen planet, and Johann Galle, at the Berlin Observatory, found Neptune within about a degree of the predicted position on the first night of a deliberate search. Thirteen years later, the same Le Verrier, confronting an analogous anomaly — the advance of Mercury's perihelion by roughly 43 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian perturbations could account for — proposed the same kind of fix: an unseen planet, soon named Vulcan, orbiting inside Mercury. Astronomers searched for it for over half a century; occasional claimed sightings never held up; and the anomaly was finally resolved not by finding the missing body but by replacing the theory, when general relativity accounted for the discrepancy in 1915.

Please analyze this double episode through the thesis of the underdetermination of theory by evidence. I already know the standard statement — that any body of evidence is compatible with rival theories, and that Duhem's point about testing only whole bundles of theory plus auxiliary assumptions means any theory can be preserved by blaming an auxiliary. What I want is a careful mapping of both cases onto that idea, followed by an honest account of where the idea breaks.

For the Neptune case: what exactly were the live alternatives in 1846 — an unseen planet beyond Uranus, some deviation from the inverse-square law, errors in the Uranus observations themselves, an unseen satellite or ring? Which of these count as auxiliary hypotheses and which as rival theories in Duhem's sense, and did the available evidence really underdetermine them, or did Le Verrier's calculation already privilege the planet hypothesis in a way that underdetermination talk obscures? For the Vulcan case: the standard underdetermination story says Newton could always be saved, and indeed the rescue stood for half a century before failing. So say precisely what kept Newton alive in the interim — was Vulcan empirically adequate in principle but observationally undetectable, or did the hypothesis quietly rot as candidate sightings failed to replicate? — and whether the eventual resolution by a new theory in 1915, rather than by any decisive refutation of the planet hypothesis, counts for or against the claim that evidence alone could never have settled the matter.

Then tell me where the thesis fails as a description of what actually happened. The two cases look symmetric on the way in — same astronomer, the same type of anomaly, the same style of proposed fix — and one fix succeeded while the other was a phantom. Does underdetermination, as usually stated, have any resources for predicting or even retrodicting which sort of case is which, or was the difference purely a matter of the world having cooperated once? And finally: does the episode show that the thesis is trivially true (bundles are testable, theories alone are not) but practically inert, or is there a nontrivial version of it — something about which auxiliaries are independently motivated, or how contrived a rescue must become before it loses its standing as a rescue — that the Vulcan half of the story genuinely supports?
