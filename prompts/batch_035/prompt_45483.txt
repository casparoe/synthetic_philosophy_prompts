I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background: twelve years writing statically typed code — Java, then Scala, and Haskell for the last five. I know subtyping, typeclasses, variance, and refinement types from using them, though I've never read a type-theory paper. I've run into a puzzle from the Warring States period in China, attributed to the debater Gongsun Long: the claim that a white horse is not a horse. In the dialogue attributed to him, the defender makes roughly three moves. First, the word for horse names the shape, while the word for white names a color; a phrase naming color-and-shape does not thereby name the shape — so a white horse is not a horse. Second, if you ask for a horse, a yellow or black horse will satisfy you; if you ask for a white horse, a yellow or black one will not — so the two differ in what they admit. Third, if horses had no color there would be nothing but horses, so where would a white horse come from; a white horse is horse-plus-white, and horse-plus-white is not horse.

Restate the whole exchange in the vocabulary I actually use — types and values, subtyping and subsumption, nominal versus structural typing, intersection and refinement types, predicates over values and their extensions, type constructors and partially applied generics — as faithfully as you can, translating each of the three moves separately. Then give me the residue report. My working hypothesis is that the everyday claim a white horse is a horse is just the subsumption judgment WhiteHorse <: Horse, and that Gongsun is covertly answering three different questions — type identity, subtyping, and what a name denotes — as though they were one, when my vocabulary keeps them cleanly apart. Tell me whether that diagnosis holds, and in particular whether anything in the original has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all: for instance, his apparent conviction that what a name names settles the type relations, whereas in my world whether WhiteHorse <: Horse is a convention the language designer fixes (nominal) or a fact about structure (structural), not something a name can settle. Is the residue a defect of my vocabulary — something about names and concepts that typing conventions cannot express — or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked?

And one further case, if you have room. Gongsun is also credited with a companion thesis: that the hardness and the whiteness of a stone are never given together — the eye receives whiteness without hardness, the hand receives hardness without whiteness — so the two attributes are mutually external. Does that translate as two typeclass constraints that can each be checked but never jointly, or does it too leave a residue?
