I'm a high school senior writing my IB Extended Essay in philosophy of mind — roughly 4,000 words over the school year — and I've tentatively chosen a case study I'd like your help pressure-testing before I commit to it. Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who works on consciousness and cognitive neuroscience, and begin with some brainstorming before settling into the structured analysis; don't just launch into conclusions.

The case is the split-brain research. In the 1960s, Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga studied epilepsy patients whose corpus callosum had been surgically severed. In the lab, when a word for an object — say, a spoon — was flashed to the left visual field, which projects to the right hemisphere, the patient could not say what they had seen, but their left hand could reach behind a screen and pick out the matching object. With chimeric face stimuli, patients verbally reported the half of the image shown to their left hemisphere while their left hand pointed to the other half. Gazzaniga later came to describe the left hemisphere as housing an interpreter that confabulates reasons for behavior it did not itself initiate. Yet outside the lab, the same patients cooked, drove, and held conversations apparently normally.

The theory I want to run through this is Global Workspace Theory, in the versions developed by Bernard Baars and later by Stanislas Dehaene: conscious contents are those that win a competition and are broadcast across a widespread fronto-parietal workspace, making them available to verbal report, memory, planning, and the like.

Here is what I want, and I want it run in both directions, theory illuminating case and case testing theory. First, the mapping itself: on GWT, what does cutting the corpus callosum actually do — create two separate workspaces, leave one workspace with a severed input route, or something in between? What, precisely, counts as a winning coalition in each hemisphere, and who or what is the audience for the global broadcast in each? Second, where the theory earns its keep: which of the classic findings does GWT explain better than a rival framework would, and are there findings that are awkward for it? Third, my own hunch, which may be confused: the callosum is not the only connection between the hemispheres — subcortical pathways survive the surgery. It seems to me that if global broadcast rides on those surviving routes too, GWT should predict the two workspaces remain partially integrated, so the patient is one partially unified consciousness rather than two, which would fit the everyday normality of these patients. Is that a fair derivation from the theory, or am I forcing precision onto a framework that was never built to answer it? Fourth, the access-versus-phenomenal problem: GWT is arguably a theory of access consciousness, not of phenomenology. If so, the question of whether there are two streams of experience may be one the theory is simply silent on, and my essay might be attempting an analysis the theory cannot deliver — I would much rather learn that now than in March.

Close by telling me what the episode shows about GWT itself: whether split-brain counts as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated, and whether some other lens — a higher-order theory, or the integrated information idea — handles it more naturally. Be disciplined about the mapping and candid wherever the theory is silent rather than stretching it to speak. And if my framing rests on a confused premise — if, for instance, the popular version of these experiments I've absorbed is misleading in a way that matters, or if the question I want answered just isn't the kind of thing GWT addresses — say so plainly instead of working around it; I can rebuild the essay around a better question.
