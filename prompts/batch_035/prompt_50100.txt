I am a professor of political philosophy, and for the past year I have been drafting a piece — provisionally entitled "The Committee as a Believer" — for a workshop on collective intentionality. My formal training is thin; my instincts come from normative democratic theory rather than social choice theory, and I have read the literature on the discursive dilemma only casually. Before I circulate the draft, I want a rigorous check on whether the package of views I defend in it actually hangs together. The package is as follows:

1. Groups are genuine subjects of belief. When the appointments committee rules, this is not merely shorthand for seven individuals ruling separately; the committee itself believes something, in roughly the way a person does.
2. The correct aggregation procedure is proposition-by-proposition majority voting. Any arrangement permitting a chair, a designated expert, or a faction to overrule the majority count is objectionably elitist and epistemically presumptuous.
3. Whatever procedure is used, the collective's belief set must satisfy the same rationality constraints as an individual's: it must be logically consistent, and it must take a stand on every proposition on the agenda.
4. No member's judgement may count for more than any other's; anonymity is non-negotiable.
5. Members ought to vote their sincere individual judgements. Strategic voting — misrepresenting what one believes in order to engineer a preferable collective outcome — is a form of dishonesty I have condemned in print and am not prepared to retract.
6. In the concrete case discussed in the draft: a three-member committee must decide whether to appoint a candidate, where the governing rule, accepted by all three members, is that the candidate is appointed if and only if she is an excellent researcher and her teaching is adequate. Member One accepts both premises and favours appointment; Member Two accepts research excellence but denies teaching adequacy, and opposes appointment; Member Three denies research excellence but accepts teaching adequacy, and opposes appointment. My considered verdict is that the committee collectively believes all three of the following: that she is an excellent researcher, that her teaching is adequate, and that she ought not to be appointed — and that this is an acceptable, even humane, outcome. Groups, like individuals, are sometimes entitled to be a little incoherent.

Colleagues who have seen the draft all attack (6) directly, which makes me suspect the real problem is hiding elsewhere, though I may be wrong about that. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: identify which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally grave, and for each one say precisely what would have to give and what surrendering it would cost me elsewhere in the package. I would also like you to consider whether any of my commitments, taken jointly, commits me to something I have not listed — for instance, whether my enthusiasm for counting hands is compatible with my insistence that the committee is a believer in its own right.

Please reply in American English, with the following sections in this order and roughly these lengths: (i) "Diagnosis at a Glance" — a brief statement of the single deepest tension, around 100 words; (ii) "The Real Tensions, Ranked" — each genuine tension with its severity, what would have to give, and the cost of giving it, around 600 words; (iii) "Merely Apparent Tensions" — those that dissolve on inspection, with an explanation of why each dissolves, around 200 words; (iv) "Recommended Revision" — the single cheapest fix to the package and its price, around 150 words.
