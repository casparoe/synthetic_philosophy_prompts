My philosophy of language reading group has spent three meetings now on whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading, and we've hit a wall that feels different from normal disagreement. One side holds that if I assert something I believe false, I've personally guaranteed its truth and then broken the guarantee, whereas if I say something strictly true and let you draw a false conclusion from it, you did the final inferential work yourself — like the case where I tell you I'll be at the office until noon when I'm planning to leave at 11:50 and I know you needed me there at noon for something urgent. The other side calls that sophistry: in both cases I intend you to come away with a false belief, and the mechanics of how the false belief got there (assertion vs implicature, saying it outright vs exploiting Gricean inference) shouldn't matter morally any more than it matters whether I shoot you with a rifle or roll a boulder off a cliff onto you. Everyone in the room grants all the cases, everyone grants the intent is the same, and yet nobody budges an inch on whether the difference-defenders are describing a real moral asymmetry or just a psychological comfort we take in the thought that we never actually said it — the mental reservation move, where casuists reasoned you could mislead with technically true statements because the deception flowed through the hearer's own inference, so your hands stayed clean.

Here's my working theory, tell me if it holds up: the debate is really two debates wearing one trench coat. One is a philosophy-of-language project — figuring out what makes lying different from misleading as speech acts — and that project is doing fine, people even agree on the taxonomy. The other is the moral question of whether that distinction tracks anything normatively, and THAT one is stalled because the two sides are implicitly weighing different things: the difference-deniers only count first-order effects (the false belief in the hearer, which is identical in both cases), while the difference-defenders keep smuggling in second-order stuff about what a habit of technically-true deception does to the norms of assertion and the trust that makes assertion useful in the first place. If that's right, the whole argument is a proxy war over whether constitutive norms of a speech practice can be morally weighty in themselves. Is that the correct diagnosis, or is it something else — maybe a hidden empirical question about whether misleaders are in fact more likely to get caught and punished (since they can point out they never said it), or just bedrock intuitions about responsibility-sharing that neither side can argue for? And honestly, what would even count as progress here — a new thought experiment, a distinction, a formal account of the norm of assertion, or is this one of those debates that runs on intuition pumps forever and we should just accept that?
