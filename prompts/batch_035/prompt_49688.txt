I lead a small analytics team at a mid-sized city housing authority, and I'm hoping you can help me find the flaw in an argument that our director is now taking rather seriously. Some background first: we built a risk score that predicts which rental units are likely to have serious habitability violations — lead, mold, failing heat — so that our dozen inspectors can decide which of the thousands of open complaints get visited first. We can inspect maybe a quarter of the units flagged in a given year, so the ranking does real work. A colleague in the city's equity office walked me through the following chain of reasoning, and it ends in a conclusion I can't accept but can't refute either:

1. A decision procedure treats groups equally only if its error rates are the same across groups — the share of genuinely hazardous units it misses (false negatives) and the share of sound units it needlessly flags (false positives) must match from group to group.
2. In our data, base rates genuinely differ: because of the age of the housing stock and decades of disinvestment, serious violations are much more common in certain neighborhoods, whose residents are disproportionately of one race.
3. The mathematical work on fairness in prediction shows that any risk score that is calibrated within each group — meaning a "70% risk" unit really is hazardous 70% of the time for everyone, not 70% for some groups and 40% for others — cannot also have equal false positive and false negative rates across groups when base rates differ; and any score with equal error rates must be miscalibrated for at least one group. This is a theorem, not a practical limitation.
4. Calibration is not something we can give up. An uncalibrated score misinforms everyone who acts on it, and quietly rescaling the score or adjusting thresholds for one group amounts to telling that group something false about the risks their homes actually carry.
5. Given (2), every procedure we could possibly deploy — including scrapping the score and letting caseworkers triage by judgment — must fail to treat groups equally in the sense of (1).
6. Equal treatment is the whole content of procedural fairness. Outcomes that differ because of history or luck are a matter of substantive justice, not a flaw in the procedure itself.
7. Therefore, whenever base rates differ, procedural fairness is impossible for any decision procedure, human or algorithmic.
8. Therefore the demand that our tool treat groups equally is incoherent, and the only coherent question to ask of it is whether it is accurate.

My colleague draws exactly conclusion (8) and is now suggesting we stop reporting group-disaggregated error statistics altogether, on the theory that they can't be satisfied and只会 mislead. What bothers me is that the argument seems to prove far too much: if it works, it also shows that the caseworkers' informal triage we used before the model existed was procedurally unfair, that the health department's lead-screening program is unfair, that any teacher's grading in a district where preparation differs by school is unfair — that every decision procedure in a world of unequal base rates is procedurally unfair, which sounds like a reductio of the notion rather than a discovery about our tool. So: which premise or inference should I reject, and why did it look so plausible? For what it's worth, I trust (3) as mathematics and I very much want to keep (4), so I suspect the trouble is somewhere in (1), (5), (6), or the slide from (7) to (8) — but I can't pin down where the equivocation, if there is one, actually happens. I have to brief our director next week, so alongside the diagnosis I'd like your honest view of which premise is least costly to give up and what adopting that position would commit us to in practice.
