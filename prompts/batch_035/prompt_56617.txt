Every few weeks my sister and I play a game we've kept up for years: one of us poses a counterfactual of the form "would it have happened anyway?", and she rules on it. She's annoyingly good at it, and I've started keeping a ledger so I can predict her before she rules. Her track record so far: she says calculus would have arrived within decades even without Newton, since Leibniz was reaching the same mathematics at the same time; she says the Americas would have been reached from Europe within roughly fifty years even if Columbus had never sailed, because too many crowns were probing the Atlantic for contact to wait on one Genoese navigator; she says a general European war would have broken out by the early 1920s even if Princip's shot had missed, given the arms race and the recurring crises of the preceding decade; and, though she allows that German unification was prepared by nationalism and the customs union, she thinks a unified German state would have arrived by around 1900 even without Bismarck — perhaps later and with different borders, but arrived. On the other side of the ledger: she is firm that without Shakespeare nobody writes Hamlet or King Lear, or anything close to them; and she doubts the United States would have ended up with anything like its actual constitution if the Philadelphia Convention had collapsed, since she thinks the union might have fragmented for good rather than reconvene.

The next round of questions has already been set, and I want to get out ahead of her. They are: (1) If Darwin had died young, would evolution by natural selection have been established textbook science by 1885? (2) If Constantine had never converted, would the Roman world by about 600 CE nonetheless have been dominated by a single universalist, scriptural religion? (3) If the first commercially successful typewriters had settled on a different keyboard arrangement in the 1870s, would people today nonetheless be typing on roughly the arrangement we actually use?

What I'd like from you, more than the three verdicts, is the rule she seems to be running. I can see at least three candidates that fit her record equally well: one on which an outcome arrives anyway just in case many independent lines were converging on it at the time; one on which it's a matter of grain — broad outcomes arrive anyway, particular configurations and particular works don't; and one on which it turns on whether the outcome required a singular window or founding moment that, once missed, wouldn't recur. Spell out what each rule predicts for the three new questions, say exactly where the rules diverge, and give me your best guess on each verdict with your reasons. Two further things: I've noticed that the first and third rules seem to agree on everything she has ruled on so far, so tell me what further case would split even those two; and check whether some tempting rule that I haven't listed — say, "technical outcomes converge, humanistic ones don't" — is quietly refuted by some verdict already in the ledger, since that would tell me a lot about how she thinks.
