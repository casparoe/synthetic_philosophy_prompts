I am a seventy-one-year-old retired physical chemist who has come to philosophy late in life, and I am weighing a decision I never expected to face: signing with a cryonics organization. Under the contract, shortly after my death my brain would be vitrified and stored, in the hope that some future organization could reconstruct the person I was — most plausibly, as the engineers describe it, by scanning the preserved tissue and running the information it contains on hardware other than my own brain. My granddaughter, a computer engineer, sees no problem at all: the brain is an information-processing system, what matters about a person is the pattern rather than the stuff it happens to run on, and so anything that keeps the pattern running keeps me. My neurologist disagrees sharply: the brain is not a computer in any sense that would let anyone lift its operation off the living tissue and re-implement it elsewhere, and the whole proposal rests on a seductive but bad analogy. Since the wisdom of signing seems to turn almost entirely on which of them is right, I would be grateful for your help in thinking it through. Specifically: what exactly would have to be true of the brain — of how it stores and carries information — for the scan-and-run proposal to preserve me rather than merely produce a very good copy of me; whether there is any sense in which that difference could be detected from the inside or the outside; and whether there is a sensible way to decide about signing given that nobody currently knows the answer to the first question. I have no philosophical training, so plain language would be much appreciated.
