You are a professional philosopher of biology with deep expertise in the literature on teleology and biological function, and you are comfortable both reconstructing the standard positions in that literature and, where the standard positions fail, building something new.

I have been recording my gut verdicts about what the function of a biological trait is, and I want to know whether they add up to a coherent account or whether I am holding contradictory commitments. I am aware that there is a familiar divide between accounts that ground function in the evolutionary history of a trait and accounts that ground it in what the trait currently does within the organism, but I would rather not commit to either label up front; I want the principle extracted from the cases, whatever it turns out to be. Here are my verdicts:

1. The function of the heart is to pump blood. Not to make thumping sounds, even though those sounds are causally indispensable to a physician diagnosing me. The thumping is not a function, full stop.
2. A badly calcified, failing heart still has the function of pumping blood. It is simply failing at its function. Function talk survives failure.
3. The vestigial eyes of a cave-dwelling fish have no function, even though they descend from eyes that were selected for seeing over hundreds of millions of years. The historical selection seems to have expired; the current uselessness wins.
4. On a modern bird, feathers have the function of enabling flight, and they also have the function of insulation, and I feel no pull whatsoever toward saying that insulation is the "real" function because it came first in evolutionary history. Both are functions, on equal footing.
5. A new mutation arises in a single fish that happens to let it tolerate a toxin that has just entered the water. At that moment — one fish, no selection yet — I say the allele does not yet have the function of detoxification. It is a lucky accident, not a functioning part. Fifty generations later, when nearly every fish carries it because carriers out-reproduced non-carriers, it does have that function.
6. Take the same toxin scenario, but suppose the tolerant fish all die in an unrelated landslide before reproducing, so the allele never spreads. It seems the function never came into being, even though the allele did exactly the same causal work in its one bearer as it would have fifty generations later.
7. A genetically engineered pig heart carries a gene for a fluorescent marker, added by the designers so that surgeons can image the heart during a xenotransplant. Just one such heart is ever made; there is no population, no differential reproduction, no selection at all. I still think the fluorescent marker has the function of being visible to surgeons. Design seems to confer function without any selection history.

Some of these sit together awkwardly, and I can see at least three ways to carve things: maybe function requires a history of selection (which fits 5 and 6), or maybe function requires current useful contribution (which fits 3 and the thumping half of 1), or maybe the history can be either selection or deliberate design (which is what 7 seems to demand). But no single one of these gets everything, and I suspect the truth is either some more complicated disjunctive principle or that one of my verdicts has to go.

Please begin by thinking carefully through each verdict and what exactly it pressures, before settling on anything: brainstorm freely at first, consider how the standard historical and current-contribution accounts of function would handle each case, and notice where each account has to strain. Then deliver the following:

- A statement of the best principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that captures my verdicts, stated as precisely as you can, with the specific case that motivates each clause.
- An honest verdict on whether the principle handles the hardest tension in my set, which I take to be this: verdict 5 seems to say past selection is necessary, verdict 3 seems to say past selection is not sufficient, and verdict 7 seems to say past selection is not necessary at all. If your principle cannot reconcile all three, tell me which verdict you would overturn and why, rather than hiding the cost in a technicality.
- Your principle's verdicts on at least two cases I have not listed: for instance, a trait that was selected for one job but that has recently come, in this population, to perform a different and more valuable job that selection has not yet had time to act on; and a version of the cave fish whose eyes are not yet vestigial but merely degraded, still detecting a faint bit of light the fish no longer uses for anything.
- A short statement of what your principle implies about the concept of dysfunction or malfunction, since verdict 2 suggests function talk has a normative edge, and I would like to know whether your account preserves that or explains it away.

Aim for a rigorous, essayistic answer, not a bulleted summary of the two textbook positions; the value is in the fit between principle and cases, and in your willingness to tell me where my gut has led me astray.
