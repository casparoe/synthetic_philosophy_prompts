I'm putting together a one-page statement of my views on the Church–Turing thesis for a philosophy of mathematics seminar on computability, so the other participants can poke at it before I present. Here is roughly where I've landed, in six items:

1. The original thesis — that a function is effectively calculable if and only if it is computable by a Turing machine — is not an empirical hypothesis about the world but a piece of conceptual analysis. No discovery about machines could show it false; at most, a discovery could show it was a bad analysis, the way a definition of "fish" that included whales had to be given up once biology got serious.

2. What justifies accepting it is what happened in the 1930s: several independently proposed formalisms — Turing's machines, Church's lambda-definability, the Herbrand–Gödel account of general recursiveness, Post's systems — all turned out to pick out the same class of functions, and Turing supplied an analysis of a human clerk working mechanically by rote that matches the machines. That convergence is why we believe the thesis, and it strikes me as a perfectly good epistemic route to it.

3. The physical thesis — that no device constructible in accordance with physical law can compute a non-recursive function — is a different claim, not settled by the first, and it is genuinely empirical: physics could refute it, and I take it to be currently open.

4. Quantum computation poses no threat to it. Whatever a quantum computer can do — Shor's algorithm, Grover's, all of it — stays inside the recursively computable functions. On this point I regard the matter as closed, and I'd be embarrassed to hedge about it.

5. So-called analog hypercomputation — machines that would read a non-computable answer off infinitely precise initial conditions, or off a physical constant into which someone has already encoded the halting problem — I dismiss outright. A device whose result was written into its initial state computes nothing, and infinite precision is not something physics ever supplies; a story that needs it is not physics.

6. Malament–Hogarth spacetimes, by contrast, seem to me a genuine live threat: general relativity permits regions of spacetime in which an observer, after only a finite stretch of their own proper time, can learn the outcome of a computation that, along another worldline, has had forever to run — enough, in principle, to decide the halting problem. I still expect the physical thesis to survive, because I expect a future physics (presumably a quantum theory of gravity) to rule such set-ups out.

What I'd like is an audit. Go through the six and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly, which tensions only look like tensions and dissolve on inspection, and for each genuine one what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would tidy up the most. Two further requests: first, I don't want to come away thinking some party line is the consensus when it isn't — wherever you rely on a claim about which people who know this area genuinely disagree (and I suspect there is more than one such place above), please flag it as contested rather than presenting it as settled, and likewise mark the claims that really are settled. Second, keep it self-contained: I'm not after a reading list, just the philosophical bookkeeping.
