I sit on the coverage comittee of a mid-sized regional health insurer (about 400k members). A few times a year we get petitions to cover treatments for conditions that are, let's say, borderline — cases where it's not clear whether we're looking at a disease or at an expensive way of medicalizing ordinary life. The ones that triggered this request: a provider group wants us to cover intensive therapy for misophonia (people who fly into rage or panic at chewing sounds), a member petition to cover hair restoration for male-pattern baldness, and a clinical network pushing coverage for grief-focused treatment of "prolonged grief" in a way that arguably blurs the line between disorder and ordinary bereavement.

Our medical director says the committee needs an actual procedure, not vibes, because whatever we decide has to be defensible in writing to regulators and to clinicians who are (justifiably) suspicious of insurers relabeling things as non-diseases to save money. And whatever we write up goes out under his signature, so it can't just be my opinion. The trouble is that when I started looking into how "disease" even gets defined, there's this whole split in the philosophy of medicine between people who think disease is fundamentally about biological dysfunction — parts of the organism failing to do what they're supposed to do — and people who think it's at bottom about harm: suffering, unwanted conditions, a worse life for the person. And both camps seem to get some cases right and others wrong, which is exactly our problem. The baldness case is roughly harm without obvious dysfunction, and misophonia is arguably dysfunction with contested harm, so we need something that handles both directions.

So: write me a decision procedure the committee can actually run, per condition, in one meeting. Structure it in these sections, in this order:

"Bottom line" — 2-3 sentences on what the procedure is and the shape of its verdicts (which kinds of conditions come out coverable, non-coverable, and gray).

"The procedure" — around 450 words, numbered steps in order, each with what counts as enough evidence to pass it, and which steps, if failed, stop the whole thing with a no. It should fold in both the dysfunction idea and the harm idea, and it has to tell us which one wins when they conflict — that's the part our current ad hoc approach just ignores.

"Worked cases" — the three conditions above, roughly 150 words each, running the procedure honestly. At least one of these will come out in a direction the committee politically won't like; include that one, don't sand it down.

"Where judgment stays" — about 200 words naming the steps that are really values calls dressed up as procedure, so we know what we're actually voting on when we vote.

"What it can't do" — one short paragraph on the failure modes we should expect, including where the procedure will misfire and when we should stop trusting it.

Constraints: the committee is six clinicians plus me (a policy person, no clinical training), we have maybe an hour per condition, and nobody on the committee has read any philosophy of medicine — so the procedure can't presuppose jargon. Fold whatever distinctions genuinely matter into plain steps. And no reading list, please — we need the finished procedure, not homework.
