I am drafting the critical section of a paper on the procreation asymmetry, and I need a case built to order for it. By the procreation asymmetry I mean the pair of claims that bringing into existence a person whose life would be worse than never existing is pro tanto wrong, while bringing into existence a person whose life would be worth living confers no pro tanto reason in favor — together with its usual person-affecting motivation: the miserable person is not worse off than they would otherwise have been, since they would otherwise not have existed, and the happy person is not deprived by never existing. The thesis the case must support is that the asymmetry is a filter rather than a ranking: it sorts options involving bad lives from options involving good ones, and beyond that it is silent, so that whenever a decision forces a comparison among options that all involve good lives, the asymmetrist must import a ranking from elsewhere — and the two natural imports arrive with their sources' famous liabilities: total utilitarianism (which prefers the larger population) comes with the repugnant conclusion in tow, and average utilitarianism comes with its own notorious verdicts, among them that a single fairly happy life can be better than billions of decent ones. Construct the case so that this becomes visible, subject to the following specifications.

The scenario must be a realistic institutional choice — a foundation boardroom or a ministry's budget office, no trolleys, no science fiction, no idealized utility meters — in which the decision-maker must fund exactly one of three programs, with inaction genuinely unavailable (for example, by a binding deed or an earmark that cannot be returned), and the demographic effects of each program must be presented as settled actuarial projections rather than left vague. The three programs: one bringing into existence a small number of additional people whose lives will be very good; one bringing into existence a vastly larger number whose lives will be barely worth living; and one bringing into existence a modest number whose lives, though containing real goods, will be dominated by suffering and on balance worse than nonexistence. The numbers must be tuned so that the total view prefers the second program to the first, and no verdicts may be stipulated: the case must generate its results from the named theories, and the asymmetrist must not be handed extra principles that amount to smuggling a complete population axiology in through the back door. Since the case is for an article rather than a classroom, that is also the standard for what counts as cheating.

The demonstration, after the scenario: what the asymmetry says about each program, pinpointing the exact step at which it goes silent; what the totalist and averagist completions of it each recommend between the two options over which it is silent, and which perverse verdict each recommendation buys in this very case; which structural feature of the scenario is doing the work; what happens to the asymmetry's position if that feature is removed; and the strongest line of defense available to an asymmetry theorist who denies that she has been forced to borrow anyone's rulebook.

Please give me your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario; (2) the asymmetry's verdicts on the three programs and the precise point at which it falls silent; (3) what the totalist and averagist completions each recommend between the two remaining options, and what each recommendation costs; (4) the feature of the case that is doing the work, and what changes when it is removed; (5) the asymmetry defender's best response.
