I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on parody objections to ontological arguments — Gaunilo's island, its descendants, and what becomes of the parody strategy against modal versions like Gödel's. Before I catalogue and grade individual parodies, I want the specification first: what must a parody of an ontological argument accomplish in order to count as a refutation, and what lesser things might it achieve that are still worth noting? Please write the desiderata.

The list should cover, at least, the following ground:

1. Formal parallelism. How close must the structural match be — same inferential form, or merely the same rhetorical shape? A parody might show the original argument is invalid, or that some premise is contestable, or only that the argument's conclusion is unwelcome; these are different achievements. Should I distinguish parodies that refute from parodies that merely embarrass, and where does the boundary sit?

2. Falsity of the parody's conclusion. Intuitively the parody must land on a conclusion that everyone, or at least the proponent of the original, rejects. But there is an obvious tension: if the parallel were exact, the original's conclusion would inherit the parody's falsity, which is what the parodist wants — yet proponents always claim some disanalogy. Which disanalogy-claims are legitimate blocking moves rather than ad hoc patches? The list should handle the standard reply to Gaunilo, namely that an island admits no intrinsic maximum while greatness supposedly does, and its generalization: for which predicates does an Anselmian supremum exist at all, and can the theist's preferred restriction on predicates be imposed without begging the question? It should also handle the case where the parody's conclusion is not uncontroversially false but merely unwelcome — a Gödel-style derivation, run on a shifted operator, of the necessary existence of some abstract entity nobody had opinions about before the parody was constructed.

3. Premise-plausibility parity. Must the parody's premises be exactly as credible as the original's? A parody that is persuasive only to those who already reject the original's conclusion proves nothing to the faithful; is that a failure of the parody or the point of it? Draw the line between parodies that work as ad hominem exposés and ones that work as genuine refutations, and say which the desiderata are for.

4. Diagnosis versus mimicry. The Kantian hundred-thalers point and the complaint that existence is not a real predicate do not rerun the argument on a new subject; they explain where it goes wrong. Is that sort of diagnostic objection a parody, a rival category, or a better one? Where does my list place diagnosis, and does the distinction between diagnosis and mimicry stay stable for modal versions, where the complaint can shift from a premise to a definitional choice — the positivity operator in Gödel-style axioms, the worry that substituting an arbitrary operator for positive proves the necessary existence of whatever one likes, and the modal collapse worry that the axioms make everything necessary?

After the desiderata, please do the following:

- Mark each requirement as non-negotiable for something to count as a parody at all, negotiable, or a preference of a particular camp — and say which camp.
- Tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that exactness of parallel and uncontroversial falsity of the conclusion pull against each other; if some version of that is right, the impossibility is itself a result about what parodies can and cannot do, and I want it stated precisely.
- Identify which single requirement does the most work: the test that, if enforced, knocks out the largest share of the standard catalogue (Gaunilo's island, the maximally evil being, the perfect entity that necessarily does not exist, the run-it-on-any-property parodies aimed at Gödel).
- Say whether the requirements differ for parodies of the first Anselmian argument, whose target is a premise about greatness, and parodies of the axiomatic Gödel version, whose target is the choice of axioms — and if they differ, give both lists rather than forcing one to do double duty.
- Keep the whole thing free of citations, reading lists, and attributions I would have to check; where you would otherwise name an author, describe the move instead.

The specification is good enough if it lets me decide, chapter by chapter, which parodies earn sustained treatment and which get a footnote of dismissal. A brief scoring of the classic parodies against the list at the end is welcome, but the criteria and their motivating cases are the point.
