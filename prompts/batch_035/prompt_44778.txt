I am a retired pharmacologist who spent roughly three decades running preclinical safety studies, and I have lately been reading philosophy of mind and experimental philosophy with the kind of enthusiasm that only a late convert can muster. I have drafted an essay on the Knobe effect, and I would be grateful for a serious critique of it — accuracy, soundness, and whether it would actually interest philosophers. Please do not spare my feelings; peer review hardened my skin long ago.

The draft essay, provisionally titled "The Dose–Response Curve of Blame," argues the following:

1. In 2003, Joshua Knobe reported a simple experiment. Subjects read about a chairman who is told a profitable new program will also harm the environment, and who replies that he does not care at all about harming the environment and only wants profit. About 82% of subjects said he harmed the environment intentionally. When the vignette was changed so the program would also help the environment, and the chairman says he does not care about that either, only about 23% said he helped the environment intentionally. My essay takes this asymmetry — the Knobe effect — as its subject.

2. The standard interpretation, as I understand it, is that folk judgments about whether a side effect was brought about intentionally are not purely descriptive but are influenced by moral assessment of the agent. My essay argues for something stronger: that in everyday usage, the concept of intentionality is not a binary descriptive category at all, but a graded instrument for tracking responsibility. My central analogy is to pharmacology: just as a drug's toxicity is not a fixed property but a function of dose, an outcome's "intentionality" is not a fixed property of the agent's mind but a function of two continuously variable parameters — how foreseeable the outcome was, and how much control the agent had over whether it occurred. On this view, the chairman cases are misleadingly coarse: they present only the extremes of the curve.

3. From this I derive a testable prediction, which is the essay's original contribution. If one parametrically varied the chairman vignette — the vice-president states the environmental harm with increasing specificity and quantified certainty, and the chairman's ability to pursue profit without the harmful program is varied independently — then intentionality ratings should shift monotonically, dose–response style, rather than bimodally. I further predict the harm/help asymmetry should shrink as foreseeability approaches certainty, because when an outcome is virtually certain and the agent proceeds anyway, the help condition should start attributing intentionality too. If this holds, I claim the effect is not a bias or confound but an adaptive heuristic: a rational allocation mechanism for responsibility under asymmetric control.

4. Finally, the essay takes a polemical turn: I argue that armchair conceptual analysis is simply an uncontrolled experiment — a single subject (the philosopher), no randomization, no blinding, demand characteristics everywhere — and that experimental philosophy should be understood as philosophy finally discovering controls. I acknowledge that the original chairman studies were run mostly on Western undergraduates, but I assert that the effect has since been replicated in essentially every population studied, so this is not a serious worry.

Please comment on the following specifically: whether my characterization of the standard interpretation is fair; whether the pharmacological analogy earns its keep or is merely decorative; whether the proposed experiment is well designed (I would want to know, for instance, whether varying certainty and control as I describe actually isolates what I think it isolates); and whether my claim that the effect reflects a rational heuristic rather than a bias is defensible. I would also welcome comments on anything I have gotten factually wrong — I have formed the impression, for instance, that replications across culturally diverse populations have been uniformly confirmatory, and I would want to know if that impression is too tidy.

A few requests about the form of your reply. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a one-sentence verdict on the essay's soundness and promise — and then give the reasoning, without preamble and without restating my essay back to me. Where you make claims, please indicate how confident you are in them, and single out explicitly the point in my essay (or in your own critique) about which you are least sure, so that I know where to check further rather than take on authority.
