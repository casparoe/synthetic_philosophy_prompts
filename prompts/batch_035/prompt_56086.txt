I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice theory — 22 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class game, played for tokens or candy rather than money, in which students run into the point where expected-utility theory starts to look like a substantive commitment rather than a definition of rationality. The specific phenomenon I want them to discover from the inside is the one at the heart of risk-weighted expected utility theory: a configuration of bets where someone who makes a series of individually defensible choices ends up, taken together, violating the Sure-Thing Principle — and where the very same gamble is treated differently depending on whether it is offered as one decision or as two sequential ones. Structure the game so that a committed expected-utility maximizer is exactly indifferent at the crucial juncture — so that whatever they choose cannot be chalked up to error or bad math — while the rest of the class splits in a pattern that is too systematic to be noise. Give me the exact bets and payoffs, a timeline for the session, a way to log choices so that the pattern can be displayed to the class before it is named, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinction: what it would take for the revealed pattern to constitute a coherent risk weighting rather than mere inconsistency, and why the one-shot versus sequential framing does any real work. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students gaming the payout scheme, indifferences produced by confusion rather than calculation — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: the theory concerns a single agent whose risk attitude is coherent across situations, whereas my evidence is aggregated classroom behavior, so tell me precisely why that gap exists, whether anything in the game can narrow it, and what I would have to supply in the debrief to close it. Do not have me lecture the theory beforehand; participants should encounter the names only at the end.
