I help run a monthly rationality meetup — about a dozen attendees, mostly longtime readers of the Sequences who can recite conservation of expected evidence but have never had to act on it, one 90-minute session. Design a betting game for us that teaches Jeffrey conditioning: the rule for updating when your evidence is itself uncertain, rather than something you know for sure you observed. The scenario I've been toying with is Jeffrey's own cloth-by-candlelight situation, transposed: participants get private, individually unreliable signals about some binary state (which of two decks we drew from, say), and bet fake currency on it. The trap should be that the intuitively right move — treat each signal as certain evidence and update hard, like a good Bayesian — is strictly dominated by soft updates that reweight both the hypothesis and the reliability of the signal, and participants should discover this by losing money, not by being lectured. Give me the full rules, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that makes the difference visible within an hour, and guidance on tuning the signal reliabilities so the naive updaters lose without it feeling rigged. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the formal rule, P'(H) = P(H|E)·P'(E) + P(H|¬E)·P'(¬E), including where exactly the naive strategy goes wrong in those terms. If you can, add a second round where different subgroups receive the same two noisy observations in different orders, so the group can see for themselves that Jeffrey updates generally fail to commute — and discuss whether that order-dependence should disturb us or not. Close with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which feature of updating on uncertain evidence no betting game can reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand.
