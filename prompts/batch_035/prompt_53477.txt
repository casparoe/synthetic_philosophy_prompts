Next term I am teaching a seminar on language and machine understanding, and I want to open the course by pairing Douglas Hofstadter's essay on the shallowness of Google Translate (The Atlantic, 2018) with Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival (2016). Hofstadter's charge, in brief, is that statistical and neural translation systems match surface forms across languages without understanding either, and that the gulf between such matching and genuine interpretation is a difference in kind — visible the moment one feeds the system an idiom, a shift of register, or anything requiring a picture of the world behind the words. Arrival seems to me one of the few films actually about translation rather than about aliens, and I want a reading that takes Hofstadter's worry seriously.

Consider the kangaroo anecdote Louise tells early in the film — an explorer mistaking a phrase meaning I-don't-understand for the name of an animal — which is a compact dramatisation of precisely the pairing-without-understanding failure Hofstadter diagnoses; and the near-catastrophe when several governments read a logogram as denoting a weapon, while Louise insists the sign may equally express tool or use, which plays out the same failure at geopolitical scale. Yet Louise's own method — pointing at objects and names, waiting for the heptapods to respond with paired logograms — looks structurally like the very procedure Hofstadter dismisses. Where does the film locate the difference between what she does and mere matching, and is it a difference Hofstadter's own criteria would recognise?

Two further questions I would like the reading to address. First, the film's Whorfian premise: acquiring Heptapod B rewires Louise's temporal cognition, so that understanding the language becomes inseparable from being transformed by it. Does that answer Hofstadter's question or beg it? Second, the heptapod logogram encodes an entire proposition in a single non-linear ring of ink, with no word order at all. Does a language of that shape make the shallow-matching problem harder than Hofstadter's hardest human examples — idiom, register, poetry — or easier, since there is no linear surface for a matcher to exploit? Finally, where does the film resist the Hofstadter reading altogether, such that an honest critic has to concede it is finally about choice and time rather than about understanding?

Please write in British English. I would like something polished enough to circulate to the class as a model of reading a film philosophically without reducing it to an illustration of a position.
