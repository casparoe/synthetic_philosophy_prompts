I'm a programming-languages researcher — compilers, type systems, meta-circular evaluators; fluent in that world, though I've only skimmed Gödel's actual proofs. A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. He thinks the central analogy — the passage from self-referential formulas to self-aware minds — is profound; my instinct is that it's quining all the way down, and that once you know quines, diagonalization, and fixed-point constructions there is nothing left to be surprised by. Restate that analogy, as sympathetically as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: quines, fixed points, diagonalization, interpreters that inspect themselves, the levels of a reflective tower. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the analogy map cleanly onto constructions I already know? Which parts had to be distorted to fit, and did the distortion quietly smuggle in something my vocabulary lacks — a system "seeing" its own truth, levels "talking to" each other? Which parts have no counterpart at all? And finally: does the residue point to something real that my vocabulary cannot express, or to places where the analogy is doing more work than the mathematics? Throughout, please separate what is settled — about what Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually establish — from what is genuinely contested, both about the strength of the analogy and about how to read Hofstadter's own claim, and don't present one reading of the book as though it were the consensus.
