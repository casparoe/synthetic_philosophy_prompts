I write novels, and for twenty years my working method has been to weigh suffering against happiness on behalf of people who don't exist. Whenever I decide that a character must endure a decade of grief so that her final chapters can mean something, or that a whole invented town may be flattened so one marriage can be rebuilt, I am making a small utilitarian calculation and calling it craft. A philosopher friend recently read my latest manuscript and told me, over a long supper, that my aesthetic instincts appear to encode a moral theory — something she called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — and that if I actually held it as an ethics I would be committed to views I would find monstrous. I have been arguing with her by post ever since, and I would like a professional moral philosopher (which is the role I am assigning you: please reason carefully, feel free to brainstorm before committing, and do not trim your conclusions to flatter me) to help me work out what my verdicts actually amount to.

Here they are. A world containing a trillion rapturously happy lives plus one child tortured for a decade strikes me as worse than a world of a billion quietly contented lives and no torture — no amount of bliss seems available to pay that debt. Yet a mild headache standing between a woman and a transcendent evening of music is obviously worth it, and even a fortnight of fairly severe pain for fifty years of deep happiness seems defensible to me, at least when I imagine it as a plot. I once wrote a character who endures a decade of crushing depression and emerges into twenty good years, and I still think that book earns its ending; my friend says my own first verdict forbids me that comfort. Spreading a very mild annoyance across every person who will ever exist rather than letting one person be tortured for fifty years seems right to me as well, though the sheer arithmetic makes me queasy. Someone who freely takes on chronic severe pain so that thousands may flourish — I find that admirable, not wrong, which suggests consent matters to me. But then here is the verdict that keeps me awake: I think it is plainly permissible to have a child whose life will contain, alongside much happiness, an ordinary share of real grief — a parent's death, perhaps a bitter divorce — and if any grief crosses the threshold my first verdict invokes, my own principle appears to forbid bringing anyone into existence at all, which I refuse to conclude.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures this pattern? Please state it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause — whether it needs a threshold on the intensity of suffering, a consent carve-out, some treatment of how mild pains aggregate, or something else entirely. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether the choice is between saving every verdict at the price of a baroque principle and adopting a simpler one that overturns one of them. I would also like its verdicts on cases I haven't given you: where exactly the threshold sits and whether it can be vague without collapsing; whether a world of pure uncompensated pleasure with no persons in it could ever make up for one instance of severe pain; and, above all, whether the principle that saves my first verdict also entails that it would have been better never to write — never to create — any of my characters, or any child at all, and if so, whether that is a bullet a reasonable person could bite or a reductio of the whole exercise.
