I have no academic background in any of this — I left school at eighteen, run a small shop, and got into probability the way other people get into chess, by reading on my own in the evenings. So if what I'm about to describe is something every philosophy student learns in week one, tell me that plainly. But I've been turning it over for months and I can't find whether it has a name.

Here is the shape of argument I keep meeting. You show that a person who holds some combination of beliefs or preferences is exploitable — not that anything they believe is false, but that a sufficiently clever counterpart, allowed to interact with them, could extract something from them that they had no reason to give: drain their money through a series of bets each of which they'd individually accept, or charge them small fees forever to keep switching between options because their preferences go in a circle. The verdict is never "your belief is wrong about the facts." It's "your position can't survive contact."

I first met it in the betting arguments for the rules of probability: if someone's degrees of confidence don't obey the standard rules, a bookmaker can offer them a set of bets, individually acceptable to them, on which they lose money whatever happens. And I confess the argument has always struck me as proving something while also seeming to prove too much and too little at once. It seems to need the bookmaker — but no real bookie offers me that exact combination of wagers, I can simply decline to bet at all, and if my losses exist only in a hypothetical encounter with a hypothetical adversary, why is that a defect in my confidence rather than bad luck about who I meet? On the other hand, I can't shake the sense that the person is somehow already in a bad state before the bookie shows up. And a further wrinkle: I could always be shown the full set of bets in advance, notice the guaranteed loss, and refuse the last one — so the argument seems to assume I can't do arithmetic, which would be a fact about my attention, not about my beliefs.

Once I saw the shape, I started seeing it in other places, and here is my real question. I think there are two different things being run together under it. In some cases the exploit reveals an inconsistency that was already there: if you'd pay to get from A to B and also pay to get back, something in you contradicts itself whether or not the fee-charger exists. In other cases the "inconsistency" only comes into being through the exchange — the way a skilled interrogator can lead an honest witness into contradicting themselves, where the contradiction says more about the questioning than the witness. And I've caught myself, and others, treating the second kind as if it were the first.

So: does this family of arguments have an established name, or a recognized subdivision into the kind where exploitation exposes a prior flaw and the kind where the interaction itself manufactures the appearance of one? If yes, I want to know how the standard treatments draw that line and where my two-way carve of it goes wrong — I suspect my unease about the betting arguments lives exactly on that line. If my description doesn't match anything established closely enough to have a name, say so, and then give me your own way of carving up the territory, with the edge cases where the carving gets hard. Please don't just hand me a label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and which of my examples I've misdescribed. Since you'll know the field far better than I do, also tell me where my picture is most likely distorted by my lack of background — which of my instances the standard view would classify differently, and what consideration would make you abandon the whole two-kinds framing.
