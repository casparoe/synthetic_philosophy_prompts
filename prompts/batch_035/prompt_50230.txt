I teach philosophy of mind, and over the years I've staked basically my whole line on something close to Dennett's real patterns view: mental states are real in the way patterns in data are real — a belief is real when tracking it lets you compress and predict behavior you otherwise couldnt, and the physical, design, and intentional stances are just three toolkits for catching patterns at different grains. I'm not asking you to defend this for me. I want the opposite: a map of where the view is actually exposed. What would have to be true for it to be wrong?

And I don't just mean the stock worry that it's instrumentalism wearing a fake mustache. I mean which possible discoveries, cases, or arguments would genuinely count against it, and which a committed real-patterns person could rightly shrug off. A few things I've been chewing on — treat them as leads, not as the whole menu:

1. The overgeneration problem, as I run it. Define the pattern "all coin flips I will ever observe plus the current longitude of Mars". Tracking it compresses my predictions about those flips — trivially, since the list is finite and I already have it — and it supports projection onto... nothing. So Dennett's own move, that real patterns are the ones that support projection to novel cases, is doing all the anti-gerrymandering work, and I can't see a non-ad-hoc reason why compression-plus-novelty picks out exactly the patterns we wanted to call real rather than some cheaper rival. If the criterion has to be patched by hand every time a counterexample shows up, hasn't the view quietly collapsed into "patterns are real iff they're the ones we already liked"?

2. The undergeneration side. Suppose someone's behavior becomes perfectly predictable from the design stance alone — a patient whose utterances are fully accounted for by their conditioning history, no intentional vocabulary needed. Does this person thereby lack beliefs? If yes, that's a bullet most people wont follow you onto. If no, then compressibility from the intentional stance wasn't necessary after all, and something else was carrying the weight.

3. The case my grad students keep throwing at me: interpretability researchers pull features out of a neural network's activations that support genuine projection — they can predict, and even edit, specific behaviors by intervening on those features. By the criterion, those are real patterns, so the network has real internal states. Fine. But the same researchers also find heaps of other patterns at every grain of analysis, most with no stable role across contexts. Is "real patterns" doing any discriminating work in that setting, or does it just relabel whatever interpretability hands it? And if it can't tell the signal from the noise there, what exactly is it telling us in the head?

For each line, tell me whether it's fatal, merely awkward, or something the view's defenders can legitimately absorb — and, if they absorb it, what that costs them. Then give me your honest overall verdict on how much of the view's survival is real resilience versus qualifications bolted on after the fact, and whether that insulation, if it's there, is a defect for a view of this kind or just what you should expect from a metaphysical thesis. One more thing: keep straight what's genuinely settled here versus what's live disagreement among people who actually know this literature — I know the textbook line on Dennett gets contested in ways the textbooks don't mention, so don't hand me contested readings dressed up as consensus.

Format: bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
