(a) State this challenge as precisely as you can, and distinguish at least two different versions of it. And here's the student's answer (transcribed as written, typos are theirs):

"The challenge starts from the fact that in morals, unlike in geography or chemistry, the experts disagree. There is no consensus among moral authorities we could defer to, so trusting moral testimony amounts to trusting one side of a controversy, which is exactly what we should not do if we want justified moral beliefs. Still, we can distinguish two worries: an epistemic one, that testimony just cannot produce justified moral belief, and a practical one, that even if it could, relying on it makes us deficient moral agents — like a craftsman who buys his products instead of making them. For the practical one, the thought is that moral judgement is a skill, and you cannot gain a skill by being told conclusions, only by reasoning to them yourself. For part (b), the evidential response fails, because if moral testimony were just evidence then deference would be perfectly fine whenever the source is reliable, which would make second-hand moral belief as good as first-hand moral reasoning, and surely that is wrong — someone who always deferred would be deficient even if every belief they aquired that way was true. Also, unlike a weather forecast, moral testimony cannot be probabilifying, since testimony only transmits belief, and you cannot transmit something like understanding. For (c), an account would need to explain why deference is fine for children but not for adults, and why deference on some topics (say, whether eating meat is wrong) feels worse than on others (say, whether one is obligated to keep a promise)."
