As part of a graduate seminar I teach on collective decision-making, I like to assign fiction that dramatizes the formal material, and for our unit on the discursive dilemma I want a short story rather than the usual journal articles. Could you write one, roughly 800 to 1,200 words long?

Here is the setup I have in mind. A three-member committee at a university must decide whether to recommend a colleague for tenure. By long-standing but unwritten practice, the committee should recommend tenure only if it judges both that the candidate's research record is strong and that their teaching is adequate. One committee member accepts both premises and so favors tenure; the second accepts the research premise but not the teaching one and so opposes tenure; the third accepts the teaching premise but not the research one and also opposes tenure. So a majority of individuals rejects tenure, yet a majority accepts each of the premises the committee's own standard requires.

Please build the story around how the committee chair, who must sign off on the recommendation, copes with this mismatch between majority voting on the conclusion and majority voting on the premises. I would like the story to do at least these things: show the chair realizing that the choice between a premise-driven procedure and a conclusion-driven vote is itself a substantive decision that no rule settles; and explore whether deliberation could dissolve the problem rather than merely route around it — for instance, by having one member reveal that her vote against adequacy of teaching rested on a threshold she never made explicit, so that "adequate" turns out to be doing hidden work as a matter of degree dressed up as a binary judgment. I am also interested in whether one member might attempt strategic voting to control the outcome, and in whether the chair should see that possibility as a defect of the procedure or as inescapable in any collective judgment.

A few constraints: please keep it literary rather than didactic — no character should deliver a lecture on the formal literature or name-drop theorists, and the paradox should emerge through the characters' conversation and the chair's private deliberation, not through exposition. The three committee members should be distinct people with recognizably different reasons for their judgments, not just placeholders for a truth table. And I would rather the story end ambiguously, with the chair's decision defensible but unsettling, since my students will be asked to argue about whether it was the right one.
