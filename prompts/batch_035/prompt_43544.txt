I train newly hired quality-control inspectors at a plant that makes machined and moulded components, and a recurring part of my job is building the example sets used in certification sessions. Every few weeks a new defect class or a new product line arrives, and I have roughly an afternoon to assemble a handful of labelled parts — good and bad — that new inspectors will study for about two hours before being tested on sorting parts on the line. The inspectors are sharp but have no engineering background; getting more physical examples is often impossible, because defects are rare or the parts are costly to scrap, so I typically have between four and eight items to teach each concept with.

The body of theory I would like operationalised is the one surrounding Bongard problems and the induction of concepts from a few examples: the observation that any small set of positive and negative instances is consistent with many different regularities, so a handful of examples teaches the intended concept only if it is deliberately chosen to rule out the competing hypotheses a learner might otherwise settle on — through near-misses, careful contrasts, prerequisites, and awareness that a learner can latch onto a co-varying but unintended feature and extrapolate confidently in the wrong direction. Please convert this into a procedure I can actually run when assembling a training set: the checks to apply to a candidate handful of examples, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each check; where the procedure should halt and tell me the concept cannot be taught from examples at all and needs a different method; and which steps are irreducibly judgment calls that a checklist cannot settle for me. It must handle three hard cases: a defect defined by a single crisp feature, a defect defined by an overall gestalt of looking wrong with no one necessary feature, and a defect so rare that I can obtain only positive examples and no negatives whatsoever. Include at least one case where the honest output of the procedure is that my planned approach fails. Present the procedure as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
