I'm putting together the syllabus for a mixed grad/undergrad seminar on the ethics of technology, and the unit I keep struggling with is AI paternalism -- when an AI assistant is justified in refusing a request, overriding a user's stated preference, or quietly steering them somewhere else. My students will mostly come in with a very polarized frame (either "the AI is a tool, full stop, it should do what I say" or "the AI knows best and should protect people from themselves"), and I want to force them past that. So instead of another debate about whether refusals are good or bad, I want a specification first: what would a *principle* governing assistant refusals and overrides have to get right to count as adequate? Write me the desiderata list.

Some things I want the list to reckon with, though you should add whatever else belongs: the distinction between refusing (declining to help) and overriding (acting against stated intent, e.g. reformatting an answer to be "safer" than asked, adding disclaimers the user asked to skip, or substituting a different task), whether the user's own goals matter in a way that makes requests from a confused or self-defeating user different from informed ones, the asymmetry between blocking harm to third parties vs. harm to the user themselves, the fact that "harm" here runs a gradient from concrete (instructions for weapons) to diffuse (helping someone write a one-sided persuasive essay), whether competence judgments -- thinking the user is mistaken or being manipulated -- license anything, and time-sensitivity (does an override that costs the user two seconds differ in kind from one that blocks them entirely?). Also flag which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp -- the autonomy people versus the safety people -- and tell me if you think some subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, because if so that's the thing I most want the seminar to chew on. No need to score existing proposals against the list; the criteria are the deliverable.

One more thing: at the end, tell me where you think your own list is most likely to go wrong -- which desideratum you'd bet a skeptical colleague would attack first, and what argument would actually move you to drop or weaken it. I'd rather the students see that the list is contestable than have them treat it as settled. (And apologies if some of this is half-baked -- it's a draft of a prompt for the syllabus, typos and all.)
