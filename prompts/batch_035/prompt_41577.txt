I came to Stoicism the embarrassing way, through productivity youtube and a couple of self-help books that kept repeating "focus on what you can control, accept the rest." Recently I finally started reading the actual Enchiridion and something about it bugs me. Epictetus says what's up to us is basically our judgements, impulses, desires and aversions — and nothing else. Your body, your property, your reputation, other people: none of it is up to us. But the modern teachers I first learned from don't talk like that atall. They say your effort and preparation are "in your control" in a graded way, that your health or your standing at work is "partly" up to you, and that the whole art is pouring energy into the partly-controllable middle while accepting the outcomes you can't guarantee. Alot of them even split things into three buckets — fully up to you, not up to you at all, and a grey zone of things you can influence — which I gather is a fairly recent invention of the revival rather than something you find in the ancient texts.

So my question is whether these are even the same doctrine anymore, or whether the revival has quietly swapped the original out. On the strict reading, the modern version seems to re-import exactly what the dichotomy was meant to fence off — caring about health, career, reputation, all the preferred indifferents — just with a disclaimer about outcomes attached. Epictetus doesn't seem to think your effort is "up to you" in his sense, since even carrying out a decision depends on your body cooperating. On the other hand the strict reading has its own problem: if literally nothing except your own judgements is up to you, then even my choice to go for a run this morning wasn't "up to me", and the dichotomy stops doing any practical work as life advice — it becomes a description of a metaphysical thesis rather than a tool.

Compare the two for me: the classical dichotomy as Epictetus actually uses it (tied up with assent and what happens in your own mind) versus the modern revival's graded, influence-based reading. Is the modern one a legitimate repair of a doctrine that's incoherent or useless taken strictly, or is it a way of dressing up ordinary goal-setting advice in a toga? Which version do you think is more defensible as a philosophy, and — a separate question, I'm aware — does the defensible one still deserve the name Stoicism? A friend of mine who did classics claims the whole "trichotomy" move is basically an Aristotelian graft onto a doctrine that was never meant to be a decision aid, so I'd be curious whether you agree and where you'd locate the real break, if there is one.
