I am grading a midterm for a graduate seminar titled "Induction, Learning Theory, and Skepticism," which covers Hume's problem of induction, Goodman's new riddle, Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication, and the no-free-lunch theorems of supervised learning and search. One exam question and one student's answer are reproduced below. Please grade the answer.

The exam question read as follows:

"Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorems are sometimes presented as a formal descendant of Hume's problem of induction: averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm can expect to outperform any other, which suggests that the success of any inductive method presupposes substantive assumptions that cannot themselves be justified inductively. A standard reply is that the theorems depend on an averaging measure — typically described as uniform over all target functions — that nobody actually believes in, and that in a world with structure, the average over all possible worlds is simply the wrong benchmark. First, reconstruct the skeptical argument from no-free-lunch to the conclusion that inductive success requires unjustified assumptions. Second, evaluate whether the standard reply defeats that argument, or merely relocates the assumptions."

The student's answer reads as follows:

"Wolpert's no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning says that if you average a learner's expected error over all possible target functions, then all learning algorithms perform identically. This holds whether we measure error in-sample or out-of-sample: no algorithm, however clever, can beat any other, including one that ignores the data entirely. The connection to Hume is straightforward. Hume argued that induction cannot be justified without assuming the uniformity of nature, and the no-free-lunch result can be read as a precise version of this: to get better-than-chance generalization, a learner must build in assumptions about which target functions are live possibilities, and no data can tell it which assumptions are correct, since the data are themselves just induction's output.

The standard reply is that the theorem holds only for the exactly uniform distribution over all target functions. Hume himself endorsed uniformity as a principle of human nature, so the skeptic is in the awkward position of relying on a measure — the uniform one — that is just as much an inductive assumption as any other. Once we replace the uniform measure with any non-uniform prior whatsoever, the theorem collapses, and some algorithms genuinely do better than others. So the reply seems to work: the no-free-lunch result is an artifact of an averaging measure chosen for mathematical convenience, not a fact about learning as such.

There is also a positive answer available on the other side. Reichenbach argued that if any method at all converges to the truth in the long run, then simple enumerative induction does as well. This shows that inductive methods dominate their rivals in the only sense that matters, which answers the no-free-lunch challenge directly.

However, the domination point only establishes long-run convergence, and the no-free-lunch theorem is about performance on finite samples. So the skeptic's residual claim survives: for any finite amount of evidence, some anti-inductive world is consistent with it, and the no-free-lunch theorem tells us we have no principled basis for discounting those worlds other than assumptions we cannot justify without circularity. On balance, then, the reply relocates rather than defeats the assumptions: uniformity over possible worlds turns out to be a substantive inductive commitment, and admitting a non-uniform prior is simply making that commitment explicit."
