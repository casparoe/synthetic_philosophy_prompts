I spent most of my working life as a biostatistician in clinical research — thirty years designing and analyzing diagnostic and prognostic studies, where calibration of a risk score was the bread and butter of my trade. A model predicting cardiovascular events that was well calibrated on one population but poorly calibrated on another was, in my world, simply a broken instrument. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science, and more recently the literature on algorithmic fairness, and I have arrived at a position that I would like stress-tested. It is this: the impossibility results of 2016–2017 — the ones usually associated with Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan on the one hand and Chouldechova on the other, and dramatized by the ProPublica dispute over the COMPAS recidivism instrument — establish that when the base rates of the outcome differ between groups, no classifier can simultaneously be calibrated within each group and have equal false positive and false negative rates across groups. From this I have drawn a fairly strong conclusion: that in any deployment where base rates differ, the demand for a "fair algorithm" in this sense is incoherent, that the choice among incompatible fairness criteria is a political or moral choice that mathematics cannot arbitrate, and that, going further, the entire project of formalizing fairness as properties of a scoring rule was probably misguided from the start — a category mistake dressed in set theory.

I would like you to take on the role of an expert on fairness in machine learning with a philosopher's suspicion of results that are cited more often than they are read. Please begin with some quiet brainstorming before answering: lay out the assumptions of the impossibility results themselves, because I suspect my confidence outstrips my grasp of exactly what is assumed. Then tell me what would count against my position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I am particularly interested in the following, though you should add whatever else belongs on the list: whether the theorems only rule out a particular pair of criteria (calibration plus error-rate balance) and leave other combinations — or other ways of individuating fairness criteria — untouched; whether calibration is even a fairness criterion at all rather than an epistemic one, so that the "trade-off" is between apples and something else; whether interventions that change the underlying risk distributions, rather than the scoring rule, dissolve the problem in a way the theorems say nothing about; and whether the escape routes (randomized decisions, group-conditional thresholds, refusing to deploy at all) are live or merely logical possibilities.

Finally, I want an honest assessment of how my position is insulated. The weaker claim — that this particular set of criteria cannot be jointly satisfied — is a theorem, so nothing can count against it; but my stronger claim, that formalizing fairness was misguided, seems to be the kind of view that can absorb any counterexample by reinterpreting it. Distinguish for me which parts of what I believe are genuinely at risk, which parts a committed defender of my view would concede count against it, and which parts I have rendered unfalsifiable by hedging — and whether, in a domain like this one, a measure of insulation from refutation is a defect or simply what it means to hold a normative view on mathematical scaffolding. I would rather be corrected than flattered, and I would particularly like to know whether my second career in philosophy has led me to overread a result that, in my first career, I would have classified as a modest constraint on instrument design.
