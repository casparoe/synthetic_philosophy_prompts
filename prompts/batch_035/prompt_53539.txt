I've been trying to work out exactly what Williams's one-thought-too-many observation establishes, because as I understand it, the conclusion seems far too strong. If a man who rescues his drowning wife rather than a stranger has had one thought too many when his reason for acting is that impartial morality permits him to favour her, then it looks like moral deliberation itself is corrosive, and the ideal agent would act on spontaneous affection without giving reasons at all. But surely deliberation isn't the problem — otherwise the point would condemn not just utilitarianism but any theory that asks agents to reflect before acting, including Kantianism and, presumably, Williams's own account of practical reasoning. Is the thought meant to be that deliberation is acceptable only before the fact, and never in the moment? More generally, I can't tell how this observation is supposed to connect to his integrity objection: does the integrity objection show that impartial theories make a certain kind of character impossible to have, or merely that they make such a character unjustifiable from the outside?
