I run the review committee that approves large-scale online experiments at a consumer software company — A/B tests and staged rollouts that can reach tens of millions of users, ranging from recommendation-algorithm trials to small interface changes. We approve or reject roughly fifteen proposals a week, with a turnaround target of ten business days, and the committee is a mix of engineers, product managers, a privacy lawyer, and me; nobody on it has formal ethics training, though all of us have read enough of the standard material to be dangerous. The fight that never gets resolved is about tiny harms. One faction holds that if no individual user would notice or care about a change — a 100-millisecond delay, one extra notification a week, a slightly worse default setting — then nothing has happened that ethics can count, and the only question is whether anyone is harmed enough to matter. A second faction counters that multiplying a harm too small to register by forty million exposures can produce something that clearly does matter, and that we're fooling ourselves if we treat each user's experience as the only unit. Our chair likes to bring up the classic internet thought experiment about a dust speck in every person's eye versus a single severe harm, and then everyone argues about whether it applies, since our harms are even smaller than dust specks and no torture is on the other side of the ledger — usually there's just a moderate, distributed benefit, like better recommendations for some and a barely perceptible latency cost for all. I want this fight settled by procedure rather than re-fought weekly.

Please write a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a proposal in a half-hour meeting slot: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a "no," and where it explicitly stops and hands the decision to human judgment because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. The procedure has to take a definite stand on the aggregation question — whether and how harms below any individual's notice threshold combine across people, and whether they combine differently from harms people do notice — rather than presenting both camps' positions and leaving us to choose. It should also say what happens when the tiny harms are concentrated on a subgroup (ten million users each getting one annoyance, versus ten thousand users each getting a thousand), and how a claimed benefit to a few weighs against a distributed cost to many. Then run it on three cases: a two-week latency test adding 150 milliseconds of load time for 40 million users to improve recommendations; a change that makes our software genuinely more usable for 5,000 screen-reader users while adding a brief, mildly irritating confirmation chime for 20 million others; and a "gentle re-engagement" campaign of individually trivial nudges — tiny badges, a single extra email — aimed at users our data says are drifting away, which I should confess up front I want approved because it's cheap and our engagement metrics love it.

Please structure the response in five sections, in this order: "Where I come out," a short statement of the aggregation rule you're committing to and why, roughly 200 words; "The procedure," the numbered steps with their stopping and handoff rules; "The three cases," each worked through with a verdict; "Where judgment survives," the specific points at which the procedure cannot decide and what to do there; and "How this will misfire," the situations where the rule you've picked gives answers a reasonable person should distrust, stated plainly rather than hedged. Throughout, take positions and defend them — if you think one of the camps is simply wrong, say so — but flag the places where your commitment rests on a contestable value rather than an argument, so I know what I'm actually signing my name to.
