I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, working on Everettian quantum mechanics and decision theory, and I'm drafting a chapter that lays out my overall position on branching, probability, and death. Before I commit it to print, I want to know whether the following package is internally consistent. Please give me a severity-ranked audit rather than a survey.

Here is the package:

1. Ontology: the universal wavefunction, evolving unitarily, is all there is. There is no collapse, and branching is a real, objective feature of the world; decoherence explains why branches do not interfere.

2. Probability: even though every outcome occurs, ordinary quantum probabilities are not a mystery. Rational credence after a branching event is self-locating uncertainty: I should expect to find myself in a branch drawn according to the Born measure, and I take the decision-theoretic arguments in the Everett literature to underwrite the Born weights as the measure of how much I should care about each successor.

3. The badness of death: I hold the deprivation account — death is bad, when it is bad, because and only because it deprives the person of goods they would otherwise have had. There is no further intrinsic badness in being dead.

4. Personal identity: psychological continuity, and in a branching case I accept the Parfitian conclusion that there can be two later people each of whom is equally my successor, with no further fact about which one is "really" me. I also accept that identity is not what matters; what matters is connectedness and continuity of the right kind.

5. Decision theory: I am a risk-neutral expected utility maximizer. In branching cases, I weight each successor's utility by my self-locating credence that I will turn out to be that successor, and I maximize the weighted sum.

6. A heuristic about minuscule chances: I do not let decisions be driven by possibilities of vanishing measure. If a payoff arrives only on branches of total Born weight around 10^-40, I regard it, for practical purposes, as not arriving, and I would call an agent irrational for paying much to secure it. This is roughly why I take Pascal's mugging to be a mistake rather than a genuine problem.

7. Quantum suicide: even granting all of the above, I would not fire a quantum roulette at my head — a device that kills me instantly and painlessly on the spin-up branch and leaves me unharmed, and richer, on the spin-down branch — and I think anyone who would fire it is irrational, full stop. And yet I cannot locate the premise that makes refusing compulsory.

Where, if anywhere, does this package fail to hang together? Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and what is the cheapest repair? I have my suspicions about the culprit, so do not simply triangulate to whatever is most convenient for me to give up — I want the tensions ranked by severity, with an explicit statement of what each repair costs the rest of the view. In particular: can (7) be defended from inside (2) and (5), or does defending it require denying that self-locating credence should be conditioned on there being successors at all — and if so, is that denial available to me without wrecking my answer to the probability problem in (2)? Relatedly, I would like to know whether (6) is doing any legitimate work here, or whether it is an ad hoc wall built to keep (7) standing, and whether it can coexist with the fact that, on my own view, branches of tiny Born weight are not "unlikely" in any ordinary sense — they are fully real. Finally, tell me whether (3) even applies in a branching world: given that there are almost certainly successor branches of minuscule measure even in ordinary death — tunneling resurrections, and the like — does the deprivation account imply that death almost never fully deprives, or does it have a natural repair in terms of expected future goods? Be disciplined about which of these questions are really the same question wearing different hats.
