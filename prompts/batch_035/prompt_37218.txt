For the final problem set in my contemporary political philosophy seminar, I have students extrapolate from Nozick's actual verdicts to cases he never lived to see. Here is the track record I give them, in paraphrase. Nozick refuses the experience machine, on the ground that we want to do things, be a certain kind of person, and stay in contact with reality — not merely have the experiences of doing so. He holds that one may not push a person in front of a runaway trolley to stop it and save five, since individuals may not be used up for aggregate gains even when the arithmetic favors it. He treats the utility monster as a reductio of maximizing aggregative views, and he accepts no positive duties of aid: constraints forbid mistreatment, they do not command rescue. His treatment of animals is more hedged but still has a direction. Killing animals for the pleasures of the palate, when nourishment is otherwise available, he finds very hard to justify; he suggests that animals have a good of their own, and he thinks we do not yet know how such questions are finally to be settled.

The new case: a firm has gene-edited a line of cattle that develop normally but lack any capacity for experience — no nociception, no valenced states, nothing it is like to be them — and proposes to raise and slaughter them industrially. Predict Nozick's verdict. Which rules fit the track record about as well as one another (a rule keyed to the quality of experiences, one keyed to beings with a good of their own, one about using or sacrificing), what does each predict here, which do you favor and why, and what further case would best tell those rules apart?
