I'm a retired physical chemist (instrumentation, mostly spectroscopy), now retired-retired and doing philosophy reading seriously for the first time, and the situationist critique of virtue ethics is the thing that grabbed me. Here's what bugs me. In my working life I sat on a lot of hiring committees, and the way we picked people was essentially: a one-hour interview plus three reference letters, and the letters and our questions were all about character — conscientious, meticulous under pressure, honest with data, collegial — as though these were stable dispositional properties like melting points you could look up once and rely on. Then I read the situationist literature (Milgram, the seminary students rushing past the slumped guy in the doorway, the old honesty studies where kids cheated or not depending on seemingly trivial features of the classroom) and apparently cross-situational consistency of behavior is way worse than common sense assumes. So here's my question: what theory of character does the whole letters-plus-interview hiring practice actually presuppose? Is it straight Aristotelian trait stability, or something weaker, or is it maybe coherent even under situationism — since the interview and the job are both situations, maybe the practice just needs within-situation predictive validity and doesn't care about traits at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, say what rival views it quietly rules out, and tell me what hiring would have to look like if you rebuilt it taking situationism seriously (structured situational tests? sampling many situations? something else?). And please be upfront about where your reconstruction is most likely to fail — what fact about how hiring actually works, or what argument, would push you to a different reading of what the practice presupposes. I'm new to this literature so dont assume I know the standard moves, but dont dumb it down either.
