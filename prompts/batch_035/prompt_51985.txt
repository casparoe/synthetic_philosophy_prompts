I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of science seminar in which we've just finished Lakatos on research programmes, and I also work part-time as a research assistant in a molecular biology lab, where I've helped my PI draft two small grant applications. The combination has started to bother me. The scoring criteria our funders use are things like novelty, significance, feasibility, and "preliminary data" — and I keep noticing that these pull in opposite directions when read through Lakatos. The panels say they want novel predictions, i.e. a theoretically progressive programme that anticipates facts nobody has seen yet; but they also demand a thick file of preliminary data showing the approach already works, which is precisely what a genuinely bold novel prediction wouldn't have — the strongest proposals are the ones that have already been half-confirmed. Meanwhile, at the renewal stage, reviewers are harsh on a lab whose past work consists of patching anomalies after they appear, adjusting auxiliaries to absorb each surprise — which sounds like a degenerating problem shift — and yet the same reviewers explicitly reward "responsiveness to previous critiques," which functionally means adding epicycles between rounds.

So my question is what philosophy of science the institution of grant peer review actually presupposes. Please reconstruct the implicit methodology from the practice itself: is the reviewing process committed to something genuinely Lakatosian — judging research programmes rather than isolated hypotheses, and weighting predicted novel facts over retrofitted accommodations — or to plain Popperian falsificationism, or is it only a bureaucratic risk-management apparatus that mimics methodological language while actually selecting for low variance in publication output? Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to be consistent with: could, say, a Kuhnian or a pure instrumentalist about confirmation license the very same scoring criteria, or do the criteria rule some of those out? And tell me whether the apparent tension between demanding novelty and demanding preliminary data reflects a defensible pluralism (funding both high-risk exploratory work and consolidation) or an incoherent mixture that no single philosophy of science could endorse. Finally, suppose a funding agency deliberately tried to build its review criteria around a strict Lakatosian standard — funding problemshifts rather than projects and scoring labs by whether their recent outputs anticipated facts rather than accommodated them. What concretely would have to change in how proposals are written, reviewed, and renewed, and what new failure mode would that system predictably produce that the current, mushier criteria somehow avoid?
