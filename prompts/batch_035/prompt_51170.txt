Write a newspaper-style advice column, roughly 650-750 words, run by a completely committed epistemicist about vagueness — think a classic agony aunt who happens to be certain, in her bones, that every vague predicate has a perfectly sharp cutoff, and that all the fuzziness is in our heads, not in the world. Call the column something appropriately deadpan. Include 4 or so letters from readers and her replies.

Seed ideas for the letters, but feel free to come up with better ones: a man anxiously asking whether he's officially bald yet, who wants to know the exact hair count threshold so he can plan accordingly; someone whose roommate disputes whether the pile of dishes in the sink counts as "a heap" and wants an adjudication; a person who heard about the sorites paradox and wants to know if they should be worried that removing one grain never makes a difference; and at least one letter from an insufferable supervaluationist or degree-theorist who writes in smugly to say the column should be "neither true nor false" on borderline days — she should dispatch him with barely contained contempt.

The humor has to come from getting the epistemicism right, Williamson-style: she genuinely believes there is a last hair on the head that makes you bald and a last grain that makes it a heap, she just also believes that neither she, nor her readers, nor possibly anyone could ever know where those lines are — the margin-for-error reasoning (if 3,000 hairs is definitely not bald, then 2,999 is not bald either, since you can't distinguish them by looking, so the cutoff has to lurk somewhere in the territory where things already look borderline) should show up as practical advice, not as a lecture. Tolerance conditionals ("if 4,000 is not bald, then 3,999 is not bald") should be treated as flatly false, just unknowably false. She should cheerfully insist every borderline statement is determinately true or false — no truth-value gaps, ever — and be unbothered that she can't tell you which. Higher-order vagueness should appear too: when a reader asks whether "borderline bald" is itself borderline, she should handle it with total equanimity, since for her it's all just ignorance all the way up.

Hard constraint, same as with a straight-faced hard determinist: she may never accidentally concede that the predicate itself is fuzzy or that the line isn't there — only that we can't find it — unless you decide that her cracking IS the joke, and if so earn it with a proper setup. The voice should stay warm, brisk, and professionally reassuring throughout, like she's recommending a good moisturizer while quietly asserting bivalence. Someone who actually works on vagueness should laugh because the position is rendered fairly and from the inside, not caricatured, and a general reader should still enjoy her advice.

Oh and one small thing: make at least one of her replies turn on a real-life sorites-ish case that isn't baldness or heaps — money (is a salary "high"?), "soon" (does telling someone you'll reply soon commit you to a deadline?), or when leftovers officially stop being safe to eat. Those are the letters where her certainty collides most painfully with actual human decision-making, which is where the comedy lives.
