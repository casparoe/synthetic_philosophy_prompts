I teach an advanced seminar on philosophy of mind, and last week we finished the unit on the knowledge argument and property dualism, closing with epiphenomenalism as the view that qualia are real, non-physical, and causally inert with respect to the physical domain. A student stopped me after class with an objection I've since been turning over: if epiphenomenalism is true, then my verbal reports about my experience are not caused by the experiences they are about, and yet those reports seem to be more than lucky guesses — which is the difficulty Chalmers treats in The Conscious Mind as the paradox of phenomenal judgment. My own working response has been to say that the paradox doesn't refute epiphenomenalism so much as expose an unexplained harmony in how our phenomenal states line up with our judgments about them, but I want to see how sturdy that really is.

Give me your credence that epiphenomenalism is true, stated precisely: that there exist phenomenal properties, that they are not identical to or realized by physical/functional properties, and that they have no causal influence on the physical. Then decompose it. I'd expect terms at least for the causal closure of the physical, for the claim that physical and functional facts fail to exhaust the phenomenal facts, for the claim that the failure of overdetermination or systematic psychophysical causal exclusion follows from the first two, and for the claim that some response to the paradox of phenomenal judgment succeeds — e.g. that the reliability of our phenomenal judgments is explained by their physical causes without the qualia themselves doing any causing, or that the harmony between qualia and judgments is a brute contingent fact we shouldn't have expected to be otherwise. Assign a rough probability to each term with one line of justification, show me how they combine, and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to.

For calibration, I hold the whole proposition at about 0.05, while my co-instructor — a physicalist who nonetheless thinks the conceivability arguments have never really been answered — holds something like 0.25. Tell me where you think our disagreement most likely lives: I suspect it is not in causal closure or even in the zombie argument itself, but in the price each of us puts on swallowing the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but I would like your honest read. And finally, name the single piece of evidence or argument — empirical or a priori — that would move your number the most, and in which direction.
