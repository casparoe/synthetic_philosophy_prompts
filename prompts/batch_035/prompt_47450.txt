I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist, a mid-list novelist named Marianne, is meant to be intelligently self-aware about her own bad habits while remaining entirely unable to correct them, and I want her pattern of behavior to be internally consistent, since attentive readers do notice these things. Her track record so far, established across earlier chapters: every Sunday she resolves to run each morning before work, runs on Monday, and by Wednesday has stopped; she buys an annual museum membership in a burst of January resolve and attends twice; a manuscript she promised herself she would finish by June has now sat unfinished for two years; yet when she promised a friend she would help him move on a specific date, she showed up severely hungover and worked all day; when her editor imposes a hard deadline, she works through the night and hits it; and when dessert is offered at a dinner party she declines it gracefully, then buys a slice of cake on the walk home and eats it alone in her kitchen.

In the chapter I'm now planning, her closest friend asks her to be her training partner for a marathon: three morning runs a week at half past five, for four months, starting tomorrow, the friend depending on her showing up each time. My question is what Marianne would do if her characterization is to stay consistent with what I've already written. My instinct is that there are at least three different rules she could be running, all of which fit her track record equally well: one on which she only ever breaks promises to herself and keeps anything involving another person who might be disappointed; one on which she succeeds at externally imposed, discrete tasks and fails at anything requiring a self-generated routine, regardless of who's involved; and one closer to the economists' picture of someone who always prefers the smaller-sooner reward to the larger-later one whenever the sooner reward is available right now — the bed versus the run — which leaves the marathon case genuinely uncertain, since the social rule and the routine rule give opposite predictions and the discounting picture depends on how she weighs the friend's disappointment each cold morning.

Could you lay out which rules genuinely fit the record, what each predicts for the marathon commitment, your best guess at the verdict with your reasons for preferring it, and — since I'd like to seed a later chapter with it — one further case involving Marianne that would cleanly tell the candidate rules apart? I'd also welcome your view on whether I've accidentally already written a scene that settles the matter, since the dessert-at-dinner-party business was intended only as a small comic touch but now strikes me as doing more work than I intended.
