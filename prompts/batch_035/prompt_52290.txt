I am a novelist, presently at work on a book set in a fourteenth-century Italian trading city, in which a young money-changer argues about interest with an older Dominican confessor. My Greek is nonexistent, and my background reading is uneven, so I have been leaning on secondary sources more than I would like. A popular history of money I picked up — one of those brisk, confident trade paperbacks — contains a paragraph on Aristotle that has shaped several drafts of the scene, and I want to know how much of it I can trust before I put any of its claims in a character's mouth. The book says, roughly, the following: that for Aristotle money was invented purely as a convenience for exchange, with no legitimate purpose beyond that; that trade between cities is natural but retail trade for profit is not; that money is barren by nature, which is why he found interest the most unnatural form of acquisition, since it makes money breed money as though money were a living thing; that a fair exchange, for Aristotle, is one in which the goods traded are of equal value; and that this doctrine, transmitted through Aquinas and the Scholastics, became the Church's ban on usury, and thereby held back European finance for centuries.

I would like you to take the role of a scholar of ancient and medieval philosophy who has spent a career on precisely these texts, and to audit the paragraph claim by claim. Please think carefully before you begin — perhaps first set out, in your own words, what Aristotle actually maintains about exchange, the household, wealth-getting, and money, and only then turn to the paragraph, marking each claim as accurate, as a defensible simplification, or as an outright misreading. I am especially unsure about two points: whether the "equal value" characterization of fair exchange is something Aristotle really holds or a common misreading, and whether the causal story about the usury ban is anything like fair, given that prohibitions on lending at interest predate any medieval acquaintance with Aristotle. Where the book's claim would make my Dominican say something an informed Aristotelian of his century could not or would not say, please tell me, since anachronism is the one error a reviewer is certain to catch. I would also be glad if you closed with a judgment on which of these claims are safe enough to give a character in dialogue and which are the author ventriloquizing a modern argument.

One request about form: please write your reply as continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I read best that way, and I intend to keep your answer beside me while I revise.
