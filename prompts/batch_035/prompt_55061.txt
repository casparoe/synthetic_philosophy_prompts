My oldest running argument from my LessWrong meetup days has finally crystallized into a request. A friend I've debated for years keeps deploying the line that there is no morally significant difference between killing someone and letting them die, and what strikes me is that he uses it to push in opposite directions depending on the occasion. When we argue about AI or about donation, he uses it upward: a system that could cheaply avert a death and refrains is, morally, a killer — an AI that watches people drown in a fire it could have extinguished is no better than an AI that lit the fire. When we argue about end-of-life care, he uses it downward: if withdrawing a ventilator is only letting someone die, and letting die is no worse than killing, then a doctor who withdraws treatment hasn't done anything worse than one who administers a fatal dose, so withdrawal inherits whatever permissions killing would have. Same sentence, opposite work. I'm sure the sentence is packaging several distinct theses, some trivial and some radical, and I'd like the packaging opened.

So: what are the different precise claims this one-liner could actually be making, laid out as a list I can bring to our next argument? I can see fragments of the menu myself — maybe there's a merely logical reading on which some killings and some lettings-die are equally bad, which seems like a platitude; a causal reading on which both the fire-lighter and the idle AI equally make the difference between life and death, so the causal structure is shared; a reading about wrongness being fixed entirely by outcomes, costs, and intentions once those are held fixed; and something more radical on which the distinction is not just weightless but somehow empty, every refraining already a doing. But I want each reading stated as an actual principle, with a verdict on whether it's plausible, whether it's doing real work or restating a definition, and — this is the part I most need — whether it supports my friend's upward use, his downward use, both, or neither. It's fine with me if the honest answer is that no single reading supports both his uses, since that would suggest he's been equivocating for a decade, which I would enjoy telling him.

A test case I keep returning to, and would like your readings to adjudicate: the standard bathtub pair, where in one case I push my cousin in and in the other I watch him slip in and merely fail to rescue, everything else matched. I think the second is bad but not murder, and I think my friend's upward AI case is importantly different from the bathtub one — the AI had an antecedent job and trivial cost, while the bystander didn't. If one of your readings explains why those verdicts of mine might be consistent, flag it; if they can't all be saved, say which one to give up. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question back at me.
