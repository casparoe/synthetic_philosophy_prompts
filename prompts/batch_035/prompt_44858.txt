I've been in a reading group going through the Twin Earth literature for almost two years now and the whole debate feels completley frozen, so I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another runthrough of the arguments.

Here's the lay of the land as we've experienced it. On one side, the externalists: Putnam's Oscar and Twin Oscar who are molecule-for-molecule identical but whose word "water" picks out different stuff, Burge's arthritis guy, the general moral that meaning "ain't in the head." On the other side, the internalists or at least the people who insist there has to be narrow content -- the Fodor line that cognitive science explains behavior by what's in your head, and if Oscar and Twin Oscar are internally identical but their beliefs have different contents, then content can't be the thing psychology traffics in, since the same psychological story has to cover both of them. Every few meetings someone brings up slow switching (you get quietly moved between Earth and Twin Earth over years without ever noticing anything) and the worry that externalism threatens privileged access to your own thoughts, and then everyone talks past each other for an hour and we go home. There's also the frog stuff -- frogs snapping at BBs they can't distinguish from flies, or two internally identical frogs in environments with different little speck-things, and whether they count as believing the same thing -- which never seems to settle anything either.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are just doing different jobs under one label: externalists are answering a question about reference (what fixes what our words latch onto in the world) and internalists are answering a question about psychological explanation (what states can do causal work inside a head), and "content" is being used for both, which manufactures the appearance of a single dispute. But I worry that's too easy, because the two-factor people already tried to split the difference by saying every state has both wide and narrow content, and if the dispute were merely verbal that move should have ended it -- instead both sides complained the two-factor view was unconstrained hand-waving (narrow content that nothing fixes, or wide content that explains nothing).

So tell me: is this verbal, substantive, two questions smuggled under one name, or something else -- maybe a clash about which cases count as the data (duplicates vs. linguistic communities)? Use what each side actually claims to support your diagnosis. And then be honest about what progress would even look like here: a thought experiment, something from cognitive science, a termininalogical reform, or is this just bedrock intuitions about whether meaning is a property of individuals or of people-in-environments that nobody can argue their way out of? If it's bedrock, I'd like to hear why the debate keeps producing papers instead of just stalemate.
