I sit on the board of a regional astronomy society, and at our last meeting we took a preliminary vote on whether to co-sponsor a public METI event — a live broadcast of a structured message toward several nearby Sun-like stars, organized by a local university group. The vote split 3–3 and we tabled it pending a written summary of the arguments, which I've been asked to draft. The real dispute was between two other board members, and I'd like a careful adjudication before I put my name on anything.

Marcus opposed co-sponsorship on dark forest grounds: if civilizations cannot verify each other's intentions, communication is slow, and a first strike is cheap relative to the cost of being wrong, then the dominant strategy for any rational civilization is silence, and our deliberately beaming a message is like striking a match to see whether the room is full of gunpowder.

Elena replied that the cat is already out of the bag: we've been leaking radar pulses and television carriers for a century, so any hunter capable of harming us already knows exactly where we are, and one more transmission cannot make us more detectable than we already are.

Marcus answered that this is just false as physics — leakage follows an inverse-square law and falls below the noise floor of any plausible receiver within a few light-years, whereas a high-gain transmission aimed at a specific star is a fundamentally different kind of beacon, so deliberate messaging exposes us to a threat that our leakage does not.

Elena conceded the point about radio but said it proves too little: Earth's atmosphere has carried an oxygen biosignature detectable by spectroscopy for hundreds of millions of years, so any hunter who kills on detecting life would have sterilized us long ago. Our continued existence, she argued, is itself evidence about what kind of forest, if any, we're in — and it isn't the dark one.

Marcus's final move: biosignatures and technosignatures are strategically different. Striking a planet for hosting microbes gains a predator nothing and costs something, so the dark forest threat applies only to technological competitors — meaning our survival despite a billion years of detectable life is no evidence against the hypothesis. Worse, he said, the very silence of the sky — no visible expansion, no leakage from anyone else — is precisely what the dark forest predicts, so Elena's "evidence" is actually his.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of science with expertise in reasoning under radical uncertainty about extraterrestrial intelligence, and think carefully before answering — I'd suggest you first brainstorm the logical structure of the disagreement (which claims are empirical, which are strategic, and which depend on unstated assumptions about hunter motivations), then adjudicate each of the five moves in turn, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification. Then tell me: does Marcus's conclusion — opposing the co-sponsorship — actually follow from his strongest surviving argument, or does the dark forest logic support at most a weaker position? And is there something both of them are missing that reframes the whole exchange?
