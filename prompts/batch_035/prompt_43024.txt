I'm preparing a position statement on material constitution for a metaphysics workshop and would like a consistency audit of my package before I circulate it. The case I use to fix ideas throughout: a lump of clay has existed since Monday; on Tuesday it is shaped into a small statuette; the statuette sits on a plinth until Thursday, when the whole thing is squashed flat. My verdict is that the clay survives the squashing and the statuette ceases to exist, and I take this verdict to be literally true rather than a shorthand for something else. The package:

(1) Realism about ordinary artifacts: there really are statues, and I refuse any reading of my view on which statue-talk is a mere manner of speaking.

(2) Numerical distinctness with spatiotemporal coincidence: the statuette and the clay that constitutes it are two objects, occupying exactly the same region for exactly as long as they both exist, and the difference in persistence conditions — the clay can survive squashing, the statuette cannot — is my main reason for saying there are two objects there rather than one.

(3) Complete qualitative indiscernibility: apart from the modal facts mentioned in (2), the two objects are alike in every qualitative respect I can find — same shape, mass, color, and microstructure, same origin and history, same causal role, same location at every moment. If there is a qualitative difference between them, I cannot locate it, and I am inclined to think there is none. I do not know what to say about whether "being a statuette" and "being a piece of clay" count as qualitative properties, and I'd be grateful if you didn't gloss over that question.

(4) Grounded persistence: a thing's persistence conditions are not primitive facts about it; they supervene on its qualitative character, most plausibly on its kind membership. I have no sympathy for haecceitism about persistence — it is not an irreducible fact about a given object, over and above what it is like, that it can survive this but not that.

(5) Leibniz's Law, understood as the principle that if x is identical to y, then x and y share every property, is a necessary truth governing identity.

(6) The identity of indiscernibles is false: distinct objects can, in principle, share all of their qualitative properties.

(7) Restricted composition: most pluralities compose nothing. There is no object made of my nose and the Eiffel Tower, no truncated-statuette object covering the top half of the statuette plus a corner of the plinth. Composition occurs only when matter is organized into a genuine kind. And the number of objects in the statuette-region is exactly two — the statuette and the clay — with no vagueness in this count and no third object for each further non-arbitrary sortal the region happens to satisfy.

(8) Endurance: objects are wholly present at every time they exist; I do not believe in temporal parts, so any repair to the above cannot go through four-dimensionalism.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and why the merely apparent ones dissolve. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging. Then take a definite position: identify the single revision that would restore coherence at least cost, describe what the revised package looks like, and say explicitly what the revision costs me elsewhere — for instance, if the revision touches (4), what happens to my confidence in the persistence verdict in (2); if it touches (7), whether I should then accept that the region contains vastly more than two objects. If you think the whole package is in fact consistent, say so and defend that verdict against the strongest objections you can construct; do not manufacture tension for the sake of thoroughness. I would rather have a committed audit with reasons I can push back on than a neutral survey of options.
