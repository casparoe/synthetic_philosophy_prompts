A friend and I spent most of Saturday arguing about whether numbers really exist. He's a mathematics student, and he maintains that when mathematicians prove theorems about prime numbers they are discovering something that was there all along, independent of us; I said that's a figure of speech — primes are a pattern we've learned to describe, and 'existing' outside of any human practice of description is something nobody has ever managed to make intelligible. After about three hours he shut the discussion down by saying that we were just using the word 'exist' in different senses, that the whole thing was 'purely semantic', and that we could each keep our own sense of the word and be done with it.

The dismissal bothered me more than the original argument did. On the one hand it sounds mature and even a little wise: we weren't going to settle the question by legislating usage, and neither of us was going to convert the other. On the other hand, it felt like a way of not having lost. If our dispute was merely about a word, why did each of us feel there was something at stake — something to be right or wrong about? And it can't be that every dispute which turns on how some word applies is merely verbal: last term we had a serious argument about whether his taking my charger without asking counted as stealing, which is also a question about the application of a word, and neither of us would have accepted that it was 'just semantics'. I've gathered that philosophers have a whole apparatus here — a distinction between 'verbal' and 'substantive' disputes, and a standing fight over whether all of ontology reduces to the verbal kind — but I don't know how any of that goes beyond what my friend said off the cuff.

Please lay out for me the distinct precise claims 'it's just semantics' could be making in a case like ours — from the weakest reading, on which we simply attach different strings to a state of affairs we already agree on, through middling readings on which there is a substantive question that our verbal disagreement is distracting us from, up to the strongest readings, on which there was never any real question once the linguistic one is settled. For each reading, say whether it's plausible as applied to our disagreement about numbers, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it actually entitles him to close the argument as he did, with each of us keeping our own sense of 'exist' and no one owing the other anything. Please present the readings as a numbered list I can send him.
