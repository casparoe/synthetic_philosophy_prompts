I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about the absent-minded driver, and I'd like to know whether my verdicts on a family of cases add up to a principle. Setup, in case the details matter: a driver passes two identical intersections, and because he is absent-minded he never knows which one he is at when he is at one. Exiting at the first intersection gets him nothing; exiting at the second gets him 4; passing both gets him 1. My verdicts:

1. Before setting out, the driver would be rational to pay up to about 1 for a device that takes over and simply continues through both intersections. Obvious.
2. At an intersection, with no device and no prior plan, my gut says continue — though I can feel the pull of exiting while he might be at the second one.
3. If before setting out he can buy a private chance device that at each intersection independently instructs him to exit with probability exactly 1/3, buying it and obeying it is rational; I've checked that the expected payoff is 4/3, which beats every fixed plan.
4. Yet in Parfit's hitchhiker — deposited safely in the city after being carried across the desert, now deciding whether to pay the driver as promised — I think flipping a coin between paying and stiffing is worthless: he should just pay. So a private die is worth real money at the intersections and nothing at all there.
5. Twin case: two drivers leave the same inn an hour apart on identical roads, and neither knows whether she is the earlier or the later one. Each faces a single exit. If the earlier driver exits, both get nothing; if the earlier continues and the later exits, both get 4; if both continue, both get 1. Here my gut says exit, on the grounds that my driving straight doesn't make the other driver drive straight — it merely correlates with it. I cannot see why this case should come out differently from (2), but for me it does.
6. The one I keep going back and forth on: before setting out, the driver can sign an enforceable contract imposing a fine of 2 if he exits at the first intersection, for a small administrative fee. With the fine in place, continuing at an intersection looks right even on the cautious, don't-count-your-influence-on-other-decisions style of reasoning, so the contract would get his at-the-wheel self to do the right thing without any exotic arguments. Part of me says signing is obviously rational; part of me says a rational agent shouldn't have to pay money to get itself to do what it already wanted, at planning time, to do.

Is there a single decision principle that captures all six verdicts — perhaps something about what you would have endorsed at planning time, or about which other decisions count as sufficiently yours for the purposes of correlation — and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why those? I would also like the principle's verdict on a case I haven't listed: suppose that before setting out the driver can buy a pill that restores perfect memory, so that at the second intersection he will know it is the second. I assume he should pay up to 3 for it, but I'd like to know whether your principle delivers that comfortably or only with visible strain, and what else it predicts in the neighborhood — for instance, whether the answer to (5) should really differ from the answer to (2), and whether randomization should help in any case where the correlated decisions are made by distinct people rather than by one forgetful agent.
