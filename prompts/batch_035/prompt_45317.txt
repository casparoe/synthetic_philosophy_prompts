I'm a retired physicist and lately I've been trying to catch up on the philosophy I skipped during my working life. Wigner's 1960 essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences finally caught up with me at a department reunion, and a former colleague of mine (she went into biostatistics) says the puzzle it raises is fake. Here's her argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Wigner's wonder is that mathematics developed with no regard for physics turns out to describe physics with absurd accuracy -- think of Riemannian geometry sitting around for decades before general relativity picked it up.
(2) But all the evidence for this "absurd accuracy" comes from successful physical theories: theories formulated in mathematics whose predictions match experiment, sometimes to many decimal places.
(3) The theories we actually have are the output of selection: physicists generate candidate theories and discard the ones that don't match, keeping the ones that do.
(4) That survivors of a selection for fit actually fit is a tautology, so it's guaranteed that our surviving theories match experiment.
(5) Every surviving theory is formulated in some piece of mathematics or other.
(6) So for the mathematics that actually shows up in physics, its "effectiveness" is guaranteed by selection, and it's no more mysterious than the fit between a glove and the hand that kept trying on gloves until one fit.
(7) Therefore there was never anything to wonder about -- we would only ever have noticed the mathematics that works, and Wigner was amazed that his keys were where he found them.

She goes further: the same logic, she says, dissolves the question of why nature seems to obey simple, elegant laws at all -- we kept the simple ones and threw out the ugly ones, end of story. That last move is where my stomach drops, because it seems to prove too much, but each step up to (6) looked fine to me when I nodded along at the reunion. Which step or move do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I'd much rather keep (4), the selection point -- it's the one part of this I actually understand from my own field.
