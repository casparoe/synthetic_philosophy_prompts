I'm teaching a sophomore seminar called Ideas of Modernity, 18 students, one 75-minute session. They've just finished a unit with Kant's 1784 essay on enlightenment, some Condorcet-style optimism about progress, and a couple of secondary readings on how the Enlightenment sat alongside empire and slavery. For the capstone I want to stage a trial where "the Enlightenment" itself is the defendant, charged with something like laying the intellectual groundwork for domination. But I don't want a generic pro/con debate — the thing I want them to run into themselves is that almost every good prosecution argument ends up borrowing the defendant's own weapons (equality, universal rights, autonomy, critique as a virtue). So here's the structure I'm imagining, and I want you to build it out into something I can actually run.

The class splits into a defense team, a judge, and two seperate prosecution teams. Prosecution A is under a gag rule: they can't appeal to any value the Enlightenment itself introduced or sharpened — no human rights, no equality, no autonomy, no appeal to "thinking for yourself", nothing post-1700ish in spirit. The judge polices this: whenever prosecution A smuggles in an Enlightenment value, opposing counsel can object on grounds of, I dunno, anachronism, and the judge sustains it. Prosecution B has no such restriction and can go full "you betrayed your own ideals" — hypocrisy charges allowed. Then a jury (drawn from whoever's left, or rotating) has to do two things in order: first say which prosecution made the stronger case and why, and only then deliver an actual verdict on guilt.

Give me the full package: role assignments for all 18 people, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, a drafted charge sheet with 2-3 counts, how to brief each team privately without the gag-rule team catching on to the point of the gag rule beforehand, how to structure the jury deliberation so the two-step order actually matters, and the debrief — specifically which moments to map onto what, like what it means if prosecution B was more persuasive, or if prosecution A just couldn't build a case at all and kept getting their arguments struck. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm bracing for the gag rule to be either unenforceable or to dominate the whole session, which one is it?), and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself — for instance whether the trial format quietly presupposes the defendant is a unified agent, and what that does to the actual historiography. End your whole answer with a final line starting "Bottom line:" — one sentence on what this teaches that a lecture on the same readings couldnt.
