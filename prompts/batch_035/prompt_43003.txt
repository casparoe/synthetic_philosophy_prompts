I teach behavioral decision-making to master's students, and every year I demonstrate the Allais preferences on myself, which means every year I get to watch myself violate the independence axiom and then immediately disapprove of the violation. This term I started cataloguing my choices more carefully than usual, and my verdicts have sprawled well beyond the standard pair, so I want to know whether they add up to any coherent principle or whether some of them simply have to go. The classics first: given $1M for certain versus a gamble paying $5M with probability .10, $1M with probability .89, and nothing with probability .01, I take the sure million; given a gamble paying $1M with probability .11 and nothing with probability .89 versus a gamble paying $5M with probability .10 and nothing with probability .90, I take the second. Now the sprawl. Scale: if I replace the payoffs with $10 and $50, I take the gamble in the first pair too, and I don't think this makes me inconsistent — stakes genuinely matter to me, and I'm fairly comfortable with that. Re-description: when a colleague made me choose between payoff schedules over 100 numbered tickets drawn from a single urn — schedule A paying $1M on every ticket, schedule B paying $1M on tickets 1 through 89, $5M on tickets 90 through 99, and nothing on ticket 100, with the analogous second pair — I chose the $5M-containing schedule both times, felt the force of the consistency argument, and then a week later, back in the ordinary framing, the preference for the sure thing returned and felt just as strong. Menus: choosing between just two candidate adjustments to my retirement portfolio, I prefer plan P to plan Q; but when a third, transparently dominated plan is placed on the menu beside them, I notice myself drifting toward Q, and while I can't defend it, I also don't experience it afterward as a blunder the way I'm told I should.

My tentative principle is something like this: the value of an option depends partly on how its outcome in each state compares with the outcome of the forgone option in that same state — a regret or comparison component — and this component only overrides expected value when the comparison is certain, i.e., when one option delivers the same thing in every state you might end up in. That is supposed to explain why the certainty case feels different from the common-ratio-style cases where I behave like a good expected-utility maximizer, and why the certainty premium shrinks when the stakes do. What it clearly cannot explain is the menu case, where nothing is certain and no one state is doing the work; there my preference seems to depend on the mere presence of an option I have no intention of choosing. I could bolt on a clause about options being evaluated relative to the menu, but then I worry the principle has become a laundry list rather than a principle.

Please structure your answer in sections, in this order and roughly these lengths. First, "My verdicts, restated" — a short formal statement of what exactly I've committed to, in your words, cleaned up into choice-theoretic vocabulary (under half a page). Second, "Candidate principles" — two or at most three principles, each stated precisely, with a sentence saying which of my verdicts motivates each clause and what the clause costs in simplicity. Third, "Predictions" — what your best candidate says about cases I haven't listed, for instance: the Allais structure where the common consequence is positively correlated with the risky part of the gamble rather than independent of it; the case where the sure option is made certain by an insurance contract I've paid for rather than by the structure of the gamble itself; and the case where the 1% chance of nothing resolves a week before the rest of the gamble. Fourth, "Verdicts to overturn" — an honest statement of which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, why you'd overturn those rather than complicate the principle further, and how I might test whether the overturning would actually stick for me psychologically rather than just on paper. Fifth, "Where I'm guessing" — where you suspect I've misdescribed my own preferences, in the sense that if you put the actual choices in front of me my behavior wouldn't match this self-report, and how you'd design that check. Don't reconstruct the literature for me or worry about who first proposed what; I want the principle-hunting done on my cases alone.
