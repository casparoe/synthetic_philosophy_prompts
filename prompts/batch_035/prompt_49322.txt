I'm grading a take-home midterm for an upper-level aesthetics course and would like a second opinion. The question was:

"State the paradox of tragedy as David Hume formulates it in his essay 'Of Tragedy' (1757). Explain Hume's own solution to the paradox. Then present Susan Feagin's meta-response solution from 'The Pleasures of Tragedy' (1983), and assess whether Feagin's account succeeds where Hume's fails."

One student submitted the following answer:

"Hume points out a strange fact about tragedy: audiences genuinely feel sorrow, terror, and anxiety at the events depicted, yet they seek out tragedies and enjoy them, and the stronger the distress, the stronger the pleasure. Since we normally avoid what causes us pain, this looks irrational or at least in need of explanation. That is the paradox.

Hume's solution rests on the fact that we know the events are fiction. Because we never fully believe that the events are real, our sorrow never becomes fully aversive; it is softened as we watch, and what we are left with is the pleasure of the spectacle. The eloquence of the writing does the rest of the work, keeping us engaged.

Feagin's solution is different. She distinguishes two levels of response. At the first level, we have unpleasant direct responses to the depicted suffering. At the second level, we take pleasure in finding ourselves responding that way -- we are pleased to discover that we are the kind of people who are pained by human suffering and injustice, since this reflects moral capacities we approve of. So the pleasure is at one remove from the distress, and there is nothing puzzling left: no one is enjoying suffering as such.

Feagin's account is clearly superior. Hume's has the embarrassing consequence that the more distressed you are, the more you enjoy yourself, which his fictional-belief story can't explain. Feagin, by contrast, explains the covariation perfectly: the more the tragedy moves you, the more evidence you have that you are responding well, and the more your meta-level pleasure increases. Her view is essentially Aristotle's, in modern dress -- the pleasure comes from the release after the buildup -- which shows how durable the idea is."

Please assign this answer a letter grade and justify it with specific comments on each of the substantive claims in the answer: identify what it gets right, where it goes wrong, and how the errors should affect the grade. I'd like the point-by-point assessment to be usable as feedback to the student.
