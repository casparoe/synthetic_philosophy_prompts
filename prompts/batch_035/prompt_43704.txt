My two labmates, Maya and Jonas, have been arguing about the same thing for two years now and honestly everyone else in the reading group is tired of it. Maya is basically a global workspace person: she thinks the workspace architecture (the broadcast that makes content globally available for report, memory, reasoning, planning) doesn't just give you access to your experiences, it more or less IS your experience, so whatever you can report on is the measure of what you were conscious of. Jonas is more of a Block-style overflow guy: he thinks phenomenal consciousness can be richer than what you can report or use, and that the workspace explains reportability and access but conspicuously dodges the hard question of why broadcast feels like anything at all. Their favorite fight is Sperling's partial report thing — you flash 12 letters, people say they saw them all but can only name 3-4, until you cue one row after the stimulus and they get that row right. Maya says that's just fragile iconic memory being read off after the fact, plus maybe some postdiction reconstructing a "remembered present," so nothing overflowed consciousness at all. Jonas says the subjects aren't inferring, they're reporting on an experience that genuinely had all the letters in it and that the cued row just pulled a subset into the workspace. They've also started calling each other names: Maya says Jonas is a "Panglossian" who assumes whatever gets represented gets experienced, and Jonas says she's a "killjoy" who keeps deflating genuine phenomenology into memory and inference stories. That part might be verbal but I can't tell.

They finally agreed to stop rehashing this at group meeting and actually do the thing people talk about doing: a written adversarial collaboration. I've been asked to draft the joint statement they'd both sign, and I want it done right, so here's what I need. First, a numbered section of claims both could actually put their name to — about the empirical base neither disputes (partial report, iconic memory decaying in a few hundred milliseconds, change blindness and inattentional blindness being robust, no-report designs like opt-out and delayed-report paradigms existing and being usable on humans and animals, etc.) and about what the workspace machinery demonstrably does, phrased neutrally. Then a disagreements table where each row states what Maya holds and what Jonas holds in wording each of them would accept — not strawmen, and not mushy both-sidesism either. I want their real positions in their own best light, including the fact that Maya has a deflationary story about the "all the letters" feeling and Jonas has to explain why the overflowed content is so useless if it was really experienced.

Then the cruxes, and this is the part I care most about. I suspect at least one crux is whether report-based methods can even in principle measure phenomenology, or whether the act of reporting is what lights up the workspace and thus contaminates the measurement — if it's the latter, the whole empirical literature might be begging Jonas's question, but if it's the former, Maya's position gets to say the burden of proof is on him. Another one might be whether the no-report paradigms (say, a delayed symbolic-comparison design where subjects never report the uncued material but their later forced choices reveal what they took in) can dissociate experience from access. For each crux, spell out what would count as evidence either way, and write down each of their predictions in advance so they can't wiggle out later — e.g., if sensitivity to uncued items shows up without any report requirement, what does each of them expect and why. And finally, at the end, tell me honestly: is any portion of their dispute just verbal, like they're using "conscious" for different things (reportable access vs. something like phenomenal character that survives the loss of every capacity we can measure)? If so, say which part and how much of the fight survives once you drain the wording out. Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then cruxes with each side's predictions, then a short verdict on how much of it is words vs. substance. Please dont soften it into "both sides make interesting points" — they'll each read it looking for any sentence the other sneaked a conclusion past them in.
