I'm a physics graduate student taking a philosophy of physics elective, mostly out of curiosity, and my study group has landed me in an argument I can't climb out of. A friend who is a committed four-dimensionalist presented what he calls a "clean" route from special relativity to the block universe, but it seems to establish far more than the block universe along the way, and I can't find which rung of the ladder is rotten. His argument runs as follows. (1) Simultaneity is frame-relative: two observers moving relative to each other, even ones passing within arm's reach, disagree about which distant events belong to "now" — and the disagreement can amount to days for events in a far-off galaxy. (2) No frame is privileged; each observer's slicing of spacetime into past, present, and future carves the same spacetime equally legitimately. (3) What lies in any observer's past, or on her present slice, is already settled for that observer — done, not open, not about-to-be-decided. (4) Settledness cannot itself be frame-relative: if an event is settled relative to one legitimate frame, it is settled, period, for settledness is a fact about the event, not about whoever's clock we consult. (5) Given any event in my future — the coin I will toss tomorrow, say — we can construct a chain of relatively moving observers such that the event is past or present for the first observer in the chain, each successive observer's "now" overlaps appropriately with the last, and the chain terminates in a slice containing me, here, mid-deliberation. (6) So by (4), tomorrow's toss is settled now, and so is every future event, including every choice I will ever make. (7) Therefore the future is exactly as closed as the past; there is no ontological difference between them, and nothing I do tomorrow could go otherwise.

Here is what bothers me. Stated as a conclusion about ontology — future events tenselessly exist — I could live with (7); I rather like the block universe. But the argument seems to arrive somewhere stronger: that the future is not merely there but necessitated, that my coin could not go otherwise, and (worse) that this follows from special relativity alone. Take a radium atom whose decay, on any standard reading of quantum mechanics, is not necessitated by anything: run the argument on the event of its decaying tomorrow, and relativity's pure geometry — a theory of rods, clocks, and light signals with no dynamics in it at all — has apparently settled a question that the complete physical state of the world, on the quantum picture, does not settle. It looks as though the argument proves that spacetime structure by itself settles matters of chance, which seems to prove too much, and I suspect there is an equivocation hiding somewhere between "settled" in step (3) and "settled" in step (4). But when I say this, my friend just asks me which observer's past is unreal, and I don't have an answer.

Tell me which premise or inference to reject, and be explicit about what rejecting it costs. If the target is (4), I want to know exactly how "settled for her" fails to transmit through the chain without wrecking the ordinary idea that the past is fixed — that transmission is what looked innocuous. If the honest move is to reject (2) and admit a metaphysically privileged foliation, I want to know what that does to the relativistic picture beyond making it look Lorentzian again. And if the right answer is to accept (7) and distinguish existing-tenselessly from being necessitated, I want to see that distinction made carefully enough that objective single-case chance survives it, because I would like to keep all three: no privileged frames, the block, and a radium atom whose decay is genuinely up in the air. Please also say why the premise I should give up looked fine in the first place — I want to understand what made the argument seductive, not just where it leaks.
