I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly climate and food systems stuff, and I've been going around telling readers that their individual choices matter — eat less chicken, fly less, vote. After an interview last month the economist I was talking to basically told me I'm misleading people, and laid out an argument that felt airtight to me right up until I noticed it proves way too much. Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in collective action and moral responsibility, take a moment to brainstorm and consider different lines of attack before committing, and then tell me exactly which step of the argument to reject and what rejecting it costs. I want an actual diagnosis, not slogans like "every drop counts."

Here's the argument as he gave it to me:

(1) An act can be wrong only if it makes the world worse than the alternative would have. No difference, no wrong.
(2) "Makes the world worse" means things actually go worse because you did it — not that they might have, or that they go worse in expectation.
(3) Industrial chicken production responds to demand in steps: farms raise birds in batches, and whether one extra grocery purchase is the straw that tips a batch is a matter of tiny probability. In the overwhelming majority of cases, your chicken dinner changes nothing downstream — the same birds get raised either way.
(4) So the overwhelming majority of chicken purchases make the world no worse.
(5) By parity of reasoning, the same goes for a single flight, a single day of driving, and a single vote in a national election.
(6) So the overwhelming majority of these acts are not wrong — and it's a mistake, not just an exaggeration, for me to tell readers they're doing something wrong each time they grab the chicken instead of the tofu.
(7) And when I raised the obvious objection — that there's still a tiny chance your purchase is the one that tips a batch — he waved it off: expected value is a tool for choosing between bets, not a standard for what makes an act wrong. If tiny-probability harm were enough for wrongness, then jaywalking with a one-in-a-million chance of triggering a fatal pile-up would be seriously wrong, which he says nobody actually believes.

Here's what keeps me up at night. If (4) is right, it's right for everyone, and it's right week after week. But the total effect of a million households each adding one chicken dinner a week is obviously more chickens raised and slaughterd. The world is worse because of what those million people did. But if nothing anyone did made the world worse, where did the extra dead chickens come from? Either the aggregate effect is somehow built out of a million individual "no differences," which seems impossible, or some of those purchases did make the world worse after all — in which case the argument's confidence about which ones matter is fake, because nobody can point to the ones that did it.

So, which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd like to keep (1) if I can — it's basically what I tell readers when they ask whether they're personally guilty just for owning a car. And separately, please tell me whether the jaywalking move in (7) is a good argument or whether it's smuggling something in. My instinct says it's connected to the real flaw somewhere but I can't articulate how. Take your time and be rigorous with me, I'm going to have to defend whatever I write in my next column.
