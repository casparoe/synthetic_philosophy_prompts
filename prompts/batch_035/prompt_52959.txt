I've been writing up my position on consequentialism and demandingness for a workshop and would like a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is the package: (1) Act consequentialism is true: an act is wrong iff some alternative available to the agent would have produced better consequences. (2) I think the demandingness objection fails as an objection to consequentialism — that the theory asks a great deal of us reflects badly on us, not on the theory. (3) I nonetheless believe it is permissible for me to spend my evenings practicing the viola and my savings on a trip to see my sister rather than giving both away, and I do not think I do anything wrong in this. (4) I think Scheffler-style agent-centered prerogatives are hopeless, because any permission to depart from the best available act needs a principled cutoff, and every cutoff I have seen looks ad hoc. (5) I accept the demanding verdicts in emergencies — if I can rescue a child from a shallow pond at modest cost to myself, I must. (6) I think a moral theory's job is to guide ordinary agents like me, and a theory that no human could live by fails as a theory of what we ought to do. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest repair? Please structure your reply as follows: "Overview" (one short paragraph); "Real tensions, ranked by severity" (for each, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me); "Merely apparent tensions" (explain briefly why each dissolves); and "Recommended repair" (one paragraph defending the single least-costly revision).
