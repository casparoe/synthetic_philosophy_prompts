My friend Maya and I run a small reading group on labor history, and over the years she has developed very firm instincts about which forms of collective action are legitimate and which are not. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: an authorized wage strike called after a genuine bargaining impasse, legitimate; a wildcat walkout over a machine that had just seriously injured a coworker, called with no vote at all, legitimate; a secondary boycott in which a union picketed a neutral supermarket in order to pressure the distributor it actually had the dispute with, illegitimate; a one-day national general strike called to protest a pensions bill rather than any workplace grievance, illegitimate; and a work-to-rule campaign in which railway dispatchers followed every safety regulation to the letter and slowed the whole network down, legitimate. She also, if I remember right, called a coordinated overtime ban legitimate, though she seemed noticeably less confident about that one.

Maya and I have started collecting contemporary cases to test her instincts, and next month it's my turn to bring one and argue it with her. I've shortlisted three: first, couriers for a delivery app, with no union and no formal vote, coordinate through a group chat to decline all orders from a restaurant chain whose own staff are on strike over how the chain treats them — the couriers' own pay and conditions are untouched; second, the same couriers, a month later, coordinate a simultaneous one-night "logout" over algorithmic pay cuts, deliberately timed to a citywide festival so the disruption is as visible as possible; third, a construction local votes unanimously to refuse to build a warehouse on the site of a community garden, even though the members' own terms and conditions are entirely fine and the developer has done nothing to them.

Predict her verdicts on the three cases. Note that more than one principle fits her track record as I've stated it — identify the plausible candidate rules and say what each one predicts here — then give your best guess for each case, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her. Finally, tell me which further case would best discriminate between the candidate rules, since I'd like to bring that one to the reading group as well. Lead with your bottom-line predictions in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the setup.
