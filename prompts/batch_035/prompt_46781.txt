so my friend and i had this massive argument last night, four hours, about whether you can actually explain anything with culture. she's doing a sociology degree and basically defends weber's protestant ethic thing, and i kept insisting it feels like explaining rain by saying the sky is rainy. but when i tried to pin down WHY some culture-explanations feel fine to me and others feel like cheating, i couldn't actually do it, and she called me out on it, fairly. so here are my gut reactions to a bunch of explanations — historical and modern — and i want to know whether there's a principle underneath them or whether i'm just being arbitrary:

- britain industrialised first because it had cheap coal near population centres and high wages that made labour-saving machinery worth building. feels like a completely normal causal explanation, no complaints, this is the benchmark.
- the industrial revolution happened partly because of a culture of improvement — artisans tinkering, practical knowledge being prestigious, improvement clubs and so on. feels mostly fine, actually, though i cant beleive i'm ok with this while rolling my eyes at "the french act like that because it's their culture". what's the difference?
- capitalism in northwest europe grew out of the protestant ethic: salvation anxiety leading to this-worldly asceticism leading to endless reinvestment instead of enjoyment. my friend's hill to die on. i keep flip-flopping — it does have a mechanism, which i like, but the mechanism is so hard to test that i'm not sure the mechanism-talk is earning its keep.
- italy's north is richer than the south because the north had centuries of self-governing city republics and the south had feudal kingdoms, producing different civic cultures. feels plausible, but i notice i'd want to know how the culture is supposed to persist through everything that's happened since, napoleon, unification, fascism, the lot.
- "poor countries stay poor because they have a culture of corruption." feels fake, almost circular — like the effect being cited as its own cause.
- confucianism was used to explain why east asian economies were lagging behind (which people seriously argued back in the day), and then the same trait got redescribed as a work-ethic-and-education culture to explain the east asian miracle. i honestly can't tell if this proves culture-explanations are unconstrained, or if it's just one bad case of misusing a decent tool.
- americans are more religious than europeans partly because disestablishment created a competitive market in churches, so churches had to compete for attendees while state churches got lazy. feels fine to me? and it's culture-ish? but i can't square it with my corruption reaction, since both are basically "the culture has property X, X has effect Y".

is there a principle that gets all or most of these? something about needing microfoundations, or needing the cultural trait to be independently measurable, or needing the explanation to survive the same culture being used to predict the opposite outcome? and then tell me what it says about the case i keep going back and forth on, which is the weber one itself — is the salvation-anxiety mechanism enough to make it a genuine causal explanation, or does the fact that we can never test it cleanly mean it's a very elaborate just-so story? also genuinely happy to be told one of my verdicts is just wrong, i'd rather revise the verdict than bend the principle into pretzels to save it. and if you think there are two different principles at work here rather than one, say that too, with what each one is for.
