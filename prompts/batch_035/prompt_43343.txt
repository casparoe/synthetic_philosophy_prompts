I am a PhD student in welfare economics, and for the past year my reading group has been stuck in a loop I can no longer take seriously as a debate. The topic is the oldest one in the field: whether interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningful. Everyone in the room knows all the moves, everyone deploys them fluently, and nobody has budged since the first meeting. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another tour of the positions.

Here is the loop as it actually plays out. The Robbinsian side opens with the ordinalist point: an agent's preferences over their own alternatives are observable up to monotone transformation, and once you drop cardinal utility, there is no operation that takes two agents' preference orderings and outputs a comparison between them. Calling one person's gain "greater" than another's is, on their view, not false but empty -- a category mistake dressed up as an empirical claim we happen to be bad at. The utilitarian side responds that we make exactly such comparisons constantly and non-idly: a triage nurse prioritizing the patient screaming over the patient dozing, a minister allocating a budget between dialysis and road safety, health economists averaging respondents' answers to construct quality-adjusted life years. The Robbinsians reply that widespread practice proves usage, not meaning -- before thermodynamics, people talked about caloric fluid fluently -- and that the QALY machinery in particular doesn't discover comparisons but legislates them, since the numbers are whatever the elicitation procedure is built to output. The utilitarians then play the Harsanyi card: extended preferences over extended outcomes (each person ranking whole histories that include "being you in that state" and "being me in this one") would ground comparisons if they exist. The Robbinsians answer that this just relocates the problem, because nothing constrains the extension: different coherent extended preference orderings, each consistent with everyone's observed ordinary preferences, license incompatible interpersonal verdicts, so the proposal presupposes exactly the comparisons it was meant to construct. Someone then brings up Sen-style partial comparability -- allowing rankings that are incomplete or invariant only over a restricted family of utility scales -- and the group splits over whether that dissolves the meaningfulness question or quietly changes the subject to "comparisons we can get away with." Then we adjourn.

Two things make me suspect the stall is structural rather than a failure of effort. First, the two sides never actually disagree about a case: show both of them a policy that plainly helps one badly-off person while plainly imposing trivial losses on many others, and they give the same verdict with the same confidence; the fight is only ever about whether that shared verdict constitutes knowledge. Second, and this is what I keep coming back to: my hospital has a pain protocol in which patients self-report pain from 0 to 10, and clinicians demonstrably use these numbers comparatively across patients to ration analgesics -- and nobody in my group thinks that protocol is meaningless, including the Robbinsians, who instead mutter that it is "a convention that works." A convention that works, which we can detect failure in and revise, starts to sound a lot like a measurement instrument. The temperature analogy cuts both ways, though: temperature had to be anchored to something invariant (the expansion of mercury, eventually thermodynamic principles), and no one has told me what the analogous anchor for utility is supposed to be.

So here is my working hypothesis, and I would like you to test it rather than flatter it: the disagreement is not about utility at all, but about what it takes for a magnitude to exist. One side holds that a quantity exists only relative to a determinate calibration procedure, so anything requiring a choice among equally admissible zero-points and units is conventional through and through; the other holds that a quantity exists wherever our verdicts are invariant across all the admissible choices -- that conventionality of scale is not conventionality of fact. On this reading the dispute is partly verbal, a standoff between an operationalist criterion for being a quantity and an invariance criterion, but with a substantive residue: whether there is anything out there, in behavior or in self-report, for the invariance to hold over, or whether the "workings" of the pain protocol and the QALY are just path-dependent institutional coordination with no fact of the matter behind it.

Given that hypothesis or whatever better one you can construct from what the sides actually say, I want three things. First, a diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute about 'meaningingfulness', a substantive metaphysical dispute about natural quantities, a methodological dispute that only looks deep because economics conflated the two when it absorbed logical positivism, or two projects -- a measurement question and a normative question about whose interests count for how much -- illegitimately sharing one vocabulary? Second, an account of what the partial-comparability program actually achieves once you characterize it in your terms: a genuine weakening of the meaningfulness standard, or a change of subject that survives only because nobody notices the question shifted? Third, an honest answer about what progress would look like: is there a formulation of the invariance condition that a Robbinsian could accept, some way the extended-preference machinery could be constrained so it stops being question-begging, or a case -- practical, experimental, or conceptual -- whose resolution would force one side to concede? If nothing could count as progress here, tell me why the debate continues at all rather than collapsing into people registering which criterion they signed up for at the start.
