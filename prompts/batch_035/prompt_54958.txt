I manage a small team that produces probabilistic forecasts for a logistics company — mostly questions like whether a given port will be congested next quarter, or whether a supplier will deliver on time. At our last retrospective, two of my senior forecasters got into a long argument about what form our stated credences are allowed to take, and I deliberately stayed out of it to see where it would land. It never landed. Let me recount it as fairly as I can.

1. Amara opened by insisting that every forecast must be a single number. Her reasoning was that if you can't commit to a number, you haven't finished the analysis; and, more practically, that numbers are what allow us to be scored and calibrated. A forecaster who reports only that a probability lies somewhere between 0.4 and 0.6, she said, cannot be graded at all.

2. Ben replied that for many of our questions the honest output just is a range, and that forcing a single number manufactures precision we don't have. He said a bare number implies a confidence in the number itself that we often lack, and that the whole point of saying 0.4 to 0.6 is to communicate that extra layer of uncertainty about one's own estimate.

3. Amara countered that a probability can't imply confidence in itself — 0.55 just means 0.55 — and that, in any case, Ben's ranges let forecasters dodge accountability: when the event fails to happen, the person who gave a range can claim never to have said it would. The entire point of scoring rules, she said, is that you can't have it both ways.

4. Ben answered that she was describing a problem with dishonest forecasters, not with ranges as such: if we insist on grading, apply the score to the midpoint, and the range still carries information the bare number would not. He then appealed to the urn experiments people have run for decades — where subjects prefer betting on an urn with a known even split of red and black over one of unknown composition, at identical stakes and odds — and said that reluctance strikes him as rational, not as a bias to be trained away.

5. Amara said that is precisely what it is — a well-known bias — and closed with an observation I found hard to dismiss: whenever Ben's forecasters do commit to numbers under pressure, the numbers are almost always 0.5, 0.6, or 0.7 — conspicuously round figures. That, she argued, shows there is no sharp number underneath; the ranges are just wearing a numerical costume. Ben's final reply was that coarse numbers are a quantization of a sharp underlying value, the way a ruler marked in millimeters still measures a continuous length, and that if she graded them on calibration, the coarseness would wash out in the long run anyway.

I could not referee this on the spot, and it is now starting to affect how we aggregate forecasts across the team. Please structure your reply in the following sections, in this order:

First, a section titled "Move-by-move verdict" covering exchanges 1 through 5 in order, at roughly 120–180 words per exchange, saying who is right at each step and why — not merely who is winning overall. If a step contains a good point and a bad one in the same breath, separate them.

Second, a section titled "What both are missing" of about 200 words. Here I would expect you to say something about how the field actually handles credences that are not single numbers: if there is an established framework for representing and scoring them, tell me what it is called and what adopting it would really commit us to, as opposed to what each of my forecasters assumes.

Third, a section titled "Practical recommendation" of about 250 words, setting out what our team should actually do starting next quarter — concrete enough that I could implement it without further consultation, and honest about what it costs us.

Please don't simply declare a winner. Tell me at which numbered step, if any, each of them goes wrong, and whether the disagreement ultimately turns on a factual question, a terminological one, or something deeper.
