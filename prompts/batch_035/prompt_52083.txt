My philosophy department has decided to try something unusual. For the past two terms, two colleagues have been arguing past each other in our seminar on the philosophy of science: one a fairly committed scientific realist who takes the success of our best physical theories to be best explained by their approximate truth, the other an anti-realist who leans heavily on the pessimistic meta-induction and the historical record of phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether. The department has now commissioned an adversarial collaboration between the two of them, and I have been asked to prepare the document they would jointly sign. I want you to draft it. Please include: the substantive claims both could sign -- about the undeniable predictive and technological success of mature science, about the genuine historical phenomenon of successful theories whose central posits were later abandoned, about what the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic meta-induction each establish and fail to establish, and about the epistemic credentials of inference to the best explanation as a general pattern of reasoning. Then set out the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as fair -- including, at minimum, whether realism's divide-and-impera move (retreating to the claim that only the working parts of past theories were approximately true) is principled or post hoc gerrymandering, whether structural survival of equations across theory change (Fresnel's equations surviving into Maxwell's theory, say) is evidence of anything more than descriptive bookkeeping, and whether approximate truth is a coherent notion that can do explanatory work. Where you find that part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive -- perhaps the difference between believing a theory and merely accepting it as empirically adequate, or the drawing of the observable/unobservable line -- say so explicitly and explain why.

Beyond the agreed claims and disagreements, I need the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one party. For each crux, identify a concrete case, historical episode, or argumentative test that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts that test would show. For example, both might agree that a systematic comparison of mature nineteenth-century theories with their successors is relevant to the selectivity crux -- but the realist will predict that a fair audit will find the successful, retained components clearly separable from the abandoned ontology, while the anti-realist will predict that any such separation can only be drawn retrospectively, with the benefit of hindsight about which parts survived. Think hard about what other tests are genuinely shared: the anti-realist will not accept anything that simply presupposes inference to the best explanation, and the realist will not accept anything that presupposes empiricism about unobservables, so the shared tests have to be carefully neutral.

Format the document as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table (disagreement, realist's formulation in her own words, anti-realist's formulation in his own words, whether verbal or substantive), followed by a cruxes section with the predictions recorded side by side. Close with a short jointly signed paragraph stating what each side concedes it would give up, and under what circumstances, were the cruxes to resolve against it. Keep the register that of an actual collaborative document -- measured, precise, and free of straw men -- since the whole point is that both parties could put their names to it.
