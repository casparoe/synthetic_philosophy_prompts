I serve on the governing board of a mid-sized public land-grant university (call it Ashford State; it resembles many real ones). Two years ago, in the wake of reporting on how land-grant institutions were originally capitalized — the Morrill Act of 1862 distributed land expropriated from Indigenous nations to fund new colleges — we commissioned our own historians to trace exactly what our founding appropriation consisted of and what became of it. Their report is now in. In broad outline it confirms the uncomfortable story, but in a way that complicates the apology we had assumed we would issue: our university received scrip for roughly ninety thousand acres, but the parcels were sold off by state officials within a few years, a large share of the proceeds went into buildings that burned or were demolished decades ago, and the archival record of how much of that money ever reached our endowment is genuinely uncertain — our historians put the plausible contribution somewhere between a trivial sum and perhaps a tenth of the founding endowment. Meanwhile, the regional council representing descendants of the dispossessed nations has asked for exactly the statement we expected to give: a formal apology acknowledging that the university was built on their land. Our student association, which pushed for the report in the first place, supports the same wording, and several trustees take the view that since the dispossession itself is not in doubt — the land was taken, and our institution benefited from the system — the accounting uncertainties are a moral quibble and a face-saving excuse.

Our bylaws require that any formal statement of institutional apology pass with a two-thirds vote of the board, and we vote in six weeks. The factions are roughly as follows: those who want the strong apology as drafted, those who want the strong apology plus a negotiated transfer of endowment funds, and a minority who argue that a present-day university cannot meaningfully apologize for acts committed by people long dead under a corporate name that has merely persisted — that whatever we issue would be theater, and worse, theater addressed to people whose primary grievance is with the federal government that actually took the land. I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation before the vote. Draft it for me: a concrete recommendation — which form of statement, if any, we should issue, or a recommendation against a formal apology in favor of some other act — with reasoning stated so that the losing factions can see their strongest arguments answered rather than dismissed. In particular, I want you to take seriously the descendants' council's objection that a hedged apology, one that apologizes for the dispossession while flagging uncertainty about the finances, is worse than either a clean apology or silence, since it reads as an apology with an asterisk; and also the minority's worry about whether we are even the right agent to apologize. Whatever you recommend should be something the board can actually vote on, with language we could adopt verbatim if we chose.
