The question, verbatim from the midterm:

"Philosophers sometimes worry about reasons of the 'wrong kind' for attitudes like belief, fear, admiration, and desire. (a) Explain the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons, giving an original example of each kind for admiration and for belief. (b) A popular analysis says that wrong-kind reasons are ones that concern the state of having the attitude rather than the attitude's object. Explain this analysis and assess whether it really captures the distinction. (c) Does the same problem arise for desire?"

And here's the student's answer, verbatim (I've fixed nothing, typos and all):

"(a) Attitudes have correctness conditions: admiration is correct when its object is admirable, belief is correct when its content is true, fear is correct when its object is dangerous. So a right-kind reason for an attitude is one that bears on whether the attitude is correct. The firefighter's courage gives me a right-kind reason to admire her, and the gathering clouds give me a right-kind reason to beleive it will rain.

Wrong-kind reasons are reasons that favor having the attitude without making the attitude correct. My example: I'm offered a fortune to admire a genuinely mediocre painting. The fee is a reason of some sort — if there were no fee I wouldn't bother — but it does not make the painting admirable. Likewise, being paid to believe it's a masterpiece gives me a wrong-kind reason for belief, since the payment doesn't make the belief true.

(b) The analysis says a wrong-kind reason concerns the state of having the attitude, while a right-kind reason concerns the object. In the fee case, whether I get paid depends on my admiring, so the reason concerns my admiring — state-concerning, hence wrong kind. The firefighter's courage concerns her, the object, hence right kind. I think this analysis is correct and I can't see how it could fail, since a reason that doesn't concern the object obviously can't bear on whether the object merits the attitude, and a reason that does concern the object obviously can. One slightly harder case: the growling dog. Fearing the dog is fitting, and the reason I have to fear it is that fear is what keeps me alive around dangerous animals — fear exists for survival, so the survival benefit is part of what makes the fear fitting, and it's a right-kind reason even though it works through the effects of being afraid. So the analysis needs a small amendment: a reason can be right-kind either by concerning the object or by concerning the attitude's function.

(c) The problem does not arise for desire, because desires aren't the sort of thing that can be correct or incorrect — there's no fact of the matter about what I ought to want. Since the whole distinction depends on an attitude having a correctness condition, and desire doesn't have one, there are only ordinary reasons for wanting things; none of them can be wrong-kind."
