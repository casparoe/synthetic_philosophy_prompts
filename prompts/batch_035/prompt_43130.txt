I'm taking a sociology class this semester and have to write a paper on whether culture can actually be a *cause* of things, so an argument my cousin and his wife got into at dinner is weirdly on point now. It started because he'd said the reason some regions industrialized earlier than others is basically culture -- work ethic, attitudes toward saving, distrust of enjoying life too much. His wife said culture can't be a cause: real causes are material (who owns what, geography, institutions), and "culture" is just what those things leave behind, so citing it as an explanation is empty. He came back with Weber -- the same merchant classes and material conditions existed in lots of places that never produced modern capitalism, but it took off first where the Protestant ethic was, so the values have to be doing some independent causal work. She said that's exactly what makes cultural explanations unfalsifiable: if Protestant regions got rich you credit the ethic, and when Catholic France got rich anyway, Weberians just tell a different value-story -- any outcome gets explained after the fact. He answered that this is a complaint about sloppy cultural explanations, not all of them: Weber's claim is comparative and makes checkable predictions, like Protestants saving more or literacy being higher even within the same country, and that stuff has actually been looked at, so it's not just storytelling. She wasn't done: even where the correlations hold, you haven't ruled out that both the values and the economic behavior flow from something further back that you haven't identified. Neither would give an inch, and honestly I couldn't follow who was winning. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right at each point -- and also whether her big opening claim, that culture can't be a cause at all, survives even if her particular objections to Weber do?
