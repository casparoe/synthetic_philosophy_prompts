Quick context: I'm a bioinformatics PhD student, and somehow I've become the designated words person in my lab. Anything public facing -- the group blog, press release drafts, the text for the university open day, slides for donor visits -- gets sent to me to check the metaphors, ever since a release about our CRISPR work said we 'rewrote the genetic code' and a philosopher from across campus wrote a very long email to the dean about it. So this is maybe twice a month, low stakes individually but embarrassing if it goes wrong, and the PI has final sign-off but she's busy and will basically rubber-stamp whatever I mark up.

The recurring problem is the whole information/code family: DNA as a code, as a language, a blueprint, an instruction manual; genes 'telling' cells what to do; 'editing' and 'find and replace'; 'junk' DNA. Some of these seem harmless and some get us in trouble, but the current decision procedure is vibes. I want an actual procedure I can run in fifteen minutes on a piece of text: questions to ask in order, what counts as a pass, and at what point I stop and flag the sentence for the PI because it's a values call (e.g. how much precision we're willing to trade for a headline) rather than a factual one. Ground it in what philosophy of biology and information theory actually say -- Shannon information vs. whatever semantic information is supposed to be, the fact that the genetic code proper is a mapping between codons and amino acids and maybe that's the only place 'code' is strictly earned, whether calling something a code smuggles in a coder, the frozen accident point about arbitrariness. Be honest about which distinctions I can drop in public-facing writing and which are load-bearing.

Then run it on three sentences: (1) The genetic code translates DNA sequences into proteins. (2) Our approach lets us rewrite the genetic code to correct disease-causing typos. (3) Junk DNA turns out to carry hidden messages. And fair warning, if the procedure tells me to cut the find-and-replace framing, I want to know why that one's special since its the one journalists love. Finally, tell me where the procedure misfires -- cases it passes that will still annoy somebody, or ones it fails that are actually fine.
