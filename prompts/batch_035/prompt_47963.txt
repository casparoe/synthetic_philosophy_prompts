I'm teaching a second-year early modern course, ~25 students, one 75-minute session, no prior background in Berkeley. The master argument never lands when I just lecture on it -- students hear "you can't conceive an unconceived tree" as either trivially true or a cheap verbal trick, and they never feel why anyone, including Berkeley himself, thought it was doing real work. So I want an in-class activity where they have to actually try to pull it off. Something like: students submit descriptions of an object existing with no mind aware of it in any way, and a panel of their classmates has the job of catching the smuggled-in observer -- the viewpoint the description is tacitly written from, the imagery that can't be there unless someone is looking. With some scoring system that pays off a genuinely clean submission if one somehow exists. I also want at least one round where they're forced to use non-observational vocabulary only -- coordinates, physics-speak, functional descriptions -- because I want them to discover for themselves whether the argument's force depends on treating conceiving as picturing, and whether the "unperceived object" survives once the picturing is taken away.

Then a debrief that maps what happened in the rounds onto the two readings of the argument -- the strong one where the attempt lands in a genuine contradiction, and the weak omission reading where all that's been shown is the triviality that you can't think of x without thinking of x. I want the Russell-style comeback (thinking about vs. thinking of) to come out of the students, not from me, if possible. And be honest about which parts of Berkeley the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debreif -- God's perceptual backstop being the obvious one. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this.
