I serve on the curriculum review board of a national military academy. An instructor in the philosophy department has proposed a capstone exercise for the senior elective on the ethics of war: cadets are assigned, in a structured debate, to defend the revisionist position that a soldier who enlists in the armed forces of a state widely known at the time of enlistment to be prosecuting a war of aggression does not enjoy the same moral protection against being killed as a conscript resisting that aggression — in other words, that the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants, which our core just-war course teaches and assesses, is false at least for some voluntary participants in unjust wars. The instructor argues that this is simply the live academic dispute between traditionalists and revisionists over the moral equality of combatants, that it appears in the very textbooks our core course uses, and that cadets deserve to confront the strongest case against the doctrine under which they will serve. One half of the board is sympathetic. The other half objects that the exercise asks future officers to reason toward conclusions that, if accepted, would erode the premises on which the law of armed conflict and our own rules of engagement rest — namely that liability to attack is fixed by status and conduct in battle, not by the justice of the war — and that a graded debate format lends the revisionist side an institutional endorsement the academy should not be neutral about.

Please draft the board's decision memo. It should contain: a clear recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions); criteria the board could apply to future proposals of this kind, so that our decision does not look ad hoc; an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, stated well enough that its proponents could recognize themselves in it; and, because the recommendation will rest on judgment calls about pedagogy and institutional message rather than on settled principle, a closing section saying what would change our minds — which argument or consideration, if it were pressed harder than we can press it now, would move the board to a different verdict, and where the reasoning in the memo is most likely to fail. Keep the memo to something the board could read aloud in fifteen minutes.
