I run a philosophy of physics elective for advanced undergraduates — about 14 students, one 90-minute session. We have already spent one meeting on the Everett interpretation, so they know the basics of universal branching, but they have not yet encountered the measurement problem in its specifically Everettian form: why should a rational agent care about outcomes in proportion to the squared amplitudes of the branches rather than in proportion to the number of branches? Design an in-class decision game in which participants first find branch counting perfectly natural, and then discover for themselves that it gives insane answers.

Some constraints on the design. The game obviously cannot literally split students, so it needs a workable proxy: for example, students acting as Everettian agents who are dealt cards or otherwise assigned to successor copies after each simulated measurement, with the facilitator or the rules playing the branching structure. Include at least one early round where branch counting and measure-weighting coincide (say, a 50/50 quantum coin), so the group settles comfortably into counting branches, and a later round where the two come apart — e.g., a measurement whose outcome with fewer branches carries the greater total Born measure, or a laboratory that splits one high-amplitude branch into many low-amplitude sub-branches, changing branch counts without changing what any physically meaningful quantity says. The bets offered in each round should be specified precisely, along with a payoff scheme using points or tokens rather than real money. At some point the participants should be asked to state, and defend to each other, the principle they have actually been using to value gambles.

Then give a structured debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the live positions in the literature: the naive branch-counting rule and its dependence on arbitrary fine-graining of branches; the Deutsch–Wallace style response, on which rational decision theory under branching constraints already forces the Born weights; and approaches that appeal to self-locating uncertainty or to typicality. The debrief should make clear which moments of the game correspond to which assumptions — in particular, whether the game smuggles in the squared-amplitude weighting through the payoff scheme or the copy-assignment mechanism, and how a careful redesign could avoid that.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be frank about which features of the actual problem no classroom proxy can reproduce — for instance, the fact that an Everettian agent cannot know in advance which successor she will be, cannot sell her stake in the branches she will not become, and faces no ordinary risk at all, since every outcome is guaranteed to happen somewhere. I want the students to leave feeling the problem as a genuine tension between decision theory and the metaphysics of branching, not as a formula to be accepted on authority.
