At a wedding last month two friends of mine, Marco and Dana, got into it about whether "left" and "right" still name anything real. Marco went first: the distinction is a fossil, an accident of where people sat in the French National Assembly in 1789, and it's falling apart now that the bundles don't cohere — plenty of people who want higher taxes on the rich are hawks on immigration, and plenty of pro-market libertarians would legalize everything the cultural right hates, so what could left and right possibly be tracking anymore. Dana said that only shows politics isn't one dimensional — everyone knows the compass has two axes — but in real electorates the axes are correlated, and left/right is just the name for that correlation: know where someone stands on redistribution and you can predict their party much better then chance, so the concept earns its keep. Marco said the correlation is an artifact of parties bundling issues — it's not that leftish views cluster in people's heads, it's that you have to pick a coalition and the coalition picks your other views for you, which makes left/right real for parties but fake for voters. Dana said that's backwards: if the bundling were arbitrary, the lineup should have scrambled generations ago, but the same basic lineup keeps reappearing, which suggests something underneath holds each side together — her candidate was that the left thinks inequality itself is the problem, while the right thinks inequality is mostly natural or earned and only unfairness is left to complain about. Marco pounced: the right claims to want equality of opportunity all the time — school choice, rising tides lifting all boats — they just disagree about the means, so by her criterion they'd count as left, and the criterion collapses. Dana's last word was that equality of opportunity is precisely the border post between the camps: the left/right question is whether you stop there or keep going to outcomes. Marco rolled his eyes and said she was defining the distinction by how people talk, not by anything out in the world. They eventually asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall — and is there something both of them are missing?
