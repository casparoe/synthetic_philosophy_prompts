I spent the better part of four decades as a physical chemist and have only lately taken up philosophy of mind, so I ask you to tolerate an analogy drawn from my own field. Panpsychism, as I understand it, locates experience at the fundamental physical level, and the combination problem is the objection that we cannot make sense of micro-experiences adding up to one unified subject like the one asking this question; William James is supposed to have pressed something of the sort against the "mind-dust" theorists of his day, remarking that however many feelings you pack together, each remains the feeling it always was, with nothing unified appearing. My instinct is to reply with the old slogan that "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts": after all, water is nothing like hydrogen and oxygen taken separately, no chemist regards this as spooky, and combination of properties is routine in nature. But I suspect the slogan, so deployed, either does no work or trades on an equivocation.

Please precisify the slogan for me. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making in this context — among them, perhaps: (i) that complex systems have structure-dependent properties their parts lack; (ii) that some properties are not additive functions of the parts' intrinsic properties; (iii) that some properties of wholes are not even in-principle derivable from parts plus relational structure; and (iv) that some forms of combination yield new *subjects* rather than mere aggregates. You need not confine yourself to these four, but cover anything essential I have missed. For each reading, tell me three things: whether it is true in physical chemistry (a brief illustrative example suffices), whether it actually helps the panpsychist with the combination problem or merely restates it in different words, and whether the "combination is routine in nature" reply depends on it. Note carefully where a reading concerns properties at one level of description versus subjects, since that seems to me the crux of whether my chemical analogy is apt.

Conclude, in no more than three sentences, with a verdict: is my appeal to chemical combination a genuine disanalogy-breaker against the combination problem, or a category error dressed up as an analogy? Keep the whole answer under 500 words, organized as a numbered menu of readings, each with its three-part verdict, followed by your concluding sentences.
