Our reading group just spent two months on the book-length exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on free will, moral responsibility, and just deserts. The discussion went well enough, but two members — I'll call them Nadia and Paul — have been continuing the argument in our group chat all week, and since I'm moderating the wrap-up session, I've been asked to referee. I honestly can't, so I'm outsourcing it. Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Nadia opened by saying that if no one ever has the kind of free will that grounds ultimate desert — if how you act always traces back to causes you didn't choose — then nothing backward-looking can justify punishment. Only prospective aims survive: deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation. She noted that this is roughly the position the free will skeptic ends up in, and that it doesn't collapse into anything goes, because those aims still constrain sentencing in real ways.

(2) Paul answered that a purely forward-looking theory proves too much: it would justify punishing a scapegoat whenever doing so deterred better than punishing no one. So some backward-looking element is non-negotiable — the person must actually have done it, and culpably — and any theory worth having has to say what that element is, not just re-describe it in functional terms.

(3) Nadia replied that the requirement that the person actually did it is functional, not a residue of desert. A system that punishes at random deters nothing, and one publicly known to punish the innocent forfeits the cooperation it runs on. The scapegoat case only has force if you imagine the frame-up stays secret indefinitely, and a theory of punishment can't stake its counterexamples on officials successfully hiding the truth forever.

(4) Paul pressed on proportionality. If only consequences matter, the sentence should be whatever best deters — yet even people who reject ultimate desert shrink from giving a shoplifter fifty years when a shorter sentence would deter slightly less well. Something other than consequences is setting the upper bound. Calling it the minimum effective sentence just relabels the proportionality it was supposed to explain.

(5) Nadia then made what she called Dennett's move: give up ultimate desert but keep earned desert. A person who developed reason-responsiveness and self-control and then exercised them badly is more blameworthy than someone who never had the capacity, and society has every reason to maintain the practice of holding them responsible. Proportionality can track what the person made of themselves — backward-looking without being ultimate — so the dilemma between retribution and pure consequentialism is a false one.

(6) Paul said that was exactly the move he found most suspect. Earned desert sounds modest, but in practice it still licenses resentment and harsh treatment as fitting responses; ask the public what a repeat offender earned and they won't draw the philosopher's fine lines between kinds of desert, they'll hear permission for precisely the retributive attitudes the skeptic set out to disown. A distinction that only behaves in careful hands isn't doing work for society, and the compatibilist who keeps desert-talk bears the burden of the uses it actually gets put to.

At this point both of them appealed to me, and I'm out of my depth. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six numbered steps, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. Then say who, if anyone, is right overall, and — since I suspect at least part of this dispute may not be substantive — identify anything both of them are missing. Please keep your answer under 600 words.
