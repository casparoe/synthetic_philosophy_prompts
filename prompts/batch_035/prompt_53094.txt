The exam question read as follows: "Explain the raven paradox. State the hypothesis and the two confirmation principles that jointly generate the result that observing a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black; explain why this result strikes us as paradoxical; and assess what you take to be the strongest available response."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The hypothesis is H: all ravens are black. Nicod's criterion says that a universal generalization is confirmed by each of its instances, so every observed black raven confirms H. The contrapositive of H — all non-black things are non-ravens — is logically equivalent to H, and the equivalence condition says that logically equivalent sentences are confirmed by the same evidence. Applying Nicod's criterion to the contrapositive, any observed non-black non-raven, such as a white shoe, confirms it. So a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black. This is paradoxical because it offends the intuition that evidence about shoes is simply irrelevant to claims about ravens: my closet cannot tell me anything about ornithology. Hempel's own way of dealing with this was to bite a different bullet: he rejected the equivalence condition and held that Nicod's criterion governs confirmation of a hypothesis considered in isolation, so that the white shoe confirms the contrapositive but not H itself. The Bayesian response, which I find more persuasive, accepts both principles and dissolves the paradox by appeal to background knowledge. If we model ourselves as sampling randomly from the objects of the universe, then since the non-ravens vastly outnumber the non-black things, a draw that turns out to be a non-black non-raven was to be expected anyway, and so it does not confirm H at all. The shoe is confirmatory only in a hollow technical sense, and the appearance of paradox comes from mistaking logical confirmation for evidential relevance."
