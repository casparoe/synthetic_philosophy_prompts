I'm a PhD student in ML interp and I've somehow volunteered to run the session for our AI safety reading group this week — about 14 people, mostly grad students plus a couple of sharp undergrads, 75 minutes. Topic is mechanistic anomaly detection. Quick sketch so we're on the same page about what I mean by that: the pitch of MAD is that instead of trying to enumerate all the bad behaviors up front, you verify your model computes its output via some set of mechanisms you actually understand and trust, and then in deployment you flag inputs where the model seems to be doing something else — implementing a mechanism that isn't one of the ones you signed off on. The thing I want the session to actually land on is the question hiding under that pitch: what counts as "the same mechanism"? Two nets trained from different seeds on the same data share zero weights but plausibly implement the same mechanism. A net and a hand-written program computing the same function — same mechanism? "Induction heads do in-context learning" and "these specific attention heads with these specific weight matrices" are different grains of description, and identity verdicts seem to flip depending on which one you pick. This is the part people nod at and then keep talking as if it were settled, and I want them to feel it.

So I'm asking you to design an activity where they run into this themselves rather than getting a lecture from me about abstraction levels. My half-formed idea: hand out pairs of toy computations (string-processing procedures, little circuits, whatever) and have everyone privately judge same-mechanism vs different-mechanism, then surface the disagreement patterns, then hit them with some kind of re-description twist — the same pair comes back described at a coarser or finer grain, and people's own votes flip. But you're the game designer, I'm just the domain person, so restructure freely if you have something better. What I need concretely: rules, materials I can actually prep in one evening (index cards/printouts, no coding required, though a little spreadsheet stuff is fine), a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the real problem — what grain-sensitivity does to MAD as a safety story, whether "the model is doing something else" is even well-defined before you've fixed a description level, and what a deployed detector could possibly be comparing its live model against (the verified checkpoint? a description? both at once?).

Also, two things I want you to be honest about. First, what the activity can't reproduce — what toy string procedures have that trained nets don't (clean modularity, maybe) and what they lack (the mess of distributed, redundant, learned structure), and what I'll have to supply verbally in the debrief since the game itself can't show it. Second, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. My own guess is at least one person digs in and insists mechanism identity is perfectly determinate if you just "look at the code properly" — tell me how to handle that without steamrolling them, since honestly they might be half right and I don't want the activity to smuggle in my conclusion.
