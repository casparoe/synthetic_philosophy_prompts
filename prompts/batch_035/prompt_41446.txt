I do the modelling for a small forecasting outfit -- think sports and gaming risk mostly, but analysts also advise clients on stuff like when to hedge. The recurring headache: our people basically never get clean evidence. What they get is "satellite imagery suggests the shipment left port, we'd put that at about 65%" or "the source is right more often than wrong but he's had a rough week." Ordinary Bayesian conditioning assumes you learn an event with certainty, and my analysts are not learning events with certainty, ever. Before I let anyone hardcode an update rule into our tooling, I want the actual spec the rule has to meet.

So write me the desiderata for an update rule that handles uncertain evidence -- what it has to do to count as adequate. Things I half-know belong on the list: it should reduce to plain conditioning when the evidence happens to be certain (and do nothing when it's uninformative), it needs some answer to the communativity problem people raise against Jeffrey conditioning -- successive updates on different partitions giving different results depending on the order -- it shouldn't be hostage to arbitrary choices about how you slice the evidence into a partition, and ideally there's a principled justification (Dutch book? accuracy? value-of-learning style argument that free evidence is never harmful?) rather than "it's a natural-looking generalization." For each requirement say why it's a requirement, tell me which ones are genuinely load-bearing and which are negotiable, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- I have a feeling the commutativity one clashes with something.

Don't assume Jeffrey's rule is the answer. It's the incumbent; treat it as one candidate among others. Also consider the naive "condition on your best guess as if it were certain" approach, the fixes people proposed for the commutativity problem (I remember a guy named Field had one), and whatever else a well-read person would put on the shortlist for updating on less-than-certain evidence.
