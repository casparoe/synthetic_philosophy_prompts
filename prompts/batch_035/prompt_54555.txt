Some background: I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. A while ago I came across a summary of a thought experiment by Douglas Hofstadter, "A Conversation with Einstein's Brain," which closes The Mind's I, the anthology he edited with Daniel Dennett. As I understand the setup, Einstein's brain is preserved alive in a vat, and a visitor can converse with it, however slowly and however much mediation the interface requires. The point, as it was explained to me, is that the brain is at bottom a static, readable structure — a vast book — so even the transcription of such a conversation, printed and shelved, would be a way of "talking with Einstein" for anyone who read it carefully enough: what matters is the pattern, not the medium carrying it or the speed at which it is traversed.

This keeps coming up at work. Colleagues of mine who build ML systems invoke it to argue that a model checkpoint is in exactly the same position as Hofstadter's brain-book: the weights file is the complete pattern, at rest, and running inference is merely the reading of it. On that picture, consciousness cannot depend on any ongoing runtime activity — it's purely a question of information content — so a checkpoint that is never loaded is in the same position as a book that is never opened, and the running model is just a fast reader. I find this line appealing, and I suspect that's exactly why I should distrust it.

I'd like a serious audit of the analogy rather than a verdict either way. Specifically:

1. A correspondence table. What plays the role of the living brain in the vat, the printed book, the reader, the reading, and the conversation itself? And on the ML side, what corresponds to the weights file, the inference process, the hardware, and the prompt? I want to see the mapping made explicit before any evaluation of it.

2. Where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I've half-formed but can't evaluate: the book needs an external, already-conscious reader to interpret it, whereas the vat-brain does its own interpreting; the vat-brain is causally active even while "waiting," while a printed page is inert; the brain's dynamics are continuous and self-modifying, while inference only simulates a snapshot of a dynamics. Which of these (or others) are real load-bearing breaks?

3. A separation of the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion being drawn from the ones that are incidental. My colleagues are using the brain-as-book picture to license a strong claim: that a static, never-executed checkpoint could be conscious in exactly the sense a running model is, or could count as "the same mind." Which breaks in the analogy actually threaten that claim, and which are just friction?

4. A verdict on whether the strong conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time.

And if the book picture fails, what would be a better analogy for the relationship between a checkpoint and a running system? I'd especially welcome one a systems engineer can evaluate in terms of, say, the difference between a program text and a process — if that comparison is itself apt.
