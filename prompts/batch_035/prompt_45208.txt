I am a high school senior who leads the discussion section of our school's economics-and-philosophy reading circle. In two weeks I have to teach the idea of revealed preference and what economists mean by utility: forty-five minutes, whiteboard only, for an audience of sharp classmates who have taken at most an introductory economics course. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

The dependency I keep getting wrong when I rehearse this: whether the claim that choice reveals preference can be introduced first, with utility bookkeeping coming afterward as a convenience, or whether the audience has to distrust the word "utility" before the revealed-preference claim will strike them as anything more than a tautology. Tell me which order to use and why. On the carrying example: the one I have been given is a person choosing tea when coffee is available at the same price, and I have come to suspect that a single observed choice is too thin to carry the session — it seems to reveal almost nothing by itself. Decide whether I need instead a minimal pattern of the same person choosing across different prices and budget constraints, and if so, design the smallest pattern that works; tell me what the example must contain and what it must be stripped of.

The misconceptions I expect, in the order I expect them: (a) that utility is a measurable substance like happiness that accumulates in a mental reservoir; (b) that revealed preference means choices are rational or good for the chooser, so that the view is secretly a defense of consumer society; and (c) a subtler one I want to trap early — that once utility is understood as a mere bookkeeping device for rankings, statements like "she ended up with more utility this year than last" are not merely hard to verify but lack any determinate content at all. Tell me exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted, which of the three must be deliberately left standing until late in the session, and why that ordering is not just stylistic.

For the close: give me one question I can ask in the final five minutes whose answer would show whether the audience has genuinely internalized the ordinal character of utility — that they see why doubling every utility number changes nothing — rather than merely tolerated the vocabulary. And tell me what I should openly decline to cover and say so: I am tempted to gesture at interpersonal utility comparisons and at the idea of a utility monster, and I suspect at least one of these belongs on the list of things I refuse to do at this level.
