In my political theory seminar we keep leaning on the late Roman Republic as the template for what ails contemporary democracies: extreme wealth concentration displacing small farmers in favor of latifundia worked by the landless, whose discontent feeds armies loyal to generals rather than to the state; the erosion of the mos maiorum after the killing of Tiberius Gracchus; the Sullan precedent showing that force could seize the city, rewrite the constitution, and be rewarded with retirement rather than punishment — all of it culminating in Caesar. The conclusion my classmates draw is that once norms start breaking in this sequence, collapse within a generation is close to inevitable: the Republic was not overthrown by a coup but by the normalization of the once-unthinkable, and our democracies are on the same track. Audit the analogy for me. Lay out what maps onto what — what in a modern democracy is supposed to play the role of the latifundia, the client army, the tribunate, the Sullan precedent — then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the inevitability claim and those that don't, paying attention to features the Romans lacked that we possess (broad suffrage, professionalized militaries under nominal civilian control, nuclear weapons, peer competitors) as well as features we lack that they had. If a closer analogy is available, name it and say what work it does better. Then tell me whether the inevitativity conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way. End with a final line of the form "Verdict: X", where X is exactly one of: carries, carries weakened, or fails.
