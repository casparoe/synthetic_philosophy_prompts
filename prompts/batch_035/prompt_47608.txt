I'm a third-year PhD student in environmental economics, and my dissertation chapter on the choice of discount rate for climate damages keeps collapsing into what is really a prior philosophical question: is pure time preference — discounting a unit of utility simply because it occurs later, as opposed to discounting future consumption because people will be richer (the ηg term in the Ramsey formula) — permissible at all? My committee is split down the middle. My advisor treats a near-zero pure rate as settled since the Stern Review; another reader is a revealed-preference type who thinks welfare analysis has no business imposing the analyst's ethics, and that a positive pure rate simply tracks what households demonstrably exhibit in market and experimental data. Before I write the section that takes a side, I want an outside view on which of the arguments in play actually carry weight.

So that you don't have to reconstruct the literature, here are the six arguments I've collected, stated in my own words and lettered for reference. Three are against pure time preference: (A) the temporal-symmetry argument, that the date at which welfare occurs is as morally arbitrary as the spatial location or century of the recipient, so a planner who discounts utility across persons just because it is later is making the same kind of mistake as one who discounts welfare in the Southern Hemisphere; (B) the double-counting argument, that the Ramsey formula already handles everything ethically defensible via growth-adjusted marginal utility and extinction risk, so an additional pure rate either charges the future twice for the same consideration or quietly smuggles in an independent moral claim; (C) the patientism argument, that temporal neutrality applies within a life as much as across lives — a person should care about her own future utility exactly as much as her present utility, and since only agents with this trait are fit to be aggregated by a planner, the planner must respect it. Three are for its permissibility: (D) the continuity argument, that personal identity over long horizons is a matter of degree, with psychological connectedness thinning over decades, so discounting within a life is not obviously like discounting across lives, and the symmetry argument, at best, covers only the cross-person case; (E) the democratic-legitimacy argument, that the citizens whose pure time preference a social planner would override are the only people who actually exist to be consulted, so a philosophically motivated zero rate is an impermissible imposition of the analyst's own intertemporal values; (F) the time-consistency argument, based on a classic Koopmans-style result, that any planner evaluating infinite utility streams who wants stationarity and consistency over time is essentially forced into discounted utility, so some impatience is not a moral lapse but the structural price of coherent choice.

Please structure your reply as follows. Section 1, "Tier list" (roughly 300 words): sort the six arguments into three or four tiers by how much they should move a reasonable economist who currently accepts pure time preference, with one sentence per argument explaining its tier. Section 2, "The top tier under fire" (roughly 200 words): take whatever you rank highest, give its strongest form, the best objection against it, and your reply to that objection. Section 3, "The bottom tier diagnosed" (roughly 150 words): explain why the weakest arguments fail and what each would need to be repaired. Section 4, "My number" (a short paragraph): commit to the highest pure rate of time preference, in percent per year, that you could defend with a straight face, and say in a sentence what evidence or argument would move that number. I care more about the carving than the verdict — I want to know which of these arguments are really doing independent work and which are restatements of each other wearing different formalism.
