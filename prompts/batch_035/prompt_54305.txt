I'm a PhD student in political theory, and in our graduate seminar on concepts of liberty we kept circling back to a line that the republican liberty people apparently love: the idea that a slave with a kind master is no more free than a slave with a cruel one. It came up most recently when we were discussing whether a deeply paternalistic employer — one that pays generously, houses its workers, and plans their careers, but would sack or reshuffle anyone the moment it suited the firm — counts as a constraint on its employees' freedom at all. A classmate deployed the slave comparison to insist that it does, and that anyone who disagrees is just equating freedom with the absence of actual interference. I suspect the one-liner is doing several different jobs at once, and I'd like to be able to see them separately.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a defense of any one reading, the distinct precise claims that the kind-master point could be making? For instance, I can imagine at least these candidates, though you should feel free to add or restructure: a definitional thesis on which freedom is the absence of arbitrary power over someone (domination), not the absence of interference, so that unfreedom is a standing condition that persists through good treatment; a modal or counterfactual thesis on which the point is just that the kind master could interfere at will, and the slave's condition depends on his continuing goodwill; a thesis about uncertainty and self-censorship, where the unfreedom consists in the slave having to shape her life around anticipating the master's wishes; and a status or relational thesis on which the point concerns neither power nor interference but the social standing of being subject to another's will — being in someone's power as such. For each reading, I'd like to know whether it is more or less trivial, whether it is philosophically defensible, and whether it would actually support the use my classmate put it to: extending the conclusion from the literal slave to the benevolent-paternalist employer case.

A last request: for each reading, also say briefly how it handles the boundary case that worries me most — the constitutional state that can interfere with its citizens (through law, taxation, prison) but only through non-arbitrary procedures they jointly control. If the kind-master point rests on the definitional reading, my classmate seems to get paternalist employers on the unfreedom side, but I can't tell whether the same move also puts the decent democratic state on the unfreedom side, which would strike most of us as a cost. I want to bring your menu to our next seminar meeting, so please keep the readings clearly numbered and the verdicts crisp.
