My roommate is a philosophy of mind major and shes got an argument that seems to prove way too much, but I can't find the bad step. It's about zombies -- the philosophical kind, physical duplicates of us with no inner experience. She runs it like this:

(1) Zombies are conceivable: I can clearly imagine a being molecule-for-molecule identical to me that has no phenomenal experience at all.
(2) Whatever is clearly conceivable is metaphysically possible.
(3) So zombies are possible, which means a physical duplicate of me could lack consciousness, so physicalism is false.

That part I've seen before. Then she twists the knife:

(4) If zombies are possible, consider a possible world physically identical to ours in which zombie-me sits down and writes out this very argument, intuitions and all.
(5) In that world the argument gets written entirely by physical processes, since there's nothing else in that world to do any writing.
(6) But the physical facts there are the same as the physical facts here, so those same physical processes produce my copy of the argument in the actual world too. My beliefs about consciousness -- including the intuition I appealed to in step (1) -- are caused entirely by stuff that has nothing to do with consciousness itself.
(7) A belief caused by something completely independent of whatever fact makes it true can't be knowledge; it's true by luck at best.
(8) So either I don't know that I'm conscious, or I don't know that zombies are conceivable. Either way the argument saws off the branch its sitting on.

She takes this to show the zombie argument is self-refuting and physicalism wins by default. My vague feeling is that something sneaky happens somewhere between (5) and (8) -- maybe the word "produce" is quietly doing different work in the two worlds -- but I can't pin it down, and I also can't tell whether rejecting (6) is even available to her without giving up more than she thinks. Which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? 500 words max, please, and in American English.
