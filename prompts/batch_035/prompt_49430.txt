For years, at our department's weekly lunch, my colleague in philosophy of physics has been issuing verdicts on whether the posits of past physics were real, and I have kept a record. Phlogiston: not real. Caloric: not real. Newton's absolute space: not real. The Victorian mechanical ether: not real — though she added, unprompted, that Fresnel's theory was getting at something real, and that what survived of ether theory into Maxwell and then relativity is the real part of it. Thomson's electron: real, even though almost everything we believed about electrons changed between 1897 and the late 1920s. The classical electromagnetic field: real. The point-masses of Newtonian mechanics: not real as things, she said, though she granted that the dynamics they carried was. Next term's question is quarks: are they real? I would also like her line on dark matter and on virtual particles. But I care less about the answers than about the rule she has implicitly been running: what are the two or three principles that fit her record just as well as one another, what does each predict about quarks — and about the other two cases — which prediction is your best guess for her and why, and which further case, one I could raise at lunch, would most cleanly drive her candidate rules apart?
