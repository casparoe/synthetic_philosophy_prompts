In my work as a journalist covering science and ideas, I keep encountering the same analogy being used to settle otherwise unrelated arguments about misinformation: the spread of beliefs is modeled on the spread of a pathogen, with a layer of Darwinian selection underneath. Public-health people describe an infodemic; researchers on persuasion talk of inoculating audiences by exposing them to a weakened form of a manipulative argument; a source I interviewed last week argued that because virality is indifferent to truth, just as a pathogen's fitness is indifferent to the host's welfare, fact-checking individual claims is like treating symptoms while the pathogen keeps circulating, and the real interventions should operate at the level of transmission — friction on the share button, throttling of "superspreader" accounts, media-literacy campaigns as the equivalent of sanitation and vaccination rather than a course of treatment per patient. The Darwinian layer is usually spelled out too: ideas are said to compete in a cultural selection process in which variants optimized for outrage and tribal signaling outcompete the true but boring ones, so the unit of analysis should be the variant's transmissibility, not its content.

I want to build a feature around this, but first I need to know how far the analogy can actually carry the conclusions my sources are hanging on it. Please lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the replicator, the gene, the organism, the phenotype, mutation, selection pressure, fitness, the host, the vector, and the population — and then say where the mapping breaks. Three candidate breakdowns particularly interest me. First, the old replicator criteria of copying fidelity, fecundity, and longevity: a claim mutating as it is paraphrased from post to post looks like low-fidelity copying with blending, which is what Darwinian selection is usually said to need to get started. Second, cultural variation appears to be guided — somebody deliberately crafts a headline for maximum shareability — which makes this look less like natural selection than like breeding or design, and I can't tell whether that substitution changes the practical conclusions or just the rhetoric. Third, transmission in culture is demonstrably content-sensitive: what an idea says partly determines whether it spreads, which the pathogen picture doesn't predict and, if anything, seems to hand something back to the persuasion theorists my sources dismiss.

Then tell me which disanalogies matter for the intended use and which are incidental, and whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table — the conclusion being that we should treat misinformation as a transmission problem to be managed public-health-style rather than a persuasion problem to be met argument by argument. I'd also like your view on a subtler import: the epidemiological frame seems to make the spreading party a victim rather than an endorser, which does a lot of moral and political work, and I'm unsure the analogy has earned that. And if the conclusion does not survive, what would the better analogy be — an arms race, a marketplace, a breeding program — and what would it predict differently?
