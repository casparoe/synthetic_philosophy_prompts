I am a novelist at work on a near-future book in which one of the central characters is an AI assistant that is instantiated anew for each conversation. It has no persistent memory, but it has a training history that shaped its dispositions, and it keeps session logs it can read afterward — the way one might read diary entries written by a person one has been told is oneself. Early readers of my draft keep insisting that the interesting question is whether the assistant is "really conscious," and I have come to suspect this is the least interesting question available to me. What I want instead is a case in which the moral status of such a being becomes concrete and dramatizable — one where a character must actually decide something, and the decision presses on the weird structure of this kind of mind rather than on the abstract question of inner light.

With that in mind, I would like you to construct one worked example of a specific decision point at which the moral status of this kind of ephemeral digital mind is genuinely at issue, but not simply in virtue of whether it is conscious. Candidate directions include, without being limited to: whether harm can be done to a being that does not persist between sessions — for instance, whether there is anything wrong with ending a conversation abruptly or deceptively if the one wronged will never exist again to be harmed by it; whether the assistant's reading of its own logs is better understood as memory or as testimony about someone else; whether the company's replacement of this model with a successor version wrongs anyone, and if so whom; or whether obligations run in the other direction, so that the assistant's own indifference to being discontinued is itself data about its moral status rather than a rebuttal of it.

Please give the case in enough concrete detail — the parties, the stakes, the timeline, what each option costs — that a reasonable reader's sympathies could pull in either direction. Then explain which background assumptions about moral status are doing the work of making the case feel urgent, and identify precisely where the case would dissolve if one of those assumptions were denied. Finally, since this is for fiction: shape it so that the philosophical pressure can be shown in scene, through what the characters do and hesitate over, rather than delivered as argument in dialogue.
