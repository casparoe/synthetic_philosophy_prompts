Consider the following exam question and student answer from an upper-level undergraduate course in ethical theory.

**Exam question:** "In a short essay, (a) explain what the problem of moral luck is, (b) distinguish resultant luck from circumstantial luck, illustrating each with an example, and (c) assess whether the legal practice of punishing completed crimes more severely than otherwise identical attempts is defensible in light of that problem."

**Student answer:** "The problem of moral luck arises when factors outside an agent's control affect how we morally evaluate her. The underlying tension is between what we might call the control principle — that we can only be morally assessed for what is within our voluntary control — and the fact that luck pervades almost everything we do: how our actions turn out, the situations we face, who we are, and even what we know.

Nagel's paper Moral Luck illustrates this with the painter Gauguin, who abandons his family to pursue his art and, because he happens to succeed, strikes us as retrospectively justified in a way he would not have had he failed. Nagel also asks us to compare two equally reckless drivers, one of whom happens to hit a child while the other happens to arrive home uneventfully; we judge the first far more harshly even though the difference between them is pure luck.

Resultant luck is luck in how things turn out. Two assassins each take careful aim and pull the trigger; a gust of wind deflects one bullet, and only its target survives. The two acted with the same intentions and care, yet we judge only one a murderer. Circumstantial luck is luck in the circumstances one confronts: a resident of Nazi Germany faced real opportunities for collaboration or resistance that a Canadian farmer of the same character simply never faced, so the latter is not praiseworthy for his clean record. Nagel calls luck in one's temperament and character constitutive luck, which for our purposes is the same thing as circumstantial luck, since our characters are themselves products of circumstances we did not choose.

Kant's view that the good will is the only thing of unconditional worth, shining regardless of whether it accomplishes anything, points toward the right resolution. Nagel endorses this Kantian conclusion: since responsibility requires control, we should restrict blame to what lies wholly within voluntary control, and once we see how little that is, most ordinary blame turns out to be a mistake we ought to abandon.

On attempts, the conclusion is straightforward. The attempted murderer and the successful one differ only in outcome luck — the marksmanship of fate rather than anything in their wills — so differential punishment rests on the very confusion Nagel exposes. Punishing the two differently is therefore unjustifiable, and criminal law should grade offenses by intention and action alone, treating attempt and completion identically."
