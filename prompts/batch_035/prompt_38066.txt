i'm a retired geophysicist — 40 years of seismic imaging, so exponentials and logarithms are basically old friends, which is probably why the Kelly criterion is the corner of philosophy I've latched onto in retirement. (Never too late, apparently.) I want you to write a dialogue between my nephew and me. He's a true believer in Kelly betting — geometric growth rates, time averages versus ensemble averages, the whole ergodicity economics thing that a physicist named Peters keeps writing essays about and that shows up in my feed constantly. I'm the skeptic, or at least half of one.

Here's the setup. An eccentric old colleague from the lab has offered me a one-shot wager: a fair coin flip, heads I get back three times whatever I stake, tails the stake is gone. My pension pot is about $600,000, I'm 71, and there will be exactly one flip — no re-runs, no series of similar bets down the road. On paper the bet's favorable. The nephew insists Kelly reasoning still applies and I should stake roughly a quarter of the pot. I say one of two things must be true: either (a) my utility for money really is linear, in which case I should stake everything and Kelly is just an aesthetic preference he's dressing up as math, or (b) my utility isn't linear, in which case Kelly betting falls out of plain expected utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function — so he doesn't have a rival theory, just a hunch about the shape of my utility curve. He thinks this misses the point entirely — something about how going broke removes you from the game permanently, so even a single flip is really one draw from the long sequence of gambles that constitutes my remaining life, and time averages are what matter for a mortal who can't average over parallel copies of himself.

The scene is the two of us at my kitchen table over coffee, about 700 words. I want real back-and-forth, not two monologues. He should land some genuine hits on me — the ruin point, and maybe the observation that I obviously wouldn't accept a 50/50 to double-or-nothing the whole pension, which suggests my revealed preferences aren't linear in wealth and maybe aren't log either. I should land some on him — the collapse-into-EU point, and pressing him on what the "time average" of a bet that happens exactly once is even supposed to mean. Neither of us gets to be a strawman, and neither of us should win cleanly. End the dialogue with the two of us managing to state, precisely, the question we're actually disagreeing about. Then, separately from the dialogue, give me a short paragraph on whether you think that disagreement is substantive or merely verbal — my wife has heard enough about this at dinner and refuses to adjudicate.
