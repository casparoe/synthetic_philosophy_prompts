I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and I'd like a careful adjudication of a dispute that ran through most of our department's weekly logic-and-language reading group. The two disputants were a second-year, Dana, and a fifth-year, Evan, both of whom I thought argued well enough that I couldn't pick a winner.

The topic was the epistemicist versus supervaluationist treatments of vagueness. Dana opened for epistemicism: classical semantics with sharp boundaries is the only option that leaves logic, the T-schema, and compositional semantics untouched, and the apparent phenomena of vagueness are epistemological rather than semantic. In particular, margin-for-error principles explain why we can never locate the boundary of a vague predicate: if you are close to the cutoff for tall, then in a nearby possible world you would have the same beliefs while believing falsely, so no amount of comparison could ever reveal where the boundary lies.

Evan answered that principled unknowability is not something to be explained away but evidence that there is nothing there to know, and that Dana's explanation is circular: the safety condition quantifies over nearby worlds in which tall retains the same sharp extension, which is only licensed if the semantics is already epistemicist. He added that undetectable-in-principle cutoffs do no semantic work, and that theoretical virtue favors a semantics on which the inaccessibility of any boundary follows from the meaning of the predicate rather than from an accident of our epistemic limits.

Dana replied that invisible structure is fine when it buys explanatory power, and that supervaluationism buys its no-boundary verdict only by building a sharp boundary into every admissible precisification and then installing a definitely-operator to distance itself from what it built. Either the iterations of that operator terminate, in which case there is a sharp boundary between the definite and the borderline cases that is objectionable for exactly the original reasons, or they do not terminate, in which case the theory's own predictions outrun what its language can express. And, Dana noted, supervaluationism has to restrict the T-schema, revising something epistemicism preserves.

Evan conceded the T-schema point was real but minor, and argued that the iteration need not terminate in the object language: the definite operator's effect can converge the way nested intervals approximate a limit point without reaching it. Dana's final word was that a theory whose predictions are that unsurveyable is no better off empirically than the epistemicist's, and that when the choice goes idle, the preservation of classical logic should decide it. Evan's was that if the choice is idle, what matters is which theory best explains the pattern of competent speakers' assertions and denials about borderline cases, and that only a theory on which borderline ascriptions are neither true nor false predicts why speakers treat them as unsettled rather than as guesses about a hidden fact.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of Dana's and Evan's contributions, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me who, if either, is more right overall, and what both of them seem to be missing. I have my own suspicions about the last part, but I'd rather not tip them and prejudice your reading.
