Every spring I teach a grad seminar on corruption and the state, and one of my little rituals is running a deck of vignettes past colleagues to see where their line actually sits, since almost nobody's line is where they say it is. This year it was my comparative-politics colleague — years of fieldwork on clientelist machines in northeastern Brazil — and I'm now trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's running before I put the newest vignette to her next week. Her calls so far: (1) a machine politician trading food baskets and cooking-gas cylinders for votes the week before an election — corruption, no hesitation. (2) A mayor who steers pothole crews and new steetlights to the precincts that delivered for her, textbook politicized distribution — corruption. (3) A procurement official in Tokyo accepting the customary summer and year-end gifts from contractors, all within the official limits and with nothing ever asked in return — not corruption, that's just manners. (4) An MP in a patronage-heavy system who funnels discretionary development money to his home district because that's the job as everyone understands it and voters expect their share — long pause, then "bad goverance, maybe, but not corruption." (5) A Dutch council member who votes to rezone a parcel owned by her biggest campaign donor without ever disclosing the relationship — corruption. (6) A US senator inserting a narrowly tailored tax provision at a top donor's request — not corruption, "that's just politics, unfortunately."

The thing is, I can find at least two rules that fit all six verdicts: corruption as breach of the impartiality norms a society's institutions actually enforce (notice 3, 4, and 6 are normalized where they occur, while 1, 2, and 5 are not), and something cruder about formal legality (1, 2, and 5 involve rule-breaking; 3, 4, and 6 don't). The legality rule feels wrong to me and I can't quite say why yet, and I suspect there are other rules that fit her record that I'm not seeing. But the new vignette is where the candidates should split. In a mid-sized city I'll leave unnamed, every bidder on a municipal contract sends the customary gifts to the members of the evaluation committee — amounts are modest, ritualized, universal across bidders; the practice is formally illegal under the anti-corruption code but has never once been enforced, and nobody involved, including losing bidders, treats it as wrong. One committee member awards the contract to the lowest qualified bidder and keeps the gifts he received.

So: what's her call going to be? More than the prediction itself, I want the candidate rules that fit her track record, what each of them predicts on the gift-committee case, your best guess at her verdict with your reasons for preferring that reading of her, and the one further vignette you'd write to discriminate between the two or three strongest candidate rules as cleanly as possible. And since she's fond of muttering, after the second glass of wine, that maybe "corruption" is just the name for whatever offends a Weberian fantasy of the fully impersonal state that no actual political system has ever matched — be honest about what her ruling either way would tell us about whether the category is universal or parochial.
