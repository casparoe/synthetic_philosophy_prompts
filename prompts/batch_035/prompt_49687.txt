I am a high school senior writing my extended essay in physics on Maxwell's demon, and my supervisor has warned me against settling for the standard textbook narrative in which the demon is neatly defeated by Landauer's principle. I would like to develop a more specific worry, and I need someone to make the argument for it properly.

Here is the objection. Landauer's principle says that erasing one bit of memory while coupled to an environment at temperature T dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat. But suppose the demon operates only once: it measures a single fast molecule, opens the trapdoor, extracts a small quantity of work, and then simply never erases its record. The bit is written in a notebook that sits in a drawer for the next century. During that century, heat has flowed from cold to hot and work has been extracted, with no compensating erasure cost paid within the lifetime of the experiment. If the second law is a claim about what happens within a closed cycle, the demon appears to have violated it, because the supposed payment is deferred indefinitely and may never come due; the eventual reset that the textbook resolution presupposes looks like an unstated and unjustified assumption, since a demon with unbounded memory could defer it forever.

Please make the case that this one-shot objection shows the Landauer resolution, as it is usually presented, is incomplete, and that what actually protects the second law is something else: the observation that low-entropy memory is itself a thermodynamic resource, one the demon must have acquired at some earlier cost. On this view the demon should be understood not as a failed violator of the second law but as a heat engine that runs on information as fuel, spending negentropy it previously banked, so that its apparent profit is really a withdrawal from an account it had to pay into beforehand. In the course of the argument, please address the obvious rebuttal directly — that the demon's unbounded memory merely postpones rather than escapes the erasure cost — and explain why, if that rebuttal is all we have, it concedes that the second law holds only in an infinite-time average rather than as a constraint on what a single device can do in a single run.

I would like the argument to be rigorous enough to survive my supervisor's scrutiny, but pitched at a reader whose physics background ends at first-year thermodynamics, and I would prefer that it not merely recite the familiar historical sequence of names and dates attached to the thought experiment. Where the argument depends on an idealization — for instance, treating the measurement itself as thermodynamically reversible — please flag it and say what work the idealization is doing.
