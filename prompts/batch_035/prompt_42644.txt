Please argue for the following position about the meta-problem of consciousness: the meta-problem is not, as it is usually framed, neutral evidential common ground between physicalists and anti-physicalists, but is instead structurally biased toward physicalism. The reason is that any successful mechanistic explanation of why humans judge, report, and insist that there is a hard problem of consciousness automatically does double duty: it explains the reports, and thereby deflates the independent evidential force those reports were supposed to have, whereas the anti-physicalist has no analogous move available that does not simply reassert the appearances the physicalist is trying to explain away. In defending this claim, address the obvious rejoinder that the bias charge can be run in reverse — that a "phenomenal explanation" of why we believe in phenomenal consciousness would render our reports reliable rather than deflated, and that appealing to how things seem is not question-begging but the ordinary epistemic default. Say precisely where, if anywhere, the symmetry between the physicalist's explanation and this rejoinder breaks down, and be explicit if you conclude that it does not break anywhere. Do not treat the meta-problem as merely a research program for philosophers of mind; argue to a conclusion about what a rational physicalist and a rational anti-physicalist should each conclude if a complete, empirically adequate mechanistic account of our consciousness-related reports and judgments were achieved tomorrow.
