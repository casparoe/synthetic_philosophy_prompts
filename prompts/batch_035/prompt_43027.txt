Design an activity for my comparative political economy seminar -- 14 undergrads, one 90-minute session -- on the question of why some nations are rich. Here's what I want it to actually do: the students shouldn't be told the answer, they should run into the inference problem itself.

My rough idea (please make it concrete and runnable): split the room into maybe five "settlements" that play a simple multi-round game -- producing, trading, building stuff that pays off over time. You secretly vary three things across settlements: starting endowments (some get rich resource draws), rules (one settlement gets strong property rights, another has a rotating "raider" role that can confiscate accumulated stuff, etc.), and nothing for the last pair -- but whatever norms they happen to develop internally just get left alone. Overhead should be minimal: dice or a deck of cards, paper, a simple score sheet. No real money needed.

Then the twist, which is the whole point: a couple of students get pulled out as "economic historians" who only see the final scoreboard and maybe a few noisy "archival scraps" you hand them, and have to explain why the outcomes diverged. They can't watch the game and they can't ask for a rerun.

For the debrief: map each element of the game onto the real thing (endowments ~ geography, rules ~ institutions, whatever the low-intervention groups evolved ~ culture), and get the historians to say out loud what they'd need to know to settle their explanation, then show them why the game denies them exactly that. Crucially I want the moment where someone says "just run it again with different rules and see" and you point out that this is precisely the move history doesn't allow.

Also include: a realistic timeline for the session, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing the raider mechanic makes people genuinely mad, and the historians finish early and get bored -- tell me how to handle both), and an honest note on which parts of the real inference problem the game can't reproduce and I'll have to just assert in the debrief -- like the fact that in real history the "rule changes" themselves were endogenous to the geography and the culture, so the three channels aren't clean separate treatments the way they are in my game. If you can think of a way to muddy that even a little within the mechanics, bonus points.
