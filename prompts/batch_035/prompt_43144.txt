My logic seminar spent two weeks on Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and I've come away with an argument I can neither accept nor refute. In the dialogue, the Tortoise grants Achilles the premises of a Euclidean inference, and even the conditional connecting them to the conclusion, but still refuses to accept the conclusion, demanding that the connection be written down as a further premise — and each new premise faces the same demand, so the regress never terminates. The usual moral is that a rule of inference cannot itself function as a premise. My tutor thinks this moral, pushed a little, proves far more than anyone in the seminar wanted to concede. Here is the argument as I understand it:

1. The Tortoise shows that for any rule of inference, someone can accept the premises and the corresponding conditional and still withhold the conclusion.
2. The regress shows the gap can never be closed by adding propositions: each added premise generates the same demand.
3. So no rule of inference is justified by any set of propositions.
4. So accepting a basic rule such as modus ponens is not propositionally justified.
5. Perceptual and testimonial beliefs are also not propositionally justified — experiences and speakers are not premises but at most causes of belief — so basic inferential rules and basic perceptual updatings stand in the same epistemic position.
6. So whatever we say of one we must say of the other: either both are arbitrary leaps or both are justified in some further, non-propositional way.
7. If both are arbitrary leaps, then even my belief that two even numbers sum to an even number is an arbitrary leap.
8. I know that two even numbers sum to an even number.
9. So both are justified in some further way.
10. But a "justification" that cannot be stated, cannot be challenged, and cannot be handed to the Tortoise is not a justification at all — it is only a description of what we happen to do.
11. So either nothing is justified, or justification ultimately rests on something that cannot itself be assessed.

Something here has gone wrong, but I cannot locate it. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost — what do you give up, and who else has to be told? Please also say whether you read the argument as containing a quiet slide — for instance between being justified by propositions and being justified, or between unjustified and arbitrary — or as valid, in which case the choice is between two premises I'd rather keep. Lead with your bottom line: name the step you reject before you explain it. Keep the whole answer under 450 words, in American English.
