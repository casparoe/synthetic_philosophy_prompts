I've recently been appointed to the committee that administers the emergency hardship fund at my university — modest grants for students facing sudden financial crises — and I've been asked to draft the eligibility criteria, since our current guidelines just say the committee should exercise discretion. My main reference point for thinking this through is my closest friend, a philosophy graduate student who is a committed luck egalitarian and who, over the years, has issued verdicts on a long series of hypotheticals about when a fair assistance scheme would owe someone help. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- A person born with a serious cognitive disability that limits earning power: assistance owed.
- A person who could have worked but preferred to surf, and lives in deliberate poverty: nothing owed.
- A person who knowingly gambled at fair odds and lost their savings: nothing owed.
- A person who freely cultivated expensive tastes and now finds ordinary goods unbearable: nothing owed.
- A person raised by neglectful parents in a deprived town, never taught budgeting or encouraged in school, now unemployable as a foreseeable result: assistance owed.
- A person who understood a well-known flood risk, could have bought cheap insurance, declined, and was flooded out: nothing owed.

Our first live application under the new criteria is a student who grew up in an insular community deeply distrustful of conventional medicine. As a young adult she heard the standard case for vaccination and grasped the risks in outline, but declined — her hesitation is, by her own account, something she absorbed before she was in any position to question it — and she is now facing a long illness and its costs. Two committee members want to deny the claim as self-inflicted.

What would my friend say? I'm less interested in the verdict than in the reasoning behind it. At least two principles fit her six verdicts: a simple informed-choice rule, under which help is owed only where the disadvantage did not result from a voluntary, reasonably informed choice among accessible alternatives; and a background-fairness rule, under which help is owed where the judgment or preference itself was formed under unfair conditions, whatever the later choice looked like from inside. A third candidate strikes me as live as well: something like a hypothetical-insurance test, under which help is owed where prudent people who did not yet know their own circumstances would have chosen to pool the risk. These seem to agree on her six cases — though I have doubts about whether the insurance test really accommodates the flood one — and, I suspect, to diverge on ours. Which rule is she most plausibly running, what does each predict for our applicant, what is your best guess about her verdict and why, and what further case, if she ruled on it, would most cleanly divide the candidate rules from one another?
