Two of my roomates had a long argument last week about how the AI alignment problem should even be defined, and they asked me to referee, but honestly I got lost somewhere in the middle. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step -- not just who wins overall?

Some background first. One of them (Dan) had been reading about how some AI safety researchers use a narrow definition of alignment -- roughly, getting the AI system to do what its operator actually intends rather than something else ("intent alignment") -- while others use a broad definition, something like getting AI values to line up with human values generally ("value alignment"). That distinction was what the fight was about.

1. Dan went first: the narrow definition is the right one, he said, because it's a real engineering problem with a crisp success criterion -- the system does what the person directing it means. Value alignment, by contrast, is a philosophy problem that's been open for centuries, and there's no coherent object called "what humanity wants" to align to, so defining the field's central problem that way just guarantees it never gets solved.

2. Farah called this a bait and switch. An AI that perfectly does what its operator intends is exactly the dangerous thing, because one operator's intentions can be terrible for everyone else. If we solve intent alignment and hand the result to whoever gets there first, we've solved the wrong problem on purpose. The whole point of using the word "alignment," she said, was to suggest alignment with humanity, not alignment with whoever happens to be holding the controls.

3. Dan conceded the misuse point but said it applies to any powerful technology -- nobody says nuclear engineering is defined wrong because states can misuse reactors. The technical problem and the political question of who should have the technology are seperate questions, and cramming them together makes the technical one unsolvable. Besides, he said, intent alignment is probably on the critical path anyway: a system that understands what a person means well enough to reliably do it is most of the way to one that can figure out what humanity wants when asked.

4. Farah said that last part was exactly backwards. A system trained to treat "what is wanted" as whatever the operator wants is being trained to index its goals to whoever holds power over it. You're not 90% of the way to value alignment -- you've built a motivation structure that follows whoever has the keys, and then you're hoping the right people end up holding them. She also said the nuclear analogy fails because for genuinely transformative AI the "operator" isn't fixed in advance: the system might end up directed by a lab, by a coalition, by some patchwork of users, even by other AI systems, and "do what your operator intends" doesn't give you a stable target at all under that kind of uncertainty.

They started going around again on whether a definition of a problem can even be correct or incorrect versus just more or less useful, but by then it was 1am and everyone went to bed. Please adjudicate the four moves: who's right about what, and is there something both of them are missing?
