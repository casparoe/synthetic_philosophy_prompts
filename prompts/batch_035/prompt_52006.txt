I teach a second-year course on philosophical logic, and next week we're doing future contingents — Aristotle's sea battle, then Łukasiewicz's three-valued tables, then the usual fight about whether statements about the future are already true or lack truth values until they're settled. I want to end the course by reading Macbeth as the case study, and I'd like your help drafting the reading.

Here's what I want explored. The witches' prophecies are all future-tense statements, and every one of them comes true. But the way they come true is strange, and I think that strangness is where the play meets the sea-battle debate. The first set wins Macbeth over because part of it — the Cawdor title — is fulfilled straightaway, by other people's decisions, before Macbeth does anything; and it's that trivially confirmed part that makes him treat the rest (the kingship) as already settled. The kingship then comes true only because Macbeth, believing it, murders Duncan: a future that stayed contingent until the prediction made him close it. So is the play taking a side — is a prediction's being believed part of what makes it true, or does the play keep the two apart? And where does that leave the second set of prophecies — no man born of a woman shall harm him, safe until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane — which come true through equivocation (the caesarean birth, the branches used as camouflage) and so were, in a sense, true in advance without anyone's act except the moving of an army? That last pair feels like the actual sea battle: statements about the future that were true no matter what anyone deliberated. And Macbeth's response is to stop deliberating altogether — the tomorrow-and-tomorrow speech reads like the psychological endpoint of believing the future is closed: if all that will be is already true, why bother weighting anything?

So the question I want the reading to answer: does Macbeth dramatise the fatalist argument Aristotle was trying to block (if the prophecy is true now, Macbeth's hand is forced), or does it dramatise the gap the three-valued logician points at — a prediction can come true without the future's ever having been settled? Or does it show a third thing, namely that the whole debate about truth values misses the difference between a prediction's being true and its being believed? Banquo seems to be offering the play's own theory when he warns that the instruments of darkness tell you just enough truth to win you over and then betray you in the deepest consequence — but note that Banquo's prophecy, about his own descendants as kings, is the pure fatalist case: it comes true despite Macbeth's active attempts to falsify it, and nobody in the play ever lifts a finger to bring it about.

Be honest about where the reading strains. I'm told the Hecate material may be a later addition, and it'd be good to know whether the play, taken as a whole, ever really presents Macbeth as having alternatives — Lady Macbeth's goading, the dagger: are those choices or causes? Also feel free to say where the logic lenses simply don't grip the play. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the reading overall, flag where you're less sure, and single out the one point you'd least want to defend in front of a room full of logicians. Keep it lively enough that students who chose the course for the logic don't feel ambushed by a tragedy.
