I've come around to a version of Benatar's asymmetry and it's starting to feel a bit too comfortable, so I want someone to lay out what would actually count against it. To be clear about which version: I accept the core claim that the presence of pain is bad and its absence good, while the presence of pleasure is good but its absence not bad (unless someone is thereby deprived), and I take that to support the conclusion that coming into existence is always a net harm or at least never a benefit. But I've also bolted on a patch, because the standard complaints bugged me. The patch is that the asymmetry only ever applies to whole lives, never to stretches within a life -- so it tells you nothing about death, and the usual slippery slope from "never coming into existence is fine" to "dying is no loss" is supposed to be blocked. My friends think the patch is ad hoc, only there to save the view from its ugliest consequence, and honestly I don't have a great answer to that.

So here's what I'm asking for: a ranked list of defeaters for this patched view, from fatal down to merely awkward. I care less about the stock objections I've already seen rehearsed a hundred times and more about the structural stuff -- e.g. whether a rule that's allowed to apply only at the level of whole lives but is switched off the moment you look at parts of a life is even coherent, or whether my patch quietly turns the asymmetry into a non-comparative claim about lives and thereby gives up the ground it was standing on. Also: are there intuitions or cases the view simply cannot describe at all (I'm thinking about people who insist that someone deprived of a life of pleasure by never being conceived is, in some extended but not crazy sense, worse off)? And which of these would a serious defender of the asymmetry concede actually counts against them, versus wave off?

One more thing I'd like an honest read on: is my patched version falsifiable at all? It feels like every objection gets handled either by "the asymmetry doesn't apply there" or by "that's a whole-life claim, not a moment claim." If a view can survive like that, is that a fatal problem, or do views about population ethics just work differently than, say, empirical views, and insulation is sort of the price of admission? I'd rather know the truth here than win the argument with my friends, who by now are mostly annoyed.
