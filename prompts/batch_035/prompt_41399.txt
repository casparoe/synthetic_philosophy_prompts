For the past few months I have been trying to work out whether there is a genuine moral difference between lying and merely misleading someone with true statements, and rather than argue from theory I have been cataloguing my gut verdicts on concrete cases. I would like your help extracting the principle behind them, or a verdict that no coherent principle captures them all and some of my judgments must be abandoned.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. A lawyer, asked in settlement negotiations whether her client has a history of accounting fraud, answers that the client has never been found liable for accounting fraud, knowing that an enforcement action is currently pending. Not fine — this feels about as bad as a lie, and perhaps worse, because it is harder to call out.

2. Asked at a party whether I smoke, I say that I have not touched a cigarette in years, though I vape constantly. Fine, or close to fine.

3. A gate agent, asked whether the flight is leaving on time, says that it has not yet been cancelled, knowing it will be within the hour. Not fine.

4. My child asks whether Santa Claus is real, and I say that Santa is as real as the spirit of giving. Fine.

5. A friend asks whether the investment fund I am promoting is doing well, and I say that it outperformed the market last year, quietly selecting the one good year out of the last five. Not fine.

6. A colleague asks whether I am seeing anyone, and I say I have been too busy with work to date, when in fact I have been in a relationship for three months and simply do not want office gossip. Fine, I think, though I cannot articulate why this does not collapse into case 5.

7. When my aunt asks how I like the sweater she knitted, I say that the wool looks wonderfully soft. Fine.

8. A used-car dealer asked whether the car is in good condition answers that it passed its inspection last month, knowing the transmission is failing. Not fine — and noticeably worse than case 2, though the two have the same shape.

9. A realtor asked about damp answers that the house has been dry as a bone since she started listing it; she started two weeks ago, at the beginning of a dry spell, and the basement floods every winter. Not fine.

I have also noticed something that complicates any theory I might have: a witness who lies on the stand in a way the jury plainly sees through still seems to me to have done something seriously wrong, which suggests that the wrongness cannot consist solely in a false belief actually being produced.

My tentative principle is that the bad cases are the ones where the speaker profits from the resulting misunderstanding: the lawyer, the promoter, the dealer, and the realtor all stand to gain, whereas the vaper, the parent, and the sweater-recipient do not. But I can already see two problems with it. The gate agent in case 3 does not profit, yet I judge that case bad; and I profit in case 6 (I get my privacy), yet that case strikes me as fine.

So my request is this. Test my profit principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and state the resulting principle — or the best small set of candidate principles — precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Then apply it to three cases I have not considered: a doctor who answers a patient's question about whether the scan results are serious by saying only that she will want to run further tests, when she already strongly suspects a terminal diagnosis; a politician whose campaign promise is phrased so that it is technically satisfiable by nearly any outcome; and a defendant's spouse who, asked directly whether the defendant was home on the night in question, says that they always stay home on weeknights, which happens to be false on that one night. Finally, tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise the verdict or the principle.
