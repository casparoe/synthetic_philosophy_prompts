**Incompleteness Is the Halting Problem in a Nice Suit**

Every few months, a discussion of Gödel's incompleteness theorems escapes mathematics and gets applied to minds, science, morality, or the "limits of reason." The applications are almost always wrong, and I think the reason they go wrong is that people miss the engineering content of the theorems. The incompleteness theorems are not deep facts about truth or the human spirit. They are facts about self-reference in sufficiently expressive systems, and programmers have rediscovered them so many times that we have names for each rediscovery.

Start with the first theorem. A proof system for arithmetic is a program: checking whether a string of symbols is a valid proof is mechanically decidable. Provability is therefore semi-decidable — I can search for a proof and halt if I find one. Now do the standard diagonal move: via arithmetization, construct a sentence that says "I am not provable in this system." If the system proves it, the system is inconsistent; if the system is consistent, the sentence is unprovable — and, being unprovable, true. This is exactly the shape of the halting-problem proof, and exactly the shape of a quine. If you have ever written a program that prints its own source, you have touched the mechanism Gödel used. Rice's theorem — no non-trivial semantic property of programs is decidable — is the same phenomenon in its general form, arguably strictly more general than Gödel's theorem. If you understand why you can't write a perfect antivirus, you understand the first incompleteness theorem. (There is some Rosser-style refinement that weakens the consistency assumption required, but the engineering content is unchanged.)

The second theorem says a consistent system cannot prove its own consistency. Programmers meet this in self-hosting compilers and trusted computing bases. A compiler cannot fully verify itself using only the reasoning it implements: verification always bottoms out in a trusted core you must assume. This is the lesson of Ken Thompson's 1984 Turing Award lecture on the self-replicating compiler backdoor: at some point you trust something you did not verify. There are bootstrapping procedures, but they all presuppose a trusted seed. Incompleteness, for the working engineer, is the theorem that the trust chain cannot be closed from inside.

Now the misapplications. The most famous is the Lucas–Penrose family of arguments (Lucas's 1961 paper, Penrose's books from 1989 onward): since we can "see" that the Gödel sentence is true, human mathematicians are not algorithms. This fails for the same reason a buggy compiler still compiles code. Human mathematicians are not sound systems; we believe contradictions all the time, catch some of them, and ship the rest as published papers. An unsound system is not touched by the theorem, and no one has ever argued that human belief sets are consistent. The second misapplication is the "science can't prove its own foundations" line: science is not a formal system, no theorem about axiomatized arithmetic transfers to empirical methods, and a theory's incompleteness is a fact about its theorems, not about its users. The third is the humanities usage — "limits of reason," "undecidability of meaning" — which is poetry with a citation attached.

Here is my main claim, and the one I expect pushback on: since incompleteness attaches to any system expressive enough to encode its own proof predicate — which is to say, anything Turing-complete — it is a banal engineering fact, true of every programming language with recursion, and nothing about truth, minds, or knowledge follows from it. The theorem is a fence around formal systems, and the interesting question is always whether the thing under discussion is a formal system at all.
