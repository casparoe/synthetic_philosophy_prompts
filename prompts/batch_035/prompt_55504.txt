I serve on the standing committee that advises our national conservation authority on species listings. Under our legislation, protection and recovery funding attach to named species, so taxonomy is not academic for us: keeping two taxa separate pays for two recovery programs, and lumping them into one pays for one. A genetic study has just come before us on two protected forms — a lowland form and an upland form of the same genus. They interbreed along a contact zone and produce fertile hybrids, which suggests one species under the biological species concept; yet each form is genetically diagnosable and follows its own evolutionary trajectory, which suggests two species under a phylogenetic species concept. Our field biologists are split roughly down the middle, and the committee chair has proposed cutting the knot by simply deferring to whichever concept the latest genetic study implicitly uses.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — lump them, split them, or decouple the listing decision from the taxonomy altogether and protect both forms regardless of their rank — with reasons the committee can act on. In doing so it should engage the underlying philosophical question honestly: does our listing decision track where nature actually has joints, if it has any, or should it be driven by our conservation goals regardless of whether the species rank corresponds to anything in the world rather than only in our classificatory practices? Give the chair's proposal of deferring to whatever the newest study presupposes a fair hearing rather than dismissing it, since it has the virtue of being administratively workable.

One request about how you frame things: please keep what is settled separate from what is genuinely contested. For instance, it is settled that the two forms interbreed and that each is diagnosable; it is not settled that there is a single correct species concept, or that species must correspond to real kinds rather than being partly a matter of pragmatic choice — and the memo should not present the biological species concept, or any other, as the consensus definition of species when taxonomists and philosophers of biology have been divided on this for decades.
