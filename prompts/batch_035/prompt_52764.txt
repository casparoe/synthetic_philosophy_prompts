I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background beyond rationality-adjacent internet rabbit holes, and my sister is an econ grad. Over thanksgiving we got into a huge argument about sunk costs and it's turned into me drafting a mini-essay I'm thinking of putting on my team's blog. She thinks the essay is bad and I think she's dodging my best point, so I want a second opinion from someone who actually knows the philosophy and decision theory literature, since I clearly don't.

Here's the essay's argument as drafted: the textbook line is that rational choice only looks forward, sunk costs are definitionally irrelevant, and feeling bad about "wasting" two years is just a bias. Fine. But there are at least two places where honoring sunk costs looks rational in a fuller sense. First, you're stuck in a repeated game with your future self, your team, your spouse, whoever — and they all update on whether you finish what you start. A standing policy of bailing the moment the ledger tips red makes your commitments incredible in the literal sense, so a policy of honoring sunk costs might be how commitments become possible at all. Second, and this is the part I actually care about: maybe it's legitimate to have preferences about the shape of your life as a whole, not just its moment-to-moment contents. Walking away from a half-finished cathedral and walking away from a half-finished shed can have the exact same forward-looking profile and still differ rationally, because one leaves your life disfigured and the other doesn't. If that's right, then "honoring sunk costs" isn't always repairing a bias — sometimes it's caring about a real thing.

My sister's counter, which I suspect is the standard move, is to redescribe: reputation, morale, self-trust — those are all just future costs of quitting, so you put them in the ledger and the sunk cost "vanishes," everything becomes forward-looking again, and the bias diagnosis survives intact. She says this proves my argument is confused. My worry is that it proves the opposite: the redescription only feels decisive because it assumes the only legitimate reasons are forward-looking ones, which is the very thing at issue. Am I right that it's question-begging, or is that me being slippery? And honest question: is my second point (lives having shapes) doing real philosophical work, or is it just sentimentality with footnotes? I keep thinking of my team's failed framework migration — three years in, we all knew it would never pay off, but there was something that seemed to genuinely matter about not leaving the old system as a corpse we'd picked over. Be brutal. Where is the draft weakest, where would a philosopher say I'm smuggling stuff in, and is there a version of my argument that actually survives my sister's move? No citations needed, I just want the argument stress-tested before I embarrass myself publicly.
