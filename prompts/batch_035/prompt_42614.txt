I'm a PhD student in linguistics (corpus semantics) taking a philosophy of language seminar to shore up my background, and we just finished two weeks on Davidson. A chapter our instructor assigned — a general-audience piece from a companion volume on meaning — sums up Davidson's project of radical interpretation roughly like this: Davidson wanted to know how we could come to understand speakers of a completely unfamiliar language, and his answer was that interpretation is essentially translation: we pair the stranger's sentences with English sentences that get used in the same circumstances, and once enough pairings are in place, the meanings of their words are fixed. The only evidence an interpreter can go on is which sentences the speaker holds true in observable circumstances, so meaning turns out to be whatever is determined by patterns of assent — which, despite Davidson's protestations, makes him a sophisticated behaviorist about meaning. The chapter then says that his principle of charity completes the picture: since an interpreter must interpret the speaker so as to make most of their sentences come out true, the interpreter's own beliefs end up setting the standard for correct interpretation, and so Davidson is committed to the claim that we could never coherently discover that we ourselves are massively mistaken about the world — agreement with us is built into the very idea of interpretableness. It closes by noting that critics found this excessive and that Davidson later conceded the point, retreating to a weaker requirement that interpretations merely render the speaker intelligible overall.

Something about this bothers me, but I can't pin down where it goes wrong. I'm inclined to think the behaviorist label at least partly sticks — starting from nothing but assent-and-circumstance data looks like behaviorism in all but name, whatever Davidson said about mental states. And the massive-error part sounds like a genuinely embarrassing commitment rather than a distortion, though I could imagine a Davidsonian saying the chapter has smuggled something in. I'd rather not embarrass myself in the seminar discussion, so I want a careful audit.

Please act as a philosopher of language with deep expertise in Davidson. Take a moment to think through what Davidson actually claims before delivering verdicts, then go through the summary sentence by sentence: say what a Davidsonian would accept, what they'd accept only with qualifications, what is a common oversimplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong — in particular, whether reducing radical interpretation to translation and to assent-patterns misses anything structural about what an interpreter constructs, and whether the principle of charity really works the way the chapter describes. Then tell me whether the chapter's final claim — that Davidson conceded his critics' point and weakened charity to a mere intelligibility requirement — is accurate, and whether any of the criticisms the chapter treats as decisive actually follow from the parts of its summary that survive correction. Keep the whole thing under 600 words, please, and lead with a one-sentence verdict on whether the summary is, on balance, fair.
