I'm a TA for a big intro phil-of-science course and every year we do the probability interpretations unit and every year the students just memorize the labels — they'll tell you they're "Bayesians" because they like the math, or "frequentists" because they think the frequentist version is the "scientific" one, and it tells you nothing about what they actually believe. This year I want to flip it: a decision-tree quiz, in the spirit of those online sorting-hats, where the students answer questions about concrete cases and end up at whichever interpretation of probability their answers actually commit them to. The tree has to be built from the logical space first, not from the list of famous names — figure out what the genuinely dividing questions are, then locate the actual positions (frequentism, both the finite and the limiting-frequency flavors, propensity views, subjective Bayesianism, logical/objective Bayesian-ish views, best-system style accounts, and plain pluralism if someone wants to hold that probability is equivocal) as leaves, and note the answer-combinations nobody lands on and why (incoherent? just unattractive? unexplored?).

The diagnostic questions should be cases, not labels, and I'd like you to work at least these in somewhere (add others if you need them to make the tree close): a loaded die that will be rolled exactly once and then destroyed, so there's no long run even in principle — was there a fact about the probability of a six before the roll? A 40-year-old asking their probability of dying within a decade, where the reference class problem bites hard. A weather forecaster saying 70% chance of rain — does correctness live in calibration over many forecasts or in matching the actual physical state of the atmosphere that morning? A single photon at a half-silvered mirror, where the ignorance story seems to fail. A coin flip in a fully deterministic universe where a demon could tell you the outcome in advance. And an event with literally no ensemble behind it, like the first-ever asteroid of its kind. Also please flag honestly wherever your tree cannot separate two positions — I know e.g. that long-run propensity views and hypothetical-limiting-frequency views are gonna end up tangled on several of my cases, and I'd rather the handout admit that than paper over it.

Give me the whole thing as structured data, one JSON object with these fields: "tree" — an array of nodes, each with an "id", the "question" text (phrased so a student could answer it without knowing where it leads), and an "answers" object mapping each answer option to another node's id or a leaf id; "leaves" — an array where each entry has the "position_name", the "answer_path" (the chain of choices that gets you there), and "awkward_commitment" (one sentence on the most embarrassing thing you have to swallow to sit there); "unoccupied" — combinations of answers no named position takes, each with a one-line "diagnosis" of why nobody lives there; "indistinguishable_pairs" — leaf pairs the tree fails to separate, with which questions fail; and finally "self_assessment" with "overall_confidence" (a number from 0 to 1 for how confident you are in the tree as a fair sorting instrument) and "least_sure_point" (the single place you're most worried you made a questionable call, e.g. where you placed a case or how you dichotomized a question). Keep the question text informal, students should feel like they're taking a quiz not an exam. Watch your commas in the JSON, I'll be pasting it straight into a render script and its picky about syntax.
