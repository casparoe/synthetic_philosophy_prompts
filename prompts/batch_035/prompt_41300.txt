I run incident reviews at a big tech company. I've facilitated maybe a hundred postmortems at this point, and I'm fully bought into blameless postmortem culture: no root cause is ever a person, root causes are contributing factors, every human action gets traced back to conditions and incentives that shaped it, and the output is action items and system changes, never punishments. I'm fluent in that whole vocabulary -- contributing factors, latent conditions, defense in depth, error budgets, "fix the system not the blamee", severity reviews, follow-up tracking. No philosophy background though.

Philosophers keep saying that when an autonomous system -- a self-driving car, a lethal autonomous weapon, a medical triage algorithm -- causes a harm, there's a "responsibility gap": nobody controlled it in the way responsibility seems to require, so nobody is properly responsible, and that's supposed to be some deep problem. There's also apparently a whole literature of people arguing back that the gap can be closed, by locating control in designers, or in human operators staying "in the loop," or in distributing responsibility over institutions, or in what gets called "meaningful human control."

Restate that entire debate in the vocabulary I actually use. What does "nobody was in control in the responsibility-relevant sense" become when the only tools I have are contributing factors, action items, and systemic conditions? Does the gap thesis even look like a problem from where I stand, or does it look like a bad postmortem that stopped at "multiple contributing factors, no single root cause" and mistakenly concluded nobody should do anything? Translate the proposed closures one by one -- designer responsibility sounds like it might just be "shift left, catch it in design review," meaningful human control sounds like it might be "keep a human in the approval path," and so on. Say which translations are honest, which require distorting the engineering practice to make it fit, and which philosopher claims have no counterpart at all in how we run incident reviews.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually care about. My suspicion is that blameless postmortems have quietly solved the responsibility gap by changing the subject -- we replaced "who is responsible" with "what fails next" and the gap dissolved. But when I imagine a fatality caused by a fleet of self-driving cars with a blameless review process, there's something the postmortem vocabulary just can't say: the victim's family wants someone to answer, not action items, and "desert" isn't a word in my framework. So tell me straight: is the residue there because my vocabulary is missing something real, or because the philosophers are clinging to a concept (individual blameworthiness) that we already know how to live without? And if it's the former, where exactly does the postmortem framework go silent?
