I run a small MA-level seminar in formal epistemology — about a dozen students, all of whom have had at least a semester of probability theory — and we're finishing a unit on the common prior assumption. For the closing session I want a diagnostic handout rather than another reading: a decision tree, tentatively titled something like "Where do you actually stand on common priors?", that walks a student from a fixed starting point to a named position. The starting point is fixed: the student accepts Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem as valid mathematics — if two agents' posteriors on some event are common knowledge, and they started from a common prior, then those posterors must be equal. Everything after that is up for grabs, and the tree should be where the students discover what they already believe about whether the common prior assumption is a defensible idealization or a false one whose falsity matters.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: construct the branching questions first, and only afterwards locate the actual positions (and, where the combination is genuinely unoccupied, say so — with a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody holds it because it's incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Each node should be a question a student can answer without knowing where it leads, and wherever possible it should be posed as a concrete case rather than an abstract one. For example: two weather forecasters who have shared all their data and know each other's forecasts still disagree about tomorrow's rain — does the persistence of the disagreement show anything about their rationality? Or the trading case from the no-trade literature: if common priors held and willingness to trade were common knowledge, Milgrom and Stokey's result says Pareto-optimal allocations shouldn't be disturbed — so is the sheer volume of speculative trading in actual markets evidence against common priors, or is it just evidence that the idealizations never bind? The tree must force a confrontation with at least these pressure points: whether real agents' posteriors ever become common knowledge in the sense the theorem needs; whether rationality constrains initial credences at all, or only updating (the thought that a prior is a fiction, since no one has credences at birth, and that any attempt to justify one's prior from previous credences generates an infinite regress); whether disagreement at the level of priors is more like disagreement in tastes — the suggestion associated with Harsanyi, that differing priors are a legitimate source of persistent, faultless disagreement — or more like one party being drunk, i.e. something that licenses debunking; and whether the right response to the regress is to go hierarchical (priors over priors, a common pre-prior one level up) or to deny that the theorem's idealizations ever apply to us.

The leaves should include, at minimum: a strict Bayesian objectivism on which persistent disagreement after full disclosure proves someone is irrational; a permissivism about priors on which the assumption is false but harmlessly so, since prior-level disagreement can be faultless; the debunking leaf, whose most awkward commitment (that differing priors threaten to undercut learning from disagreement in general) you should state explicitly rather than leave implied; and the hierarchical escape. Aim for eight to twelve leaves total, each with a name — or an honest "unnamed, as far as I know" — and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate, rather than papering over the distinction. Keep the handout citation-free: positions should be described, not referenced, since it has to be usable standalone. Finally, add a short instructor's note telling me which branch you expect students to get stuck on, why, and what I should have ready to say when they do.
