Most conservation campaigns take it for granted that destroying habitat is bad for wild animals: they lose their homes, and many die. But I keep running into an argument that turns this on its head: if the typical wild animal's life contains more suffering then wellbeing (short lifespan, constant hunger and fear, most offspring get eaten or starve before adulthood), then a forest that's flattened and stays flattened means fewer such lives in the future, so less total suffering — so maybe habitat preservation actually increases suffering by guaranteeing whole future generations of miserable animals. Please give me the most important implications of taking this argument seriously for ordinary conservation decisions: protecting a wetland from development, rewilding old farmland, campaigns to save a species from extinction. Exactly five implications, as a numbered list, each item one or two sentences, and no intro or conclusion. Also, don't stay neutral on me: for each implication, say clearly whether you think it actually follows from the argument or where the reasoning goes wrong, and give your reason — I'd rather get definite answers I can push back on than a list of pros and cons.
