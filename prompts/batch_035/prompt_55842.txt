I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on epistemic modality and anti-realism, on whether the verificationist can survive Fitch's paradox of knowability. Before I start assessing the various escape routes people have proposed, I want to be clear about what a successful escape would actually look like -- so write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate response to the paradox must meet, with the case or consideration behind each.

As I understand the setup: from the knowability principle (if p is true, then it is possible that p is known), together with the factivity of knowledge and the claim that knowledge distributes over conjunction, one can derive that all truths are actually known -- which is absurd if there are any unknowable truths, and the conjunction of any truth p with the claim that p is never known seems to be just such a truth. The derivation itself is elementary, which is exactly why the responses to it need scrutiny. Here are some of the things I suspect a solution owes us, though I want you to correct, refine, or reject these:

1. A diagnosis, not a denial. The solution has to say where the argument fails -- which premise or which rule -- and say why that premise or rule looked irresistible. Simply denying the knowability principle while keeping everything else concedes too much: the anti-realist's whole position was that truth is epistemically constrained, and "some truths are unknowable, but not many" isn't a stable fallback unless the solution says which ones and why.

2. Non-triviality. A reformulated knowability principle has to retain real anti-realist content. If the fix is to restrict the principle to propositions that are already knowable, it becomes a tautology and vindicates no one. I want a requirement that formalizes this -- something like: the restricted principle, combined with the rest of the theory, must still rule out some metaphysical picture the anti-realist opposes (e.g., truths that obtain independently of any possible cognitive achievement).

3. Robustness to the generalization. The derivation doesn't use anything specific to knowledge: any operator that is factive and distributes over conjunction generates the same paradox, and there are analogues for provability and decidability in the literature. Should a solution have to work at this level of generality, or is it legitimate for it to exploit something distinctive about 'knows'? I genuinely don't know where this requirement should fall.

4. Logical economy. Some solutions revise the underlying logic (going intuitionist or paraconsistent, or restricting the classical rules used in the derivation). But many anti-realists were already intuitionists for independent reasons, and I've been told the derivation can be reconstructed to put pressure even there. A requirement that the solution not cost more logically than the anti-realist's existing commitments seem worth -- is that right, and how should it be stated?

5. Ad hocness. The restriction chosen should be motivated independently of the paradox. If the class of propositions to which knowability applies is carved out precisely to exclude Fitch-style conjunctions, that's suspicious in the same way a badly gerrymandered analysis is.

You should also tell me: which of these are non-negotiable and which are, honestly, just the preferences of one camp dressed up as constraints; whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable (in which case the list itself is the interesting result -- if the verificationist cannot have both non-triviality and classical logic, say, I want to know that up front); and which single requirement does the most to eliminate the main contenders -- I have in mind the Dummett-style restriction to decidable or basic propositions, Edgington's maneuver with the 'actually' operator, Tennant's restriction to what he calls cartesian propositions, the paraconsistency route, and flat-out denial of the knowability principle. Keep the scorecard to a short paragraph at the end; I care much more about the criteria.

Finally, since this is going into a paper where I'll be defending some of your verdicts: tell me how confident you are in each of the calls you make, and single out the one requirement you're least sure is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice -- and say what would show it to be one or the other.
