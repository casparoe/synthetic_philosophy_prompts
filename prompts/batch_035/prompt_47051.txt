For context: I am a retired physical chemist who, after four decades of thermodynamics, has spent the past few years reading seriously in metaphysics and the philosophy of language. I now record a small interview programme for the seminar series at my old institute; the audience consists mostly of graduate students who will tolerate technicality but not filler.

Next month's guest is a philosopher whose recent book defends a single-level answer to Bradley's regress. Her line, as I understand it, is this: the regress gets going only if you first picture a fact as separately available constituents lying side by side, which then need something extra to stick them together. If one never accepts that picture — if the tie relating a and b is not a further entity standing behind the fact but simply the fact's own articulation — then the demand for a further relation relating the relation to its relata never gets started. She extends the same treatment to the unity of the proposition: a proposition is not assembled out of independently given parts, so there is nothing left over, above the parts, for a binding relation to fail to relate.

I can follow all this well enough while reading, but I cannot yet phrase the objections crisply under the pressure of a live recording. Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a one-sentence follow-up anticipating her likeliest reply. Three of them must do specific work:

(1) One pressing the familiar charge that her answer relabels rather than solves — that calling the tie the fact's own articulation leaves as obscure as ever the very item the regress was supposed to illuminate — with a follow-up keyed to the response she is most likely to give, namely that the charge of obscurity presupposes exactly the compositional semantics she rejects.

(2) One on how the picture fares with the structured-looking cases: negative or general propositions, where even friends of the unity of the proposition concede that something inside it seems to need arrangement, not mere articulation.

(3) One anchored in the old observation that a list of the words in a sentence is not yet a claim — how does the sentence get beyond the list on her view, without quietly reintroducing the binding relation she has banished?

Distribute the remaining questions as you judge best. Keep the entire set — questions and follow-ups together — to 350 words or fewer.
