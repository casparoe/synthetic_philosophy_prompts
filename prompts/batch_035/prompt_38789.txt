I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level metaphysics seminar on the identity of indiscernibles, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree that starts from Max Black's two-sphere universe and walks a student to a named position on individuation. The point is diagnostic: each internal node should be a question about a concrete case that the standard positions actually come apart on, phrased so a student can answer it without already knowing which school it feeds. No node should ask "are you a haecceitist?" or "do you accept the PII?" — labels belong only at the leaves.

The cases I want on the tree, used wherever they do the most work:

- Black's original setup: a universe containing exactly two qualitatively identical spheres in otherwise empty space, one mile apart.
- The variant where the relational properties are made irrelevant: a universe that is a perfectly symmetric circle of indistinguishable spheres, so every sphere has the same purely relational profile as every other.
- An infinite lattice of identical spheres with perfect translational symmetry.
- Kant's mirror-image hands: two objects alike in every intrinsic and apparently every relational respect, yet supposedly discernible because one cannot be transformed into the other by rotation and translation.
- A single sphere alone in an otherwise empty universe, for probing whether bare numerical identity needs any ground at all.

The distinctions I want the tree to carve, in whatever order the logical space demands: strong versus weak versions of the principle; the response that relational differences alone (each sphere having its companion on a different side, or each occupying a different region of spacetime) suffice for discernibility; the response that spacetime positions or regions do the individuating, and whether that commits you to substantivalism about spacetime; the haecceitist line that the two-sphere world and a one-sphere-swap of it are genuinely distinct possibilities; primitive identity, on which there simply is no qualitative ground of numerical difference; and the deflationary or verificationist move, on which the question of whether there "really" are two spheres in the symmetric circle universe has no fact of the matter. Build the tree from the questions first and only then locate the named positions in the resulting space; note explicitly which cells of the logical space are unoccupied, and say whether each emptiness is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — for instance, if primitive identity and haecceitism end up answering every question identically, say so rather than manufacturing a fake fork.

Please deliver the tree as a JSON object with the following schema. A top-level field "nodes": an array of objects, each with "id" (a short string), "question" (the case-based question in plain language), "answers" (an array of the permitted answer labels, two or three per node, each with a one-sentence gloss of what someone choosing it is committing to), and "children" (a map from answer label to the id of the next node, or the string "LEAF:<leaf-id>"). A top-level field "leaves": an array of objects with "leaf_id", "position_name", "one_line_commitment" (the position's core claim, no citations or references needed), and "awkward_case" (the case above, or a minor variant of one, that the position handles worst). A top-level field "unoccupied_cells": an array of objects with "answer_path" and "diagnosis" (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored, with one sentence why). A top-level field "inseparable_pairs": an array of strings naming pairs of leaves the tree cannot distinguish and why. Keep the whole thing to roughly eight to twelve internal nodes and at least six leaves, and make sure the first question a student faces is about the original two-sphere universe, not an abstraction.
