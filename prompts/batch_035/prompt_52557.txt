At my university's forecasting and governance society we ran a session on futarchy last week. One member raised the objection that it is technocracy with extra steps: if speculators with deep pockets decide which policies get adopted, then even if ordinary citizens keep voting, the citizens have lost all say over the empirical premises of policy — they merely choose goals whose meaning is fixed by people with money at stake. The chair, who is fond of Robin Hanson's slogan 'vote on values, bet on beliefs', replied that the slogan settles the matter: democracy retains control of the ends, and only questions of means, questions of fact, are delegated to markets, so nothing important is lost.

I suspect the slogan is doing more, and less, work than that reply assumes, but I cannot prise it apart on my own. Please lay out, as a structured menu I can bring to our next session, the distinct precise claims 'vote on values, bet on beliefs' could be making, from the weakest reading that is a platitude through to the strongest that is substantive and contestable. Specifically, for 'vote on values': what 'values' might amount to, from a vague statement of national goals, through a legislated measurable welfare metric, to a complete ranking over social states — and for each reading, who has to do the work of converting it into something a market can condition on, and whether that conversion is itself a factual question the market was supposed to settle for us. For 'bet on beliefs': whether prediction markets merely forecast and advise a human decision-maker, whether market prices directly trigger the adoption of policy, or whether traders are rewarded only insofar as the policy they backed turns out well by the values metric — including the difference between forecasting an outcome outright and producing the conditional estimates that would be needed to compare adopting a policy with not adopting it. For each combination of readings, say whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, or straightforwardly false, and flag any genuine tension between the two halves of the slogan: can one keep the betting while dropping the voting, or the voting while dropping the betting, and what sort of regime does each combination actually amount to? Finally, the part I most need: say which reading, if any, genuinely answers the technocracy objection, which readings concede it, and which readings make the objection look misdirected because the 'values' citizens vote on are so thin that the promise of democratic control was never sincere. End with the single question I should put to the chair that would most quickly reveal which reading he is actually running.

Please write your answer in British English.
