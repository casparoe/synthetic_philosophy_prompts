I'm a software engineer — twelve years in the industry, mostly backend and infrastructure work, the last few years on ML-adjacent systems. I have no formal philosophy or economics training, so please bear with me if I'm using terms loosely.

A colleague of mine who follows AI forecasting closely walked me through an argument for a very fast takeoff, and I can't locate the flaw, though I'm confident there is one, because the argument seems to prove far too much. Here it is, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Sometime in the next several years we will have AI systems that match a competent human AI researcher on essentially every task that matters for AI research — reading the literature, forming hypotheses, writing and debugging the training and evaluation code, and interpreting results. Call the moment this happens "arrival."
2. After arrival, compute is the only scarce input to AI research. Running a copy of the system costs a few dollars per hour and the copies can run around the clock.
3. So immediately after arrival, a lab can field the equivalent of thousands of competent researchers working in parallel, at a cost that is trivial by R&D budget standards.
4. Research output will then be limited only by compute and by the serial depth of the problems — how many steps must be done one after another because each depends on the last.
5. Serial depth is small. The big breakthroughs — the ideas behind the current deep learning stack — look, in hindsight, like things a small team would have found within weeks once the surrounding context was in place. It's not like each one required a decade of necessarily sequential work.
6. Therefore, within a few months of arrival, we get the equivalent of decades of AI research.
7. Decades of AI research compressed into months means systems well beyond the arrival-level systems, which then improve the systems further, so the argument can be re-run from a higher starting point. Conclusion: roughly superhuman AI within a year or so of arrival — a very fast takeoff, driven almost entirely by software, with no new hardware and no new lab equipment.

Each step sounds defensible to me in isolation, and the conclusion isn't crazy. What bothers me is this: the skeleton of the argument — parallelize the researchers, note that the scarce input is cheap, claim the problems are serially shallow, infer explosive progress — looks like it should apply much more broadly, and in those other settings it predicts things that plainly didn't happen. Substitute "human organizations" for "copies": over the twentieth century, the population of researchers in most fields grew from thousands to millions, working in parallel at enormous aggregate cost, and by any account the landmark discoveries look shallow in hindsight. If steps (3) through (6) were sound as a general recipe, research output should have scaled at least linearly with researcher headcount and we should have seen something like a research explosion in every field that grew its workforce — but the people who study this seem to find strongly diminishing returns to R&D headcount, with output scaling much worse than linearly. So either something in the argument is specific to AI copies in a way that human research organizations are not, or one of the steps is just wrong.

My own suspicion is that the slide happens somewhere between (4) and (6) — that "decades of research" is doing some illegitimate work, because decades of median research is not the same as the decade or two of key discoveries, and because at any given moment most of what researchers do is exploration that gets thrown away. I also half-suspect (2), since I've watched plenty of projects where the engineers were not the bottleneck and the waiting was — experiment queueing, evaluation, hardware procurement. But I can't turn either suspicion into a precise objection.

So, my questions: Which premise or step should I reject? Why did that step look fine to me (and to my colleague)? And what does rejecting it cost — e.g., if the problem is that parallel copies are correlated rather than independent, so a thousand copies explore the same ideas and the argument only yields a modest speedup, does that push takeoff out by years or only months? If the problem is in (5) instead — hindsight bias about how shallow the breakthroughs were — what does the argument still establish? I'd like to keep the conclusion that takeoff is fast, if I honestly can, but I want to know what I have to give up to keep it, and what the corrected version of the argument actually shows.
