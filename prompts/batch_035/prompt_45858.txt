I'm on the service-planning committee at a mid-sized regional transit agency, and I need an actual decision procedure, not a philosophy reading group. Our committee meets monthly and votes on things like cutting low-ridership branches, relocating stops, shifting frequency between corridors, and changing fares — the kind of decisions where someone always ends up worse off and writes us an angry letter about it. Right now we basically muddle through with a utilitarian-ish gut sense (total rider-minutes saved, roughly) plus political pressure from whoever shows up angriest at the public comment session. My director recently read about Scanlon's contractualism and got very excited about the slogan that a policy is wrong if no one could reasonably reject it, and now she wants us to run our decisions through that instead. I sort of understand the idea but I've also heard there's a famous problem about aggregation — something about how contractualism refuses to just add up lots of small complaints to outweigh one person's massive one, and honestly that seems like it might tie our hands in exactly the cases we care most about.

So what I want from you is a procedure the committee can literally run at a meeting: the questions we ask about a proposal in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, at which point the procedure returns a clean no, and — this matters — where it gives up and hands the decision back to human judgment because the question is about values or severity comparisons that no checklist can settle for us. I want you to be honest about which steps are just judgment dressed up as procedure. It should tell us how to handle the stuff that makes this hard: complaints that differ enormously in severity (a wheelchair user losing their only accessible route vs. 20,000 commuters each losing 90 seconds), complaints that are only risks spread across unidentified people (running older buses that strand somebody somewhere a bit more often), and complaints from people who can't be individually named at all.

Then please run the procedure on three real-ish proposals of the kind we actually face: (1) eliminating a spur branch that serves about 40 disabled riders so we can add frequency on the main trunk for 20,000; (2) deferring fleet replacement for three years, which spreads a small chance of being stranded over every rider on every route; (3) cutting overnight service, where each affected rider is burdened a lot but there are few of them and the savings are real. For at least one of these I want the case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like and tells me so straight — I suspect it's the fleet one, since "you may each be slightly worse off" is exactly what contractualism is supposed to be picky about, but I'd like to see whether the procedure can even say that coherently. And finally tell me what the procedure flat out cannot do: where it will misfire, what kinds of decisions we should refuse to use it for, and whether the aggregation problem means my director's new favorite theory is just going to deadlock us in a room until midnight. Keep the philosophy load-bearing but readable — about half the committee are engineers, one is a retired bus driver, and nobody but the director has read a word of Scanlon.
