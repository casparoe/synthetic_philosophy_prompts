I am a student in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on ethical theory, currently covering consequentialism and its critics. For our midterm, the instructor has us peer-review an anonymized classmate's answer; each of us must assign a letter grade with pluses and minuses and write point-by-point comments that justify it, which the instructor will then compare against her own grading. Because our grade partly depends on how well we grade, I want to be careful not to let anything slide and not to penalize anything that is actually fine. Below are the exam question and my classmate's answer. Please act as my second opinion: tell me what grade you would assign and give the specific, point-by-point comments that justify it.

The exam question read:

Bernard Williams argued that utilitarianism, by treating agents as conduits for utility and by asserting what he called negative responsibility, fails to respect the integrity of individual agents. (a) Reconstruct the integrity objection, illustrating it with Williams's own cases. (b) Explain the point of Williams's one-thought-too-many remark. (c) Explain one consequentialist reply to the objection and assess whether it succeeds.

And here is my classmate's answer:

Williams's integrity objection is, at bottom, a demandingness complaint. Because the utilitarian counts all states of affairs someone can affect as equally within her responsibility, the theory will constantly require her to drop her own projects whenever promoting them does less good than promoting something else would. That is the real force of his principle of negative responsibility: the utilitarian is no less responsible for what he fails to prevent than for what he actually brings about, so the doing/allowing distinction does no moral work, and the floodgates open — every preventable evil in the world becomes his doing. Williams illustrates this with the case of Jim, a botanist who stumbles on a military officer, Pedro, who is about to execute twenty prisoners and offers Jim the chance to shoot one himself, in which case the other nineteen go free. Since utilitarianism is maximally demanding, it says Jim must shoot, since nineteen lives outweigh one; and Williams objects that the theory treats Jim's hands-on agency, his own will, as morally irrelevant. The deeper point is that a person's long-term projects and commitments give her life its character and meaning, and utilitarianism alienates her from them by making everything hostage to the best outcome.

Williams's one-thought-too-many remark is about Jim as well. Williams thinks that if Jim pauses to deliberate about whether to shoot — weighing his moral purity against the lives at stake — that hesitation is itself one thought too many. Williams's view is that Jim should simply act on instinct, and that immediate unreflective response is generally the mark of an integrated agent. The remark thus shows that Williams valued spontaneity over deliberation, and that he sided with the utilitarian conclusion about what Jim should do but not with the utilitarian's way of reaching it.

The best consequentialist reply is Peter Railton's sophisticated consequentialism, from his paper on alienation and consequentialism. Railton distinguishes the criterion of rightness from the procedure an agent should use in deliberation. A sophisticated consequentialist can decide cases by consulting her ordinary commitments, projects, and affections, and only assess outcomes from a detached, theoretical standpoint at a distance from daily life. Since a life structured by stable projects tends to produce the best consequences, the sophisticated consequentialist's projects are safe from constant trade-off, and she is not alienated from them. The reply therefore succeeds, and Williams's objection collapses: integrity is vindicated, but from within utilitarianism itself, which is all Williams needed.
