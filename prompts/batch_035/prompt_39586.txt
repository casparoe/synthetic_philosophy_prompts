My reading group has been going through Xunzi and one guy swears he's found the actual engine of the "human nature is bad" essay, not just the famous rhetoric about how we're born coveting and envious. He lays it out like this and I can't find the exit:

(1) You only desire what you lack. You don't desire the money already in your pocket, only the money you don't have. Desire tracks absence.
(2) Everyone keeps desiring goodness — even the genuinely cultivated are still striving after a goodness they don't yet possess. Nobody ever says they're finished with it.
(3) So goodness is not in anyone's nature; if it were, they couldn't desire it, any more than you can desire what's in your pocket.
(4) A nature with no goodness in it just is a bad nature. "Not good" and "bad" come to the same thing when you're talking about what's in you from the start.
(5) Therefore human nature is bad, and goodness has to come from somewhere outside nature — ritual, teachers, accumulated effort.

He adds that the first part of (1) is actually Xunzi's own move: people desire worth and goodness *because* they lack it, the way the poor desire wealth.

Here's what bugs me. If desire only tracks absence, then the desire to become good also tracks an absence, fine — but Xunzi also seems to think desires are just what we're born with, not something you learn or work at. So the desire for goodness is sitting in our nature, pointing at something that isn't. And now I can't tell whether (4) is allowed to say our nature is *bad* rather than merely *not yet good*, since something aimed at good is apparently in there. It feels like something slips between (3) and (4) but I can't name it, and I'm not even sure the slip isn't in (1) instead. Also, honestly, the argument seems to prove way too much — wouldn't it equally show our nature lacks anything we ever train at, even the stuff Xunzi is happy to call natural?

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd rather keep (5)'s picture of ritual and effort doing real work than defend some innate-goodness story, but I don't want to get there on an argument this fishy. And if you tell me its just equivocation over what "in your nature" means, spell out which premise reads it which way, because I keep going back and forth.
