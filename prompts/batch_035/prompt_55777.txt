**Question (10 marks):** A perfectly rational Bayesian agent is logically omniscient: it assigns probability 1 to anything derivable from what it already knows. Yet working mathematicians have intermediate degrees of confidence in open conjectures such as Goldbach's conjecture long before any proof or refulation is found. Explain why this is a problem for Bayesian epistemology as a model of real reasoners. Then describe how the logical induction framework of Garrabrant et al. (2016) approaches the problem: what the deductive process is, how the market of traders works, and what the logical induction criterion says. Finally, explain what the framework does and does not guarantee about the beliefs a logical inductor converges to, and whether it yields a practical method for settling open conjectures.

**Student's answer:** Logical uncertainty is uncertainty about facts whose truth values are already fixed. Whether Goldbach's conjecture holds was settled before anyone was around, so there's no possible observation to condition on; yet mathematicians are more confident in it than in its negation. A Bayesian agent can't represent this state: once it can prove something, its probability for it must be 1, and coherent probabilities over logically related sentences must respect all the consequences of what's known — logical omniscience. So Bayesianism models the ideal reasoner but not the working one.

The logical induction framework repairs this by modelling the reasoner as a market. Each day, the market posts a price between 0 and 1 for every sentence in the language of, say, Peano arithmetic; the price is the probability. A deductive process runs alongside and emits theorems day by day, as it finds them. Traders watch the prices and look for combinations that guarantee profit — a sentence priced above 1, say, or a provable theorem priced below something it implies. A market that traders can't systematically profit from satisfies the logical induction criterion: no computable trading strategy can extract unbounded returns from it in the limit. Because traders can detect any persistent pattern, the market's beliefs converge to the truth value of every sentence — if Goldbach is true its price goes to 1, and to 0 if false — so in principle running a logical inductor long enough would settle it. Nicely, the market even anticipates the deductive process: beliefs in theorems the prover will eventually state tend to 1 before the prover gets there (provability induction), and it learns to treat unpredictable-looking sequences statistically, e.g. giving about 10% probability to any given late digit of pi. Day to day, the market simply updates on the newly generated theorems as evidence, exactly like Bayesian conditioning, so the usual machinery applies unchanged.
