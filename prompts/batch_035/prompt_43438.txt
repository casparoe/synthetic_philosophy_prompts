I teach an upper-level seminar on ethics and security policy — 13 students, one 90-minute session left before spring break. Next topic is the ticking time bomb case, and half the class has already read the standard pro/con pieces, so a plain discussion will just be two camps reciting at each other. I want an activity instead.

The thing I want them to *feel*, without me announcing it upfront, is how much of the case's intuitive force is smuggled in by stipulation — the words "knows," "will," and "in time." The detainee is definitely guilty, definitely has the info, torture definitely works, nothing else would, no institutional consequences, no precedent set. In the real deliberations the case is modeling, every one of those is uncertain, and the classic intuition is partly a response to the uncertainty being subtracted.

My half-formed idea: a role-play of a crisis deliberation with realistic epistimic mess baked in — intel says 70% confident this is the right guy, the timer estimate is a range, there's a competing lead, someone's job is to worry about what it does to the service that starts doing this. Then a second round with the clean philosophy-textbook version of the same facts and see whether their votes move. But you design the actual thing: the roles (interrogator, agency lawyer, physician, the utilitarian advisor, whatever else it needs), handouts with what each role knows and doesn't, a timeline that keeps pressure on, and a decision procedure at the end where they have to actually vote and we keep score — meaning if they torture the wrong guy, or don't and the bomb goes off, that cost gets tallied, small stakes, just recorded publicly. Then the debrief: map each thing they quietly filled in during the messy round onto the corresponding stipulation in the clean version, and onto which side of the debate that stipulation was secretly working for. I want them to notice themselves that the "obvious" answer in round two was manufactured, not discovered.

Also tell me honestly: which part of the philosophical problem no classroom version can reproduce no matter how well I run it, and what will predictably go wrong the first time (my guesses: someone plays the interrogator as a cartoon villain, or the group waffles and refuses to vote at all — tell me if there are worse failure modes I haven't thought of). They shouldn't see the phrase "ticking time bomb" or any of the authors' names until the debrief — a few will recognize the setup anyway, that's fine, but the activity can't depend on them having read anything. No real money, no tech, everything printable from index cards and handouts.
