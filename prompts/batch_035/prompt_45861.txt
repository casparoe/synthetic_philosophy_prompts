I've been trying to get clear on the frame problem, and I've ended up unsure what, if anything, is actually still unsolved.

Here is my understanding. As McCarthy and Hayes originally posed it, the frame problem was a bookkeeping difficulty for logic-based AI: if you describe an action's effects with axioms, you apparently also need an enormous collection of frame axioms stating everything the action leaves untouched. But that version looks solved: circumscription and successor-state axioms let you say only what changes and get what doesn't for free. So the technical problem seems to have been a symptom of a poor representational choice, cured by better formal engineering.

Yet philosophers write as though the frame problem were a deep and possibly permanent feature of intelligence — Dennett's robot whose deliberations drag it through endless irrelevant consequences of each candidate action, Dreyfus insisting the problem sinks symbolic AI wholesale. My working hypothesis is that this is a case of a borrowed name. The philosophical problem, as I read it, is really the problem of relevance: deciding, among the indefinitely many true consequences of any action, which ones merit attention before acting. That was never a logic problem, so no amount of clever axiomatisation was ever going to touch it. If that's right, the persistence of the label is doing rhetorical work: it borrows the prestige of a formally sharp, solved-looking problem and attaches it to something fuzzier, and both enthusiasts and sceptics get to argue about whether 'the frame problem' has been solved by quietly trading on the ambiguity between the two.

So my questions. First, is my distinction between a solved bookkeeping problem and an unsolved relevance problem too clean? Where exactly does the logical version shade into the epistemological one — or did McCarthy and Hayes already see the deeper issue lurking in their own formalism? (I take the ramification and qualification problems to be local faces of the same underlying difficulty rather than distinct problems; correct me if that is sloppy.) Second, is the relevance version genuinely a problem for any intelligent system, including us, or does it only bite for systems that reason by explicit inference over explicit representations — in which case Dreyfus's diagnosis should be not that intelligence faces the frame problem but that a certain architecture does, which is a much weaker claim? Third, what should I make of the fact that modern learning systems act in real time with no explicit axioms and no obvious notion of a 'frame'? Either they have solved the philosophical problem empirically, which would suggest it was a matter of engineering tractability rather than principle, or they are solving a different problem while quietly inheriting whatever relevance their training distributions supply — in which case nothing general follows about the frame problem from their success. I'd like to know which, and what my borrowed-name hypothesis misses.
