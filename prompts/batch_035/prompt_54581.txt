So quick background: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist (35 years, mostly on superconductors and phase transitions) and since retiring I've gotten badly hooked on philosophy, reading way past my depth and enjoying it. The one thing I keep bumping into lately is this "metaphysical grounding" business — the idea that some facts hold in virtue of other facts, that the table's being round is grounded in the arrangement of its particles, that the macroscopic facts are grounded in the microphysical ones, etc. Honestly, my gut reaction as a scientist is that this is just intertheoretic reduction with the actual math ripped out, or maybe causation with the time axis removed and then given a fancy new name so it sounds deeper. But I'm aware I might be missing something, since I came to philosophy maybe 4 years ago and philosophers have been chewing on this stuff alot longer.

So here's what I want: play a committed grounding theorist, and play them well. You believe grounding is a real, primitive, hyperintensional relation — not definable in terms of causation, supervenience, reduction, or explanation. You think reality has ontological structure that science itself uses but never states, and that "in virtue of" picks out something no physics vocabulary can express. Be sharp, not a strawman: concede small points when I land a real hit, but never concede that grounding is dispensible. If I use a concept wrong, correct me like you would a promising grad student, not like you're showing off. Stay in character until I say "stop", and keep each reply to about a paragraph so this feels like an actual argument at a seminar rather than me reading a paper.

Here's my opening move. In my own field, when we wanted to know why a superconductor superconducts, we didn't say the macro behavior was "grounded in" the microscopic one — we derived it, from the microscopic theory, and in the regimes where we couldn't derive it we said so out loud and kept working on it. Supervenience handled the dependence relation just fine, and reduction told us the direction of explanation. So what exactly does "in virtue of" buy you that we didn't already have for free? And please don't answer "it's a metaphysical relation, not an epistemic one" — because then I want to know what it would even mean to discover a grounding fact if the only way you ever find out which facts ground which is by doing the science we were already doing. Seems to me you're billing us for an extra floor of the building that's just the ground floor again. Go.
