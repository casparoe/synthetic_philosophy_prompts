So I write about science and AI for a mid-size outlet, and lately every panel I sit on has someone -- usually a lab person, sometimes a VC, once a philosopher who should know better -- lean on the same move: water isn't wet per molecule, wetness shows up when you get enough molecules together, and likewise nothing fundamentally new is needed for intelligence, you just need enough of the stuff we're already scaling. It's doing a lot of work in the scaling debate, since if it's right, the "we don't know what we'd build" worry kind of evaporates -- it's the same old chemistry, just more of it. My editor loves the analogy ("finally a way to explain emergence to readers!") but something nags me, because from what I remember the water case is actually the poster child for successful *reduction* -- thermodynamics got swallowed by statistical mechanics, and the macro properties turned out to be exactly derivable, nothing left over. That's a funny thing to cite if your point is that something surprising and qualitatively new shows up when you cross a threshold. Can you audit the analogy for me before I put it in print? Give me what maps onto what (molecules, temperature, wetness, phase transitions, the amount-of-stuff axis -- I want the actual correspondence table), then where the mapping breaks, and crucially what it smuggles in: is "emergence" being used in the predictable-intertheoretic-reduction sense, the threshold/phase-transition sense, or the strong "principled novelty" sense, and does the water example quietly borrow credibility from whichever sense is easiest to prove? Then tell me straight whether the scaling conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy for the position these folks actually want to defend, I'd take that too. I have maybe 1500 words of print space and readers who are smart but haven't had a physics class since high school, so keep it usable -- and be honest if the honest answer is "the analogy is popular precisely because it can't be pinned down."
