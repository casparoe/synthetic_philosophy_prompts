I'm an undergraduate in a seminar this term on animal minds and animal ethics (cross-listed between philosophy and biology), and our final essay topic is the distribution of consciousness across animals. After three weeks on invertebrate sentience, my seminar partner and I have compared our numbers and found them embarrassingly far apart, so before I commit anything to an essay I want a second opinion with the reasoning fully laid bare.

Here is the proposition as we've been given it: decapod crustaceans — crabs, lobsters, crayfish — are sentient, where sentience means having valenced experience: there is something it is like to be them, and some of it feels good or bad. The evidence base we were assigned is the familiar package for decapods: nociception, protective attention to an injured area, motivational trade-offs (most famously the hermit crab experiments, where crabs abandon a good shell under electric shock more reluctantly than a poor one, and pay more to keep the better shell), lasting changes in feeding and behavior after injury, and stress- or anxiety-like states that reportedly respond to anti-anxiety drugs. Our professor is also fond of what he calls the shared-ledger test: the decisive marker of sentience, he argues, is not any single reflex but whether the animal can price different goods against one another on a common scale — shell quality against shock, shelter against food — since valence just is the currency in which such prices are quoted. My partner puts her credence that decapods are sentient at 0.75; mine is 0.35. We have since realized we may not even be answering the same question.

Give me your credence, decomposed. At minimum I want terms for: (a) the probability that phenomenal consciousness can be realized in a decentralized, ganglionic nervous system at all, rather than requiring a centralized, integrating brain; (b) the probability that the behavioral package described above is sufficient for felt experience rather than fully implementable without it — the decapod-zombie term; (c) the probability that sentience across taxa is graded rather than binary, and if it is graded, whether a sharp credence in "sentient or not" is even well-posed — if you think the question needs re-engineering, say so, and give your number for the re-engineered version; (d) the probability that the shared-ledger test is a good operationalization in the first place, since a functional comparator that weighs costs against benefits could, for all we know, run entirely in the dark; and (e) any further terms you think belong — for instance, whether the UK's 2022 extension of animal welfare law to decapods, following an academic evidence review, should count as any evidence at all, or merely as a political fact about how such reviews get received.

Structure your reply as follows. First, a section titled The question, made precise (roughly 100 words), stating how you are reading the proposition and whether you had to re-engineer it. Second, The decomposition (roughly 350 words): each term with a number and a one-line justification. Third, The combination (roughly 100 words): your combining rule, including what independence you are assuming, the final credence, and the main caveat. Fourth, The weakest joint (roughly 100 words): the term you are least confident in, and what discovery — neurophysiological, behavioral, or theoretical — would move your number the most. Fifth, Where our disagreement lives (roughly 150 words): given my partner at 0.75 and me at 0.35, which term we most plausibly differ on.

Please use real numbers throughout, even for terms that are barely knowable; refusing to quantify is the one thing I already know how to do. And no citations or reading lists — I have the seminar materials, and I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
