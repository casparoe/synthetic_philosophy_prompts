I've been vegan for a while for loosely utilitarian reasons, and over the past year I've found myself half-persuaded by the scale argument about insects. Roughly: there are on the order of 10^18 insects at any given moment (maybe more depending what you count), with lifespans of weeks, so even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is really low — a fraction of a percent — the expected amount of suffering in nature utterly dwarfs everything humans do to vertebrate livestock, and possibly everything humans do, full stop. My version is stronger than most people's: I think the argument survives even when the per-insect probability is so tiny that most people would just round it to zero, because the numbers are so astronomical that rounding off is precisely the mistake. So tell me what would count against this. I don't want the standard "insects probably aren't conscious" pushback rehearsed at me — I want the stuff that would actually move someone committed to the argument, with the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

A few things I suspect belong on the list but don't know how to weigh, so tell me if I'm picking the right ones: the fanaticism problem for expected-value reasoning when you multiply astronomically many beings by vanishingly small probabilities; the fact that "probability of sentience" isn't one number but a stack of separate uncertainties (whether valenced experience exists at all in that lineage, whether it needs particular neural machinery, whether their short lives even contain more suffering than pleasure) whose errors may not multiply together the way the argument needs; and whether moral uncertainty between utilitarian and non-utilitarian views breaks the argument or only softens it. Also, and this is the part I most want an honest answer on: is there a point where the argument's own defenders should concede it's become unfalsifiable — like, if no possible empirical discovery about insect cognition could push the expected value below the threshold where we're obligated to act, does that mean the argument isn't tracking the science at all, and is that a problem for it or just a feature of expected-value reasoning generally? If you think some of the objections I listed are more annoying than damaging, say so rather than flattering me. Keep it under 1000 words, lead with the single defeater you think bites hardest.
