Here's how I understand the tortoise argument, tell me if I have it straight. Achilles writes down premises A and "if A then B", and the Tortoise says fine, I accept both of those, but I still don't accept B. So Achilles adds "if A and (if A then B), then B" as a third premise. Tortoise accepts that too and still won't budge. And this can go forever, so no matter how many premises you add, you can never force the conclusion on someone who accepts all the premises.

So basically what Carroll proved is that modus ponens itself is unjustifiable — any attempt to justify it just adds more premises that the skeptic can accept while still withholding the conclusion. Which means deduction is in the same boat as induction, arguably worse: Hume at least left us habit, but here even the paradigm of good inference can't be defended without going in a circle.

Given that, I'm planning to tell my students that logic can't actually be normative, it's just a description of what most people happen to find compelling, and honestly I'm not sure why we make them memorize truth tables. My chair is going to have opinions about this, so before I go scorched earth: is the standard line just that circularity is fine when it's small enough? That seems like special pleading and I'd rather not teach it if there's a better response.
