Midterm Question 4, worth 20 marks, read as follows: "(a) Explain what cognitive penetration of perception is supposed to be and why it matters philosophically. (b) Consider the following line of thought: if a subject's beliefs or expectations can alter the phenomenal character of their experience, then perception is penetrated; and once perception is penetrated, experience can no longer play its traditional justificatory role, since what we experience would merely reflect what we already believe. One student's answer reads: "Cognitive penetration is the claim that what we believe, expect, or categorize can literally shape how things perceptually appear to us, and not merely what we conclude about them. The stakes are high, because if perception is penetrated then observation is theory-laden in a strong sense: two observers with different background beliefs could undergo different visual experiences of the same scene, which threatens the idea of perception as a neutral tribunal for settling disputes and puts pressure on Fodor's picture of input systems as informationally encapsulated. The notion must be distinguished from other top-down influences. If an expensive label makes a taster rate a wine more favorably, that may be a change in judgment or reporting rather than in how the wine tastes, and directing attention to one part of a display can change what gets processed without changing the character of the experience itself, so neither case establishes penetration. The classic New Look experiments by Bruner and Postman, in which subjects were slow to identify playing cards printed in incongruous colors, are suggestive but inconclusive for the same reason, since the data consist of reaction times and verbal reports, which cannot by themselves separate a change in perception from a change in judgment. A recurring worry about arguments for penetration is what Susanna Siegel calls the El Greco fallacy: the mistake of concluding, from a subject's inability to discriminate between two conditions behaviorally, that there is no perceptual difference between them, since a distortion operating equally in both conditions would be invisible from the inside. Finally, once we accept that expectations can alter the phenomenal character of experience, the epistemological consequences follow directly: what we experience merely reflects what we already believe, so experience can no longer provide independent justification for our beliefs, and perceptual justification collapses into circularity."
