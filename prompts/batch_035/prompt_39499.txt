I teach a bioethics elective on psychiatric advance directives, and for the unit on Ulysses contracts — self-binding arrangements in which a person, while competent, gives instructions that her future, compromised self must follow, after the model of Odysseus ordering his crew to keep him tied to the mast regardless of what he later says — I want to screen Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000) and read it as a sustained thought experiment about precommitment, in dialogue with the literature running from Elster's Ulysses and the Sirens through the constitutional-precommitment debate over entrenched rights and limits on amendment. The surface fit is obvious: Leonard, unable to form new long-term memories, uses tattoos, Polaroids, and notes to bind each of his future selves to a single mission and to protect them from manipulation. But the film's ending complicates the parallel in ways I want taken seriously rather than smoothed over, since Leonard, in his last lucid minutes, deliberately plants false evidence — copying Teddy's license plate as his next clue and scrawling a warning on Teddy's photograph not to trust him — so that his future self will kill Teddy and stay inside the comforting story he has chosen. Please develop a reading along the following lines, and say plainly where the film resists being an illustration.

First, does Leonard's system even count as a Ulysses contract, given that what he binds is not merely his future actions but his future beliefs? A mast-and-rope contract constrains what the bound self can do; Leonard's system curates what the bound self can know, which is closer to a one-man ministry of truth than to ropework. Is binding future evidence rather than future behavior a coherent extension of the precommitment idea, or a different and more sinister thing, and does the constitutional analogy (framers entrenching not just rules but a worldview) track this distinction?

Second, the standard defense of Ulysses contracts in both psychiatry and constitutional theory is that they extend rather than betray autonomy: the earlier, more competent self governs on behalf of the later one. The film attacks exactly this premise, because the self doing the binding is shown at his least trustworthy, doctoring the record seconds before he forgets. Does this show that precommitment presupposes a cross-temporal integrity that Leonard lacks — or something worse, that any binder can rig the game for those who come after, so that the legitimacy of a Ulysses contract depends on facts about the binder we usually cannot verify? Consider, if you find the reading defensible, the suggestion that Leonard has already rewritten even his remembered past (the possibility, hinted in the flashes of his wife, that the insulin story belongs to him rather than to Sammy Jankis): if memory itself is curated, then the authoritative past self in whose name the contract is made may be a fiction, which would trouble advance directives and constitutions alike.

Third, defenders of psychiatric self-binding worry about trapping a future self who has genuinely changed her mind or her values; the film gives this problem its most literal form, since the bound selves are not suffering rebels against the contract but apparently content executors of it. When the future self is happy inside the illusion the past self built, whose autonomy is the contract protecting — and is that question made easier or harder by the reversed structure of the color sequences, which leaves the audience as ignorant of each scene's cause as Leonard is?

Throughout, be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to a case study, and do not bend the precommitment literature to fit it. Tell me what the film dramatizes about Ulysses contracts that the arguments do not, and whether I can show it to future clinicians without misleading them about the ethics. Conclude with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by one of the labels "vindication," "condemnation," or "cautionary tale," giving your overall judgment of whether the film ultimately endorses or indicts self-binding.
