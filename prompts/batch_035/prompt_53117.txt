I'm a senior engineer at a ~40 person startup and I keep bumping into something I suspect philosophers have a name for, though I don't know what it is. A while back I read about an old debate between two guys — Clifford and James, I think — where one of them basically says it's always wrong to believe anything without enough evidence (his example is a shipowner who talks himself into believing his rusty ship is seaworthy, sends it out full of passengers, and it sinks), and the other says that's too strict, because sometimes you have to choose anyway and believing you'll succeed is part of what makes success happen. I don't want the philosophy lecture. I want the operating procedure.

The situation I actually face: over and over, someone on my team proposes that we treat an unverified thing as true. The third-party auth library is fine to ship because the senior dev vouches for it and nobody's done an independant audit. The founder wants everyone to believe we'll close the big enterprise deal by Q3 because "the team believing it is part of what makes it happen". We quietly assume our main competitor isn't building the same feature because it would be really inconvenient if they were. Every one of these is a belief we're adopting ahead of the evidence, and right now we decide by vibes and seniority.

So write me a decision procedure I can actually run in ten minutes: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no, go get the evidence first", and where it honestly hands off to a human because it's a values question and not a facts one. Somewhere it has to deal with the awkward bit my founder keeps exploiting — that sometimes believing p makes p more likely (morale, confidence, grit) and sometimes it just makes us feel good while p stays just as unlikely as it ever was — and I want it to handle that distinction for real, not wave at it. And at some point it has to face the fact that you can't exactly choose to believe things on command, since half the time "let's believe we'll hit the deadline" is really "let's all pretend and see". Run the procedure on the three cases above, and make sure at least one comes out with an answer I won't like — I'd rather the procedure embarass me than flatter me. Assume decisions above a certain stakes level go to the founders, the rest get signed off by me.

Two constraints on how you write it up. First, no formatting whatsoever — no bullet points, no headings, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose like a long email, and yes I realise that's an annoying way to deliver a checklist, live with it, write the steps as flowing sentences. Second, please seperate what's genuinely settled in this whole area from what people who study it for a living still argue about — if half the field thinks believing ahead of the evidence is sometimes fine and the other half thinks it's never fine, say that plainly rather than picking a side and dressing it up as the consensus. British English too if you don't mind, half my team sits in Manchester and they've started mocking my spellings.
