I'm TAing intro epistemology this semester and we just wrapped up the problem of induction — Hume, then Goodman's new riddle. The problem is my students treat grue as a toy: they hear it once, think "ok, that's an obviously gerrymandered predicate invented to cause trouble," file it away, and never feel the pressure of it. I want to fix that by handing out a case where the pressure runs the other way.

So here's what I want you to build me. Construct a scenario in which a grue-structured predicate — one defined partly by when or under what conditions something gets observed — is the entrenched, well-behaved, projectible predicate for an actual community of practitioners, while the predicate that looks natural and simple to us (the green-like one) is, from inside their practice, the weird disjunctive gerrymander that no serious person would project. Constraints: it has to be set in a realistic occupation, not a philosophy seminar and not an alien planet — I keep thinking about trades where some material genuinely behaves differently depending on conditions of inspection, like gemstones that shift color under different lighting, or dye batches that only reveal their final shade after curing, but pick whatever works; no stipulating by fiat that their inductive practice has worked, the flip has to fall out of their history and what they've had reason to care about; and no magic or exotic science. This is for teaching, so a scenario that just declares their predicate projectible fails — the verdicts have to be generated by the actual machinery, which is the whole point of the exercise.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough. Tell me what a straightforward Humean, a Goodmanian leaning on entrenchment, and someone who insists only natural, non-disjunctive properties are legitimately projectible should each say about the case, and note where the natural-properties person has a move available other than just biting a bullet — I suspect they'll want to say the community's predicate is secretly tracking a real underlying property, and I'd like to see whether that response actually goes through or just relabels the problem. Then identify which single feature of the case is doing all the work, what happens to the flip if you delete that feature, and — one twist I care about — whether the case could be used as evidence that entrenchment is epistemic idle wheel-spinning that merely tracks something else, like the past reliability of their usage, or whether the Goodmanian can win that exchange. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, scenario first, and write it so I can basically read it aloud in section without editing. My students are sharp but first-years, so no unexplained jargon, and please dont bother with citations, I'll handle references myself.
