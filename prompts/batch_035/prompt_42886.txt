In discussions of moral psychology I keep running into the analogy that moral foundations theorists themselves lean on: the human moral sense is like the tongue. Everyone is born with the same small set of receptors — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and perhaps liberty — just as everyone has the same taste buds. Political ideologies are then like cuisines: the left is a cuisine that seasons heavily with care and fairness while barely touching the others, while the right is one that uses the full range of flavors. The analogy gets pulled in two different directions. One camp concludes that moral psychology is purely descriptive, like food science: it can tell us why the left and right find different things palatable, but no more about who is right than a chemistry of taste receptors can tell us whether cilantro is delicious. The other camp draws a stronger inference: since the right engages a wider range of receptors, it has a broader moral palate, and its intuitions pick up morally real features of situations that the left literally cannot taste.

I'd like an audit of the analogy and of both conclusions it is being used to support. Please give me an explicit mapping — what plays the role of the receptor, the tongue, the individual variation in sensitivity, the cuisine, the culinary tradition, the trained palate of the food critic — and then identify where the mapping breaks. In sorting the disanalogies, I'd especially like you to attend to the ones that matter for the two uses above and to bracket the ones that don't. A few I have in mind, though you should add and weigh your own: taste receptors plausibly have no correctness conditions — there is no fact about what caramel really tastes like — whereas moral foundations at least purport to detect real things (suffering, cheaters, pathogens, betrayal), which would make them less like pure preference and more like perception, which can be veridical or biased; cuisines are acquired preferences, while the theory claims the foundations are innate, which puts pressure on which side of the analogy the ideology is supposed to sit on; and more channels is not the same as more accuracy — a suspicious person also uses more channels than a trusting one. Relatedly, tell me what each camp's conclusion smuggles in: whether the food-science reading quietly imports taste's lack of correctness conditions, and whether the broader-palate reading quietly equates engaging more foundations with tracking more moral truth.

Finally, take a committed position. Does the analogy, once its disanalogies are on the table, still support the claim that moral foundations theory is normatively neutral — that it can explain our disagreements but never adjudicate them — or does that neutrality claim only stand if certain disanalogies are emphasized and others suppressed, in which case the analogy was doing philosophical work it wasn't entitled to? And is there a better analogy available — one that preserves what's true in the taste picture while leaving room for the possibility that some palates are distorted — or does every attempt to keep the descriptive psychology separate from the normative verdict collapse one way or the other? Please argue for one answer rather than leaving the options open.
