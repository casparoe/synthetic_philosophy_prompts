Here is a question I'd like your actual credence on, with the reasoning laid open rather than buried. Consider a fully specified Buridan case: an agent faces two options that are exactly symmetric with respect to every consideration she cares about — her preferences between them are not merely balanced but strictly identical, no feature of either option differentiates it even minutely, and no third option (such as delaying, or choosing by some further goal) is available. The proposition I want your probability for: in such a case, selecting one option by arbitrary selection — flipping a coin, applying a tie-breaking rule adopted before the tie arose, or simply "just picking" one — is rational, and the resulting act is an exercise of the agent's agency rather than a failure of it.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions, assigning a rough probability and one line of justification to each:

1. That perfectly symmetric choice situations are genuinely realizable for rational agents at all, rather than being an idealization that any real agent's noisy or incomplete preferences would always break somewhere.
2. That rationality permits, and does not merely tolerate, choosing without a reason for the particular option chosen — that "rational" does not require a preference-grounded reason at the level of the individual option, only at the level of the choice situation.
3. That the three routes above are on a par, or whether they come apart. In particular: whether a pre-adopted rule like "take the left option when tied" smuggles in a preference that destroys the symmetry it was meant to break (if she prefers left-on-ties, were the options ever really tied?); and whether "just picking" with no procedure at all is even coherent as an intentional action, or whether it collapses into something that merely happens to the agent.
4. That an agent in a perfect tie who has no tie-breaking device and simply fails to act, and starves, is thereby irrational — as opposed to the case functioning as a reductio showing that our concept of rational choice silently presupposes that ties never occur.
5. Whatever further terms you think belong in the derivation, if any.

Show how the terms combine into your overall number for the proposition, and tell me which single term the final credence is most sensitive to. Then say explicitly how confident you are in the overall verdict and single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure of. Finally, name the one argument or consideration that would move your number the most in either direction. I am not interested in "it depends on your theory of rationality" as an answer — make the dependencies explicit and then commit to the number anyway, since that is what the decomposition is for.
