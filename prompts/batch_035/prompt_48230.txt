I'm a psychiatry resident taking a bioethics course, and I keep snagging on what our institutions actually assume about whether my earlier self can speak for my later self. Consider three features of practice that everyone in the hospital recognizes. First, in ordinary medicine: a woman, while competent, writes a living will refusing artificial nutrition should she ever have advanced dementia. Decades later, demented, she seems content — she smiles at visitors, enjoys music, eats willingly when offered — and nobody doubts she would accept food. Yet the directive is honored: the staff withhold or withdraw the tube feeding, and nearly everyone in the practice treats this not merely as permitted but as required, as a way of respecting her. Second, the demand side: had she instead written that she should be kept alive on a ventilator at all costs, whatever the doctors say, the directive would not bind — in somatic medicine an advance directive can refuse treatment but cannot compel it against clinical judgment. Third, closest to my daily work: some patients with psychotic illness, while lucid, sign psychiatric advance directives of a self-binding, Ulysses-contract kind — if I am psychotic and refuse medication, treat me over my objection. Some clinicians honor these during the episode, even over the violent protest of a patient screaming that he doesn't want it, on the grounds that this is what he asked for; others refuse, and in most jurisdictions a directive cannot compel treatment the treating team does not itself endorse. What strikes me is that the self-binding psychiatric case feels far more fraught to most people than the dementia case, even though in both an earlier, competent self gave an instruction that a later, incompetent self visibly contradicts, and in both the earlier self's word prevails over the later self's wishes.

So here is my question: what theory of the authority of the earlier self does this pattern of practice presuppose? What view of the relation between the earlier and the later self makes it the case that the woman's earlier refusal should trump her demented self's contented continuation, or that the man's earlier signature should trump his present screaming — while also explaining why the demand side doesn't bind, and why the psychiatric self-binding case strikes so many as harder to justify than the dementia case? Some candidate views worth distinguishing, at least: that the earlier competent self just is the patient in a fuller sense than the later incompetent one (a metaphysical claim about identity over time); that directives protect the critical interests in a life going a certain way, which only a competent self can grasp, so that the later self's merely experiential interests can be overridden; that the earlier self's authority is really a protective arrangement made on the later self's behalf — a contract with future caregivers — rather than the later self being bound at all; or that the practice rests simply on distrust of incompetent wishes, taking them to express the disease rather than the person. Reconstruct the implicit theory as faithfully as you can, mark where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (compatible with several of these views), and identify where its parts presuppose inconsistent things. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which the later self's interests count in their own right, whoever the later self turns out to be.

For format: give the main analysis as prose, then follow it with a table with these columns — "Feature of the practice" (one row per element: advance refusal honored; advance demand non-binding; psychiatric self-binding honored only over objection; present competent refusal sovereign); "What it presupposes about the earlier self's authority"; "Rival views it quietly rules out"; and "Strictly required or underdetermined?", meaning whether several of the candidate views could justify that feature or only one. Include a final row covering the practice as a whole, and close with a one-paragraph verdict on whether the implicit theory comes out coherent.
