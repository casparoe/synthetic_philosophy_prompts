For a graduate seminar paper I'm drafting on W. E. B. Du Bois as a literary gatekeeper, I want to work out what rule actually generates his editorial verdicts on works of fiction, rather than the rule he said he was following, before I commit to an interpretation. His track record, as I've assembled it from The Crisis and his essays: he condemned Thomas Dixon Jr.'s The Clansman as mendacious pro-Klan propaganda; in the 1926 Crisis symposium on how the Negro should be portrayed in art, he pressed writers toward truthful depictions serving the race's own purposes and was suspicious of portraits shaped by white approval; in the essay Criteria of Negro Art he declared that all art is propaganda, while insisting that truthful ugliness, suffering, and heroism all belong in the picture so long as the work reveals the race's humanity; he reviewed Claude McKay's Home to Harlem in 1928 with something close to disgust, reporting that reading it left him feeling like he needed a bath, and he was plainly irritated that white readers were devouring its cabarets, casual sex, and shiftlessness; and, as far as I can tell, he was reliably warm toward Jessie Fauset's fiction of striving Black middle-class life.

The test case I want to pose is hypothetical, so treat it as fully specified rather than looking for it. Suppose it is 1929 and a first novel, Red Dirt Sunday, arrives: a naturalist novel by a young Black writer about a Mississippi Delta sharecropper family crushed by debt peonage, gambling, colorism, a hypocritical preacher, and finally a white mob. The prose is genuinely distinguished, the indictment of the plantation economy is unmistakable and sincere, and there is one thin counterweight of aspiration, a daughter who dreams of Fisk. There is no uplift arc and no organized resistance. The book is issued by a major New York house, sells overwhelmingly to white readers, and is celebrated by white critics as raw, authentic, and refreshingly free of what one of them calls the propaganda novel.

What would Du Bois's verdict be? I'm less interested in the answer than in the machinery: identify at least two rules that fit the track record equally well — for instance, one keyed to whether the work truthfully advances the race's cause, one keyed to whose appetite it feeds and whose praise it wins — and say what each predicts about Red Dirt Sunday, since they seem to me to diverge on it. Then give your best guess and the reasons for preferring it, and tell me what further case, or what minimal change to this one, would most cleanly separate the candidate rules. I'd also like to know if there's a verdict he could render here that his track record genuinely underdetermines, and what principle he'd be committing himself to either way.
