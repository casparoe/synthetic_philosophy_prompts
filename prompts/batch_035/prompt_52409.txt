I teach a sophomore-level course on democratic theory, and in two weeks I hit the unit on the epistemic case for majority rule — Condorcet's jury theorem, the miracle-of-aggregation stuff, and the ways people have tried to extend or attack it since. My seminar has 17 students, one 75-minute block, movable desks, and a whiteboard. I want to run it as an activity rather than a lecture: they should feel the aggregation result happen to them before they ever see the formula. Design the whole thing for me — setup, materials, rules, timeline, how to tally results, and the debrief.

Here's roughly what I imagine working, but you should feel free to override me: a bunch of rounds of binary quiz questions (stuff with a right answer, but hard enough that individuals are maybe 60-70% right), everyone answers privately each round, and we track two running scores on the board — the median individual and the majority. Then at some point I want them to see the size effect somehow, maybe by drawing random sub-committees of 3 or 5 and comparing how often those are right against the whole group. And crucially I want at least one round where it breaks: something that gets everyone's errors correlated — maybe a question with a built-in misleading cue, or a planted biased source everyone consults — so they watch the majority confidently march off a cliff together. If you can think of a clean way to induce correlation without it feeling rigged, that's the part I'm least sure about.

For payoffs: no real money, but candy or something on the board leaderboard is fine. I want them to answer sincerely though, so nothing that rewards strategic voting until we get to the debrief, where I'd like to talk about what sincerity is even doing as an assumption.

Also two asks on top of the design itself. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — herding, people copying, the questions accidentally being too easy, whatever — and how I should handle each. Second, be honest about where the activity can only gesture at the real theorem: 17 students and a couple dozen rounds can't show asymptotics, and I assume there are other gaps I haven't thought of. And since I might be overcommitted to my subgroup-comparison idea: tell me which part of the design is most likely to flop or actively mislead, and what evidence during the session would make you scrap or swap that part on the fly. If you think one of my rounds teaches the wrong lesson, say so.
