I run a tiny newsletter on memory politics stuff and I want to do one of those "which position do you actually hold?" quizzes, but about the duty to remember vs. the case for letting things fade (the Margalit / Rieff corner of things). Please write me the quiz: 8 questions, each one a concrete case with 2-3 answer options, and then a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto positions.

The positions that should show up as leaves: (a) the full-on "never forget" maximalist who thinks every grave wrong creates a standing duty of remembrance, (b) the Margalit-style view that there's a real duty to remember but only for radical evil / crimes against humanity, while ordinary injustices call for memory without obligation, (c) the Rieff-style pragmatist who thinks remembrance often feeds grievance and that sometimes the morally better move is to let go, (d) a reconciliation-first view where the value of memory is entirely instrumental, so it stays only as long as it serves repair between groups, and (e) what I privately call the archivist position, for people who split remembering-as-knowledge from remembering-as-commemoration and only ever want to defend the first one.

Ground rules for the questions. They should be cases the reader can answer without knowing any of the labels above. At least one question should turn on the knowledge vs. ritual commemoration split (my archivist friends always out themselves here). At least one should use the distinction between a community remembering something that happened to it vs. an outside community having any duty at all. And at least one should be about digital stuff, because I suspect people's intuitions there are totally different from their intuitions about monuments and anniversaries. For that one use this case I made up: a memorial platform where each victim of a disaster gets a page, and the designers are debating whether to add a feature where, after 25 years, the pages get demoted from search and become reachable only if someone goes looking on purpose — not deleted, just harder to stumble into. People can vote yes, no, or "yes, but only once the affected community itself flips the switch." I think that third option is doing real work sorting people.

Also please include one question about a truth-and-reconciliation style amnesty-for-testimony arrangement, since the reconciliation crowd and the Rieff crowd give opposite answers there and it's the cleanest separator I know of.

Two more things. First, after the scoring key, add a short paragraph on combinations of answers that don't map onto any of the five positions (e.g. someone who says there's a duty to remember everything but also votes for the 25-year demotion thing) — say whether you think that combination is incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored. Second, flag any pair of positions your questions can't actually seperate, if there is one.

Don't just ask people things like "do you agree with Margalit?" — the whole point is sorting by what they say about cases, not by which name they like. No citations or reading lists, I just want the quiz itself. Keep the whole thing (questions + key + notes) under 1000 words. Casual register is fine, it's a newsletter not a journal.
