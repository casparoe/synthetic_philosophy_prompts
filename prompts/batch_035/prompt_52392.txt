I write a column on mathematics and ideas for a general-interest science magazine, and a recent piece on the two-envelope paradox has brought in a flood of letters proposing variants. I find I have settled verdicts on most of them, but I can't locate the principle behind my verdicts, and a couple of them sit awkwardly together. In the plain case — two envelopes, one holding twice as much as the other, no information about the amounts — I think swapping is obviously pointless: whatever calculation makes the other envelope look worth 1.25 times mine can be run just as well from the other side, and I take the endless swap-back-and-forth regress to show that the calculation is broken from the start, not merely that it can't be iterated. Yet if I'm allowed to open my envelope and find $100, I feel a real pull toward swapping, since the other envelope now holds either $50 or $200 — and I feel this pull at pretty much any amount I might see. If I'm told the organizer never puts more than $300 in an envelope and I've opened mine to find $250, I flatly refuse to swap. If instead the organizer tells me he filled my envelope with $100 and then flipped a fair coin to decide whether the other holds $50 or $200, I think swapping really is advantageous, even though from where I sit the situation looks no different from the plain case. And one reader proposes a version in which the amounts are generated by repeated coin flips, doubling on heads, so that the expected value of each envelope is infinite; there I honestly don't know what to say, except that something seems to go wrong before any expected gain can be counted. Is there a single principle about when the expected-value argument for swapping is legitimate that captures all of this — in particular, can my pull toward swapping after seeing $100 be reconciled with my refusal to accept the argument before opening? What does the best principle say about the infinite case, and if some of my verdicts have to go, which ones should I give up and why?
