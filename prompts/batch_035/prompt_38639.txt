I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I read a fair amount of rationalist-adjacent writing on decision theory, and one puzzle has been living in my head rent-free lately: Parfit's hitchhiker, the thought experiment Derek Parfit used in Reasons and Persons, where a dying driver rescues a stranded hitchhiker in exchange for a promised reward at the next town. Once the hitchhiker has been saved, handing over the money is irrational from the vantage point of that moment, so a perfectly rational hitchhiker can never make a credible promise to pay — and the driver, knowing this, drives on by. My friends summarize the lesson with phrases like "rational irrationality" and "the ability to bind yourself is sometimes worth more than being able to change your mind," and I can never quite tell which precise claim is being made when they say it. I understand the one-boxing debates around Newcomb's problem well enough, so I'm not asking for the basics — I'm asking for the version of the argument that actually goes through.

Last weekend I rewatched Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove for the first time in years and realized it might be the same problem in cinematic form, and I want to develop this properly because I'm leading a film-and-philosophy night at my office. The obvious case is the Soviet doomsday machine: Ambassador de Sadesky reveals that the USSR has built a device that will automatically annihilate all life on earth if the country is attacked, precisely so that no human being — no wavering, bribable, rational human being — can ever be in a position to decide whether to retaliate. That looks like a textbook commitment device solving a Parfit-style credibility problem: the threat is believable exactly because the decision has been removed from every future agent who might be rational not to carry it out. Yet Strangelove's own diagnosis in the War Room is that the machine is worthless as a deterrent unless the adversary knows about it in advance, which is a strangely narrow complaint — and the film seems simultaneously to treat the machine as decision-theoretically elegant and as the ultimate act of collective insanity. And then there is General Ripper, who to me is the mirror image of the machine: a man who has irrevocably committed to a mad course of action and therefore cannot be argued, threatened, or reasoned out of it, which makes him immune to the entire machinery of deterrence that is supposed to keep the peace. The whole film is an ensemble of agents whose fixed intentions — some institutional, some mechanical, some deranged — do all the causal work that rational responsiveness cannot.

Please read the film through the lens of Parfit's hitchhiker and rational irrationality. Does the film know the difference, and does it ultimately take a position on whether the doomsday machine is the rational answer to Parfit's hitchhiker or the reductio of the whole way of thinking?
