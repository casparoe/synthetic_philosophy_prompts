I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a column on a finding from experimental philosophy: Joshua Knobe's so-called side-effect effect. In the best-known version, people are told about a chairman who proceeds with a profitable program while merely foreseeing, and not caring about, some resulting environmental harm; most judge that he brought the harm about intentionally. Yet when the same vignette is changed only so that the program foreseeably benefits the environment as a side effect, most deny that he brought the benefit about intentionally. The asymmetry is striking because intentionality appears to track whether the outcome is bad, not what the agent was aiming at. I want to set this against the doctrine of double effect, the traditional principle from just war theory and medical ethics holding that harm foreseen as a side effect of an otherwise legitimate act can be permissible where equivalent harm aimed at as a means would not be. The two seem to pull in opposite directions: the Knobe finding suggests that ordinary people erase the intended/foreseen distinction for bad outcomes, while the doctrine insists that the distinction carries all the moral weight. Compare the two for me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two in length, with no introduction or conclusion. At least one point should identify where the two genuinely converge and at least one where they pull apart, for instance on whether foreseeing a harm while being indifferent to it amounts to accepting it. One point should take up the pragmatic worry about the Knobe data — that the intentionality judgments are really a way of signaling blame, a conversational implicature rather than a fact about the concept of intentional action — and say what would follow for the doctrine's grip on real cases if that reading were right. One point should address the helpful-side-effect case, where the two frameworks seem to agree that the agent gets no credit, and explain whether they agree for the same reason. The fifth point should state, in a single sentence, which asymmetry you take to be more fundamental — the one in people's judgments about intention or the one in the doctrine's permission structure — and why.
