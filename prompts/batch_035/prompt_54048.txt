I'm a PhD student in information theory, working on error-correcting codes for synthetic DNA data storage. So the vocabulary I'm fluent in is Shannon's: source and channel coding, capacity, rate-distortion trade-offs, codebooks, joint source-channel separation, feedback, the fact that "amount of information" is only well-defined relative to a source ensemble. I can read a paper on LDPC decoders or on the achievable rate region of a channel with state without effort. My grasp of philosophy of biology, by contrast, comes mostly from having the words "the genetic code" in my field's peripheral vision.

Here is my problem. People keep telling me, in two different registers, that DNA is a code. In the innocent register, biologists say the genetic code is the mapping from codons to amino acids, mediated by tRNAs and the translation machinery — a codebook, more or less. In the loaded register, intelligent design advocates argue that codes require coders: every codebook we engineers have ever built was built, so a codebook found inside cells implies a designer. And then there's a third, philosophical register I only know secondhand: some philosophers apparently deny that genes carry information in any serious sense, treating "genetic information" as a metaphor that has outlived its usefulness, while others argue that genes do carry information but only in a way that smuggles in function and teleology — that saying DNA is "about" or "for" the organism's traits is where the real philosophical work hides. I want to know which of these people, if any, is talking about something I would recognize.

Restate this whole debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. In particular, I'd like the following translated, each on its own terms: (1) the claim that the genetic code is a codebook, including what I gather is a real literature on whether the standard codon assignments are arranged so as to minimize the damage from translation errors — i.e., whether it looks like a code designed against a noisy channel; (2) the claim that the genome as a whole carries information about the organism, where I already have a worry: in my vocabulary, a channel is defined relative to a source and a receiver, and I'm not sure either exists here in the right way — is the "sender" the parents, the ancestral lineage, the past environment, or nothing at all?; (3) the deflationary view that this is all a metaphor and "information talk" in biology should be paraphrased into plain causal talk; and (4) the design argument, translated as the claim that a codebook presupposes an encoder, and evaluated on those terms — in my field, codebooks are engineered, but I can also imagine a mapping arising naturally as whatever correlation happened to stabilize, so I want to know which side of that line the actual genetic code falls on and why.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto things I know — capacity, redundancy, codebook arbitrariness, the fact that a codebook is underdetermined by the messages it carries? Which parts had to be distorted to fit, and where did you have to fudge? And which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all — I suspect "aboutness," "function," and "for the sake of" are the candidates, but I'd like to know whether that's right and whether anything else fails to translate. Finally, the question the residue is supposed to answer: when something won't translate, is that because Shannon theory is missing apparatus it was never built to have, or because the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked in the first place? I have a prior that the deflationary philosophers are right and the rest is anthropomorphism, and I'd like you to tell me whether that prior survives contact with an honest translation or not.
