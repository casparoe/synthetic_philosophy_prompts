I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and we've just finished the Searle–Dennett exchange on intentionality, culminating in Dennett's two-bitser example from The Intentional Stance: a vending machine built to accept two-bit coins, whose "belief" that a two-bit coin has been inserted survives unchanged even after circumstances change (say, the coins become valuable to collectors and people feed it indistinguishable slugs), so that the machine's dispositional state is now better described as the belief that it has been handed a two-bit coin or a slug. For our final position paper we have to state our own views and defend their coherence, and before I commit anything to the page I'd like an audit of whether mine actually hang together. Here is roughly where I stand: (1) Searle is right that the man in the Chinese Room understands nothing, and I find the systems reply sophistical — getting the right input-output mapping by shuffling symbols according to a rulebook is not, by itself, understanding. (2) I am nonetheless a functionalist: mental states are individuated by their causal roles, nothing is metaphysically special about carbon, and a silicon system with the right organization could genuinely believe things. (3) I think Dennett's two-bitser argument works: the vending machine's content is derived from and parasitic on its designers' intentions, but a long line of iteratively redesigned descendants, getting smarter and more autonomous by stages, could gradually accrue content that is no longer usefully called derived — so the original/derived distinction is a matter of degree, not a difference in kind. (4) Yet I also want to insist that there is a principled line somewhere: my cat has original intentionality and the two-bitser does not, and this is not just a hazy matter of where one falls on a continuum. (5) Finally, I think a system's contents are fixed by its design and selection history — by what it was built or selected for — rather than by any intrinsic causal power of its physical substrate.

Please go through these five commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each tension is. For each real conflict, say what would have to give and what the revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; for the merely apparent ones, explain where the tension dissolves rather than just asserting that it does. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would much rather find out now than in the Q&A after my presentation, so please be candid rather than diplomatic.
