I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and my dissertation defends a version of Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness: roughly, that a perfectly loving God would ensure that every capable person is actually in a position to enter a personal relationship with the divine, that this rules out nonresistant nonbelief — lack of belief in God through no fault of the nonbeliever — and that the existence of such nonbelievers is therefore strong evidence against the existence of a God of perfect love. My supervisor, and lately a workshop audience, keep pressing me on a different question than the one I defend against in the chapter: not which replies fail, but what, if anything, would genuinely count against the argument. I want to face that question honestly rather than rehearse my standard responses.

Please take on the role of an analytic philosopher of religion who has published on hiddenness but holds no brief for either side, and think carefully before answering — brainstorm the candidate defeaters first, discard the weak ones, and keep only the strongest five. Then give me a numbered list of exactly five considerations that would count against the argument, ranked from most to least serious. Each item should be one or two sentences: name the consideration, state what would have to be true or what case would have to obtain for it to bite, and note whether a committed proponent would concede it as a genuine defeater or dismiss it as a misunderstanding — for instance, whether it trades on conflating nonresistant with resistant nonbelief, or on assuming that making relationship available to everyone would require coercion. At least one item should come from within theism's own conceptual resources rather than from skeptical theism, and at least one should be empirical, concerning the actual distribution of nonbelief across history and geography. No introduction, no conclusion, and no reading list — just the five items. For what it's worth, the reply that worries me most personally is the empirical claim that nonresistant nonbelief is rarer or shallower than the argument assumes, so please assess that one candidly rather than in my favor.
