My friend Elena and I spent most of a long train ride arguing about which computations carry morally considerable experiences, and I came away with a list of her verdicts but no sense of the rule behind them. She is confident that: a brain replaced neuron by neuron with silicon that preserves each neuron's input-output behavior remains conscious; a software emulation of a scanned brain is conscious on any hardware, however slowly it runs; an interactive lookup table of astronomical size, storing every possible history and merely returning the next state, is not conscious; a billion clerks passing notes so as to execute the emulation's update rules in real time would be collectively conscious; and two bit-for-bit identical emulations on separate machines are two subjects of experience rather than one computation somehow happening twice. The train ride ended before we reached the case I actually care about. A geologist colleague of hers has shown that, over one particular afternoon, the macro-states of a weathering cliff face — cracks, dust, moisture — pass through a sequence that, under a mapping he constructed after the fact (disjunctive, referencing a clock, gerrymandered in every way I know how to describe), is isomorphic to the state sequence of a ten-minute run of that emulation, with the inputs supplied interpretively. Nobody designed the cliff for this; nothing in its physics is organized around the mapping. If I put this case to Elena, what will she say, and which rule is she implicitly running? Her verdicts look jointly consistent with more than one principle: at minimum, a view on which what matters is that the transitions are genuinely computed, so that medium, speed, and number of implementations are irrelevant, and a more liberal view on which the mere existence of the right state-mapping suffices — though the liberal view may have to strain to accommodate her verdict about the lookup table. Tell me what each candidate rule predicts for the cliff, which prediction you would bet on and why, and what further case, one I could actually pose to her, would best tell the rules apart.
