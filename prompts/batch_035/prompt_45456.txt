I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and in that world "Hegel" has mostly functioned as a byword for unfalsifiable fog, so I was embarrassed to find myself genuinely gripped by the master–slave dialectic — the passage Hegel's translators now dress up as "lord and bondsman" — when I finally sat down with the chapter on self-consciousness alongside a re-read of Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. The fit is almost embarrassing in the other direction: Stevens, the butler of Darlington Hall, has organized his entire selfhood around service, his stated theory of dignity being that a great butler attaches himself to a great man and lends his small talent to a large enterprise, while the man he serves never registers him as a moral agent at all — at one point telling a guest, in front of Stevens, that of course Stevens has no views of his own on the affairs of the world.

My reading-group friend argues that the novel is not an illustration of the dialectic but a demonstration of what happens to a self-consciousness that refuses to enter it: Hegel's struggle requires risking one's life for recognition, and Stevens's whole ideology of dignity is precisely the elimination of risk — he wants recognition as a salary rather than a prize. I half buy this, but it seems to leave the ending uninterpretable: at Weymouth, Stevens weeps on the seawall at dusk and then resolves to practice "banter" with a perfect stranger, and I can't tell whether that is symmetrical recognition finally achieved on flat ground, or something too cheap to count, since a stranger's pleasantries carry no recognition payload at all. My own instinct, as someone who restates everything in decision-theoretic terms, is that the dialectic can be rendered as a bargaining problem — recognition as the good, the life-risking struggle as a sunk cost that establishes bargaining position — and my worry is that this rendering quietly drops the work-and-formation half of Hegel's story, which is exactly the half Ishiguro seems to be writing about.

So here is what I want: pick exactly five moments from the novel — number them one through five, a sentence or two each, nothing before item one and nothing after item five. One of the five must be the pier scene, and at least one must be a moment where you think the Hegelian lens genuinely distorts rather than illuminates (my own candidates for that slot are the dismissal of the Jewish maids and Miss Kenton's marriage, but pick your own if you disagree). Somewhere across the five, adjudicate my friend's claim that Stevens never enters the dialectic at all, and say whether a novel about a self that never risks anything is a vindication of Hegel, a refutation, or the one thing his apparatus cannot process.
