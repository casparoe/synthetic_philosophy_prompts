I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, working on a system that ingests equipment reports and updates reliability scores, and a colleague with philosophy training walked me into an argument about conditional evidence that I can't get out of. She says it's a variant of what philosophers call the Judy Benjamin problem, though I've restated it. A pilot bails out at night over unfamiliar terrain. She knows only that she has landed in one of three places — region A or region B, which together constitute enemy territory, or friendly territory outside both — and her prior is uniform, one third each. Her radio delivers a single message from a source she fully trusts: "If you have landed in enemy territory, then you have landed in region A." The argument runs:

(1) If a fully reliable source tells you something, you should become certain of what it told you.
(2) What the source told the pilot is true exactly when the corresponding material conditional holds — that is, she is not in enemy territory or she is in region A.
(3) Rational updating on evidence is Bayesian conditioning on the proposition learned.
(4) So the pilot should condition on "not in enemy territory or in region A," which zeroes out region B.
(5) On the uniform prior that takes her credence in being in enemy territory at all from 2/3 down to 1/2; on a prior concentrated on region B (say 0.98 on region B, 0.01 on each of the others) it takes it from 0.99 all the way down to 1/2.
(6) In general, on this treatment any purely conditional message lowers your credence in the antecedent: news solely about how things stand within enemy territory would make the pilot fairly confident she never landed there, and in my line of work a report about which machines in the bad cluster fail first would always make me doubt the server is in any cluster at all.

My colleague insists (6) shows the argument proves too much, and I agree the conclusion is absurd, but each premise looked plausible to me when I first heard it. Please tell me which step or inference to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — in particular what I would be giving up if the failure is in (2) versus in (3), since those are the two I would most like to keep. And if you think the argument is valid as far as it goes, say so and tell me which premise is least costly to abandon.
