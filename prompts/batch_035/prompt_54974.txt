I'm a novelist about to begin the final revision of a manuscript I've lived with for four years, with two full drafts behind it. My editor wants cuts and streamlining, and I've noticed that in past revisions I've destroyed things that turned out to be load-bearing: a scene cut for pacing had planted the motive for the ending; a flat minor character was quietly doing thematic work. I've come to think of revision as a Burkean problem — the draft is an inheritance I only partly understand, and Chesterton's fence (don't remove a fence until you know why it was put up) seems to apply to my own earlier choices as much as to institutions. But taken literally the principle would make revision impossible, since much of what's in the draft got there by accident rather than for a reason, and Burke's distinction between preserving what works and mere timidity doesn't map neatly onto a single author who *is* the tradition in question.

Please turn this into a decision procedure I can actually run, chapter by chapter: the questions to ask in order about any element I'm inclined to cut, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, when the procedure says preserve despite my editorial judgment, and where it must hand the decision back to me because only I can make the call. It should distinguish elements that are genuine fences (put up for a reason I or the draft can still recover) from elements that are drift or noise, and say how to tell them apart when I've forgotten my own reasons. Then run it on three cases from my draft: a chapter narrated by a character the novel otherwise abandons, a repeated image I no longer remember intending, and the original opening chapter that an early reader hated. Please be honest about where the analogy to political conservatism breaks down and where the procedure will misfire.
