I spent thirty-five years as a research physiologist, and for most of that time my work involved animal subjects — dogs, rats, later primates — under conditions that I believed, and still believe, were governed by genuine concern for the animals' welfare within the constraints of the science. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy in earnest, partly to test that belief. At a family dinner last month my granddaughter, who is vegan, brought an uncomfortable exchange about my old career to a close by declaring that "all animals are equal," and that preferring human interests simply because they are human is a prejudice structurally identical to racism. I recognised the sentence immediately, though in a rather different register: in Orwell's Animal Farm it is one of the commandments the pigs paint on the barn wall, and it is satire — later amended, famously, to concede that some animals are more equal than others. I have since discovered that Peter Singer used the same words in dead earnest as the title of a 1974 essay that became the opening chapter of Animal Liberation, and that the sentence carries a great deal of the weight of the modern animal-ethics literature I am now working through. I am too old to be either converted or wounded by a slogan; what I want is to know precisely what it claims.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a defence of one reading, the distinct precise theses that "all animals are equal" could be expressing. I can see it might mean something nearly undeniable — that the suffering of sentient animals counts morally at all — or something much stronger — that species membership is never itself a morally relevant property, so that no amount of human benefit can justify what I did in the laboratory, or that a human's pain and a pig's pain of equal intensity must weigh identically in any moral calculation. It might be a claim about equal consideration of interests, or about equal moral status, or even about equal rights, and these seem to come apart. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or simply false, and — this is what matters most to me — which of the readings my granddaughter's conclusions actually require. In particular, I would like to know whether the analogy to racism is itself part of the claim, and whether it holds up or is a disanalogy that critics have pressed.

One request about the character of your answer: please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. My impression is that the bare claim that animals feel pain and that their pain has moral significance is now widely accepted, while the stronger claims about species membership being morally irrelevant, and the utilitarian framework Singer builds on, remain very much disputed by serious philosophers. If that impression is wrong, correct it; but wherever reasonable people disagree, I would like to be told that they do, rather than being handed one side's verdict as settled doctrine. I would rather leave your answer still uncertain than falsely reassured.
