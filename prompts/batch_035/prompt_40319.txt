Write a satirical internal memo, maybe 700-800 words, from the newly founded "Office of Measurement Integrity" at a mid-size tech company. The setup: every single KPI on the exec dashboard is deep green -- engagement is up, ticket resolution time is down, developer velocity is through the roof -- and yet the company is visibly dying (churned customers, no actual revenue growth, everyone quietly hates working there). The memo announces the office's bold solution: a new meta-KPI called "Metric-Target Alignment Score," which will itself be tracked, bonused against, and reported quarterly to the board.

The humor has to come from getting the Goodhart/Campbell content right, not from making the executives cartoonishly evil -- they should be earnest, well-meaning, and completely blind to what they're doing. Make sure the piece works in the actual distinctions: regressional vs. extremal vs. causal ways a measure breaks (e.g., selecting on a noisy proxy drags the target back to the mean, or pushing to the proxy's tail severs the link entirely), the difference between a proxy and the thing it was supposed to stand in for, Lucas-critique-style behavior change once people know the measure drives rewards, and at least one moment where the memo cheerfully proposes a fix that is just Goodhart's law one level up (e.g., a bonus for employees who self-report gaming their own metrics, or an "anti-gaming clause" with its own gaming loophole spelled out in the fine print).

Include a short glossary sidebar of the company's internal jargon ("velocity-adjacent work," "green-ness," "pre-aligned metrics") where the definitions are funnier than the terms. End with the memo proposing, straight-faced, that the obvious next step is to measure how well the Alignment Score is aligned -- with itself.

Deadpan corporate voice throughout, no winking at the reader. Someone who knows the measurement literature should feel the satire is fair and precise; someone who doesn't should still find it funny as corporate self-parody. Don't name any real people or companies.

Also casual note: feel free to have one character (a junior analyst, maybe) ask the one obvious question in an embedded "Q&A from the all-hands" section, which the memo answers at lenght and completely misses the point of.
