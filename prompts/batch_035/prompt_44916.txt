I'm a retired physical chemist -- 40 years of spectroscopy, now I read philosophy instead of doing crosswords, and I apologize in advance if I frame things like a paper referee. Anyway: I finally read Popper's paradox of tolerance in the actual footnote in The Open Society and Its Enemies (chapter 7, note 4), not the meme version, and I was surprised how narrow it is. He says intolerant movements shouldn't be met with tolerance, that society should counter them with rational argument first, and only in the last resort suppress them by force -- and he's mainly worried about movements that refuse to play by the argumentative rules, not just people with obnoxious views. So here's what I want: analyze the Skokie episode of 1977-78 through this lens, and be honest about where the theory does work and where it goes silent. Skokie was a Chicago suburb with a large population of Holocaust survivors; the National Socialist Party of America, a small Nazi group, announced a march there, the town threw every legal obstacle it could at them (ordinances requiring insurance, banning military uniforms and hate symbols), and the ACLU, to enormous public fury, defended the Nazis' right to march and essentially won in the courts. Map it carefully: who is Popper's "intolerant movement" here, what counts as "meeting intolerance with intolerance" versus ordinary law enforcement, whether the courts blocking or permitting the march maps onto his suppression-by-force at all. Then deal with the messy facts that a tidy theory hates: the ACLU lawyers who were themselves mostly Jewish and some the children of survivors, the counter-demonstrators who outnumbered the Nazis, and the punchline that the march in Skokie never actually happened -- the Nazis got their permit, held a rally in Chicago instead, and the group basically dissolved a few years later. As a scientist I want to know what this case would even count as: a test of the paradox, an argument that the meme version of Popper is worse than useless, or a case his actual footnote never anticipated because he was thinking about interwar Austria, not American courts. And don't be sloppy about which claim is doing the work -- if the paradox of tolerance supports the ACLU here, or supports the town, say exactly why, and if it genuinely can't decide, say that too. I'd rather hear "the theory is silent here" than watch you force it to speak. Maybe 600-800 words, and you can use my chemist's habits against me if I've made some category error somewhere.
