ok so I want you to act as a philosopher of science who also knows the victorian natural history scene well — like, someone who could hold their own in a seminar on 19th century biology but whose real trade is the philosophy of discovery. think carefully before you write, and maybe start with a short brainstorm of the moving parts so you dont just fall into the standard narrative.

the episode: mid-1858. alfred russel wallace, collecting specimens from ternate in what's now indonesia, mails darwin a short essay that lays out evolution by natural selection — essentially the idea darwin had been sitting on for something like twenty years without publishing. darwin more or less panics, and his friends hooker and lyell engineer a fix: at a linnean society meeting on july 1, 1858, extracts from darwin's old unpublished writings get read out together with wallace's essay, so that both men are granted "priority" at the exact same moment. wallace wasn't there and only found out after the fact; he later said he thought the arrangement was generous and fair. almost nobody at the meeting treated it as a big deal — the society's annual report for that year supposedly gave the whole thing a shrug.

analyze this as a case study in the philosophy of simultaneous discovery, running in both directions:

1. theory → case. use the multiple-discovery framework — "ideas whose time has come," the thought that once the conceptual ingredients are lying around, somebody will assemble them — to explain what happened: why two people, why then and not fifty years earlier or later. but be honest about where the framework goes silent. it has basically nothing to say about the difference in what each man actually had: darwin had two decades of notebooks, barnacles, pigeons; wallace had a flash essay. and it papers over the places the two theories diverged — wallace later balked at sexual selection and at applying selection to the human mind, darwin didn't. so on your view was this ONE discovery or TWO? I want you to actually take a position and defend it, not survey options forever.

2. case → theory. two separate questions here. first, what does the episode do to the inevitability thesis — does two guys landing on the same idea within a few years of each other show that the discovery was, in some sense, waiting for whoever came along? second, the joint reading was a credit-allocation deal that hooker and lyell basically cooked up between themselves — there was no rulebook for this. does that support the view that "who discovered it" is less a fact sitting out there in the world and more a convention we settle, mostly for incentive reasons, so that people keep bothering to make discoveries at all? and would that arrangement fly today — picture a modern journal getting two simultaneous submissions like this; is there anything in current norms that would permit the linnean society solution, or was it purely a product of a small gentry network where everyone knew everyone?

one last thing, and it's really the point: the counterfactual. suppose darwin dies on the beagle voyage and never exists as a factor. wallace's essay still gets written in 1858. do we still get something recognizably like the origin, in content and in cultural force, or does the history of the idea come out importantly different? whatever you answer there is effectively your answer to "does it matter who made it," so make those two parts of your response consistent with each other.
