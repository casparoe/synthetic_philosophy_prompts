I am applying for a six-month postdoctoral fellowship at an institute for logic, language, and decision theory, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a polished three-page proposal. The fellowship is meant for foundational conceptual work rather than empirical or computational projects, so the proposal must stand on its formal-philosophical legs.

My idea concerns the problem of logical omniscience, but not the textbook version of it. The textbook version treats non-omniscience as one phenomenon: the idealized agent of epistemic logic and Bayesian decision theory knows every logical consequence of what she knows, and real agents plainly do not. The standard responses each relax exactly one thing. Awareness semantics says the agent may lack the concepts needed to state a conclusion; impossible-worlds semantics says her belief worlds may violate logic outright; resource-bounded approaches say inference costs something; syntactic theories simply deny closure as a principle about knowledge. My suspicion, which I have not been able to shake, is that these are not four solutions to one problem but quiet answers to four different questions, because failing to know a consequence of what one knows covers at least three distinct states: the agent cannot so much as formulate the conclusion; the agent could formulate it but has not run the inference; and — this is the state I care about — the agent has run the inference, knows the inference rule is valid, and still does not trust her own tokening of it, the way a competent logician re-checks a proof she has already checked. I want to argue that this third state, inferential self-distrust, is the one that actually matters for epistemic game theory, because iterated reasoning about other players' reasoning is precisely where agents must model the reliability of their own and others' inference, and yet it is the state that none of the standard semantics represents cleanly: each of them models an agent who is either ignorant of a consequence or treats her own derivation as automatically knowledge.

Please draft the proposal with the following components. First, a precise statement of the research question: whether inferential self-distrust is a coherent and formally expressible epistemic state, and whether representing it changes anything of substance in settings where knowledge of knowledge matters — common knowledge constructions, iterated dominance arguments, and reasoning about reasoning about reasoning. Second, a section explaining why the existing positions leave this open, framed not as a survey but as an argument: each standard approach, I believe, either collapses self-distrust into ordinary ignorance or into logical error, and both collapses lose something the game-theoretic applications need. Third, the method: what formal object I would actually build and study over the six months — I imagine a semantics in which an agent's attitude toward her own inferential outputs is a distinct parameter from her information partition, with the standard omniscient agent as a limiting case — what properties would need to be proven about it (what replaces closure, whether anything like common knowledge survives, how the fixed-point definition of common knowledge behaves when each level of the iteration carries a self-trust discount), and a realistic month-by-month plan. Fourth, a statement of what would count as success and, more importantly, what would count as failure: what would it take for this project to reveal that self-distrust is merely a notational variant of existing treatments, and would that negative result itself be publishable or just a dead end? Fifth, an honest scoping paragraph: which parts of the formal program are at real risk of being too large for six months, and what I would cut first. Finally, a short section anticipating the objection I expect from the fellowship committee, which is that the phenomenon I describe is a performance error belonging to psychology rather than a state belonging to the theory of knowledge — I want a reply that concedes what should be conceded while defending the formal project.

Do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; I will supply references myself. The proposal should assume readers who know epistemic logic and decision theory at the graduate level but have not thought specifically about logical omniscience in years, so the framing should make the problem vivid before it makes it precise.
