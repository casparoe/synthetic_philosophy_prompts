Context: I'm a 3rd year econ PhD student, decision/game theory track, and our bargaining theory reading group has this tradition where each of us has to stage a "fight" between two solution concepts for everyone else to referee. My turn is coming up and I picked the classic matchup: Nash bargaining solution vs Kalai–Smorodinsky. I want to hand out a written dialogue rather than slides, since slides always turn into me just listing axioms at people.

Write that dialogue. Two game theorists who end up on the same committee, having to recommend a settlement in an actual dispute, and they fall into arguing about whose solution concept to run the numbers with. Make the dispute concrete: a university and its grad-worker union negotiating a contract package, where (in utility units, normalized so the disagreement point is (0,0)) the feasible agreements are roughly the convex hull of (0,0), (60,60), and (75,40) — the (75,40) point is the deal that's great for the administration and merely okay for the workers, (60,60) is the balanced one. The Nash product picks (60,60); KS, drawing the line from the disagreement point to the ideal point (75,60), lands around (66,53) and tilts toward the administration. So the two of them genuinely disagree about what to recommend, and they know it.

I want the argument to be about the axioms, not vibes. The Nash person should press independence of irrelevant alternatives hard (deleting options nobody would have picked anyway shouldn't move the recommendation) and should have a real answer when the KS person says that's exactly the property that lets one side gain when the feasible set expands and the other side's outside options don't budge. The KS person should frame individual monotonicity as "if your ceiling rises and mine doesn't, my payoff shouldn't drop," and the Nash person should give the best version of the comeback — e.g., that KS secretly cares about ratios of what you get to what you could maximally get, and why is a ratio the thing that matters? And let them push past the axiom-swapping into the deeper stuff: is a bargaining solution supposed to be a prediction of what rational bargainers will agree to, a normative recommendation for an arbitrator, or just a benchmark — and does the answer to that change which axioms count as reasonable? The KS person can needle Nash about the disagreement point being manipulable (burning your own surplus as a commitment tactic etc.), the Nash person can needle KS about what the ideal point even means when it's off the frontier and nobody can actually get it.

Both characters have to be genuinely good at this — no strawmen — and each one should lose at least one exchange, meaning actually concede a point or have to reformulate something mid-dialogue, not just talk past each other. Also they should notice where they agree (symmetry, Pareto, invariance to utility rescaling) instead of pretending every axiom is contested. Don't have anyone "win": end it unresolved, but with both saying what kind of evidence — experiments on how people actually split, arguments about what arbitration is for, whatever — would actually settle their disagreement. If they grumble for a line or two about Rubinstein's alternating-offers model having eaten both their lunches, that's a nice touch, but keep it short.

Length: whatever feels right, just long enough that the axiom fight gets real rather than each side reciting their axiom list once and stopping. And I need to be able to fact-check the numbers they cite, so keep any computations they do out loud honest — if one of them does arithmetic sloppily, have the other catch it.
