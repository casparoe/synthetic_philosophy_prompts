I'm writing a seminar paper on whether resistance to Nozick's experience machine tracks genuine contact with reality or only a cluster of confounds — deception, isolation, suspiciously intense and simple pleasure, and the all-or-nothing framing of an eternal swap. What I want is a case that strips those confounds away, so that whatever intuition remains can be attributed to something specific rather than to the package deal.

Construct the thought experiment to the following specifications. (1) No deception: the subject knows exactly what the machine does, and no belief they form inside it is false — the simulation is engineered so that everything they come to believe about their life inside is true of that life, and they retain the memory of having chosen it. (2) No isolation: the relationships formed inside are with other real people who independently chose the same machine, and whose simulations are coordinated so that they genuinely meet, talk, and befriend one another — the friendship is not with a fiction. (3) No hedonic inflation: life inside is no more pleasant and no simpler than the best life the subject could realistically live outside — ordinary mixtures of satisfaction, boredom, and disappointment, not guaranteed bliss. (4) Real stakes: the decision is a one-time, informed choice by an adult who has lived enough to know what they are giving up, and there is a genuine external cost — the outside world permanently loses their labor as, say, a rural physician — so the choice isn't free on consequentialist grounds either. The scenario itself should be one paragraph, with the person sketched in enough psychological detail that we can imagine how they think, not a bare "subject S."

After the scenario, do three things. First, walk through the verdicts of hedonism, unrestricted desire-satisfactionism, an informed-desire or endorsement-based theory, and an objective-list theory that includes contact with reality on its list — and derive each verdict from the theory's own machinery rather than stipulating it: say, for instance, which desires are satisfied and whether that satisfaction counts as genuine on each theory's terms. Second, identify which of the four design features is doing the work in whatever residual intuition remains: if the informed-desire theorist and the objective-list theorist still get a refusal out of the case, say whether it comes from the no-deception clause, the no-isolation clause, the hedonic parity clause, or from something none of them covers — for instance, the thought that a life needs a shape or an author, which Nozick gestures at but arguably never develops. Third, vary the case: remove exactly one feature at a time, especially the clause that the people inside are real and coordinated, and report what happens to each verdict. I want to know whether contact with reality survives as an independent ground of refusal or dissolves into the confounds once they're peeled off.

Two honesty requirements. If you think the specification is unsatisfiable — for instance, that a machine containing real people, no false beliefs, and ordinary hedonic quality is no longer an experience machine case at all, so the thought experiment self-destructs before it can do any work — say so plainly and explain why, rather than quietly building a weaker case and presenting it as meeting the spec. And if a hedonist could plausibly shrug at whatever intuition survives, on the ground that hedonic parity leaves the theory indifferent rather than embarrassed, flag that instead of presenting the case as a clean victory for one side.
