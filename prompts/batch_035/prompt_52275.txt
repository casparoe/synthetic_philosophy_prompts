I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of science course, and for my seminar presentation next week I will be defending a qualified version of van Fraassen's Reflection principle: the idea that, as a matter of rationality, my current credence in a proposition should match what I now expect my future credence in it to be, provided I regard my future self as a genuine expert on the matter rather than as someone who will merely have different evidence or have lost evidence through forgetting. The audience is the seminar itself: our instructor is a strict Bayesian who treats the probability calculus as the whole of epistemic rationality and is fond of Dutch book arguments, and several classmates have recently read the literature criticizing Reflection, including the objection that diachronic incoherence is not really incoherence at all -- that a diachronic Dutch book only catches you because the bookie can wait, and that there is nothing irrational about planning to change your mind. Please draft my question-and-answer prep sheet: the six or seven objections I should actually expect from this particular room, ordered by how much damage they could do, each phrased the way a Bayesian or a well-read classmate would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. I want you to include the objection that Reflection, read as an expert-deference principle, either collapses into triviality or threatens absurd deference to any "expert," including my future self in cases where I now know that my future self will have forgotten relevant evidence. I would also like one objection about whether the principle can be extended backward, to deference to my past self, and an explanation of why the asymmetry matters for my defense. Finally, identify the question I am hoping nobody asks -- the one that targets the weakest joint in the qualified version I am defending -- and tell me how I should handle it if it comes anyway.
