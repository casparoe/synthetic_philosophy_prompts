I'm taking a seminar this term on lying, misleading, and the ethics of assertion, and I've fallen into the habit of testing my flatmate's intuitions on cases we invent over dinner. She is very consistent — she never hedges, she just rules — but I've started to suspect that her track record doesn't pin down any single principle, and I want to make a proper go of working out what she's implicitly running before I raise it with her. Her verdicts so far, in roughly the order they came up: (1) a CEO, asked point-blank in due diligence whether the plant has any pending safety violations, answers "No" when there are three — she says this is seriously wrong, full stop; (2) a witness who testifies "He was at the bar when I arrived at ten," when the man actually showed up at 10:40 — again seriously wrong, no qualifications; (3) a friend who, invited to a party, says "I have a ton of work that weekend" — which is true, though her real reason for declining is that her ex will be there — she says this is perfectly fine; and (4) a dinner guest who says "I can't stay late tonight," said only to create the impression that he would otherwise love to stay — also fine, even charming.

Notice, as I only did later, that in every case she condemns the speaker literally asserts something false under serious stakes, and in every case she excuses the speaker the sentence is true but engineered to trigger a false inference under trivial stakes. So at least two rules fit her record equally well. On one reading, she holds the standard view that lying and misleading differ in kind: a false assertion breaches a trust that mere implicature-manipulation doesn't, so the mechanism alone settles the verdict. On another reading, she sees no intrinsic difference between the two, and all her verdicts are really tracking stakes and reliance — how much the deceived person is betting on the answer — with the lie/misleading split in her cases being a coincidence of our examples.

Next week's dinner case, which I've designed to break the tie: a woman selling her used car is asked by the buyer, "Has it been in any accidents?" She answers, "It has a clean title, and it's never given me a day of trouble" — both strictly true — although she knows it was rear-ended and rebuilt before she bought it, knows the question is aimed precisely at accident history, and knows the buyer is deciding whether to buy it for his teenage daughter. Predict her verdict. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her earlier rulings, say what each predicts for the car case, give me your best guess along with your reasons for preferring that reading of her, and then tell me which further case — one I could put to her — would most cleanly separate the rules, since my current backup case (telling a grandmother her lumpy knitting is lovely) seems to cut in opposite directions depending on which rule she's running.
