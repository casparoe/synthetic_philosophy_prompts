I am a retired physical chemist who spent thirty-eight years running a spectroscopy laboratory, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy in earnest. This term I am facilitating a continuing-education seminar — fourteen adult students, one ninety-minute evening session, none of them specialists — and we have just finished struggling through Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language. I confess that on the page the skeptical argument leaves me cold in a way it should not: I have followed analytical protocols my whole working life, and I cannot feel what is supposed to be missing when the skeptic says no fact about my past use fixes the next application. I want my students to feel it rather than nod politely at it.

Please design an in-class game in which the participants run into the problem themselves, before anyone names it. My half-formed idea, which you should improve upon or replace outright: teams infer an arithmetic rule from a series of worked examples, wager points on the outputs for cases they choose themselves, and then the facilitator springs a second, secret rule that agrees with every example seen so far but diverges on precisely the cases at stake — engineered so that no finite stretch of the game could have distinguished the two rules in advance. That secret second rule should be one you invent fresh for this design rather than lifted from the literature (in a dry run of my own I used a variant of addition that changes behavior once an operand passes 57, but do not feel bound to that), so that no participant can arrive with the resolution pre-memorized. The payoff scheme should use points rather than money, and the divergence should cost the confident teams something they genuinely feel losing.

Take the role of a veteran workshop designer who has run philosophy activities for non-specialist adult groups many times, and think carefully before writing — in particular about how the rules and incentives, rather than anything the facilitator announces, create the trap. Then set out your design as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the setup and materials; (2) the rules and payoff scheme; (3) the divergence and the mechanics of how it is sprung; (4) the debrief, mapping what the players experienced onto the skeptical challenge and onto whatever you take the book's response to be; and (5) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including an honest statement of which essential feature of the problem a game of this kind cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.
