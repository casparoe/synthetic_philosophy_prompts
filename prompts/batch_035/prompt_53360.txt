So I'm a novelist, and my current project has a premise an old professor of mine would call a ring of Gyges story: my protagonist, a mid-level hospital administrator, comes into possession of a near-future device that makes her effectively unobservable. Cameras glitch as she passes, digital traces of whatever she does dissolve within minutes, and — this matters — she's tested it enough that she genuinely believes no consequence can ever reach her. It's not magic, it's tech, but functionally she can act in the world and never be seen or held to account. The question the novel turns on is whether she stays good, and how fast and how far she drifts.

Last weekend my writing group got into it over the draft. Marta, who's read the first half, said the slow-decay arc I'd sketched was a lazy cliché: people don't behave well only because they're watched, conscience gets internalized like shame that works in an empty room, and since I've written her as someone who actually cares about her patients, she'd carry that into invisibility. The interesting arc, Marta said, isn't her decaying, it's her discovering which of her scruples were only ever habits of surveillance. Dev, our resident cynic, came back with what he called the thermostat point: conscience is maintained by its social function, and take away the function and it decays like an unused muscle, so a decay arc is just the realistic one. As evidence he pointed at how people behave in comment sections, effectively anonymous, and it's not pretty. Marta said that was a bad analogy: online anonymity is never total, records persist, platforms can deanonymize you whenever it suits them, so people half-know they might be seen — her device removes even that, so the comment-section comparison tells us nothing about how fast anything decays. Dev granted that but said total unobservability has its own check on it: she still has to live with herself as the person who did those things, and if her self-respect can't be moved by that, then I hadn't written a person, I'd written a sociopath, which is a different book.

Marta's last word was that knowing your own story isn't the same as being held to account: people narrate themselves kindly, self-deception is exactly the thing the device makes easy, and the internal audience she'd be performing for is modeled on real audiences, which the device has just deleted — so it can fade too, just slower than Dev thinks. Dev said if that's right then nobody ever had a conscience at all and morality was always a performance with no performer, which he refused to believe. By then I'd stopped taking notes, honestly. I keep flip-flopping depending on which chapter I'm rewriting, and I can't referee this myself, so: can you go through their exchange and tell me who's right at each step? I'd especially like a real verdict on the thermostat argument and on whether Marta's bad-analogy reply actually works, because whichever way that goes basically decides the shape of the second half of the book. And if they're both missing something — some third option for the arc — that would honestly be a gift.
