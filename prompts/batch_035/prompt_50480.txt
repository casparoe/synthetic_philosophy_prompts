I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk at a magazine, and I'm putting together a retrospective long-read on the Browning–Goldhagen fight, since it's coming up on thirty years since the two books turned explaining Holocaust perpetrators into a very public brawl. Quick version of the ground, in case it helps: Browning studied a German police battalion of middle-aged working-class men from Hamburg who shot and deported Jews in occupied Poland, and argued they were "ordinary men" pushed along by conformity, deference to authority, the gradual normalisation of the killing, and the quiet fact that those who balked could mostly step aside without real punishment — and mostly didn't step aside anyway. Goldhagen pushed back that this badly understates ideology: on his account, a specifically eliminationist antisemitic culture, not peer pressure, explains why these men killed readily and often zealously. My angle isn't "who won" but what still rides on the answer, because the situation-vs-culture framing gets exported everywhere — into police-misconduct debates, peacekeeper training, corporate whistleblowing guidance, even takes on online mobs. So the question is what the debate actually taught us about people in these positions, and where it's still open.

First, there's the problem that Milgram-style findings, which Browning leaned on, have been reinterpreted — the "engaged followership" line, where obedience is reframed as identification with the enterprise rather than blind compliance — and I honestly don't know how much that undermines the situational case. Second, there's a selection problem no one in the debate seemed to hammer: these battalions weren't randomly sampled, so calling the men "ordinary" already assumes a lot, and situations also select for dispositions, which muddies the whole dichotomy. Third, there's the comparative question — non-German auxiliary units often behaved similarly, which looks like trouble for a culture-specific explanation, but I'd want to know how strong that inference really is. And fourth, the awkward meta-question of what evidence could ever have decided this: polling public opinion inside a dictatorship is basically impossible.
