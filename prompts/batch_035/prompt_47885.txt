I am a high school junior writing a term paper for an elective called Contemporary Moral Problems, and I want to be careful not to misrepresent the philosopher my paper is about. In class we read about John Taurek's 1977 article "Should the Numbers Count?", which discusses a case where a rescuer can save either one person or five people who each face the same threat. When I asked a friend who took the course last year to refresh my memory, she gave me the following summary, and I would like to know how much of it I can trust before I build on it:

"Taurek's position is that numbers must never matter when we are deciding whom to rescue. His central claim is that each person can lose only his own life, so in a choice between five and one there is no individual who suffers five losses, and therefore five deaths are not a worse outcome than one death in any sense. Because the numbers give us no reason at all to prefer the five, the only fair thing to do is flip a coin, giving each of the six people an equal chance of survival. And this is required, not merely permitted: a rescuer who saves the five instead of flipping has treated the lone person's life as worth less than the others'. So in the end Taurek's whole view rests on one simple principle, that morality demands we give everyone's claims equal weight, and that principle by itself rules out counting people."

Please help me audit this summary. Which parts of it would a defender of Taurek accept as accurate, which parts would strike that defender as overstated or misdescribed, and which parts quietly run two different claims together? Finally, my friend insisted that if Taurek's argument works, it would also show that any nonrandom way of choosing among equally endangered strangers, such as a hospital's first-come-first-served rule or a triage policy, is unfair to those who arrive later or are treated second.
