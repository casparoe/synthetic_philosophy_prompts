I'm a science journalist, and years of working with sources have left me with a set of firm gut judgments about when it's fine to rely on someone who turns out to be right for reasons that don't hold up, and when it isn't. I've come to suspect these judgments add up to a position on the old Meno puzzle — the question of why knowledge is supposed to be worth more than true belief, since a true belief guides action just as well as knowledge does. But I can't find the principle behind my own reactions, and I'd like your help extracting it.

My verdicts on cases, stated plainly:

1. A source with a long record of accurate inside information gives me a tip; it checks out. Relying on her feels entirely legitimate, and I'd do it again.
2. A numerologist writes to me that a drug trial will fail on a date derived from the Bible; the trial does fail. Even after the fact, I feel I'd have been wrong to run the story on his say-so, even though he was right.
3. A whistleblower gives me documents that conclusively establish a claim, but the whistleblower himself has no idea what he's talking about and believes the claim for paranoid reasons. I'd run the story without hesitation — my true belief rests on the documents, not his competence, and that seems fine.
4. A climate model with a known bug in its cloud physics happens to nail a decade of temperature trends because two errors cancel. Using its past performance as a headline feels dishonest in a way that using a sound model's does not, even though both were right.
5. A colleague's article contained a factual error that, by luck, didn't change the conclusion; the conclusion was true and everyone believed it on the strength of the erroneous support. I feel the readers were wronged even though they ended up with true beliefs, but I can't say what they lost.
6. The case I keep going back and forth on: an anonymous tipster gives me a claim I cannot independently verify before deadline. If I run it and it proves true, was I justified? My gut says it depends on something about the tipster, but I can't say what, since the numerologist shows that a confident source with a lucky record isn't enough, and the documents in case 3 show the source doesn't even need to be right about why he's right.

Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts, or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why those? Please structure your answer as follows: "The candidate principle" (state it precisely, in roughly 200–350 words, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it); "Where it overrules me" (the verdicts, if any, that the principle cannot accommodate, with an honest case for why they should go, roughly 150–250 words); "The unlisted case" (what the principle says about a source I haven't mentioned — say, an AI system that flags a safety problem in a clinical trial with uninterpretable reasoning and a track record of correct flags — and whether I should report it before human verification); and "The weakest point" (roughly 100 words on where the principle is most vulnerable, so I know where to probe next). If you think my verdicts force a choice between a baroque principle that saves them all and a simpler one that overturns one or two, make the choice explicitly and defend it.
