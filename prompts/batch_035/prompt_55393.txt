You are a political philosopher with a serious background in democratic theory and social choice theory, and I want you to think carefully about this one — ideally sketch out the analysis before committing to conclusions, because I suspect the tempting answer and the right answer come apart here.

Some context on me: I'm active in local politics, and my reading group recently spent a couple of months on Rousseau, so the phrase "general will" has been in the air. At the same time, I keep hearing our campaigners lean on an analogy I can't fully audit on my own. The analogy is that a people is like a single person. A person has interests, deliberates, and forms a will; an election is the people deliberating, the outcome is its decision, and the leader is its voice. Two conclusions are drawn from this. First, that a referendum result binds the losers too, and binds them more strongly than a mere majority vote would, because voting is the people talking to itself, and you don't get to disobey your own decision — one campaigner put it that the 48% who voted the other way are like the part of you that still wanted cigarettes after you decided to quit: overruled, not ignored. Second, that the leader, as the people's voice, can't properly be checked by courts, bureaucracies, or legislatures, since no part of a person outranks its own mouth. When I pushed back, someone muttered that social choice theory had proven there's no such thing as the will of the people at all, but couldn't elaborate, and I don't know whether that's right, partly right, or a red herring.

So: audit the analogy rather than reacting to the politics. Start with the correspondence table — what in the collective case is supposed to play the roles of an individual's beliefs, preferences, deliberation, memory over time, and the will itself. Then set out where the mapping breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions the analogy is being used to license from the ones that are just imperfections of the picture. I'd also like you to address what the analogy quietly imports: does the people have a will prior to the vote in anything like the way I have an intention before I speak, or does the vote create the will rather than reveal it — and does the answer matter differently for the two conclusions? Is the leader really like a mouth, an organ that merely executes, rather than like an interpreter, which is a different and much more dangerous role even within a single person? Where does the social-choice point about aggregating preferences fit — is it a fatal objection to the analogy, or does it only touch some ways of using it? And finally, your overall verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, which of the two conclusions, if either, survives, and if the analogy is doing damage rather than work, is there a better comparison that captures what's true in the idea of a people deciding as a unit — or should that idea be given up?
