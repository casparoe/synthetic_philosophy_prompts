I teach ethics, so aesthetics is a spectator sport for me, but there's a pattern I keep running into in the forgery literature that I can't find a settled name for, and it's starting to annoy me.

Here's the pattern. When van Meegeren's fake Vermeers were exposed right after the war, a lot of the people who had stood in front of the Supper at Emmaus and written glowing reviews didn't just say "well, I was fooled about the facts." Many of them claimed they could now see the painting was bad all along — that the kitschiness had been sitting right there on the canvas, and their earlier judgment was just an error, like a misreading of a measurement. Same thing in the big wine fraud scandals: experts had written detailed tasting notes on the fakes, and after the exposure some of them wanted to say the notes described a wine that never existed, as if the liquid in the glass had retroactively changed. And it runs both directions — when a canvas that sat in storage for decades labeled "workshop copy" gets reattributed to the master, suddenly the same critics find depths in it that a century of viewing somehow missed.

So the thing I want a name for is: sincere, recorded aesthetic judgments being retroactively revised when believed provenance changes, with the revisers insisting they're correcting an error rather than updating a preference. Not just "forgeries are worth less," which I take to be well-trodden — the standard move there is that a painting is partly a historical performance, so the forgery is a different performance even if visually identical. Fine, but that explains why the object is less valuable; it doesn't explain why people's past *experiences* get rewritten.

Is there an established concept for the revision itself? If yes, I want to know how it's distinguished from plain snobbery and from ordinary forgetting/self-serving memory. If no, coin something — and I care about the edges: does it count when nothing was written down and people just misremember liking it? Does it count when the person refuses to revise ("still love it, sue me")? And is there anything on whether the belief about authorship literally changes the perceptual experience vs. just the report of it? A bare term won't help me, I need the carving.
