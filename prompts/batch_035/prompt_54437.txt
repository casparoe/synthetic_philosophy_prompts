Been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've been around EA money circles long enough to watch the culture shift from an ethos of "multiply it out, but only where the evidence is solid" to something more like multiply it out, all the way up, however thin the tail. Here's the tension I keep chewing on. The global health wing of the movement is famous for refusing to fund things without randomized trials, cost-effectiveness estimates with error bars, explicit penalties for optimism — deep uncertainty is basically a veto on a grant. The longtermist wing happily funds things whose own proponents give a 1% or 0.1% chance of working, on the theory that a small probability of an astronomically large payoff dominates the arithmetic, and that robustness of evidence shouldn't change the expected value itself, only the estimate going into it. Same donors, same forums, people moving money between both pots. Everyone waves it off as different problems needing different tools, which strikes me as exactly the sort of thing people say when they havent worked out the actual commitments.

So what decision theory does this two-faced funding practice presuppose, if any? Reconstruct it for me, because as far as I can tell there are a few candidate stories with very different implications. Story one: everyone's a pure EV maximizer, and the evidence-demanding side is just a debiasing correction — we systematically overestimate small chances, so demanding RCTs is a heuristic for cleaning up credences, not a principled departure from maximizing expected value, and the robustness requirement should melt away once estimates get honest enough. Story two: the community is implicitly risk-averse, or scope-insensitive, at heart, and only tolerates the fanatical bets because the numbers are too big and slippery to feel real — meaning the longtermist portfolio rests on a foundation the health wing already rejected. Story three: something like EV-is-right-but-tiny-probabilities-of-huge-payoffs-deserve-special-suspicion, i.e., the practice presupposes a non-fanatical decision theory that nobody will state out loud, since stating it would put a ceiling on the biggest bets. I want to know which of these the practice actually needs, where it's genuinely underdetermined such that several views would license it, where the two halves flatly contradict each other, and what a funder who made the presupposed view explicit would have to change — does the RCT requirement go, or do the long-shot bets?

And since I keep getting the different-domains defense at parties: tell me whether it holds water. Is there a coherent position where you weight the reliability of evidence differently precisely when the stakes are astronomical, or does the whole edifice only stand because no one funder is forced to bet the same way twice?
