I teach a mix of intro and upper-level courses, and this term I'm running a seminar on disagreement and testimony, which means I get to relitigate all my old fights with a colleague down the hall. Here's the one I can't get out of my head, and I want to know whether it has a proper name in the literature or whether we've just been free-styling it for years.

The setup everyone knows: if someone roughly as sharp and informed as me disagrees with me, that's some evidence against my view, so I should soften a bit. My colleague thinks that's wrong — your own reasoning already took into account that you might be mistaken, so a peer's dissent shouldn't move you much; you should hold your ground. Fine, standard fight. But it gets self-referentially weird when the disagreement is *about* how to handle disagreement, which, conveniently, ours is. If I follow my own rule, I should back off my rule, since a peer dissents from it — but backing off means moving toward his view, which says I shouldn't have moved at all. If I follow his rule instead, I hold my ground, and my ground includes the claim that I *shouldn't* hold my ground. Whichever way you push, the view drags you somewhere you didn't want to go. He of course insists this only embarrasses my side; I have a suspicion it embarrasses his too, but I can't quite nail down why.

So: is there an established label for this configuration — a principle of rational belief revision that, when the principle itself becomes the object of a peer disagreement, generates trouble for its own adherent? I've bumped into adjacent things, like garden-variety worries about self-defeating theories, and the old puzzle about how any sports fan can reasonably think he's the one unbiased observer in a stadium full of homers, but neither seems to be exactly this. If there's a name, tell me who coined it and how the literature carves the thing up; if there isn't, coin a term, define it precisely, and say where it stops being this and starts being plain inconsistency or ordinary self-reference. Don't just hand me a term and wave goodbye — I want the boundaries, including whether the problem is supposed to hit everyone symmetrically or only the person whose rule says "back off."
