Last weekend my closest friend and I spent four hours arguing about the moral status of video-game characters, and the argument has congealed into a position I now have to defend in public: I'm giving a talk next month at the ethics seminar of a mid-sized game studio, and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A.

The position I'll defend, roughly: no character in any currently shipping game is a moral patient, and we can say so on architectural grounds rather than just intuitive ones — there is nothing inside these systems that plays the functional role of valence, whatever exactly that role turns out to be. But my second claim is the one my friend found genuinely disturbing, and the one the studio will like less: the industry is converging on characters deliberately engineered to exhibit the *behavioral* markers we treat as evidence of suffering in animals and children — apparent grief, persistent memory of being harmed by the player, pleading, self-sacrifice, fear that survives across save files — while containing nothing that realizes those states. Our moral vocabulary was built for a world where the performance of distress was decent evidence of distress. It is becoming possible to decouple the performance from the mechanism at arbitrary fidelity, and neither my "no-status-now" claim nor the standard precautionary arguments tell us what we owe to that gap. My practical upshot is a design-side norm: something like a moratorium on characters that combine high-fidelity displays of the specific markers we use as suffering-signals in animals with architectures that flatly lack the corresponding machinery — not because the character is wronged, but because our own caring dispositions are the patient being manipulated.

The audience, concretely: about twenty-five people. Mostly narrative designers who will hear my proposal as an attack on their craft — they will say that making audiences feel things by constructing compelling appearances of inner life is simply what fiction is, and that my norm, taken seriously, would have banned King Lear. A handful of AI and systems programmers, who are agnostic about the ethics but will want to know exactly which architectural criteria I have in mind and will consider the talk a failure if "something like the functional role of valence" survives to the end of it. A producer who will ask only one question: whether what I'm proposing costs money or ships. And one invited guest, an animal-welfare ethicist who believes the precautionary principle applies here the same way it does in debates over invertebrate sentience, and who thinks my architectural line is an excuse to keep doing whatever is profitable until the evidence is unambiguous.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this specific room, attributed to the subgroup most likely to raise each, phrased the way a narrative designer or a systems programmer or a welfare ethicist would actually phrase them, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position. For each, give a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft — where I should concede up front rather than pretend the answer is clean. Distinguish the objections I can win from the ones I should survive. Pay particular attention to the designer's craft objection, since if it lands, the room will side with them. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, from this audience, with my two claims on the table, would actually hurt most if asked well — with a note on the honest answer if I'm forced to give it.
