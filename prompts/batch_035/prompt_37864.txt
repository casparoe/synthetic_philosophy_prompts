I hold a position I'd call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: suffering above a certain intensity has lexical priority over everything else, so that no amount of happiness, however widely distributed, can compensate for even one instance of above-threshold suffering; below the threshold, pleasure and pain aggregate and trade off in the ordinary utilitarian way. I want you to map what would actually count against this view. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, and say which objections committed defenders of the view would concede have force versus which they would dismiss.

Consider, among other things: formal pressure points, such as the fact that lexicographic orderings violate the continuity assumptions built into standard expected-utility frameworks, and whether that is a problem for an axiology or merely a repudiation of decision-theoretic orthodoxy; empirical findings that might bear on the commensurability assumption, including work suggesting pleasure and pain are processed partly on a common neural currency versus work suggesting they are dissociable systems, and evidence like the gap between experienced and remembered well-being or the self-reports of people with severe chronic pain who nonetheless judge their lives worth continuing; and thought-experimental exposures, such as the question of where exactly the threshold sits and whether the view's verdicts are so sensitive to that placement that the view risks being endlessly requalified — threshold lowered to dodge one counterexample, raised to dodge the next — until it is insulated against anything and ceases to predict anything distinctive about real choices (triage in disaster medicine, analgesia versus other medical research, whether gustatory pleasure could ever justify factory farming as practiced).

I'd also like your honest assessment of two structural worries: first, whether the view collapses into a rival at the limits — collapsing into classical utilitarianism if the threshold is set high enough, into pure negative utilitarianism if set low enough — and whether any non-arbitrary principle fixes its location; second, whether the lexical priority itself is doing real work or is best understood as a claim about the shape of the hedonic scale, and what observations, if any, could tell those two interpretations apart. Rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude by telling me whether the view, as I've stated it, risks anything at all.
