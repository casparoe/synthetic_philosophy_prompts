So, a bit of background: I spent thirty-five years running a lab in molecular biology, retired two years ago, and have since been working through the philosophy shelf my daughter left at my house, which is how a sixty-eight-year-old who still dreams about gels ended up reading Parfit cover to cover. The teletransporter material has been living in my head rent-free ever since. In the lab you learn that if you want to know whether a variable matters you have to be able to pull it apart from the variables it's normally glued to, and Parfit, to his credit, does exactly that to identity and survival: in the branching cases he prises them apart and argues you can have what matters without identity. Fine. But it struck me that everyone, including him as far as I can tell, only pulls them apart in one direction — cases where the psychological continuity story survives but the identity verdict fails. The mirror-image experiment, where the verdict comes out that you ARE the same person but nothing that matters came through, is the one I actually want to run, and nobody seems to build it.

So here's the job. Construct a thought experiment in which personal identity is preserved but what matters in survival is not — the same verdict on identity that a normal person and a psychological-continuity theorist would both give, but where, on reflection, Parfit's question (is there anything here that makes continued existence worth wanting?) gets the answer no. Constraints, because in my experience if you don't constrain the design people cheat: no branching, duplication, or fission — that's Parfit's own move and I want the opposite one; the identity judgment has to fall out of the case's structure, not be stipulated (you can't just write "and suppose it really is her" — that's like labeling a tube instead of running the assay); and the mechanism by which continuity is maintained should be describable without hand-waving, even if it's futuristic. One paragraph for the scenario itself. Then the analysis, and this is the part I actually care about: walk through what the psychological-continuity theorist says, what Parfit's reductionist says, and why they can still agree on identity while coming apart on what matters — or if you think the two verdicts can't actually be pulled apart in that direction, tell me that instead and say what blocks it, because I'd genuinely like to know whether the mirror experiment is unbuildable in principle or just unpopular. Finish with which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts when you remove it, and what a defender of the view that identity itself is what matters would say to attack the case. My suspicion, for what it's worth, is that identity and what-matters are like those enzyme pairs where knocking out either one alone does nothing — but I've been wrong about coupled systems before and it cost me two years of my life, so talk me through it properly.
