I help run a monthly metaphysics workshop for graduate students and a few philosophers of science, and for over a year our recurring topic — what it means for a kind like race or gender to be socially constructed — has been stuck in a cycle we can all recite in our sleep. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a rerun of the arguments.

Here is the loop we are in. Money gives us no trouble: we agree it is constituted by collective acceptance and could not exist without it. The moment we move to gender and race, the same four moves replay in the same order. One participant presses the deflationary point that causal social construction is trivially ubiquitous — my being classified as a professor changes what I am like, so by that standard every kind involving humans is constructed, and the thesis has lost its bite — and the constructionists answer that they mean constitutive construction, the social facts do not merely cause but partly make the kind what it is. Someone then asks what that could even mean without circularity: to say the kind exists in virtue of our classifying practices seems to presuppose the very kind, since the practices would need to be practices of classifying that. The naturalist at the table rejoins that for race there is a separate factual question about population structure — real, non-arbitrary genetic variation, just not tracking the folk categories — so the whole discussion proceeds at the wrong level. Finally, someone makes the ameliorative move: instead of asking what our terms actually pick out, we should ask what they ought to pick out to serve antiracist and feminist purposes, and the metaphysical question dissolves into a political one. Everyone nods, the meeting ends, and next month we start over. Nobody has changed their mind about anything in a year, and I no longer believe it is because anyone is confused about the arguments.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the dispute is not really about ontology at all but about what a kind term earns its keep by doing. The naturalist is asking whether the term supports induction and explanation in the way homeostatic property clusters do; the ameliorativist is asking whether it anchors norms, entitlements, and the assignment of standing; the deflationist and the constitutivist are talking past each other about the direction of fit between classification and kind. If that is right, the disagreement is partly verbal — the word 'real' doing two jobs — but the verbal overlay conceals several genuinely different substantive questions that never get separated. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a real first-order metaphysical disagreement under the verbal noise? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: a sharpened distinction, a decision to split the question into the taxonomy question and the normative question, an argument that one of the four positions is unstable, or nothing short of abandoning the shared vocabulary?

One request about how you answer: please keep what is genuinely settled in this area — for instance, the broad agreement about how paradigm cases like money work — separate from what remains contested among people who know the literature, and do not present a contested position, including any of the four above or your own diagnosis, as if it were the consensus view.
