I am at work on my third novel, a work of literary science fiction in which the only practicable way to travel between the stars is a scanner that records a person's complete physical and psychological condition, disassembles the body, transmits the record at lightspeed, and reconstitutes the traveler at the destination. The narrative turns on a refusal: my narrator's daughter departs this way for a colony world, and the narrator — and, if I have done my work, the reader — must live with the conviction that the woman who arrives there is a new person, and that the daughter is dead. I have therefore built the book around a philosophical position, and I want to be honest that I hold it myself, though less firmly than my narrator does: that the traveler does not survive; that however exact the psychological continuity, stepping into the scanner is dying, and what emerges is someone else who falsely inherits a life. I am aware that Parfit devised the teletransporter precisely to wear this conviction down, and that he went on to conclude that identity is not what matters — which, if he is right, threatens to make my book a three-hundred-page elegy for a mistake.

Before I lock the structure, I want a map of the position's exposure. Tell me what would count against it: not only the familiar branching case (which I have in fact built into the plot as a "redundant mode," an hour's grace in which the original body is not yet disassembled and must watch its double say goodbye), but the harder pressure points — gradual replacement of neurons by synthetic duplicates, the spectrum of cases between ordinary biological survival and full teletransportation, dementia and other ordinary failures of psychological continuity that we do not treat as death, the apparent arbitrariness of treating the route by which continuity is secured as decisive. I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, a clear separation between what a thoughtful defender of the view would concede as counting against it and what such a defender would dismiss, and an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by appeals to a "further fact" of identity — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a metaphysical thesis of this kind or simply what metaphysical theses are like.

One last, practical matter. I cannot have my narrator deliver a philosophy lecture, so I also need to know which of these defeaters can be dramatized rather than argued — which ones can live inside scenes, in the way the daughter's recorded messages, meant to be opened by "whoever she becomes," are meant to do. And I need to know the worst case for me as a novelist: if the reductionist view is correct, is my narrator's grief still intelligible, or does it collapse into something a well-informed reader must see as obtuse? If it remains intelligible, tell me why, in terms I can put into her mouth.
