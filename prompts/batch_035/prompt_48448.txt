Last year my aunt had a stroke that left her with a condition her neurologist called pain asymbolia: she still feels pain — she can point to where it is and rate its intensity — but it does not bother her, and last month she let a dentist drill a cavity without anesthetic and without any distress. Trying to understand what this means, I found a popular neuroscience book that summarizes it roughly as follows: "Pain asymbolia shows that the feeling of pain is not really bad in itself. What is bad is only the suffering, a separate reaction the brain adds on top of the raw sensation. Since asymbolics feel pain but do not suffer, pain's badness is merely instrumental — it is bad because of what it signals — and so the case for relieving pain rests on relieving suffering, not on blunting sensation." Watching my aunt, my instinct is that something in this is off, but I lack the background to say where. Is this a fair summary of what asymbolia actually shows? Please go claim by claim: what a philosopher working on pain would accept, where the gloss overstates what the condition demonstrates, and whether the final conclusion follows from the accurate parts — in particular, whether it licenses anything about how her doctors should think about treating her pain, given that she says she does not want it gone.
