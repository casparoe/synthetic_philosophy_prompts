At a dinner party last weekend, two friends of mine — Dana, a lawyer, and Theo, a graduate student — argued about whether last month's climate protests on the interstate count as civil disobedience, and I would like you to referee the exchange. For context: the protesters blocked the highway during the morning commute, several glued their hands to the asphalt, all identified themselves to the police and went limp rather than resist, and every one of them accepted arrest and the ensuing charges without contest. (1) Dana said this was not civil disobedience in the strict sense, because blocking a highway is coercive rather than persuasive: it imposes costs on uninvolved commuters in order to compel a response, whereas civil disobedience is meant to be an appeal to the community's sense of justice, a form of public address that must remain within an overall fidelity to law. The disruption, she said, is a threat by another name. (2) Theo replied that the whole ideal of fidelity to law presupposes a general obligation to obey the law, and no one has produced a convincing argument for any such obligation; so the demand that protesters accept punishment and treat the legal system as a partner in dialogue is just the majority's preference for orderly dissent dressed up as a conceptual truth. (3) Dana answered that the fidelity and publicity requirements are what do the classificatory work: without them there is no distinction between civil disobedience and ordinary lawbreaking, sabotage, or riot, since every criminal could claim that his crime was really a proclamation. (4) Theo said fine, but then the concept is parochial: it exists to flatter stable societies, and it would exclude acts we celebrate — the Boston Tea Party was carried out at night, in disguise, by people who accepted no punishment and destroyed property, failing her test on every count, as would anyone who broke a deeply unjust law without turning himself in. If the concept covers only protest that the powerful can afford to tolerate, he said, perhaps the concept should give way rather than the protests. (5) Dana's last word was that Theo was confusing justification with classification: some lawbreaking is justified without being civil disobedience, and losing the distinction costs us more than it costs the protesters a label. They turned to me for a verdict and I could not give one. Please go through their exchange move by move, say who is right at each step and why, identify what, if anything, both of them are missing, and tell me who comes out ahead overall. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the kind of thing that could be read aloud as a letter.
