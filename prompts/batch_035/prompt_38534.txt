I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and last month I was sent to a conference on decision theory to write a piece about whether expected utility theory is "dead" (editors love that framing, ugh). The panel went over time and everyone spilled into the hotel bar afterwards, where two of the panelists -- I'll call them the economist and the cognitive scientist, since they're both fairly well known and the exchange got a little heated -- spent over an hour going at each other about the Allais paradox, and I couldn't decide who was winning, which is honestly rare for me.

The cognitive scientist opened with the classic setup: would you take a million dollars for sure, or a gamble with a 1% chance of nothing, 89% chance of the million, and 10% chance of five million? Most people take the sure million. Then: would you prefer an 89% chance of nothing with 11% chance of a million, or a 90% chance of nothing with 10% chance of five million? Most people now take the second. He said that this pattern is just a fact about human beings, and that Allais himself thought there was something genuinely rational in clinging to certainty when certainty is actually available -- that treating a sure thing and a near-sure thing as interchangeable was a hidden assumption of the independence axiom that had never actually been demonstrated. The economist said it doesn't matter how you dress it up: the two choices, taken together, violate independence, so at least one of them is normatively irrational, and the fact that most people make them just means most people are irrational in a systematic, predictable way. The cognitive scientist came back with: irrational by what standard? If the axiom can't be independently defended, using it to condemn the choices is question-begging. The economist replied that dropping independence has a price -- if your preferences aren't separable across states of the world, you can be turned into a money pump, or end up paying to avoid hearing news, and once you bless "whatever feels deep about certainty," you've given up the ability to say anything systematic about choice at all. The cognitive scientist said money pumps presuppose agents who accept every sequential offer someone dangles in front of them, which real people and real markets don't do, and that anyway the weird-consequences argument cuts the other way: if defending the axiom generates conclusions people find absurd, maybe that's evidence against the axiom, not the people. The economist's closing move was that "every violation is fine if you attach a plausible-sounding story to it" is exactly how a field rots from the inside.

They eventually adjourned to catch their trains without either conceding anything, and I stood there with my notebook genuinely unsure. The economist kept insisting that normative standards can't be dethroned by survey responses, the cognitive scientist kept insisting the burden of proof was on the axiom to justify itself before it gets to convict anyone. I have to file my piece soon and I dont want to just present it as "two experts disagree" -- that's lazy journalism and my editor already definately flagged me for it once. Can you walk me through their exchange move by move: who's right about which specific point, where did each of them overreach, and is there something both of them are missing that would let me write something sharper than a he-said-she-said?
