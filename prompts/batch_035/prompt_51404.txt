Quick question that came up doing research for my next book -- I'm a novelist, not a lawyer or philosopher, so bear with me.

My plot needs a character who's acquitted at his criminal trial and then, a year later, found liable for the same events in civil court on basically the same evidence. I assumed that was a legal impossibility until I learned it actually happens (the OJ Simpson case, basically). Digging further, I found the US system doesn't even have two standards of proof, it has three: preponderance of the evidence for most civil suits, clear and convincing evidence for weightier things like deportations or terminating parental rights, and beyond reasonable doubt for crimes. So the exact same body of evidence can be "enough" in one courtroom and "not enough" in another, and the difference tracks how much is at stake for the person if they lose -- their money, their residency, their kids, their liberty.

Here's what bugs me. That seems to presuppose that how much evidence you need before you're entitled to conclude something isn't a fixed property of the evidence itself -- it varies with what rides on the conclusion. Like the same photo of the defendant at the scene is stronger evidence when you're deciding whether to fine him than when you're deciding whether to cage him. Is that a real position in epistemology, or does the law just quietly assume it? And is it coherent as a package, or does it collapse into something uglier -- "we act on 51% confidence whenever we don't care that much about the defendant"? A friend mentioned there's some debate about whether stakes can change what counts as knowledge, but I don't know the litrature and don't want a reading list, just the ideas.

Also: if you swapped the whole ladder of standards for one flat probability threshold -- the same number everywhere -- what would actually change in how trials run, and what would we lose? I'm asking partly for the book and partly because I can't tell anymore whether the three-tier thing is deep or just historical accretion.
