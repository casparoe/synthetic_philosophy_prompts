I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of art course that is currently spending two weeks on aesthetic testimony — whether, and in what sense, one can come to know that something is beautiful or delicious or moving on the say-so of others, rather than through encountering it oneself. One of my classmates, Ravi, issues snap verdicts on precisely these questions whenever they arise, and I have started keeping a list of them because I cannot work out what principle generates them. His record so far:

1. At a wine bar, a friend of ours asked whether she could know, on the sommelier's word, that her glass was corked before tasting it. Ravi said no: she would be fully justified in believing it, but she would not know it — the glass was sitting right in front of her.
2. Ravi has never read the nine-hundred-page nineteenth-century French novel he mentions when the topic comes up, and says he never will, yet he insists he knows it is a masterpiece, on the strength of what he calls generations of critical agreement.
3. Of a legendary late-sixties concert for which no recording survives, Ravi says we can know, from the testimony of the people who were in the room, that it was an extraordinary performance.
4. He has never seen the Grand Canyon and says he probably never will, but he says he knows it is sublime, on the basis of photographs and the agreement of essentially everyone who has stood at the rim.
5. When a waiter swore that the duck was the best thing on the menu, Ravi said that believing him is one thing, but you do not know the duck is delicious until you have tasted it.
6. His grandmother describes the northern lights over the fjord where she grew up as the most beautiful thing she has ever seen. Ravi says he does not know that they were beautiful; he knows only that she experienced them as beautiful.

I have stared at this list long enough to see that more than one principle fits all six verdicts. One candidate: testimony can yield aesthetic knowledge only when first-hand experience is realistically unavailable to the believer. Another: it can yield such knowledge only when it is robustly corroborated rather than resting on a lone witness. There may also be a reading on which his denials are not about knowledge at all, but about some richer thing — appreciation, or understanding why — that he refuses to dignify with the word he uses for the easy cases.

Last week a fresh question came up at dinner, and I want to predict Ravi's answer before our seminar gets to it. A retired perfumer of apparently impeccable honesty and discernment tells him that a certain perfume, discontinued decades ago, was the most beautiful ever composed. The surviving bottles are in collectors' hands and are never opened; the perfumer is the only person Ravi has ever heard describe it from memory, and the perfume's original public reception was, by all accounts, lukewarm. Ravi will never smell it.

So, three requests. First, lay out the candidate rules that fit the six recorded verdicts, and say what each one predicts about the perfume case — I believe the rules I've spotted come apart on it, and I'd like to know whether that's right. Second, give me your best guess at what Ravi will say, with your reasons. Third, tell me which further case would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate rules — I have a suspicion that something both readily available and massively corroborated would do it, but I would like you to confirm or correct that, and to say which verdict on such a case would support which rule. And finally: does his record look like a version of the acquaintance principle from our seminar discussions — the thesis that genuine aesthetic judgment requires first-hand experience of the object — or is he tracking something that principle cannot express? I am interested in the reasoning itself, not in references or a reading list.
