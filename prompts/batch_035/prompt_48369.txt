Two members of my ethics reading group have spent the past year arguing about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and they have finally agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration. One of them holds, in the tradition of the agglomeration argument, that there can be no genuine moral dilemmas: any apparent case in which you ought to do A and ought to do B, while being unable to do both, must be redescribed as epistemic confusion, as a clash of pro tanto duties one of which simply overrides the other, or as a self-inflicted predicament for which earlier wrongdoing is responsible. The other holds, in the tradition of the moral-residue argument associated with Bernard Williams, that there are real cases in which each of two incompatible acts is morally required, so that whichever one does, one does wrong — and that the fittingness of guilt or remorse about the foregone option, over and above regret about its outcome, is precisely the datum that proves it.

Write the joint statement these two should be able to sign. Structure it as follows, in British English and in your own words throughout (do not rely on quotations, titles or references; state the positions descriptively):

1. Grounds of agreement (~300 words): a numbered list of claims both camps accept — for instance, that conflicts between moral requirements are common; that the distinction between epistemic and ontological dilemmas matters; that the phenomenology of residue is real rather than mere self-indulgence; and that pro tanto duties can conflict without anything genuinely dilemmatic following. Phrase each claim so that neither party would balk at signing it.

2. The disagreement, stated (~250 words): a table with three columns — the question at issue, the impossibilist's formulation of her position, and the dilemmist's formulation of his — with each cell worded so that its own side would accept it. Cover at least: whether 'ought implies can' applies to each requirement individually or only to their conjunction; whether agglomeration holds for all-things-considered oughts as opposed to pro tanto ones; and whether doing wrong in a dilemma means violating an obligation simpliciter or merely a pro tanto obligation.

3. Cruxes (~450 words): the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, each with a note on what argument or case both would accept as bearing on it. I suspect one crux is the target of the residue: is the guilt that survives doing the best one can directed at the act itself (guilt proper), or merely at the circumstances (grief or agent-regret)? Another is whether the permissibility of flipping a coin in symmetric cases shows that both options were in fact permitted. A third is whether genuine dilemmas can arise for a purely consequentialist agent, or only for agents with deontological constraints and role-based obligations.

4. Test cases and predictions (~350 words): at least three cases both sides agree bear on the cruxes — one symmetric (two drowning swimmers, a single rope, everything else equal), one asymmetric (a promise kept versus a life saved, where breaking the promise is clearly the worse option), and one self-imposed (a rescuer whose earlier negligence created the very conflict he now faces) — and, for each, each side's written-down prediction about what residue would be fitting, whether 'I did wrong' would be true, and whether a coin flip would be both permissible and residue-free.

5. Verbal or genuine (~150 words): state which parts of the residual dispute, if any, are verbal — for example, merely a disagreement over the word 'wrong' — which parts are substantive, and why.

The document should be signed by both parties in name only; write it as though each had veto power over every sentence, so that nothing appears that either would refuse to put her or his name to.
