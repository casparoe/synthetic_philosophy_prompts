I cover AI and science for a general-audience outlet, and over the last couple years I've quietly become a realist about neural network representations. When interpretability people pull a "golden gate bridge feature" out of a big model, and ablating it or steering with it predictably changes what the model says about bridges, I take that at face value: the model really has bridge-ish stuff in it, and interpretability researchers are discovering it, not inventing it. My editor, who is a pain in the best way, keeps pushing the anti-realist line at me: that features are like constellations, where we draw the lines and the network just has a huge correlated smear of directions, and any halfway decent coordinate system on that smear would look like a "discovery." I want to stress test my view before I write a big feature on it. So: what would actually count against realism about interpretability, and how much of it is already in the data?

Some candidate defeaters I half understand, please rank them and add ones I'm missing:

1. The null-hypothesis problem: steering with a genuinely nonsense direction (or a direction from a garbage dictionary) works about as well as steering with the "real" feature. I gather random-direction controls are run sometimes, but I don't know what the actual results look like or how much weight to give them.
2. Feature splitting: the same concept fragmenting into more and more features every time you make the dictionary bigger. Is that discovery resolving into finer grains, or an artifact of the method? And how would you even tell the difference?
3. Two independently trained dictionaries that both work fine but carve up roughly the same region differently, with no fact of the matter about which carving is "the" concepts.
4. The causal-smear worry: large models are robust to all kinds of perturbation, and ablating almost anything causes large downstream effects, so maybe the clean causal stories we tell about features are cleaner than the evidence.
5. Universality failing -- analogous features just not showing up across architectures, training runs, or scales.

For each, I want to know what a committed realist would concede as genuinely counting against them versus dismiss as a methodological hiccup, and where the concede/dismiss line should honestly sit rather than where partisans draw it. And finally the meta question my editor would ask: has realism been qualified into something like "the network approximately implements whatever our best current method finds," which nothing could ever refute -- and if so, is that fatal for a view like this or just what you get in a young empirical field? Be blunt, I can take it, and I'd rather lose the argument with you than with him in print.
