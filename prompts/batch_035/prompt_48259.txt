My father died in March, and I have since become the executor of his estate. Most of that work is clerical, but some of it isn't: his will contains conditions that reach past his death. The lake house goes to my brother and me on the condition that we not sell it outside the family for twenty years; a bequest to a nephew is contingent on his finishing the apprenticeship my father arranged for him; and, apart from anything in the will, the whole family made a promise at his bedside that we would keep the business he built out of the hands of the private equity firm that had been circling it. The probate court registered all of this without comment, as though there were nothing philosophically strange about a dead man imposing binding requirements on the living, decades into the future.

What I would like to understand is what this body of practice presupposes about death and about the interests of the dead. Some years ago I read the Epicurean argument that death cannot be bad for the one who dies: when death is present, he is not, and what does not exist cannot be deprived of anything. If that argument is sound, it is hard to see how anything that happens after my father's death could be bad for him rather than only for us — including our breaking the bedside promise, or heirs auctioning off the heirlooms he meant to stay together. Yet the law, and my own conscience, seem to treat his interests as surviving him: the promise feels owed to him, not merely to the living members of the family who would be distressed by its breach.

So please reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the enforcement of conditional bequests, and the moral weight we give to deathbed promises, presuppose some view on which a person's welfare can consist in states of the world he never experiences — a dead man's desires being fulfilled without anyone doing the desiring? Does it presuppose that a life has a shape that extends beyond its last moment? Or is the practice underdetermined: could a consistent Epicurean still enforce the will and honor the promise, on the grounds that these serve the living — the testator's peace of mind while alive, or the survivors' interest in being the kind of people who keep faith? I'd also like you to address the places where the practice seems internally uneasy: the law limits how far into the future a will can reach, and refuses to enforce conditions it judges contrary to public policy, which suggests the presupposed view is only half-heartedly held. And tell me what would actually change in the practice if we adopted the Epicurean view outright — or whether, on reflection, almost nothing would.
