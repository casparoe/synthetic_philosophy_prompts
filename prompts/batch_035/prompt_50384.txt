Write a dialogue between two researchers who've just come out of a conference session on the science of consciousness and are arguing over coffee. One is a committed heterophenomenologist in the Dennett tradition: she thinks the only data a science of consciousness can use are third-person observations — subjects' reports, reaction times, brain scans, behaviour — treated as texts to be interpreted, with no special authority granted to the subject's own interpretation of their experience. The other is a phenomenologist who thinks this throws out the very phenomenon: for him, first-person experience is the primary datum, and reducing it to reports is like studying a storm by only reading the weather station's printouts. He thinks subjects can, with training, be treated as skilled observers of their own minds, not just as measurement devices that occasionally malfunction.

Make it a genuine back-and-forth where each responds to the other's strongest point, not their weakest. A few threads I'd specifically like them to cover: (1) whether confabulation experiments (split-brain patients, choice blindness, that sort of thing) show that introspective reports are just more behaviour, or only that they're fallible like any instrument; (2) the trained-observer question — if a meditation practitioner gives a disciplined, fine-grained report of their attention, is the heterophenomenologist really allowed to just file it under "subject's text" with no more weight than a naive report; and (3) whether treating subjects' self-reports this way is quietly paternalistic or disrespectful, given that in every other part of science the thing being studied can't also be a person who might object to how they're being described.

Since this is for a piece I'm editing, a couple of requests: use British English throughout (behaviour, colour, realise, etc.), and at the end add a short note on where the dialogue takes liberties — e.g., where one character's position leans on a contested reading, and what consideration would most plausibly push each of them to change their mind. Don't feel you have to pick a winner, but if the note does come down on one side, say what evidence or argument would flip that verdict.
