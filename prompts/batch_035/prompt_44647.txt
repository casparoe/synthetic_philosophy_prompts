My team is three years and way too much money into an internal tool that everybody privately admits is doomed, and at the retro yesterday our director killed the whole "should we keep funding it to save face" discussion with the old line "don't throw good money after bad." It sounded wise, so nobody argued. But afterwards I got to thinking: I genuinely don't know what precise claim that proverb is making, and I suspect different readings give different verdicts about our project. Can you lay out the candidate claims for me?

Here's roughly what I want sorted:

1. The pure accounting reading: money already spent is unrecoverable, period. That seems trivially true, but it also does zero work — it tells you the money is gone, not what to do next.
2. The decision-theory reading: past expenditures give you literally no reason, not even a weak partial one, to spend more. Is this one actually defensible? I've seen people argue that reputational costs, learning effects, and signaling to future teams make some "sunk" costs not fully sunk — does that break the reading, or is it just smuggling in consequences that were never sunk to begin with?
3. A "seperate decisions" reading: each incremental funding decision should be evaluated on its own, on future costs and benefits alone. But here's the thing that bugs me — I read about the dollar auction setup, where the second-place bidder also has to pay their bid, so quitting costs you something too. In that game the advice to stop is only correct once continuing is negative-value going forward. So under reading 3, does the proverb even tell you to quit? Or does it just tell you how to compute, and quit only falls out under extra assumptions?

For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's flat-out false. Then tell me which reading my director would need for the proverb to actually settle our argument — because the annoying possibility is that she used a truism to close a question that needed the substantive (and contested) reading. And if there's a reading where the word "bad" is doing real work — say, the money only counts as "bad" if continuing has negative expected value going foward — I'd like that spelled out too, since that seems like the reading doing the most work while being the least obviously true.
