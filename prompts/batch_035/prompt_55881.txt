Quick background: my roommate and I watched a documentary on split-brain patients (the corpus callosotomy stuff from the old epilepsy surgeries) and ended up in a genuine argument that neither of us can settle, so I'm farming it out. The question: what's your credence that after a full callosotomy, a patient genuinely hosts two parallel streams of conscious experience, rather than one stream that's just degraded in its access to information? My roommate is at like 0.9 — he thinks the experimental results basically settle it, the left hand doing things the verbal hemisphere can't explain, a word flashed to the right visual field being unreachable by the left, the right hemisphere answering questions by pointing while the talking hemisphere denies knowing anything. I'm closer to 0.4, because I keep worrying that 'two streams' presupposes some metric of unity that nobody has defined, and because these patients don't feel like two people — they walk around, drive, live normal lives, and report being one person, which seems like it should count for something.

So don't just give me a bare number — decompose it. Here's how I'd start carving the question, but feel free to restructure: the probability that consciousness in a human brain admits of division at all, versus being all-or-nothing and bound together by something (attention, a global workspace, whatever) that no amount of cortical cutting can split; the probability that the corpus callosum is actually the thing doing the relevant binding, versus subcortical pathways keeping enough integration alive that what we're seeing is one partly-disconnected mind rather than two minds; the probability that the surgery creates a second experiencer rather than making one experiencer selectively blind to some of its inputs; and the probability that 'one versus two' is even well-posed rather than a verbal dispute about what counts as a separate stream. Give each a rough number with one line of reasoning, show how you combine them into your final credence, and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to.

Then there's a twist my roommate threw out that I could not answer at all, and it's honestly the part I most want your take on. Suppose you could somehow ask the right hemisphere, through a channel only it can use, whether it believes the left hemisphere is conscious — and it answers no (or yes, or 'how would I know'). Now each hemisphere apparently has its own credence about the other's consciousness. If those credences disagree, does the disagreement itself tell us anything about how many streams there are, or is it just confabulation from a system that has no introspective access across the midline and is guessing like anyone else? And more basically: should the very fact that I can coherently pose that second-order question move my 0.4 up or down, or is it a fun distraction from the decomposition? My instinct is that a framework where the two halves can hold conflicting beliefs about each other is already halfway to the 'two minds' answer, but I can't tell if that's a real argument or just the intuition doing the work again.
