ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on the hedonism thing. the classic objection is that "pleasure" isn't one thing -- the pleasure of scratching a mosquito bite, the pleasure of getting a joke, the pleasure of revenge, the pleasure of a long shower after hiking -- and if there's no single property they all share, then "maximize pleasure" isn't even a coherent instruction. fine. but every paper I find just proposes one candidate (some "feeling tone," or an attitude of enjoyment toward its object, or whatever) and argues it sorta works. before I keep reading I want the spec. write me the list of requirements that ANY adequate answer to the heterogeneity problem has to meet, with the case behind each. some I can already half-see:

- it has to cover the full spread of cases people actually call pleasures, and the spread is genuinely weird: bodily ones, intellectual ones, the "hurts so good" ones (deep tissue massage, spicy food, the ache after a run), relief when pain stops, anticipation of things not yet felt, and the nasty ones like schadenfreude and the torturer who sincerely enjoys his work. a theory that quietly only fits the bodily cases has failed even if it never says a false word
- no circularity: you can't define pleasure in terms of things that are only intelligible as pleasures already ("liking" the sensation, finding it "good" in the relevant way, etc.). this one seems non-negotiable to me but you tell me
- it has to say something about whether the sadist's pleasure is a *counterexample* to hedonism or just an uncomfortable instance of it -- I've seen it go both ways and I don't know which the theory is allowed to decide vs. which is stipulated in advance
- it has to be the kind of thing that can be added up and compared, since hedonism as a theory of well-being needs pleasure to be aggregable within a life and comparable across lives, not just individually identifiable
- it can't collapse into desire-satisfactionism without saying so -- if the "common property" turns out to be just "wanted while occurring," that's a different theory and it should be announced as a concession, not smuggled in

and then the parts I actually want your judgment on:

1. which of these are real requirements and which are camp prejudices? I suspect "there must be ONE property" is itself the prejudice, and that the correct move is a family-resemblance / disjunctive answer -- but a disjunction feels like giving up, so tell me if the "no cop-out" demand and the "no false unity" demand are jointly satisfiable or whether one has to go
2. which single requirement knocks out the most candidates? my guess is the no-circularity one kills every "phenomenological feel" answer at once, but maybe I'm wrong
3. if you have room, a real quick scorecard: how do (a) the felt-quality view, (b) the attitude/enjoyment-of-object view, and (c) the just-a-disjunction view do against the list. keep it short, the criteria are the point

you can be opinionated. if a pair of requirements can't be jointly met, say that plainly -- I'd rather have an unsatisfiable spec than a fake one. no table needed, plain prose is fine
