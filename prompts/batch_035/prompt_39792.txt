I've been hanging around rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, so the idea that has really colonized my brain is Dennett's Darwin/Turing thing — the "strange inversion of reasoning" where you explain competence by subtracting comprehension instead of adding it: evolution produces exquisitely designed organisms with no designer anywhere, and a Turing machine cranks through rules with nobody home who understands anything. I've seen that point made with evolution and with chess engines a hundred times, but I just finished Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun and I can't stop turning it over, and I'd like a proper reading of the novel through that lens.

Here's the part that gets me. Klara is genuinely competent — she reads people so well she can pass as Josie, she plans, she navigates a world she barely understands, and she's basically the only agent who ever acts to save Josie's life. But her "comprehension" looks like a pile of false models: the Sun is a kind person with healing nourishment, the pollution machine is his enemy, and her big move is a bargain — she tracks down the Cootings Machine and destroys it, at the cost of some of the fluid in her head and possibly her own cognitive abilities, in exchange for the Sun saving Josie the way he saved the beggar man and his dog. And the novel seems to let her be right in effect: the clouds eventually part, Josie recovers, and Klara ends up in the AF scrapyard content, still thinking the Sun came through for Josie.

So the question I want a reading to actually take a stance on: does the novel vindicate the strange inversion — Klara's competence at caring for Josie never required true beliefs, and her solar theology is just the machinery doing its job, maybe even doing it well, the way a false model can steer you right — or does it complicate it? Because that false theology is what motivates everything she does: she only destroys the machine, only keeps pleading at the barn, because she believes the Sun bargains. If the false comprehension is load-bearing for the competence, is that still competence without comprehension? And there's the whole Capaldi business, where he insists there's nothing inside Josie that an accurate replica couldn't carry on and Klara quietly disagrees — I honestly can't tell which side of that argument the competence-without-comprehension frame lands on, and I'd love the reading to say so. Please take both the novel and the actual Darwin/Turing point seriously, and be honest about where the novel resists the reading rather than forcing it to fit. No need for quotes from the book, just plot-level reading.
