I'm a novelist, and I'm fighting with a scene where a character has to choose between doing something that ends a life and just standing back and letting it end — I won't bore you with the plot mechanics, but it's basically: administer the overdose, or walk out of the room and let nature take its course. And I keep flip-flopping on which one to give her, because I honestly don't know whether the two options are morally different or whether that's just something I was raised to feel. So I want to read Of Mice and Men through the doing vs. allowing, killing vs. letting die debate, because I've come to think Steinbeck basically ran the experiment twice.

Candy's dog: Candy lets Carlson take the old dog out and shoot it, and afterwards his regret is very specifically that he ought to have shot the dog himself rather than let no stranger do it. Then at the end George does the doing himself with Lennie — it's even the same gun Carlson used on the dog — in order to preempt a worse death (the lynch mob, and you can be sure the mob wasn't going to be quick about it). So you've got one character who allows a killing by handing it off to someone indifferent, and one who kills so that a crueler killing doesn't happen, and the novel seems to put its weight on the who-does-it question, not the whether. Candy's regret reads as regret about delegation, not about the dog being dead — the dog was suffering and everyone agrees shooting it was for the best.

Things I'd love you to take seriously:

(1) Does Steinbeck treat the doing/allowing distinction as morally real, or only narratively real — a thing characters organize their guilt and regret around, without the book committing to whether those feelings track anything? I read somewhere (can't remember where, some popular philosophy thing) that some philosophers think the distinction collapses — there's a famous pair of cases where one man drowns his cousin for the inheritance and another stands by and lets his cousin drown, and everything else about the two situations is identical, and the claim is that if motive and outcome are the same there can't be a moral difference in the means. If that's right, is Steinbeck just trading on the reader's unexamined intuition in exactly the way that argument accuses common sense of doing?

(2) George clearly kills Lennie — no ambiguity there, the gun is in George's hand. But the scene is written slow and quiet, with George telling Lennie about the little place they'll have, and it's easy to read it as if something is being allowed rather than done — as though Lennie's death is the natural terminus of everything already in motion and George just arrives there first, ahead of the mob. Is that a real feature of how the scene works, and if so, is the novel pulling a trick — making a doing feel like an allowing? I'm tempted to steal that exact trick for my scene and I'd like to know whether it's honest.

(3) This last one is what I actually need for my book. Candy's regret — it's not regret that the dog is dead, it's regret that the doing passed through somebody else's hands, somebody who didn't love the dog. Is there a version of the doing/allowing debate that captures regret like that — located in the agent's relation to the act rather than in the outcome? And if a philosopher who works on this distinction read the two scenes side by side, what would they say the novel gets right that the journal articles don't?

Don't reduce the book to an illustration of the distinction, and push back on my reading where it doesn't fit — tell me where the novel resists being read this way at all, if it does. No citations or reading lists needed, just the reading.
