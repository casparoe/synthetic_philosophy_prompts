I'm not a lawyer or a philosopher, just someone who keeps noticing a weird pattern and cant let it go, so maybe you can straighten it out. In the US (and elsewhere, but the pattern is cleanest there), courts have knocked down official school prayer as an establishment of religion, yet legislatures still open their sessions with a chaplain's prayer, witnesses are sworn in on the Bible, the currency announces that we trust in God, and the pledge of allegiance manages to mention God under the nation — and all of that survives challenge, on the reasoning that it's tradition or ceremony at this point, not real religion. People sometimes call this phenomenon ceremonial deism, whatever we think of the label. Here's my question: what does that whole arrangement presuppose about how something counts as religious in the first place? The rationale seems to be that repetition over generations drains the religious content out of words — as if a prayer's religiousness were a function of how fresh it is, or how likely the audience is to actually hear it as worship rather than as a warm-up ritual. That strikes me as a strange theory of language and ritual, and I'm not sure it's coherent. And then there's the public reason angle, which is what got me started: if you think religious reasoning shouldn't ground law because not all citizens can accept it, is ceremonial deism a victory for that view or a cheat? Is a de-fanged prayer exactly what public reason looks like when it gets religion to promise not to say anything, or is it public reason letting religion in through a ceremonial back door while pretending the door was bricked up? Please reconstruct whatever implicit view of religion, and of endorsement, makes the pattern hang together; tell me whether the parts presuppose inconsistent things (oaths on the Bible seem to need the Bible to still mean something, whereas the currency motto is supposedly fine precisely because it doesn't); and say what would have to change if we replaced the meaning-fades-with-use idea with something like the actual hearer's uptake deciding what counts as religious. Don't bother with case names or citations — I don't care about the law per se, only about the commitments hiding under it.
