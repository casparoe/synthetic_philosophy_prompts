Our AI safety reading group has been stuck for two sessions now on the worst-case vs average-case guarantee debate — the thing where one side argues that safety arguments based on average performance over some test distribution are basically worthless for alignment, because the catastrophic failures live in the tail, because the deployment distribution won't match your eval distribution, and because a capable enough system is (effectively) optimizing against your evaluation, so you need worst-case-style properties (formal guarantees, real adversarial robustness, red-teaming by people actually trying to break the thing) — and the other side says worst-case demands are fantasy, nothing that matters ever gets verified that way, and the honest position is quantified average-case claims plus iteration. We keep going in circles with hypothetical superintelligences, so I want us to test the whole framing on an episode that has nothing to do with AI: the 737 MAX / MCAS disaster.

Facts as I understand them (correct me if I've got any of this wrong): Boeing added a flight-control automation, MCAS, that trimmed the nose down based on the angle-of-attack reading, and it trusted a single AoA sensor. When that sensor failed and stuck, MCAS kept commanding nose-down, the pilots were'nt even told the system existed, and two crashes — late 2018 and early 2019, Lion Air and Ethiopian Airlines — killed 346 people and grounded the fleet worldwide. On top of that, a lot of the certification work was effectively done by Boeing employees acting on the FAA's behalf, and the design logic behind trusting one sensor was roughly "a failure like that is extremely improbable, so redundancy isn't required" — which is a straight-up average-case / probabilistic reliability judgment.

So here's the ask: analyze the MCAS episode as a case study in the worst-case vs average-case distinction from the alignment literature. Do the mapping carefully — what plays the role of the test distribution, what would a worst-case guarantee even have looked like for a plane-pilot-sensor system, is the certification process something like a benchmark suite, and is the Goodhart pressure people worry about in eval-based alignment the same thing that made the average-case reasoning so convenient for the people doing their own certifying? Then tell me honestly where the mapping breaks, because there's obviously no adversarial intelligence anywhere in MCAS — no learned policy, no goal, just a deterministic control loop and a broken vane. And yet the worst-case people's core intuition (average over your assumed distribution is fine until it isn't, and the stakes are asymmetric) looks vindicated here. Does that mean the worst/average distinction shouldn't be cashed out in terms of adversarial optimization at all — maybe in terms of who chooses the evaluation scenarios, or fatness of the failure tail, or something else? And finally, what does the episode show about the alignment debate itself: confirmation that you need worst-case (or average-case-with-honest-tails), an anomaly (aviation is the domain where average-case reliability reasoning usually works brilliantly, and nobody calls a 737 misaligned), or a case the framing never anticipated? I genuinely don't know the answer and neither does the group, so dont sand it off into a both-sides thing.
