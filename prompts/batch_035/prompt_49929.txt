I chair the research oversight committee for stem cell and organoid work at a large university. A neuroscience lab has applied to amend its approved protocol for human cortical organoids. Under the current protocol, organoids are grown for sixty days and then discarded. The amendment would keep a subset alive for two years or more and place them under a training regime: patterned optogenetic stimulation intended to get networks of organoid neurons to learn simple predictive tasks, with performance measured by how well their spontaneous activity comes to anticipate stimulus patterns. Pilot data attached to the application show that some organoids develop self-sustained oscillations and stimulus-specific responses. The stated scientific aim is to model network-level features of early cortical development relevant to epilepsy.

A postdoc in the lab has filed a formal objection to the amendment. Her position, in brief: our welfare protections for animal subjects tacitly assume that what matters morally is not the biological substrate but the functional organization of a system — what it does, not what it is made of. If that assumption is right, then a mass of human cortical neurons that is active, organized, and capable of learning is at least a candidate for some minimal form of valenced experience, and we currently afford these organoids less consideration than a planarian in a learning experiment. The principal investigator's reply is that the organoids have no body, no sensory periphery, no homeostatic loops, and no structures beyond a rough cortical analog; whatever function they exhibit is not the right kind of function to carry experience, and imposing animal-level welfare review on a culture dish would effectively end the research program.

The committee must issue a written decision: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Draft the decision memo we can circulate before next month's meeting. It should:

- state a clear, actionable bottom line, including — if you recommend approval with conditions — conditions specific enough that the lab could implement them and the committee could audit them (for example: defined endpoints, monitoring criteria tied to measurable network properties, limits on duration or stimulation intensity, or a staged schedule in which continued cultivation requires renewed review);
- take an actual position on the functionalist issue rather than deferring everything to "more research is needed," engaging both the multiple-realizability point (if minds are substrate-independent, organoids are in principle in the running as realizers) and the strongest counterargument available to the PI — for instance, that morally relevant function may require embodiment, integration with a sensorimotor loop, or global organizational features that a disembodied cortical analog lacks;
- treat the postdoc's objection and the PI's reply evenhandedly, explicitly acknowledging the strongest consideration on whichever side loses; and
- remain honest about the committee's uncertainty without allowing that uncertainty to function, by default, as a reason to do nothing.
