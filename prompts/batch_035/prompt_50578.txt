I am a novelist drafting a story set aboard a generation ship whose onboard fabricators, after two centuries of self-repair, self-modification, and repurposing, have begun to behave in ways the crew can only describe as protective. My technical advisor, a biochemist, keeps waving all of this off with the one-liner that life is just chemistry. So far she has used it three times: to rule my fabricators non-living, to veto a subplot about a crystalline growth that faithfully copies its own defects, and to insist that my opening chapter should not call a virus alive. I suspect the sentence is quietly doing several different jobs. What precise claims could it be expressing — the near-tautological ones that hold of every organism anyone has actually examined, the substantive ones about what life must be made of or cannot be made of, the deflationary ones on which being alive is just a term of art for a certain class of chemical systems — and which readings, if any, are plausible and would actually license her three rulings? Please set the readings out as a numbered list, with a verdict on each, and say explicitly which of them would survive contact with her virus case.
