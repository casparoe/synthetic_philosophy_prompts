I host a small-circulation philosophy podcast, and my next guest is a compatibilist who has just published a book defending the position Daniel Dennett takes in his debate with Gregg Caruso over just deserts and legal punishment — the view that free will worth wanting survives the truth of determinism, and that a limited, non-retributive notion of just deserts is both coherent and something we should not give up. Please draft nine to ten interview questions for her. I do not want questions that just invite her to restate compatibilism or to bash hard determinism; each question should put her on the spot somewhere specific. Include at least one question on each of the following:

- Galen Strawson's basic argument for the impossibility of ultimate moral responsibility — does the kind of desert she defends still deserve the name if it concedes that no one is responsible for the character and circumstances from which their actions flow?
- Pereboom-style manipulation arguments, where an agent produced by deterministic causes is claimed to be relevantly no different from one produced by design — and whether her preferred reply (for instance, that the manipulated agent lacks the right kind of self-control or reasons-responsiveness, or that manipulation introduces injustice that mere determinism does not) actually grounds the distinction it needs.
- Caruso's charge that Dennett's forward-looking rationale for punishment — holding offenders responsible for the sake of their future selves, giving them an incentive to reform — smuggles a retributive element back in, or at least licenses harsher treatment than pure prevention and rehabilitation would justify on their own.
- Caruso's public health-quarantine model of criminal justice: can she accept large parts of it while still insisting that some residue of genuine desert is doing work the quarantine rationale cannot do, for example when an offender is dangerous to no one?
- The "difference that makes no difference" worry: Dennett and Caruso converge on a great deal of penal policy, so what really turns on refusing to call oneself a free will skeptic — including whether Dennett's claim that desert-talk keeps us treating offenders as agents to be held responsible rather than as broken things to be managed is a substantive moral point or a framing preference.
- The distributive dimension Caruso presses: given that actual punishment falls disproportionately on people dealt the worst hands in the lottery of birth and circumstance, whether she thinks her account of desert can do more than Dennett's critics allow to constrain sentences in an unjust society, or whether that constraint comes entirely from fairness considerations external to desert itself.

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, since she will have heard the standard objections many times. Finally, an epistemic request: alongside each question, note briefly whether the point I would be pressing is a standard pressure point that most people working in this debate would recognize as fair, or whether it rests on an interpretation of Dennett's position that his defenders dispute — I want to avoid putting a contested reading to her as though it were agreed background, and to know in advance where she is entitled to push back on my framing rather than answer the question as posed. Keep the questions sharp but fair: she should be able to answer each one, but not comfortably.
