I teach an upper-level seminar on population ethics, and I am writing the take-home final. Act as an experienced examiner in moral philosophy who has set many finals and knows exactly what a prepared-but-shallow student looks like. Write me one essay question on the nonidentity problem that cannot be passed by reciting Parfit. The question must be built around a case I invented for this exam, so no student will have seen it, and the case must be stated in full inside the question. The case is this: a provincial government must choose between two flood-defense programs. The Rapid Program is completed in three years through round-the-clock shifts; the disruption of daily life changes which conceptions occur when, though not how many children are eventually born, and the defenses it builds prove underbuilt, so that a century later the people alive — all of whom owe their existence to that choice, since their parents would never have met on the other timeline — live cramped, anxious lives that are nonetheless worth living. The Phased Program takes twenty years, and its labor-migration and deferred-marriage side effects change which conceptions occur and also shrink the total number of children born; the smaller later population lives long, comfortable lives behind strong defenses. No one exists under both programs. The question must force students to notice that this is not a standard nonidentity case, because both the identity and the number of future people depend on the choice, and to work out what becomes of the familiar same-number rescue of person-affecting views once the number varies. Please think carefully about what a student who has merely memorized the labels would write, and make sure the question defeats that answer. Then write a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme that names the specific moves an A answer must contain, the moves a B answer may omit, and the way the memorized-label answer fails. Deliver everything as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables — as one would write in a letter.
