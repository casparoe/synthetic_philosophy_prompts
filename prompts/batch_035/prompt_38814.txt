In 2011 the OPERA collaboration reported that muon neutrinos fired from CERN to a detector under Gran Sasso, roughly 730 kilometers away, arrived about 60 nanoseconds early — a six-sigma result that, taken at face value, put them faster than light by about 25 parts per million. The collaboration pointedly did not claim a discovery: they presented the anomaly and asked the wider community to scrutinize it and, ideally, repeat the measurement. Months later the whole thing collapsed into equipment failure — a loose fiber-optic link to the master clock had shaved over 70 nanoseconds off the reported flight time, while an off-spec clock oscillator had pushed modestly the other way. I'd like this episode read through the epistemology of higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I want the mapping done carefully, because I suspect it's less tidy than it first appears.

Here's the mapping as I see it, along with my doubts. The collaboration's first-order evidence — the timing data, the GPS synchronization, the error budget — apparently supported the proposition that these neutrinos outran light. Their higher-order evidence apparently opposed it: special relativity is about as well confirmed as anything we know, and the neutrinos detected from supernova 1987A, after traveling some 160,000 light-years, arrived within hours of the light — yet 25 parts per million over that distance works out to years of lead time, so had the OPERA effect been real, those neutrinos should have shown up years before the supernova was visible, and nothing of the sort was observed. The physicists seem to have occupied the mirror image of the textbook akratic: taking their own measurement to support p while declining to believe p. But I can't settle several things. First, classification: is the supernova comparison really higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of their procedure — or just further first-order evidence about neutrino speeds, in which case the case may have nothing to do with defeat and everything to do with ordinary weighing of evidence? And what about the confidence in relativity itself, which rests on measurements made by instruments of the very kind they were trusting? Second, a self-undermining worry: if the OPERA team should discount their result because their instruments and analysis might be faulty, shouldn't the same hesitation apply to the far-flung measurements that built the confidence in relativity in the first place, and if not, why not? Third, the vindication: when the loose cable turned up, did that show suspending judgment was the rational policy all along — or would the identical policy have been followed had the cable been tight and the neutrinos genuinely fast, in which case what exactly was vindicated?

A colleague of mine insists there was never any deep puzzle: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and the team was just doing ordinary Bayesian bookkeeping. I'm not sure the bookkeeping metaphor earns its keep, since it doesn't tell me whether the team's pre-cable state counts as akratic in the technical sense, or as something else — perhaps believing that their evidence supported p while withholding belief that p — and whether that distinction does any real work here or quietly collapses. So: fix the mapping first (say exactly what p was, what the first-order evidence was, and which pieces of the relativity and supernova considerations belong where and why), then tell me whether the collaboration's doxastic state was akratic, whether it was rational, and what the episode teaches the theory — whether it confirms the standard thought that higher-order evidence defeats first-order support, or exposes a case the standard treatments of akrasia and defeat mishandle.
