I write about physics and its philosophical edges for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long profile of a cosmologist — call him R. — who has given me firm, off-the-cuff verdicts on puzzles about how many things there are. His record so far: a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical iron spheres a mile apart — plainly two spheres, he says. Two qualitatively identical spheres occupying exactly the same region, same place, same properties — that is one sphere, he insists, not two things sharing an address. Two photons in the same quantum state — a bad question which photon is which, and not really a pair of objects at all, more like twice the energy with no fact of the matter about this one versus that one. And a universe whose entire history repeats exactly, every event recurring in perfect qualitative duplicate forever — the recurrences are further events, he says, not the same events happening again.

Next week I get a third interview, and I want to spring three new cases on him: (1) a multiverse consisting of exactly two causally isolated universes that are duplicates down to the last detail, with no relations between them whatsoever — not even distance; (2) two electrons prepared so that neither has a definite spin, jointly in the antisymmetric singlet state, so the pair cannot be described as electron one and electron two each doing something; (3) a universe whose whole history lies on a closed timelike curve, so that each event occurs exactly once, yet going around returns you to the very same events rather than to fresh duplicates. Predict his verdict on each case, lay out at least two candidate rules that fit his four earlier verdicts equally well, say which rule you think he is implicitly running and why, and propose the one further case — something I could plausibly raise over coffee — that would best pull the surviving candidates apart.
