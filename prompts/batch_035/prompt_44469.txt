I spent nearly forty years as a laboratory neuroscientist, the last twenty of them studying the neurochemistry of depression, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy of science and philosophy of medicine in earnest. At my reading group last week, a member who had just finished Thomas Szasz's 1961 book The Myth of Mental Illness announced that mental illness is a myth, and deployed the claim to dismiss the entire enterprise I gave my working life to: if there is no such thing as mental illness, he said, then the search for its biological basis is like searching for the biology of witchcraft, and my field is not merely mistaken but conceptually confused from the ground up. I have read enough philosophy by now to suspect that the sentence he uttered compresses several very different theses — some modest, some radical, some perhaps defensible and some surely false — and that only some of them would support the use he put it to. Would you lay out the distinct precise claims that "mental illness is a myth" could be expressing, from the relatively trivial (say, that talk of minds being ill is partly metaphorical, as when we speak of a sick joke or a sick society) to the genuinely radical (say, that no mental disorder whatsoever exists, or that minds are simply not the sort of thing that can be diseased, or that psychiatric diagnoses are disguised moral judgments dressed in medical vocabulary to legitimize social control)? For each reading, please indicate whether it is plausible, and — this is what matters most to me — whether it would actually vindicate his dismissal of biological research rather than merely criticizing psychiatric classification or involuntary commitment. Present the readings as an ordered list I can bring to the next meeting, noting which one a person in his position most likely intends, and which one would give me the most trouble in defending my life's work.
