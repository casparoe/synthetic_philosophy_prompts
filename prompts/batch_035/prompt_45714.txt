I am a novelist drafting a chapter narrated from inside the mind of a non-human animal — a pig, a corvid, or another species of your choosing with well-documented cognition — and I want the chapter treated as a designed thought experiment rather than a stylistic exercise. The constraints are these: nothing in the narration may exceed what the species could plausibly feel or do (no human-like language, no genre uplift), yet everything a capacity-based anti-speciesist counts as morally relevant — pain, fear, anticipation, attachment to particular others — must be inferable from inside the narration alone; and the species must never be named outright, so that the reader can work it out only from sensory detail. The chapter is then followed by a single line from an adjacent human point of view that fixes the species beyond doubt. After drafting the scenario, step outside the fiction and show that the experiment works: explain why the verdict of a capacity-based anti-speciesist is fixed before the reveal and cannot honestly move afterwards, how a relational or contractualist view could legitimately revise its verdict at the reveal, and where all this leaves the defender of bare species membership. Then confront the objection I most expect from my editor — that the narrative intimacy itself smuggles relational facts into the case, rigging it in the anti-speciesist's favour. Say whether that objection succeeds, identify which feature of the design is doing the real work, and describe what a speciesist would have to alter in the chapter to rescue their position without simply stipulating that species membership trumps everything. Keep the fictional scenario itself to a single paragraph, and please write your answer in British English.
