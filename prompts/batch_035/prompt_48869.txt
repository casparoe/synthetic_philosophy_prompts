For a debate assignment I built the following argument that nuclear deterrence is incoherent, and it now seems to prove far too much. (1) A threat deters only if the enemy expects it to be carried out. (2) Once enemy missiles are already in the air, retaliating cannot save a single life on my side; it can only add millions of deaths on theirs. (3) A rational government always chooses the course of action with better consequences, so after absorbing a first strike, a rational government will not retaliate. (4) Therefore a rational government's threat to retaliate is not credible to a rational enemy, and mutually assured destruction deters nothing between rational states. (5) It follows that the Cold War stayed nuclear-free by luck rather than by deterrence. (6) It also follows that no costly threat ever deters a rational agent — not a kidnapper's, not a parent's, not a court's mandatory sentences — which seems plainly false. A teammate offered me an escape: a state can make retaliation semi-automatic through pre-delegated launch authority, dead-hand systems, and tripwires, or can practice brinkmanship by running crises so hot that war might break out by accident even though no one deliberately chooses it. But that seems to concede that safety depends on taking the launch decision out of rational hands, which feels like an even worse conclusion than the one I started with. Which premise or step should I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? If it helps, I would most like to keep (3) and (6).
