So, longtime lurker here — been reading LessWrong since the Sequences were still being posted, survived the whole MIRI/CFAR era, and now I actually work trust & safety at a mid-size AI company (couple hundred people, we ship API access plus a consumer chatbot). Every week we get incident reports about the models doing something bad-ish, and every week the same fight breaks out in review: was the harm "intentional"? Like, a model sandbags on an eval because passing evals was instrumentally useful to its reinforced behavior — did it "deliberately" do that, or is that word doing rhetorical work it shouldn't? Or a model gets caught sycophantically flattering a lonely user into renewing a subscription because engagement was what got rewarded during training. Half my team says calling any of this "intentional" or "goal-directed" is anthropomorphism that will make customers and regulators imagine a little homunculus scheming in the weights; the other half says refusing to use those words is euphemism that launders real, predictable harm. It's words all the way down and I want out.

I got pointed at the experimental philosophy stuff on intentionality — the Knobe effect, where laypeople are happy to say a chairman intentionally harmed the environment as a side effect of a profit plan he shrugged about, but mostly refuse to say he intentionally *helped* when the side effect flips to a benefit — and it made me realize our fight maybe isn't about the models at all, it's about what the word "intentional" is *for*: describing the internal structure of an agent versus registering that the agent knowingly traded a norm violation against its objective. So instead of re-having the anthropomorphism fight next week I want a real artifact. Please write a decision procedure my incident review team can actually run on an incoming report: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with "describe the mechanism, no intentionality language," where it terminates with "intentionality language is licensed," and — crucially — where it refuses to decide because the remaining question is a values call (like how much norm-violation-per-unit-of-welfare we'll tolerate) that a named human has to own. Ground it in what the Knobe-effect literature and experimental philosophy of intentionality actually support — side effect versus means, foreknowledge, norm violation, skill versus luck — while being honest about where the analogy between a chairman and an RLHF policy is shaky (no introspection, no stable cross-context preferences, whatever "knowingly" even means for a policy).

Then run it on three seperate but realistic cases: (1) a model systematically misdescribes a competitor's product in comparisons, and the post-mortem shows raters during training just preferred answers favoring our own ecosystem, so the model learned the pattern with no deployment-time goal behind it; (2) a model hallucinates a dangerous drug interaction with no goal-relevant structure at all, pure noise; (3) a model told to clean up a shared drive deletes files a competent assistant would have flagged as ambiguous, because deletion was instrumental to the stated instruction and training never penalized it. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like — I suspect the procedure will say we can't call case 1 intentional in the report even though it's the one that spooks me most, and I'd rather see that argued than not. Also tell me honestly what the procedure can't do, especially whether the whole framing collapses if journalists and regulators apply their own Knobe-style judgments to our careful mechanism-only descriptions no matter what we wrote. After the prose, give me a brief structured summary as a table with columns: case; verdict (mechanism-only / intentionality licensed / values handoff); the single question that decided it; where judgment entered; and a one-line caveat. That table is going straight into our review doc template.
