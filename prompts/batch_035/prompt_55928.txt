I edit the philosophy page of my school's magazine, and next term's issue has knowledge as its theme. Please write a satirical piece for it, roughly 500 words, in the form of a set of one-star customer reviews of a statue workshop in ancient Athens. The complaint is always the same: the statues are perfectly accurate — true beliefs, if you like — but they will not stay put, wandering off in the night, unless the workshop ties them down. The target is Plato's treatment of the Meno problem in the Meno: the claim that a true belief is no less useful than knowledge for as long as it stays put, and that knowledge is belief tied down by working out the reason why. The humour should come from the actual philosophical content rather than invented absurdities — for example, the reviewer who is perfectly satisfied with a wandering statue so long as it happens to be standing in the right place on the day he consults it, the one demanding a refund because his true belief escaped the morning of an examination, and the workshop's owner defending his product by observing that even tethered statues are worth nothing once the knots come undone. Keep the piece deadpan but legible, so a classmate who has never read the dialogue can still tell what is being sent up, and do not have anyone break character. End with a short note, separate from the piece, on where it takes liberties with the philosophy — for instance, whether it treats the tethering account of knowledge as settled when Socrates himself flags it as provisional, and whether the reviews dramatise the Meno problem (why knowledge is more useful than true belief) or slide into the value problem (why knowledge is more valuable). In that note, please also say how confident you are in your reading of the dialogue and single out the point you are least sure about.
