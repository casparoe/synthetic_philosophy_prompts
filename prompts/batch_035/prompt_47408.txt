For a while now I've been thinking about the philosophical debate over testimony: on one side, the reductionist view (going back to Hume and Locke) that believing what someone tells you requires your own independent evidence of their reliability; on the other, the anti-reductionist view (associated with Reid and, more recently, Coady) that trust in testimony is our default entitlement, and only special reasons for doubt need to be shown. I have no philosophical training, so keep the theory side accessible, but you don't need to explain the Iraq war from scratch. The facts as I understand them, in brief: after the UN inspectors left Iraq in 1998, Western intelligence had almost no independent access to the country, so the case that Saddam still held banned weapons came to rest heavily on testimony — above all from Iraqi defectors, many of them passed along by exile opposition groups who wanted the invasion to happen. One defector, handled by a foreign intelligence service, described mobile biological-weapons laboratories; his story reportedly reached Washington as reports of reports, and it ended up, presented as established fact, in Secretary Powell's UN presentation of February 2003. Some people inside the intelligence world doubted these sources even beforehand, and after the war the weapons turned out not to exist.

Analyze this episode through both theories, running the analysis in both directions. First map the setup carefully: who counts as the testifier here, who the hearer, where the chains of retelling enter, and what the reductionist's required positive reason for trust would have to consist of in this case — and whether it was actually available at the time, or only afterwards. Then say what the episode shows about the theory itself: does the disaster confirm reductionism (a hearer with no independent evidence should simply have withheld belief), or does it instead suggest that both positions miss the point, since they model a lone individual weighing a single speaker, while in reality testimony is gathered, filtered, and summarized by institutions before anyone with power ever hears it? Consider also the two features I find hardest to place: the hearers appear to have wanted the testimony to be true, and the deadline pressure left no time to check anything. If either theory is simply silent on some aspect of the case, say so plainly rather than stretching it to speak.
