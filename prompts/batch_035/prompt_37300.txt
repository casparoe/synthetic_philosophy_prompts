I am a novelist, and for a decade two friends of mine have argued across my kitchen table whenever a new review of one of my books appears. The first, a poet, is a thoroughgoing pessimist about aesthetic testimony: she maintains that no one can come to know that a novel is good, moving, or subtle on the say-so of a critic or a friend, because an aesthetic judgement must be grounded in one's own experiential acquaintance with the work. At best, she concedes, a review transmits the fact that someone else judged favourably — a verdict about the reviewer, not the book. The second, a long-serving literary editor, is an optimist: when a critic whose judgement has tracked his own for twenty years calls a book a masterpiece, he says he knows it is one, and he insists that the poet's scruples would render critical expertise, prize juries, and the whole institution of reviewing unintelligible — worse, they would make it impossible ever to plan one's reading, since one would have to finish every book before learning anything of its merits.

They have finally agreed to sit down with a neutral chairperson, and they have asked me to suggest what such a document should contain. I would like you to draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration ought to produce. Please include: the claims about testimony, aesthetic judgement, and critical practice that both could sign, however deep their disagreement; the exact points of divergence, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; and the cruxes beneath the divergence — I suspect one is whether the poet's complaint is about knowledge specifically or about any kind of justified belief, and another concerns whether acquaintance with a work is a state testimony could in principle supply, as it plausibly can for ordinary perceptual facts. For each crux, give a concrete case both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I would suggest testing them against at least these two cases of my own devising: the novelist whose works survive a catastrophe only as a summary in an encyclopaedia, so that no living person has read a word of them and yet posterity's verdict persists purely through testimony; and the editor who knows a foreign masterpiece only in a mediocre translation, so that even his "own" experience is second-hand in a way neither party has sorted from the testimony problem. Please also say, if you conclude that part of their quarrel is merely verbal — for instance, a dispute over the word "know" rather than over anything substantive — which part that is and why. Format the result as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a table of disagreements, followed by the cruxes with their associated predictions.
