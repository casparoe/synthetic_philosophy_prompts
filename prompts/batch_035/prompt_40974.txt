The working title is "Constitution as Intention-Inclusive." The idea, in rough form:

Everyone agrees the standard moves on the statue and the clay are all unhappy. If the statue just is the clay, then it can't be true both that the statue could survive being squashed into a ball (well, actually it can't — but it could survive being transported, or having an arm replaced) and that the clay could survive being squashed. If the statue and clay are distinct but coincident objects, we get the "too many minds"-style problem: why doesn't every arbitrary region of the clay get its own statue-shaped companion? Eliminativism about statues seems desperate, and four-dimensionalism helps only if you buy temporal parts, which I don't want to.

My proposal: we've been setting up the relata wrong. The statue is not constituted by the clay alone. It's constituted by the clay together with a certain intentional history — roughly, the sculptor's design-and-execution process. Call these two things together the "intentional-organic whole." The constitution relation holds between the statue and that whole, not between the statue and the bare clay. So there is no mysterious coincidence between statue and clay, because the statue and the clay were never the two relata to begin with. The clay is just a proper constituent of the thing that does the constituting.

Nice payoff #1: the modal asymmetry. Squashing the statue destroys the intentional-historical constituent but leaves the clay fine, so the statue goes out of existence while the clay persists. No coincidence, no mystery.

Nice payoff #2: indiscernibility. The statue and clay never shared all their (intrinsic or otherwise) properties, since the statue has historical/intentional properties the clay lacks, being partially constituted by them. So the classic "how can they differ in their modal properties if they're intrinsically the same?" argument never gets started.

The obvious worry, which I plan to address in the last section: does this generalize to persons and bodies? I want to say it doesn't — persons aren't constituted by bodies plus intentions, the way statues are constituted by clay plus intentions — but I admit I don't have a principled reason for why not. Maybe the intentionality involved in personhood isn't external in the right way?
