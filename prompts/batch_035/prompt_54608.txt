Been chewing on the pain asymbolia stuff for a while now (mostly via the secondhand blog riffs on it, plus the occasional actual paper) and I want your honest credence, decomposed, because mine keeps flip-floping and I suspect the tree is doing more work than the number. The question, stated so it actually has a truth value: for any given pain, its badness-for-the-subject is entirely constituted by the aversive side of it — the wanting-it-to-stop, the distress, the hurtness as opposed to the felt intensity — so that a pain which kept its full sensory character but had the aversiveness stripped out would be no worse for you than some mildly odd tactile sensation. Pain asymbolics are the stress test: people who, as the story usually goes, after damage somewhere around the insula or cingulate cortex, still report feeling pain at normal intensity, can localise it, will even call it painful when asked to rate it, but claim they don't mind it, sometimes smile while the examiner keeps the pin in. If their introspection is accurate, that's an existance proof that sensation and aversiveness come apart, which is trouble for anyone who thinks pain is non-derivatively bad just qua sensation.

So give me your number for the main claim, but decomposed honestly: your probability that the asymbolia reports are accurate in the relevant respect (genuine pain phenomenology, genuinely absent distress, and not just flattened affect or a culture of stoic reporting); conditional on that, your probability that the right theory of wellbeing makes badness track aversiveness rather than the raw feel — think desire-based views where pain is bad only via its tie to a standing aversion, versus hedonists who have to say the sensory character itself carries negative valence; your probability that the whole dispute is partly verbal and that the asymbolics' state just isn't pain anymore once the aversiveness is gone, so it's no counterexample; and anything else you think belongs in the tree, like whether morphine patients saying the pain is still there but it doesn't bother them count as an independent data point or the same phenomenon rebranded. Then combine the terms however you think they actually combine (tell me if you think they don't factor), say which term the final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what finding would move that term most — I'd especially want to hear what you'd predict if someone ran the obvious experiment, dialling aversiveness down in healthy volunteers and having them choose between repeat sessions of high-intensity low-aversiveness pain versus mild ordinary pain.

One more thing that keeps snagging me: the moral stakes. If aversiveness-only is right, then the badness of pain in beings that can't report anything is hostage to whether they've got the aversive circuitry, not just the nociceptive circuitry, which changes how I should think about crustaceans and, awkwardly, about certain kinds of AI systems. Should that consideration feed back into your credence or stay quarantined as a downstream consequence? I genuinely don't know the right answer to that and I want yours.

Also: if you think I've smuggled a false dichotomy into the main claim — maybe aversiveness isn't one thing, or maybe intensity and valence only come apart in corner cases — say so and restate the proposition properly before assigning the number. I'd rather answer the right question than defend my framing. But I will be annoyed by a bare 'about 0.7' with no derivation; the decomposition IS the deliverable.
