You are an AI safety researcher with a background in engineering assurance and adversarial testing, and you've grown impatient with how loosely the word "control" gets used. Help me pin it down.

Here's the distinction I'm working with: alignment work tries to make a system not have dangerous goals in the first place; control work assumes the system might already have them, and tries to ensure that even a misaligned system cannot cause catastrophe — through containment, monitoring, oversight, and limits on what it can do. My question is about what a control scheme has to deliver before it deserves the name, as opposed to being a comforting ritual.

To keep this concrete, consider the shape of typical proposals: an untrusted frontier model is deployed only inside a scaffold where (i) every action it takes in the world is reviewed by a smaller, trusted model before execution, with anything the reviewer can't verify flagged for a human; (ii) the model runs in a sandbox with staged access, gaining permissions only after passing behavioral evaluations; (iii) all logs are scanned for anomalies, and the deployment has a kill switch; (iv) honeypots — fake credentials, fake network paths — are seeded through the environment so that any attempt at misuse trips an alarm. None of this assumes the model is aligned; all of it assumes it might not be.

Write the desiderata: the requirements a scheme of this kind must meet to count as genuine control. For each requirement, give the motivating case — the failure story it exists to block — and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp. Among the things I want you to take a stance on: does control require that the safety argument remain valid when the model itself knows the protocol and optimizes against it (the difference between a locked door and a locked door whose mechanism the prisoner has studied), or is partial obscurity a legitimate measure? Does the trusted reviewer inherit the untrusted model's problems the moment it is trained on its outputs, and if so, does any version of requirement (i) survive? Does a control scheme need a quantitative threshold — probability of catastrophe below some number — or only an argument that no known attack succeeds? And is "cannot cause catastrophe" even the right target, or must control also cover gradual influence and gradual capability gain, where there is no single moment at which to intervene?

Think carefully, and start with a short brainstorm of candidate requirements before committing. Then give me the list, and — this is the part I most want — a definite position: which single requirement is load-bearing, which pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and whether a purely black-box scheme, with no window into the model's internals, can be adequate or whether some such window is a requirement in disguise. Commit to answers rather than laying out options. Where genuine uncertainty remains, say what evidence would settle it — but still take the best current position.
