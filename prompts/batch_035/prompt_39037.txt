I'm a charge nurse on a medical-surgical unit, and I sit on our regional health network's continuing-competence committee, which recertifies nurses every two years. Part of that recertification has always been a closed-book medication-dosage examination: weight-based calculations, drip rates, pediatric conversions, taken with nothing but a pencil and scratch paper. The question now before the committee is whether that format survives, because over the last three years our network has embedded an AI dose-checking assistant directly into the medication workflow — every nurse verifies doses through it on the ward, and many of our younger nurses have never hand-calculated a drip rate in actual practice. The committee has narrowed the options to three: (a) keep the unaided exam exactly as it is, on the grounds that it is our safety floor for the day the system is down, wrong, or mid-transfer between vendors; (b) let candidates sit the exam with the assistant available, testing the nurse-plus-tool unit we actually rely on at the bedside; or (c) some hybrid, for instance an exam in two parts where an emergency-scenario portion must be completed unaided.

I have no philosophy background — my training is entirely clinical — but I've been chewing on this in my own time, and I came across an idea that seems to be about precisely our situation. Two philosophers, Andy Clark and David Chalmers, published a paper in 1998 called "The Extended Mind" arguing that when an external resource is reliably at hand, automatically trusted, and used the way internal memory is used, it starts to count as part of the person's mind rather than a mere tool. Their example is a man named Otto, who has memory problems and writes everything he needs to remember in a notebook that goes everywhere with him; they argue the notebook plays the same role for Otto that biological memory plays for Inga, who just remembers things. If that's right, it seems to bear directly on our question: if the dose-checking assistant is genuinely part of how a nurse knows the dose, then testing her with the tool confiscated tests a configuration she never works in — a bit like demanding Inga recite her childhood from Otto's notebook. But I can also hear the strongest reply on the other side, and I want it taken seriously rather than waved off: Otto's notebook doesn't go down for maintenance, receive a bad software update, or silently disagree with him the way a network tool can, and a nurse who has never hand-calculated may be helpless precisely in the emergency that the unaided exam exists to guarantee against.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate ahead of next month's meeting: it should recommend one of the three options, or a concrete variant of your own if you think the committee's list is badly framed; give the reasoning in terms the committee can act on; and honestly engage the best argument for whichever option it rejects. It should also state, in plain terms, what our recommendation commits us to more generally — whether we are treating competence as something that lives in the nurse alone or in the nurse-plus-system, and what would have to be true about the tool (its reliability, its uptime, who owns and updates it) for that treatment to be justified. The committee is eleven people with mixed views, so the memo should be one a skeptical colleague could read without feeling steamrolled.
