You are to act as a professional philosopher with joint expertise in Wittgenstein's later philosophy and the philosophy of science — the sort of person who has taught the Investigations to graduate students and also served on biology curriculum committees. Please think carefully and systematically before answering: begin by laying out the commitments below, work out what each one commits me to, and only then assess the set as a whole.

Some background on me: I am a recently retired evolutionary systematist. I spent forty years describing, delimiting, and arguing about species, and I have come to philosophy late, mostly through reading Wittgenstein and the literature around him in retirement. I want an audit of my current position, not a survey of the field. Here is the position, stated as plainly as I can manage:

1. I am persuaded by Wittgenstein's remarks on "game": many ordinary concepts have no essence, no set of necessary and sufficient conditions uniting their instances, and are instead held together by overlapping similarities — and this is not a defect needing repair but simply how such concepts succeed at what they do.
2. I am also persuaded by the rule-following considerations, on the reading where no definition, formula, or mental state fixes the full range of a rule's or a term's correct applications in advance; whatever settles borderline and future cases is ultimately the practice of the community of speakers, not the rule itself. I take this to apply with full force to mathematics, indeed to mathematics above all.
3. Nevertheless, I believe there is a principled difference between vernacular and scientific vocabulary: a scientific concept earns its standing by approaching explicit definition — operational criteria, necessary and sufficient conditions — and where a scientific concept remains family-resemblance-like, that is a symptom of immaturity, a scaffolding that mature science should eventually replace.
4. From my own field: I believe "species" is family-resemblance-like through and through — no criterion unites everything we call a species, and the dozens of species concepts are not converging on one. Yet species was indispensable to my research and to all of systematics, and I cannot honestly say our work would have gone better had we insisted on a single sharpened definition. I have come to doubt that any such sharpening is coming.
5. I believe water is H2O, that this is the paradigm of science replacing a vague vernacular concept with an essence-bearing one, and that the same trajectory — from family resemblance to essence — is what maturity in a science looks like. I confess I expected biology to follow.
6. Finally, I believe that at least mathematical and metrological concepts — the number series, the meter, molar mass as I used it in the lab — really do have perfectly sharp boundaries fixed by their definitions, and that whatever is true of "game" is not true of them.

Please treat this as a consistency audit of my own position, not as a request for a lecture on Wittgenstein. Specifically: identify which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ranking the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what giving it up would cost. And I want a committed verdict, not a menu: in the end, name the single commitment I should abandon, or tell me precisely how the package can be held together if you think it can — take a definite position and defend it, including on the question I care most about, namely whether my proposed line between vernacular and scientific vocabulary can survive the rule-following considerations at all. Do not pad the answer with hedges or with a rundown of every position anyone has held; argue for a view.
