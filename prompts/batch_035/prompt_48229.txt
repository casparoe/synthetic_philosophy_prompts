Science journalist here — I cover AI and ideas for a general interest magazine, mostly translating research for readers. Something I keep bumping into at the labs and can't get a straight answer on, and I think it's actually a philosophy story. The safety people will tell me two things in the same breath: the model we're about to deploy is very likely fine, and here's our plan in case it's secretly not — automated monitors reading everything it writes, restrictions on what tools it can touch, and (the part that got my attention) making sure the model can't tell which parts of its output are being watched or by whom, becuase if it knew, it might behave differently while it's being evaluated. They even run drills where they tell a copy of the model to try to sabotage some task, just to check the monitors would catch it, and then deploy the thing claiming it's safe. So what I want for my piece: what does this whole posture actually presuppose about the thing being deployed? Like, does the hide-the-monitoring-from-it step only make sense if the system is assumed to have something like goals and a model of its own situation — you don't conceal surveillance from a thermostat? Does the rest of it presuppose that alignment can't just be inspected once and settled? And the part my editor keeps asking: does running a practice like this commit the labs to any view about whether these systems have interests, or is it like casino surveillance of dealers, where nobody doubts the dealer is a person with a life? You're a philosopher of mind and technology, think carefully and brainstorm the candidate commitments before deciding anything — then tell me which ones the practice strictly needs, which it merely fits well with, and whether the different parts of the practice are pulling against each other philosophically. Also, since I don't want to publish something dumb: flag where your reconstruction is most likely to go wrong and what consideration would push you to a different reading. No citations, just reasoning.
