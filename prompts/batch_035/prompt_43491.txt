I'm a video-streaming engineer. My working vocabulary comes from building low-latency live pipelines, and I'm fluent in it: decode order versus presentation order (with B-frames, a frame can be decoded after a frame it is displayed before), jitter buffers, playout deadlines, PTS timestamps, frame reordering, immutable event logs, post-hoc reconciliation, and the difference between what a client played out and what the session log later says it played out. I have no philosophy background; I half-read something by Daniel Dennett years ago and remember almost none of it.

Here is the debate I want translated, with enough setup that you shouldn't need the source. In the "color phi" phenomenon, a subject is shown two brief flashes in succession at nearby positions on a screen: a red spot, and then, a couple hundred milliseconds later, a green spot at the second position. Subjects don't report two flashes. They report one moving spot that changed color midway along its path — and, probed further, they say the change happened at a particular point in the trajectory, even though at that point the brain could not yet have known the second color was coming. Something has to give, and the two candidate stories are these. On the Stalinesque reading, the feed is doctored in the projection room before it reaches consciousness: the content is held back and edited, so the subject never had the unedited experience and nothing is misremembered — like a show trial where the script is fixed before the performance. On the Orwellian reading, the subject really did have the original experience, and the memory of it is subsequently rewritten in the manner of a ministry of truth: the record is altered, the subject later sincerely reports the edited version, and the divergence between what was experienced and what is reported is real but lies entirely in the past. The question the debate poses is which story is true — that is, at what moment did the subject really experience the color change, before it or after it. Dennett's own resolution, as I understand it, is that this is a badly posed question: there is no place or time at which content arrives in consciousness, no point where it all comes together, so there can be no fact of the matter about whether the edit happened before arrival or after; both the Stalinesque and the Orwellian storyteller presuppose such an arrival point, and it does not exist.

Restate this entire debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Then give me a residue report in three parts, and be honest about the seams:

1. What maps cleanly. Which parts of the debate correspond to things my pipelines actually do? My guess is that the Stalinesque story becomes something like a transform applied to frames held in the jitter buffer before they hit the playout clock — nothing is ever displayed that hasn't been edited — and the Orwellian story becomes something like frames played out as captured, with the session log rewritten afterward so that a later query returns the edited record. But check whether that's the best mapping or whether I've got the correspondences wrong, and say what each mapping does with the subject's later report: is the report a query served from the log, or a readback from the display, and does the debate care about the difference?

2. What had to be distorted to fit. Where does the translation quietly change the content? For instance, in my world the playout clock is a real, externally observable thing and the display is a real final stage, so "when did the viewer see it" always has an answer; if Dennett is right that the mind has no such final stage, then every term I would reach for to express the original question — pre-roll, on-air, arrival at the render — already presupposes the display he says doesn't exist. Identify each place where you had to fudge, and whether the fudge was forced by my vocabulary or by the original debate's unclarity.

3. What has no counterpart at all. This is what I care about most. My suspicion is this: the residue is not a gap in my vocabulary that a richer framework could fill; it is that my vocabulary cannot even pose the original question, because the question itself presupposes a definite presentation surface. If that's right, then the Orwell-versus-Stalin dispute was less substantive than it looked — two stories that agree on every observable and differ only about a fact of the matter that was never there. If that's wrong, tell me what my vocabulary is missing, and whether it's missing it for a reason my engineering practice can articulate.

Close by being specific about which parts of your translation were doing real work and which were smuggling in, under the name of translation, the assumption that consciousness has a render stage.
