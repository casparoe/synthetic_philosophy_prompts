I teach intro political philosophy, and every semester when we hit the Mill unit somebody whips out the old line that your right to swing your fist ends where the other man's nose begins. Last week a student used it to shut down a whole discussion about whether doxxing someone's home address, or selling people's location data to whoever wants it, could be regulated. His point was basically: no nose, no contact, no business of the state. Everyone nodded along and we lost twenty minutes of seminar to it, which, fair enough, partly my fault for not having a crisp comeback.

Here's what bugged me though. The slogan feels obviously right, but I genuinely don't know what claim it's making anymore. At the weakest reading it's just true by definition — of course unrestricted freedom isn't a thing, every freedom has a limit somewhere. At the strongest reading it's carving up the whole world into "physical contact with my body" versus everything else, which would mean fraud, defamation, threats, blackmail, and dumping chemicals upstream are all fair game as long as my nose stays unswung. And somewhere in between there's presumably something that's actually close to Mill's own principle, where "my nose" is doing metaphorical work for interests or rights. The three readings give you wildly different answers about my student's doxxing example, and I suspect he was sliding between them without noticing.

So: act as an expert on Mill's On Liberty and philosophy of law, and think carefully before you answer — do a little brainstorming on paper first, then give me the polished version. I want you to lay out the distinct precise claims that fist/nose line could be expressing, ordered from most defensible to least, and for each one tell me (a) whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or just false, and (b) whether it would actually get my student his conclusion about doxxing and data-selling, since neither of those involves anything remotely nose-shaped. Also flag where Mill himself would and wouldn't endorse the reading — I know his actual position is more complicated than the bumper sticker, e.g. he had things to say about harm that comes via markets and public opinion rather than fists, but I want that woven into the readings rather than bolted on at the end. Format it as a list I can put on a slide, since I plan to open the next seminar with it. Please don't pad it with the standard boilerplate biography of Mill — my students can read wikipedia, what I need is the disambiguation.
