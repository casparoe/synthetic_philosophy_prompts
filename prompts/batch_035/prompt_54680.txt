I basically accept Benacerraf's dilemma as a real problem and have for a while: either your semantics for math is uniform with everything else (so math truths are about abstract, causally inert objects) and then it's a mystery how we know any of them, or you give a story that makes mathematical knowledge explicable but then math truths stop looking like truths in the normal sense. My grad students keep needling me about it tho and I've realized I don't have a clear sense of how exposed the position actually is. So: what would count against it? Not just the standard counter-moves recited back to me — I mean, Field grabbed one horn and just bit the bullet on mathematical truth, deflationists about truth think the other horn is a paper tiger, and people say the causal theory of knowledge the dilemma leaned on is dead anyway. For each of those (and anything else), I want to know whether a serious defender of the dilemma would concede it as a genuine defeat or insist it misses the point, and why. Two specific worries of mine: first, does the dilemma survive being rebuilt on reliability instead of causation, or does the rebuild just smuggle causal intuitions back in under another name? Second, if the problem generalizes to knowledge of modality, morality, and maybe even deep history, is that a strength or does it trivialize the special thing about math? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest with me if the position has just gotten so well-insulated by now that nothing short of a revolution in epistemology could touch it — and whether that insulation is a vice for a view of this kind.
