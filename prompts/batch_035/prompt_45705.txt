I have been testing my own judgments about whether "better than" is transitive, and my verdicts across cases do not line up neatly; I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them, what it predicts for cases I have not considered, and which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Take cups of coffee sweetened with sugar. Between a cup with 1,000 grains and one with 1,001, I have no preference; neither is better. But between the 1,000-grain cup and one with 1,500 grains, I strictly prefer the latter. The same holds for bathwater one degree apart (no preference) versus fifteen degrees apart (strict preference), and for living eighty years versus eighty years plus an hour (no preference) versus eighty versus eighty-five (strict preference). In every case like this, my adjacent verdicts say "neither better nor worse" while my endpoint verdicts say "strictly better."

Yet with money I judge differently: between $100.00 and $100.01 I do have a strict preference for the larger sum, even though I would never cross a room to get it. So one awkward pair is that I call adjacent coffee cups equal while calling adjacent dollar amounts strictly ordered, and I cannot say what the difference is — the penny is no more perceptible, nor more consequential to me, than the grain.

A second awkward pair: I say of the adjacent coffee cups that neither is better than the other, but I balk at saying they are exactly equally good — that would demand a precision in the comparison of distinct things that I do not think I possess. I am drawn to saying they are in some third relation, but I cannot state it, and I do not know whether it is transitive.

A third: in choices among vacation packages, where Rome beats Paris on sights, Paris beats Rome on food, and I rank Rome over Paris overall both times I am asked to compare them directly, I nonetheless find that when all three pairwise comparisons are laid out I sometimes endorse a strict cycle, and I cannot tell whether that is a failure of my introspection or a genuine structure in my preferences.

So: is there one account — perhaps transitive strict betterness with a nontransitive tie-or-parity relation, perhaps something else — that delivers most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which clause each of my cases motivates, and be explicit about which verdicts it overturns rather than accommodates. Then tell me what the account says about two cases I have not listed: first, whether I am exposed to being money-pumped along the coffee or bathwater series, given that each adjacent swap is one I say I do not care about; and second, two houses for sale, one better on commute and worse on kitchen than the other, where I judge neither better overall — what should the account say, and what does my gut feeling that I would regret either choice equally imply about the relation between them?

Please keep your response under 700 words, leading with the principle itself before turning to the cases.
