**Exam question:** "G. E. Moore noticed that sentences like 'It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining' sound paradoxical even though they appear perfectly consistent: it can be true that it is raining and true that the speaker does not believe this. (a) Explain why such sentences are nevertheless absurd to assert, and whether the problem is logical or pragmatic. (b) Distinguish the form above from the commissive form, 'It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining,' and say whether the two forms raise the same issue. (c) What, if anything, does the paradox suggest about the relationship between assertion, belief, and truth?"

**Student's answer:** "Moore's sentence is not a contradiction in the logical sense, because 'it is raining' and 'I do not believe that it is raining' can both be true at once; there is no possible world in the strict sense that rules this out, since a person can be ignorant or mistaken about the weather. The paradox arises because anyone who asserted the sentence would be asserting something while simultaneously revealing that they do not believe what they assert, and this violates what assertion is for: to assert is to present oneself as believing. So the problem is pragmatic rather than logical. Since Moore himself showed that the sentence is self-contradictory, the only way to save his own view is to treat 'I believe that p' as adding something like 'and it is true that p' to the assertion, which explains the absurdity. The commissive form is just the omissive form in disguise, because not believing that it is raining amounts to believing that it is not raining, so both versions raise exactly the same issue. Note also that the absurdity is tied to the present tense: a speaker who said 'It was raining yesterday, but I did not believe it at the time' would be just as Moore-paradoxical, since the same clash between assertion and avowed disbelief is present. Finally, the moral of the paradox is that first-person present-tense belief ascriptions are truth-preserving: if I sincerely say 'I believe that p,' then p must be true, which shows that belief logically entails truth in the first-person case."
