Exam question:

In his early work on folk judgments about intentional action, Joshua Knobe found a striking asymmetry between how people judge harmful side effects and how they judge helpful ones. (1) Describe the classic chairman/environment vignette and the asymmetry it reveals, being careful about the direction of the effect. (2) Explain why this result was surprising relative to the then-dominant assumptions about the folk concept of intentional action. (3) Discuss what Knobe himself took the result to show about that concept. (4) Describe one objection to, or complication of, the original finding, and evaluate it.

Student's answer:

The famous study here is Knobe's chairman case. A company chairman is told that a new program will increase profits but will also harm the environment. He says he doesn't care at all about the environment and only cares about profits, and starts the program anyway. In the harm condition, most participants say the chairman harmed the environment intentionally. In the help condition, where the program will help the environment and the chairman again says he only cares about profits, most participants judge that he helped the environment intentionally too — the effect is that people blame him either way, since the profit motive is constant and the side effect is foreseeable in both cases. So the folk concept of intentionality is heavily sensitive to whether the agent approves of the outcome.

This was surprising because philosophy of action had long assumed, following Anscombe and Davidson, that the concept of intentional action could be analyzed in purely descriptive, non-normative terms: an action is intentional when the agent has the right kind of causal story connecting desire, belief, and outcome. The Knobe effect — sometimes called the side-effect effect — shows that ordinary people's intuitions about intentionality are contaminated by moral judgment, which should be irrelevant on the traditional analysis.

Knobe himself argued that this shows the folk concept of intentional action is defective and ought to be replaced or revised, since a concept whose application depends on whether the outcome is good or bad cannot do the work philosophy needs it to do. So the result is really a debunking one: experimental philosophy here functions like psychology's heuristics-and-biases tradition, exposing systematic errors in ordinary cognition.

As for complications, the main one concerns the participant pool. The effect was initially found with Western undergraduate samples, and subsequent work suggests it is largely restricted to them: in many non-Western populations the asymmetry weakens or disappears, and even among Westerners it fades when more concrete, individually identified victims replace the abstract environment talk. So the effect may be an artifact of WEIRD samples and abstract materials rather than a stable feature of the folk concept. That said, even if the effect is culturally local, it still tells against the purely descriptive analysis, since a contingent concept is still a concept that violates the traditional assumptions.

One more point worth adding: the results can be read as an empirical vindication of the doctrine of double effect, since Knobe's vignettes show that foresight of a harm is what makes people judge it intentional, which is exactly what Aquinas's doctrine predicts about how side effects work. This suggests the medieval theory was tracking the folk concept all along.
