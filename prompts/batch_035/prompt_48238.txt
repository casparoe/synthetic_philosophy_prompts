my reading group is wrapping up a semester on philosophy of emotion, and the last session is on the paradox of fiction. we keep circling the same three points and none of us can let go of any of them: (1) it seems just true that i grieved for Anna Karenina, was afraid for the girl in the horror movie, etc. — the feeling was real, i wasn't faking; (2) i also stably beleive these people don't exist and that nothing they do puts anyone at risk, and i don't stop believing it while i'm reading; (3) emotions like fear or grief seem to need a believed-in object — fear needs something believed to be dangerous. pick any two and you have to deny the third, which is exactly what none of our solutions managed to do convincingly. so instead of one more discussion i want to close the session with a poem.

i want a villanelle, and i think the form is almost designed for this: two lines that come back no matter what the middle of the poem does about them, alternating, and then forced to stand side by side in the final couplet. make one refrain the flat factual denial — the character was never there, nothing happened — and the other the equally sincere report of the feeling itself, the sleep lost over someone. don't let either refrain soften over the course of the poem; a lot of the essays we read cheat by quietly redefining "belief" or "emotion" until the tension evaporates, and i don't want the poem to cheat the same way. also, villanelles don't have a volta, so all the turning has to happen in the tercets: i'd like the middle stanzas to each try out one of the escape routes we discussed — the make-believe route where the emotion is real but only quasi, felt within a game i know is a game, like a child sprinting from a tag-ghost; the half-belief route where i briefly take it all as real; the route where fear just doesn't need belief, only vivid thoughts and imagery doing the work belief was supposed to do. no name-dropping of philosophers, but someone who's grappled with the paradox should recognize each tercet as one of the standard moves, and should see why each one fails to actually dissolve the two refrains.

act as a poet who has also put real time into philosophy of mind, and think carefully before writing — maybe start by listing which tercet gets which escape route and in what order (we argued about ordering too: strongest first, or most tempting first?). the poem should be good enough as a poem that someone with no philosophy would still like it, but accurate enough that a specialist wouldn't wince. and the ending must not resolve anything — the two refrains standing together in the last couplet should be the paradox itself, not a truce. that's honestly where our discussion ended up. after the poem, add a short note (a few sentences, not an essay) on how you used the form — which tercet is doing what — so i have something to point to when we talk about it.
