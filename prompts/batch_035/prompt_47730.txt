I'm a graduate student preparing for a field exam in formal epistemology, and the topic I drew is updating on uncertain evidence. I understand strict conditionalization well enough, and I've spent the last couple of weeks on Jeffrey conditioning — the idea that when you can't be certain that E, but your credence in E shifts, your new credence in any H should be a mixture of your old conditional credences in H given the cells of your evidence partition, weighted by the new credences in those cells. The running seminar example was the dog: late at night you hear a sound that is probably but not certainly your dog barking; your credence that the dog is barking moves from near zero to 0.7, and rather than pretending you learned the barking for certain, you let the shift propagate to everything downstream of barking in the old proportions. So far so good. But my reading list now branches into a thicket of alternatives — approaches that treat the noisy observation itself as a random variable to be conditioned on within an enlarged model space, proposals that blend old and new credences by some fixed weighting, and so on — and before I try to adjudicate among them, I want the evaluation standard written down.

That's what I'd like from you: the desiderata. Write the list of requirements that any adequate rule for updating on uncertain evidence must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark each one as non-negotiable or as a preference that some respectable camp has actually rejected. At a minimum I want your view on the following, though feel free to add or prune items. (1) Certainty reduction: when the new credence in the evidence is 1, the rule must collapse to ordinary conditioning — otherwise it isn't a generalization of Bayesian updating but a rival to it. (2) Conservativity: only the credences over the evidence partition should change on the evidence's own say-so, with the conditional probabilities given each cell carried over unchanged — the faint sound should raise the probability of a bark without, by itself, altering how a bark is correlated with an intruder. (3) Order-independence: two successive uncertain updates applied in either order should land in the same place, since otherwise final credences depend on the sequence in which the agent registered the same impressions, and alternating updates can drift or cycle without ever settling. (4) Insensitivity to arbitrary partitioning: whether I update on the coarse pair bark / no bark with credence 0.7, or first split bark into loud and quiet and assign the same total mass to the two taken together, should not matter — or, if it must, tell me why that dependence is a feature of the rule rather than a defect that disqualifies it. (5) Something to say at the extremes: a rule for uncertain evidence is supposed to be an improvement on strict conditionalization precisely where the old rule is dogmatic — credence 0 or 1 in the evidence — so say what the rule should do when an agent starts out certain and shouldn't be.

Finally — and this is the part I most want to see — flag any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. The case I keep circling is the unreliable instrument: the very same faint sound that pushes credence toward the dog's barking is also, if my hearing is known to be bad at night, evidence about the conditional probabilities themselves — the correlation between barking and an intruder, given what I heard, is not what it was before I learned how I hear. If conservativity is non-negotiable, that case seems to make the list unsatisfiable; if it is negotiable, I want to hear which camp gives it up and what that costs them elsewhere. Then close with one brief paragraph scoring three contenders against your list — strict conditionalization pretending the evidence is certain, Jeffrey conditioning as standardly stated, and the move that models the observation as a variable in a richer space and does ordinary conditioning on it. Keep the scorecard short: the list is what I'll be examined on.
