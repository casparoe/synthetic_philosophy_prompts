been in an aesthetics reading group for about a year now and the same fight keeps happening every few months about aesthetic testimony, and i've started to suspect it's not really one fight at all. so here's the setup as we've hashed it out: nobody in the room thinks there's anything *conceptually* weird about saying "yeah my friend told me the new film is great and I believed her" — but everyone balks at the idea that you could thereby come to *know* it's great, or that your belief would be justified in anything like the way testimony justifies ordinary factual belief. we've covered the standard moves: the aesthetic empiricist line that you need first-hand experience of the work to get aesthetic knowledge at all, the replies (Meskin-style, and later Robson and others) that this is way too strong and makes aesthetic knowledge look like a weird curiosity among knowledge kinds, Hopkins trying to thread the needle with something about acquiring the ability to imagine the right way rather than just receiving information, etc. and here's the thing: after a year, people's positions haven't moved an inch, and I can predict who will say what before anyone opens their mouth.

my working hypothesis, which I want you to take seriously but not just rubber-stamp: I think there are actually two disagreements bundled together and everyone treats them as one. one is about *justification transmission* — whether the aesthetic case is disanalogous to, like, testimony about the weather in a way that blocks the usual justification transfer — and the other is about something more like the *point or function* of aesthetic judgement, where the anti-testimony side thinks that if you believe a work is beautiful purely on someone's say-so you're in some sense doing aesthetics wrong, failing to participate in the practice, even if your belief happens to be true and reliably formed. the people in my group who argue against testimony sometimes slide between "you can't know it" and "you shouldn't be satisfied with believing it that way" without noticing, and the pro-testimony people attack the first claim while never touching the second, so they talk past each other. but maybe I'm wrong and it's really one disagreement about whether aesthetic properties are in some sense essentially experiential, which would make it a cousin of debates about secondary qualities.
