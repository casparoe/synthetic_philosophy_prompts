The midterm question they were answering was:

"We have distinguished three broad theories of emotion. Judgment theories (Solomon, Nussbaum) hold that an emotion is an evaluative judgment about how things stand relative to what one cares about — fearing the dog is, at least in part, judging that the dog threatens something you value. Perceptual theories (in the style of Tappolet) hold that an emotion is a quasi-perceptual experience of an evaluative property: fear is a perception of danger, the way a visual experience presents a color. The content is nonconceptual, and the state has correctness conditions of its own, independent of what the subject is prepared to endorse. Feeling theories (James, and Prinz's neo-Jamesian version) hold that an emotion is the feeling of a pattern of bodily change — fear is the felt racing heart, the tightness, etc. — where any evaluative content is carried by the bodily response itself standing for a concern, not by an act of judging. Explain what is at stake between the judgment and perceptual theories, and assess whether recalcitrant emotions — emotions that persist against the subject's better judgment, like fearing a dog you are fully convinced is harmless — favor the perceptual theory."

And here's the student's answer:

"The difference between judgment and perceptual theories is not a disagreement about which mental states are emotions but about what kind of state an emotion is. The judgment theorist treats fear of the dog as a judgment: to fear the dog is, at least in part, to judge that the dog threatens something I care about. The perceptual theorist denies that the emotion involves endorsing any proposition. Fear presents the dog as dangerous, the way the Müller-Lyer lines look unequal even to someone who has measured them and knows they are equal. So the perceptual theorist can say the emotion has a content and can be correct or incorrect, without saying the subject believes that content.

Recalcitrant emotions are the strongest argument for the perceptual side. Suppose I am afraid of a dog although I have known this dog for years and am fully convinced it is harmless. If fear were a judgment that the dog is dangerous, this case would be impossible: a person cannot judge that the dog is dangerous while judging that it is harmless, since a belief excludes its own negation. So the judgment theory predicts recalcitrance cannot happen, and since it plainly does happen, the judgment theory is false. The perceptual theory handles the case the way vision handles the Müller-Lyer lines: the presentation persists even after judgment changes, and since this is familiar from perception it is no mystery.

It is worth noting that Tappolet's own version of the perceptual theory is closer to James than it might sound. On her view, what we perceive in fear is our own bodily response — the racing heart, the tightness — but this perception is also a registration that something of value is at stake. So her view ends up between the feeling theory and the judgment theory, which is perhaps why it is tempting to describe the perceptual theory as just a weaker judgment theory: same content, less commitment.

A judgment theorist might reply that the recalcitrant case involves background judgments that persist without the subject noticing, for example an unconscious judgment that dogs in general are dangerous. But this reply makes the theory unfalsifiable: any case where the subject denies the judgment gets redescribed as a hidden judgment, so nothing could ever count against the view. That seems like a cost, though it is a cost about methodology rather than about the nature of emotion itself."
