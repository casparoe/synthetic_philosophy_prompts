I teach intro epistemology at a state school, mostly to students who are there for a distribution requirement, so I've heard a lot of bad summaries of the regress problem over the years. But a colleague in the psych department just lent me a trade book she'd been reading — pop-epistemology for general readers, I'll spare you the title — and its chapter on the regress of justification annoyed me in ways I'm having trouble pinning down, and I want to check my instincts against someone who knows the literature.

The gist of the chapter, as best I can reconstruct it: if you want to be justified in believing something, you need a reason for it, and that reason needs a reason, and so on. There are exactly three ways this can go. Either the chain of reasons comes to an end at beliefs that justify themselves without needing further reasons (foundationalism), or the chain loops back on itself so everything supports everything else (coherentism), or the chain simply never ends (infinitism). The book then dispatches the first two pretty fast. Foundationalism fails because nothing is immune to doubt: your senses can deceive you, so perceptual experience can't serve as a foundation; even the deliverances of introspection can be misdescribed. Coherentism fails because a perfectly consistent system of beliefs can still be a fantasy — internal coherence just isn't truth, so coherence can't be what justification is. That leaves infinitism, which the book admits nobody actually defends, and which it rules out on the grounds that finite human beings obviously cannot complete an infinite chain of reasons. Conclusion: justification as philosophers have traditonally understood it is a fantasy, the regress shows the whole concept is broken, and we'd do better to talk about degrees of confidence, like sensible scientists do.

So, help me audit this. Which parts would working epistemologists accept as a fair statement of the problem? Where does it conflate things the standard views keep apart, or overstate modest theses, or present standard objections as if they were knockdown refutations? For instance, I seem to remember coherentists having a more interesting response to the "consistent fantasy" objection than the book gives them credit for — something about whether coherence alone was ever supposed to be sufficient, versus necessary, and about what happens when the system includes beliefs about our perceptual situation. And the foundationalist literature on experience as a "given" seems to me more subtle than "your senses can sometimes deceive you." Am I remembering right, or am I being to generous to the traditional views because I teach them? And finally the ending: is the move to degrees of confidence as radical a departure as the book makes it sound, or is it something a lot of traditional epistemologists could more or less shrug at? I'd like to know which of my annoyances are justified and which are just professional turf-defensiveness, if you can separate those out.
