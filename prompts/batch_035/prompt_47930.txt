I'm in a small ethics reading group (two philosophy grad students, a lawyer friend, and me) and for the better part of a year we've been circling the same fight about what deception and coercion actually do to consent, and we are completely stuck. The setup: everyone agrees lying to someone to get them into bed is wrong, and everyone agrees blackmailing them is worse. The fight is about the mechanism. One camp says consent is basically a normative power you exercise — like signing a contract — so when you're deceived, the consent still *happened*, it's just tainted, and the wrong is a separate wrong (fraud, manipulation) layered on top. The other camp says consent is fundamentally a choice, and a choice made on false premises isn't a corrupted instance of consent but no consent at all — the deception doesn't make the consent bad, it makes it absent. Our lawyer keeps muddying things by pointing out that contract law has already solved this in her favor: contracts induced by fraud are void*able*, not void, so why should bedroom consent work differently? And the grad students keep insisting that the actual patchwork in criminal law is the real data any theory has to explain — deception about the nature of the act voids consent outright, deception about identity sometimes does, deception about your income or your fidelity basically never does — and that any account that can't reproduce that graded structure is just not fit for purpose.

We've tried the obvious moves. Someone proposed distinguishing deception about the act itself from deception about the surrounding world, and that lasted one meeting before the counterexample about a swapped twin (same "act," deceived about who) killed it. Someone else proposed a "but-for" causal test — if she wouldn't have agreed had she known, no consent — and that collapsed immediately because it would invalidate consent obtained by, say, hiding your vasectomy from someone who wants kids, which nobody at the table wanted to call rape. Coercion produces the same stall in miniature: we all agree a gun to the head vitiates, we all agree a boss leaning on a subordinate at least taints, but no one can say *where* the line is or what makes the line fall where it does, and every criterion we propose (would a reasonable person refuse? was there a threat or just an offer? was the pressure wrongful or just unfortunate?) gets picked apart within twenty minutes.

My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to actually evaluate it rather than just take it on: the stall isn't because anyone is being dense, it's because the two camps are answering two different questions under one word. The "consent as choice" people are really doing moral psychology — they care about what happened inside the person's head and whether it counts as an autonomous exercise of agency. The "consent as signature" people, and probably the law, are really doing normative bookkeeping — they care about what permissions and liabilities the act generates downstream, which is why the law can afford graded responses (void, voidable, valid-but-actionable) that the psychology camp finds incoherent. If that's right, then maybe the disagreement is largely verbal and the real fight is about which function the word consent *should* be reserved for, which would be a normative question about conceptual engineering, not a discovery about the nature of consent. But I'm not sure — the camps also seem to genuinely disagree about whether deception victims are *wronged in the same way* as outright refusals, which doesn't feel verbal. So: diagnose the disagreement for me. Is it verbal, substantive-but-ill-posed, two projects with one label, or a real disagreement about which of consent's functions we should care about most? Use what each side actually argues, not a survey of positions. And then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a test case, a way of splitting the question, or whether this is just one of those debates where the sides should agree to use different words and stop.
