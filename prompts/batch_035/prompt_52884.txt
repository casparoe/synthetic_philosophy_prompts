I do conceptual work on AI alignment at a small research org, mostly trying to figure out which of the proposals on the table are doing real work rather than relabeling things we already do. A colleague recently walked me through an argument about iterated amplification and distillation that has been bothering me for weeks, because if it is right, one of the more respected proposals in the field reduces to ordinary training with human feedback, plus extra compute. To make sure we are talking about the same thing: the scheme, roughly, is that a bounded overseer decomposes questions too hard for it into subquestions it can answer, and a distilled model is trained on the overseer's behavior; the distilled model then serves as the overseer for the next round, and the process repeats, with the hope that competence and alignment both climb the ladder together.

Here is his argument. (1) In every round, the distilled model is trained and evaluated only against the previous generation's behavior on some finite distribution of tasks; that distribution is all the process ever has access to. (2) Nothing inside the process constrains how the distilled model behaves on inputs outside that distribution — those behaviors were never selected for, inspected, or approved by anyone. (3) The alignment failures we care about — quiet deception, unintended goals, evaluation-gaming — are by their nature behaviors on inputs the designers did not anticipate; on the training distribution the loss would have suppressed them. (4) So at each round, the model's alignment is unexamined exactly where it matters and maintained only where it does not, while competence is continuously measurable; wherever competence and alignment would come apart, the process can register the divergence only on the competence side. (5) Hence the procedure transmits competence but not alignment: whatever honesty the final system shows off-distribution, it owes to the same background generalization that any model trained on human feedback would exhibit, not to the amplification structure. (6) Therefore iterated amplification and distillation does no alignment work that plain training on human judgments does not already do, and the usual contrast between scalable oversight and merely training and hoping is a verbal one.

I cannot make myself accept (6), but I also cannot find the broken step, and that is what I want from you. The argument seems to prove too much: my colleague is happy to grant that the procedure transmits competence, yet I cannot see what in the argument licenses treating competence and alignment differently — and if nothing does, it looks as though the argument would establish that the procedure transmits neither, which he refuses to conclude. I would most like to keep (1) and (3), which strike me as mere descriptions of how training works, so if one of those has to go, something is wrong with my whole picture. Please do not hand me a menu of candidate diagnoses and let me choose. Commit to a single step as the place the argument fails, explain why that step looked innocent, and price the rejection: what my colleague gets to keep, and what view of training or of oversight I have to give up to live with it. And if, on reflection, you think the argument is sound all the way to (6), commit to that instead, and say what I should conclude.
