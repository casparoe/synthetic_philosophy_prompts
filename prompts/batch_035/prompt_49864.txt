In Anglo-American tort law, a bystander who watches a child drown, though he could have rescued her at no risk to himself, is not liable: his omission is not treated as a cause of her death. The employed lifeguard who fails to make the same rescue is liable, and the courts apply to his omission the same but-for test they would apply to a positive act: had he acted, the child would have lived, so his failure counts as a cause of the death. Meanwhile, the very same legal system insists in other settings that we punish the person and not the harm, that outcomes beyond the risk which made the defendant negligent cannot ground liability. And outside the courtroom, public-health bodies do the reverse of the courts: they routinely quantify deaths attributable to omissions — to non-vaccination, to absent safety regulation, to a failure to screen — with no duty marking anything out, and those attributions then feed back into regulatory cost-benefit analysis and even into litigation itself. So one institution treats an omission as a cause only where a prior norm designates it, while another treats counterfactual dependence on an absent good as sufficient for attribution, and the first institution quietly relies on the second whenever a statute or an agency imports those numbers.

What does the legal practice presuppose about causation? Specifically: does the duty rule presuppose that omissions are genuine happenings in the world — absences that, like bullet wounds, bring harm about — or does it concede, by routing omissions through duty, that what the court calls a cause in these cases is largely its own normative assignment, with the world supplying only the counterfactual dependence? Consider the candidate implicit theories: (1) a straightforward counterfactual theory, on which the bystander is exactly as much a cause as the lifeguard, and the duty rule is an unacknowledged normative carve-out sitting on top of it; (2) a restricted counterfactual theory, on which, among the many conditions whose presence would have prevented the harm — a rescuer, a guardrail, a regulation, the child's never having gone near the water — only the deviation from a background expectation counts as a cause, so that causation by omission is really a claim about what is normal rather than a claim about the world; and (3) an eliminativist view, on which omissions never strictly cause anything and the lifeguard case is better described as compensation for breach of contract-like duty than as recovery for harm caused. Which of these does the practice actually need, which does it merely sit comfortably alongside, and where is it underdetermined — that is, could several of the three license exactly the same outcomes in the cases courts actually decide?

Then tell me what would have to change. If the law replaced its implicit view with a pure counterfactual-dependence view, what happens to the bystander, to the flood of absent preventers that follows (every non-rescuer a tortfeasor, every non-regulator a defendant), and to the doctrines that currently ride on the duty rule? And conversely: if the duty rule is the practice's way of admitting that omission-causation is norm attribution rather than metaphysics, does that admission generalize — should we read the epidemiological attributions, and the legal system's reliance on them, as making the same admission under a different name? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, a verdict on whether the practice is coherent as it stands, and an indication of the two or three places where the replacement would genuinely change outcomes rather than merely change the vocabulary.
