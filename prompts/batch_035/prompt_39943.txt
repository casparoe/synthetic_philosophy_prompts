You are a moral philosopher who specializes in aggregation problems — the ethics of summed tiny harms, threshold and discontinuity views, and the ways small burdens can or cannot add up to large ones. Approach this analytically: before committing to any answer, brainstorm the candidate principles that could fit the track record below, check each against every verdict, and only then argue for your best guess. Be rigorous and concrete; toy cases with explicit numbers are your natural habitat.

My friend Dana and I have kept a running game for years: I pose pairs of hypothetical outcomes, and she says which is worse. The rules of the game are strict — purely impersonal comparisons of overall badness, everything else held equal, no one choosing anything, no special obligations between the people involved, all lives normal and equal in length otherwise. Her verdicts so far:

1. Five billion people each feeling a one-second paper-cut sting, versus one person with a badly broken leg involving months of significant pain: she says the stings, taken together, are worse.
2. Ten thousand people each with a week of the flu, versus one person with a full year of it: she says the ten thousand are worse.
3. One person in unrelievable agony for fifty years, versus any number of people each with a severe one-week migraine: she says the fifty years of agony is worse. When I pushed her on absurdly large numbers — I have gone up to a googolplex of migraine-sufferers — her answer did not change, though she paused noticeably longer than usual before repeating it. When I asked whether some even larger number could eventually flip it, she said she genuinely did not know.
4. One person in that same agony for fifty years, versus ten people in the same agony for five years each: she says the single fifty-year case is worse. She answered instantly and volunteered that the badness seems to compound — that the fiftieth year is worse than the first.
5. One death versus any number of broken legs: she says the death is worse, though she flagged this one as less certain than the agony cases.

Next week's case: one person in unrelievable agony for fifty years, versus ten quintillion (10^20) people each in the same agony for a single day.

I want a prediction, and the reasoning behind it. Specifically:

- Reconstruct at least two, and ideally three, distinct rules or principles that jointly fit all five of her verdicts, being explicit about what each rule must say about her hesitation in verdict 3 — some rules can read that hesitation as the pressure point where she is unsure whether to keep holding a hard line.
- Say what each rule predicts for the new case.
- Give me your best guess as to what she will actually say, and defend the preference for one rule over the others using everything in the record: which verdicts she answered instantly versus slowly, where her confidence flagged, and which rule would make her overall pattern of hesitations intelligible rather than accidental.
- Tell me which further case — a single well-chosen hypothetical I could put to her — would best discriminate between the candidate rules, and what each possible answer from her would reveal.
- Finally, be honest about where her track record is strained or internally tense, if it is.

Two cautions. First, keep separate the question of what Dana will say from the question of which outcome really is worse; I am asking about her, and a prediction contaminated by your own verdicts is worth less to me. Second, do not assume her view is any named position from the literature; it may be an unstable hybrid, and if so, say so.
