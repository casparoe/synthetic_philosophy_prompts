I'm TAing a 19th-century American philosophy course (PhD student, mostly grading, one discussion section) and I'm mid-grading of the midterm and want a second opinion on one answer. The exam question was:

Peirce claims, in How to Make Our Ideas Clear (1878), that the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical effects, and that truth is the opinion that inquiry is fated to settle on in the ideal limit. James, in Pragmatism (1907), holds that true ideas are those we can verify and assimilate, the ones that work for us, though he stresses in the long run and on the whole, not just immediately. (a) Explain the core difference between the two accounts of truth. (b) Apply each to the following case: an isolated sect holds a detailed belief about the afterlife -- a specific judgement, rewards, and so on -- that structures their whole lives, gives them lasting solace, and that they insist cannot be settled by inquiry in this life. Does their belief come out true, false, or does truth not yet apply, on each view? (c) Briefly: does the Peirce/James difference even show up for ordinary beliefs about observable matters, or only in cases like the sect's?

The student's answer, as written:

The pragmatic maxim says the meaning of an idea is its conceivable practical effects, so Peirce's theory of truth follows directly: a belief is true if acting on it pays. Peirce thinks inquiry, if pursued long enough, converges on one settled opinion, and that settled opinion is the truth. James agrees that truth is what works, but for James what matters is expedience, so if a belief is useful it is true for the person who holds it. On the sect: James would say their afterlife belief is true right now, because it gives them solace, and this is basically the Will to Believe doctrine applied -- we're entitled to believe whatever it benefits us to believe. For Peirce, the belief is true too, since the sect will never complete an investigation into the afterlife, so their opinion will never be overturned; fated agreement among them settles the matter. On (c), the difference is small in everyday life since both say true beliefs work out, but it gets large exactly in cases like the sect's, where James goes by present usefulness and Peirce by what inquiry would eventually say.
