A friend and I spent most of a recent evening in a long argument about civil disobedience and, underneath it, about whether there is any general obligation to obey the law. We got into it after she mentioned that she had been arrested at a protest and had refused a plea bargain, insisting on the full penalty. Neither of us budged, so I want to lay out the exchange as it actually happened and ask for a point-by-point verdict. The argument ran as follows:

(1) She opened with the claim that no one has a general obligation to obey the law. The social-contract story fails because nobody signed anything, and the fallback story — that we consent by using public services — fails because there is no realistic way to opt out of the state's services. So a protester needs no special license to break the law; the only question is whether the cause is just, just as it would be if there were no state at all.

(2) I answered that her own conduct cuts against her theory. She protested openly and, far from evading the law, accepted arrest and the full sentence. On the traditional account — the one behind Martin Luther King's letter from a Birmingham jail and Rawls's treatment of civil disobedience — accepting punishment is precisely what separates conscientious disobedience from ordinary lawbreaking: it displays fidelity to the legal order while resisting a particular law. If she really believed no obligation exists, the consistent course would be to evade arrest, and her refusal to do so looks like a tacit concession to my side.

(3) She said I had confused two different things: what is strategically wise and what is morally required. Openness and the acceptance of punishment are communicative — they show that she is not pursuing private advantage and is willing to answer to her fellow citizens — but that is a preference about how best to persuade, not an admission of duty. Moreover, she noted, other classes of lawbreaker are never asked to turn themselves in: no one thinks a speeder owes the state a confession, so it is unclear why a protester owes it one.

(4) I then shifted from consent to fairness. Even if consent is a myth, she receives enormous benefits from a cooperative scheme she did not create and could not replace — courts, public order, infrastructure — and those benefits exist only because most people comply most of the time. On the fair-play principle, those who take the benefits of cooperation owe their share of its burdens, and the chief burden is obedience to law even when one dislikes particular laws.

(5) She replied that fair play, if it works at all, grounds a reason to support the system's just arrangements, not a reason to obey any particular law: no one thinks that accepting the benefits of membership obliges you to comply with a club rule that excludes members by race. And a state that breaks the rules of its own scheme — by enacting rights-violating laws — is in no position to demand reciprocity from anyone.

(6) I pressed the instability problem. If only just laws bind, and every citizen judges for herself, then law's claim collapses into each person's private morality, and the cooperative benefits she wants to keep depend on exactly the general compliance she denies she owes. I added that if reciprocity can be forfeited by the state's misdeeds, this at most suspends obligations toward the offending laws, not the general duty.

(7) She closed by observing that the general duty cannot survive the way its defenses keep narrowing: consent fails outright, and fair play is gradually being downgraded into a pro tanto reason to comply with just laws — at which point "political obligation" names little more than the moral weight that good laws would carry anyway, and the real question is not obedience but the terms on which citizens hold one another accountable.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the seven numbered moves, say whether it is correct, mistaken, or correct about less than it claims, and briefly justify the rating. Then state who, if either, came out ahead overall, and identify anything important that both of us missed. I have a suspicion of my own here: that we were running together two distinct questions — whether there is a duty to obey the law, and what makes disobedience to a given law permissible — and I would like to know whether that suspicion is correct, and if so, how separating the questions would have changed the argument. Please write your response in American English.
