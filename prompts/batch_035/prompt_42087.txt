I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and the question I keep circling is why the debate between the Aumann camp and the peer-disagreement camp has gone nowhere in roughly two decades, and what kind of disagreement it actually is.

On one side there is the formal result and its enthusiasts: Aumann's 1976 theorem that agents with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's posteriors must have equal posteriors; the Geanakoplos-Polemarchakis corollary that simply trading posteriors back and forth converges to agreement; Cowen-and-Hanson-style arguments that if real disputants are honest, priors are genuinely shared, and evidence is genuinely common, then persistent disagreement between peers is a smoking gun of some epistemic failure, most likely overconfidence. On the other side there is the mainstream epistemology of peer disagreement, running through the equal-weight and conciliationist literature: the cases where two people share evidence, track each other's track records, and yet remain rationally permitted — perhaps required — to keep their divergent credences. The formal people treat the informal side as failing to understand that the assumptions do the work; the informal people treat the formal side as arguing from an idealization so thin that nothing follows about creatures like us. Both literatures cite each other's labels without either camp moving, as far as I can tell, since roughly 2007.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the impasse comes from the two sides using the same word, disagreement, for two different objects. The Aumann camp is talking about a fixed point that an abstract pair of agents must occupy if certain informational conditions literally hold, while the conciliationist camp is talking about what a finite, socially embedded agent is permitted to retain when she knows the conditions don't literally hold and can't tell by how much they fail. On this reading the dispute is not about evidence or about intuitions at all — it is about how far idealizations can be normatively loaded, and neither side has an argument that settles that in general because both have perfectly good ones in their home idiom.

But I want a real diagnosis, not just mine. Is the disagreement substantive — is there, for instance, a hidden empirical question about whether human priors are in fact close enough to common for the theorem to bite, or about whether agents who talk long enough do in fact converge? Is it verbal, with the two camps using 'rational' and 'disagreement' to mean different things? Is the common-prior assumption doing the whole job, such that the entire debate is a proxy for the older and equally stalled fight over where priors come from? Or is the crux the common-knowledge condition, given that the number of rounds needed to approach common knowledge of posteriors grows uncomfortably fast with the size of the state space — a point the complexity-minded critics of the theorem keep raising and the epistemologists keep ignoring?

Given whatever diagnosis you settle on, tell me what would count as progress. A distinction that dissolves the fight? A weakening of the formal result that the informal side would actually accept as news? Empirical work on whether structured dialogue between honest disputants with shared evidence actually produces convergence, which would at least tell us whether the idealization is asymptotically honest or an artifact from top to bottom? I'm not asking for a survey of either literature — I can get that anywhere — but for an account of the structure of the disagreement, what each side's own best arguments commit them to, and an honest verdict on whether this debate is hard, ill-posed, or merely waiting for someone from one side to learn the other side's formalism well enough to say something its partisans can't ignore.
