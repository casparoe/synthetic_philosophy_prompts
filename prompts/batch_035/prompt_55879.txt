After a long argument with a friend over dinner, I'm left puzzled by a norm I've sat under for years without questioning: on hiring committees, thesis defenses, and grand rounds, we go around the table with the most junior member speaking first and the chair last, precisely so that the chair's stated judgment doesn't pull everyone else along with it. My friend insists the practice smuggles in a whole epistemology of testimony — that it treats the expressed opinions of senior members as noise to be suppressed rather than evidence to be weighed, even though those members were presumably selected for their judgment in the first place. What does the norm actually presuppose about how beliefs and evidence flow through a group: a cascade picture on which each public statement partly overwrites the private signals of those who haven't yet spoken, or something about independence being a scarce resource to be conserved until everyone has committed? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the same institutions' opposite habit elsewhere — deliberately publishing senior endorsements, prize committees announcing a unanimous first-round verdict, central figures releasing their recommendations early precisely to move others — amounts to a real inconsistency or only an apparent one. Argue for a verdict rather than surveying options, write it as continuous prose with no headings, lists, bold text, or tables, and tell me plainly where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration would move you to the opposite verdict.
