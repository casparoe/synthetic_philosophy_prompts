A certain argument against institutional reform keeps recurring in my circles: Chesterton's fence, the counsel that you should not remove a fence until you know why it was put up. It is now used against reforms of everything from marriage norms and licensing regimes to academic customs, and lately even against ablating inscrutable components of a trained neural network. But the analogy's source is a deliberately erected artefact — an identifiable builder, a purpose that presumably still holds unless you can name it — while most of its targets have no builder and no rationale at all, only effects. Please audit the analogy: give the correspondence table (fence, builder, purpose, remover, cost of removal), then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conservative conclusion from the idle ones. Three stress points I care about: (i) many inherited institutions were not designed but selected for, so the question of why they were 'put up' has no answer anyone ever knew — does the counsel get stronger or weaker when its epistemic demand cannot be met even in principle? (ii) Chesterton's traveller is simply ignorant, whereas modern reformers often have tolerable documentary evidence that a rule's original rationale has expired; (iii) the counsel slides between two readings — a burden-of-proof rule for the ignorant, and an explanatory demand which, once satisfied, permits removal — and I suspect the conservative conclusion needs the first while the argument only supports the second. Does the fence analogy carry the conservative conclusion once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the conclusion carrying the analogy? Please end with a final line beginning 'Verdict:' giving your one-sentence answer.
