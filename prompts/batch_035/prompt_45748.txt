I'm a physics undergrad and last friday my housemate Jo (philosophy PhD, works on quantum foundations) and my lab partner Sam got into it at the pub about whether the wavefunction is a real thing out there in the world or just bookkeeping for what we know. I'm picking my thesis supervisor this term and one of the candidates does foundational stuff while the other is very shut-up-and-calculate, so I'd honestly like to know who was right. The argument went roughly like this:

(1) Sam said the wavefunction obviously can't be real — it's complex-valued, it lives in an enormous configuration space, you never detect it directly, and the whole formalism looks like a bookkeeping device for probabilities, the way a probability distribution is about our knowledge and not about the world.

(2) Jo said there's a theorem from around 2012, PBR or something, that kills that view: if quantum states are just information about some underlying physical state of the system, and if systems prepared independently really have independent underlying states, then the model ends up predicting that some measurement outcome which quantum theory says must occur has zero probability. Contradiction. So distinct quantum states have to correspond to physically different situations — the state is out there, not in our heads.

(3) Sam said hold on — the contradiction comes from the independence assumption, and a knowledge-view person can just deny it: hidden conspiratorial correlations between "independent" preparations is maybe unappealing, but a wavefunction that's physically real and lives in 3N-dimensional space isn't exactly appealing either. So the theorem rules out one version of the view, not the view.

(4) Jo said that's special pleading: every no-go theorem can be escaped by denying one of its assumptions (that's exactly how defenders of local hidden variables dealt with Bell for years), and anyway Sam's own story can't explain interference — a real wave spreads out through both slits and interferes; knowledge doesn't do that, it just sits in your head.

(5) Sam answered that interference is in the statistics, not in the world — the pattern on the screen is just what your uncertainty does when it evolves by the rules — and threw back that if the wavefunction is really spread out there, why do you only ever find the electron at one point?

Then the pub closed. I could not referee any of this. Can you go through the five moves and tell me who's right about each one, not just overall? In particular: is Jo's statement of the theorem even right, or did she (or I, in the relaying) garble it — and does Sam's comeback in (3) actually work, or is it the kind of escape hatch that costs more than it saves? Also if the whole question rests on something confused, like assuming there's a fact of the matter here at all, just say that plainly instead of splitting the difference to be polite.
