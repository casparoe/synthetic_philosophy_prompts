My metaphysics reading group spent two sessions on Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-sphere universe, and I've come away from them stuck in a standoff with a friend who thinks she can dissolve Black's counterexample entirely rather than merely answer it. Her argument runs as follows. (1) The identity of indiscernibles is a necessary truth: there could not exist two distinct objects sharing all their purely qualitative properties, relational ones included. (2) In Black's universe — two perfectly uniform spheres of the same size, a mile apart, in an otherwise empty and perfectly symmetric space — the spheres share all purely qualitative properties; that is the entire point of the setup. (3) So that universe is not a possible world at all: its description is covertly inconsistent, and vividly picturing it no more confers possibility on it than vividly picturing a round square does. Hence there is no counterexample, and the principle stands. I had assumed the right move was simply to reject (1) and accept that the two-sphere world is possible, but before I could say so she generalized, and this is where I lost the exit. (4) Consider empty Euclidean space itself: any two of its points share all purely qualitative properties, since space is homogeneous and there is nothing else in it for the points to differ relationally over. (5) So by (1) there is at most one spatial point, and space is not extended. (6) Consider, likewise, a world consisting of a single changeless sphere existing forever: any two instants of its history share all purely qualitative properties, so by (1) there is at most one instant, and nothing in that world endures for any time. She even concedes, with something like a shrug, that by the same reasoning no perfectly uniform sphere could have two indiscernible halves, so even the spheres inside Black's universe could not be internally homogeneous. (7) If her principle can prove that space and time are unextended and that nothing homogeneous has parts, it seems to have proven too much. Her rejoinder is that she is a Leibnizian about space and time: points and instants are not objects at all but merely a system of relations among objects, so (4) and (6) never get a grip, while spheres are objects and (2) stands — and she is prepared to say that a world of two bare spheres is, strictly, exactly as impossible as a world of bare points, however easily we picture it.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For the record, I am confident that space has many locations and that time is extended; I am strongly inclined to think the two-sphere world is possible; and I have no taste for individuating objects by haecceities or bare thisnesses, which my friend claims is the only thing left to keep her two spheres distinct once I give up her principle. But I would rather understand what I am signing up for than win the standoff. In particular, I want to know whether her objects-versus-merely-relational-structure line is principled or an ad hoc fence built precisely where the counterexamples are coming from — and, if it is principled, whether it leaves her any room to accept a world of two spheres standing in a distance relation that she will not also let me have. I would also like to know what the cheapest consistent package on the table actually is: rejecting the necessity in her first premise outright, rejecting her claim about the spheres' indiscernibility, biting some Leibnizian bullet, or something I have not considered. Please be specific about what each option must pay, and especially about whether anything forces her argument to a conclusion she has not noticed rather than merely letting her keep the conclusions she wants.
