my advisor sprung this on me after my philosophy of mind talk and i genuinely can't tell if it's a cheap trick or a real refutation:

(1) Epiphenomenalism: phenomenal states are caused by brain states but don't cause anything — not behavior, not other brain states, nothing.
(2) My judgments are, or depend entirely on, brain states.
(3) So my pain never causes, even partly, my judgment that I'm in pain.
(4) If the fact that p plays no causal role in producing my belief that p, then I don't know that p.
(5) So I don't know that I'm in pain when I'm in pain.
(6) That's absurd — if I know anything at all, I know my own pain.
(7) So epiphenomenalism is false, and qualia have to be causally efficacious.

The step I keep circling is (4), because it seems to prove way too much. By the same standard I couldn't know that the Big Bang happened (nothing about it causes my belief), or that 17 is prime, or arguably even that Paris is in France when my belief came from a teacher rather than from Paris itself. But I also can't fully buy the standard epiphenomenalist reply — that psychophysical laws keep pain and pain-beliefs correlated even without causal contact — because a law-governed but causally idle match feels like a lucky coincidence rather than knowledge, and it's weird that evolution would shape my judgments to track facts it never had causal access to. So: which step do I give up, what does giving it up cost, and does anything survive for the parallel people who run this same style of argument against causally inert things like non-natural moral facts?
