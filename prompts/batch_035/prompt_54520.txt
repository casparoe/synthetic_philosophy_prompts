I'm an engineering lead at a mid-sized company, and I've been assigned to write the official postmortem for a project failure — a platform migration that ran a year over schedule, burned through its budget, and was quietly shelved last month. The situation has turned politically charged. A director on the review committee keeps insisting that the failure was entirely foreseeable, pointing to specific memos and dashboard metrics from early in the project as proof that "all the warning signs were there" and that continuing the project after a certain point amounted to negligence. A couple of engineers who wrote those memos now say they were certain at the time that the project would fail, though my own recollection is that they were much more tentative back then, and at least one has told me privately that her confidence only crystallized once she knew how things ended. The director also has a tidy story ready: we chose the migration because of one dramatic vendor pricing announcement, we ignored the doubts, and the collapse followed straight from that original sin. It's a very satisfying story. It also happens to be the only framing I've heard anyone propose, which itself makes me uneasy.

My problem is that I genuinely don't know how to evaluate any of this. Is there a principled way, when writing the postmortem, to separate decisions that were actually bad given what was known at the time from decisions that were reasonable but turned out badly? The director's "it was all there in the data" argument has force — the memos really do exist — but something feels off about treating a failed outcome as proof that the intervening judgments were errors, and I can't articulate what. And I worry that even if I try to reconstruct what the decision-makers actually believed and knew at each point, I'll be doing it with the ending already in hand, unconsciously rearranging the events into whatever arc makes the ending feel inevitable. That's what the committee wants, frankly: a narrative. I'd like practical advice on how to structure a retrospective like this so it's fair to the people being judged — but more than that, I'd like to understand whether the sense of inevitability I keep feeling when I reread the project timeline is a genuine feature of the events or an artifact of my position at the end of them, and what, if anything, can be done about it from where I sit.
