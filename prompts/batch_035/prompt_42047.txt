A pop-econ book I picked up secondhand explains information cascades like this: back in the early nineties, economists showed that if people can observe what others do before deciding, you get cascades — once a couple of early movers go the same way, everyone after them just copies the majority and throws out whatever their own private information says. That's supposed to explain fads, bank runs, speculative bubbles, restaurant choices, all of it. The book's take is that this shows mass conformity is basically a failure of independent thinking — people in a cascade are being sheep, even if individually rational-seeming — and because each person who joins adds to the pressure on the next one, a cascade, once started, is essentially unstoppable and only an outside shock can break it. The final chapter moral is that if everyone just made up their own mind using their own information, these panics and fads would mostly not happen.

How fair is this summary? Go through it piece by piece and tell me what an economist who works on herding would accept, what they'd want to qualify, and what's just subtly wrong. I'm especially unsure what to think about the claim that people in a cascade "ignore" their own information — my understanding was that these models have people updating in a pretty smart way, but maybe I'm mixing it up with something else. And does the book's moral actually follow — is the problem supposed to be individual behavior, or is something getting lost in jumping from each person acting reasonably to the aggregate outcome being bad? Also curious whether "unstoppable" is right, since I vaguely remember reading that cascades are supposed to be fragile, but I might be thinking of a different phenomenon.
