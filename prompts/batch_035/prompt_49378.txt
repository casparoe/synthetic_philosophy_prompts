For years I've treated foreign-policy judgments as forecasts: I read the rationalist blogs back when they were still arguing about Bayes on Overcoming Bias, I've absorbed the LessWrong habit of separating decision quality from outcome quality, and I've read enough Tetlock to be suspicious of my own hindsight. And yet when I line up my actual verdicts on humanitarian interventions, they refuse to cohere, and I'd like you to force coherence on them — committedly, not by laying out options.

My honest verdicts, case by case:

- Rwanda, 1994: our refusal to intervene was one of the great crimes of the last century, and I hold with high confidence that a modest force without a perfect plan would have saved most of the victims.
- Sierra Leone, 2000: a small British deployment, invited by the sitting government, broke the rebellion in weeks. Clearly right; it doesn't even feel like a hard case.
- Kosovo, 1999: bombing without Security Council authorization, and I still think it was justified. The ethnic cleansing was ongoing, and authorization was never coming, since Russia would veto. I've never much cared that it was technically illegal.
- Iraq, 2003: not justified — but I'm not sure why, and I distrust my own reasons. The humanitarian record was real; what bothers me is the pretext, the absence of any plan for the day after, and the fact that the humanitarian case came from people who would never have made it without the WMD story.
- Libya, 2011: here I lose the thread. Resolution 1973 authorized protecting civilians, and I think the initial protection was right. But NATO slid into regime change and the country came apart into a decade of civil war. I oscillate between "the intervention was right and the execution betrayed it" and "the execution was predictable, so it was wrong at the gate," and my verdict literally changes depending on whether I evaluate ex ante or ex post — which is exactly the failure mode the Sequences warn about.
- Syria: I believe the retreat from the "red line" in 2013 was a genuine moral failure, and I despise the sophistry that was deployed to excuse it. With equal confidence I believe a no-fly zone would probably have become another Libya. I hold both and cannot make them stop contradicting.

I do have a tentative principle: intervene when there is ongoing mass killing, when the intervener's aims are narrow and it has a plausible story for the day after, and — the clause I care most about — when the intervener would still accept the intervention if it fails in the median way that interventions of its reference class fail. But I don't trust it. It smells like something I engineered to save my Kosovo verdict and my Iraq verdict simultaneously, and I can't tell whether that's a feature or a bug.

What I want is a definite position, especially on Libya and Syria — not a survey of what "one might" conclude, and no appeals to ambiguity. Please organize the answer under these headings, in this order:

1. "The principle, stated" — the principle you actually endorse, in one or two precise sentences, and whether it's mine repaired or a rival. (Roughly 150 words.)
2. "Running the cases" — apply it to all six, saying for each whether it saves my verdict, overturns it, or splits it into an ex ante and ex post answer you then adjudicate. (Roughly 500 words.)
3. "The verdicts I give up" — an honest accounting of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and why the principle should win rather than them. (Roughly 200 words.)
4. "Cases I haven't listed" — what your principle predicts for at least three new situations chosen to stress it, stated concretely enough that I could recognize them in the news. (Roughly 250 words.)
5. "The one-line version" — the whole thing compressed to a sentence I could actually apply in real time. (Roughly 50 words.)
