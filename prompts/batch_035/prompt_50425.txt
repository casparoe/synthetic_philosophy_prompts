so my friend and i had a three-hour argument last night that ended with her leaving my apartment still holding my copy of the Lectures on Religious Belief (long story, i'll get it back eventually). the fight: i took the Wittgensteinian line that religious belief isn't a hypothesis — her belief in the Last Judgement isn't like her belief that her landlord will raise rent next year; it's not playing the same evidential game. i leaned on the hinge stuff from On Certainty and on the idea of a religious belief as more like a picture that organizes your whole life than a claim you weigh. she thinks this whole move is secretly condescending. she's a smart Catholic who has actually read her apologetics, and her position is: the resurrection is a historical claim, there's evidence for it, and if a genuinely good argument against it came along she'd have to reckon with that, not shrug and say it's just a different language game. she says the Wittgensteinian reading takes away her right to say her belief is TRUE, and that for her is the whole point of having it.

and she had one comeback that has been bugging me since: Wittgenstein himself got irritated at Frazer for treating the fire rituals as proto-scientific errors — so he clearly thought you could badly misdescribe religious practice. but does that license the further step, that the practice is somehow beyond being wrong about anything at all? that's roughly where the argument died.

can you write a dialogue between the two positions? one character holding my line — a philosophy-of-religion type who can bring in hinges, the Frazer thing, the believing-in vs believing-that contrast — and one holding hers: an intelligent evidentialist believer, absolutely not a strawman, who knows the distinction and thinks believing-that still genuinely applies to at least some of her commitments. set it somewhere concrete, like the two of them driving back from a wedding in one car. make them actually engage with each other's points, each one scoring something real, each one having to give something up.

and please don't end it in a polite draw where they agree it's all very interesting. i want you to commit: decide which one ends up on firmer ground and show it in the dialogue — the loser should concede something specific, not just go quiet. then add a short note at the end telling me which reading of Wittgenstein you actually endorsed in how you resolved it, because i want to know whether you just talked me out of my own position. she would find that hilarious.
