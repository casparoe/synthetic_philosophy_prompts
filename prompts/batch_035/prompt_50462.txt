I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing on anthropic reasoning, and I keep circling the same question: is Bostrom's simulation argument a disguised version of the doomsday argument, or genuinely different in structure? Both extract a striking conclusion from an indexical fact about oneself. Yet Bostrom himself has drawn a line between them: the doomsday argument rests on the contentious premise that one should reason as if one were a random sample from all the people who will ever live, whereas the simulation argument, he maintains, requires no such typicality assumption and delivers only a disjunction — either the human species is unlikely to ever reach a posthuman stage, or posthuman civilizations rarely run ancestor-simulations, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. Please compare the two arguments on precisely this point, and take a definite stand: either identify exactly where the simulation argument smuggles in its own self-sampling premise (for instance, in how it treats the possibility that we are among the many simulated observers rather than the few originals), or pinpoint the step in the doomsday argument that has no analogue in the simulation argument. Argue for one of these two verdicts and defend it; don't lay out both and leave the choice to me.
