I keep running into this weird configuration in everyday life and I can't find a name for it, so let me try to describe it. When I plan something out, the "me" who actually executes the plan often can't tell which stage of the plan he's at — not because he's dumb, but because the situation itself doesn't carry the information. Example: I drive to my night shift along a rural road with two identical intersections, and at 4am, groggy, I know I won't reliably remember whether I've already passed the first one when I reach the second. So a plan like "turn off at the second intersection" is useless, because future-me standing at *an* intersection can't tell first from second. The best I can do is pick one rule for "when at an intersection, do X" that commits to the same action at both, and the best such rule is noticeably worse than what I'd do if I just knew where I was. The plan basically has to be written for an amnesiac.

It's not precommitment, which is the thing I first reached for — I've read the Ulysses and the sirens stuff — because I'm not fighting temptation, I'm fighting missing information about my own progress. It's also not ordinary forgetfulness management like a shopping list, because a shopping list works by letting future-you re-read the plan and update as you go; my intersections come with no state variable I can check off. And I've noticed there seems to be two different ways of coping. One is picking the best state-independent policy, like the intersection rule. The other is redesigning the environment so states become distinguishable in the first place — after my grandmother's stroke we couldn't count on her remembering whether she'd taken her pill, so we got one of those organizers with the days printed on the lids, and suddenly "which state am I in" became readable off the world instead of out of her memory. So I guess the deeper question this is gesturing at: when the executor of a plan can't observe how far along the plan is, is it ever better to just accept a cruder one-size-fits-all rule rather than patch the observability, and when does the redesign win?

Does this general situation — planning for an executor who can't distinguish the states he's in — have an established name or literature attached to it? If something close exists I'd want to know how exactly it differs from what I described, whether it covers partial or probabilistic forgetting, and how it relates to the nearest neighbors like precommitment or weakness-of-will stuff. If nothing fits, I'd honestly settle for a decent coinage with a careful definition and some boundary cases. Either way, please don't just hand me a bare term and stop there — the word isn't really the point, I want to understand what the phenomenon is and isn't.
