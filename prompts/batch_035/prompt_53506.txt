I'm near the end of a novel whose protagonist, a composer in her late thirties, must choose between staying in her dying mother's house in the Fenlands and taking up a conducting post in Buenos Aires that she has wanted, in one way or another, since she was nineteen. My editor, who is wary of the ending I've drafted, keeps reaching for the same analogy in our notes calls: choosing between incommensurable goods, she says, is like being paid a salary in two different currencies with no exchange rate between them — you cannot say which sum is worth more, only that you can spend the one and not the other. She uses this to defend the ending I wrote, in which the reasons run out, the protagonist recognises they have run out, and she chooses the way one flips a coin: arbitrarily, with the arbitrariness itself made visible on the page. My editor takes the currency picture to show that when the scale cannot decide, nothing can, so the coin toss is honest.

I half believe this and half suspect the analogy is doing all the work. Money, after all, is the one thing designed to be commensurable; currencies usually do have exchange rates, and where they don't, a black market invents one by lunchtime. I've also heard philosophers say that in hard choices the options are neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal, but "on a par" — a fourth relation — and that a chooser can put herself behind an option in a way that creates reasons rather than merely recording them. That, if true, would leave my composer something to do that a coin cannot.

Please write a response, in British English, organised under the following headings in this order:

1. **Your verdict** — two or three sentences up front: does the coin-toss ending survive, or was the currency analogy carrying it?
2. **The exchange, mapped** — a table of correspondences: what in my novelist's situation plays the role of each currency, the missing exchange rate, the spender, the goods you can buy with each; roughly 100 words of table and commentary.
3. **Where the ledger breaks** — where the analogy fails, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the ending from those that don't; 250–350 words.
4. **What the picture smuggles in** — what the currency frame quietly assumes about the choice (for instance, that value is a quantity awaiting conversion, that the absence of a common measure means the absence of anything further to say, that choosing is passive spending rather than active committing); 150–250 words.
5. **The ending, revisited** — whether there is a way to keep arbitrariness out without pretending the scales tipped; if you think a different analogy would serve the book better — parity, or on-a-par options, or something of your own — say what it is and what ending it licenses; 200–300 words.

Please engage with the parity idea only as much as it earns; I'd rather have the distinction examined carefully than name-dropped.
