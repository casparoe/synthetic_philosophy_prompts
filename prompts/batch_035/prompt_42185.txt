Background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired physical chemist, 35 years running a spectroscopy lab, and since retiring I've been trying to catch up on philosophy of mind, mostly as a hobby that got out of hand. Somewhere in there I fell into the free energy principle literature and I've now worked myself into an argument that I half want to be right, because I've been a falsifiability guy my whole career and it would be very satisfying if this whole framework collapsed. But I also suspect I'm being sloppy somewhere. Here it is:

(1) The free energy principle says, roughly, that any self-organizing system that persists — an organism, say — has to stay within a small set of states, the ones characteristic of its kind, because a creature that ends up in states atypical of it's species doesn't get to be part of the story for long. The fossil record took care of those.
(2) Staying in that set just is keeping average surprise about one's sensory states low, and free energy is a quantity that bounds surprise from above.
(3) So any organism that exists is an organism whose free energy has been low on average — and this is bookkeeping, not a discovery. The high-free-energy organisms are the dead ones, and the dead ones don't need explaining; they just get subtracted.
(4) Therefore the principle cannot fail. Whatever an animal does, the theory retrodicts that it did it to keep free energy down, and if the animal does something that looks like the opposite — a whale beaching itself, a hunger striker starving — that's treated as evidence the minimum was somewhere I didn't expect, never as a strike against the theory.
(5) A theory that cannot fail in principle tells you nothing. It's not a principle about living systems; it's the anthropic principle wearing equations.
(6) So the free energy principle isn't a scientific claim at all. At best it's a definition of what it means to persist, dressed up in variational formalism.

I have a second complaint I want to fold in, because I suspect it shares a root with the first. If minimizing surprise is the imperative, the optimal organism should curl up in a dark, quiet room and never leave, since that's about the most predictable environment there is. Yet I left the house this morning. I know the dark room thing is a well-worn objection and that the proponents have replies, but the fact that it slots into my argument so neatly makes me think both complaints are really one complaint.

What I want from you: tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. My own suspicion, for what it's worth, is that the sleight of hand happens between (3) and (4) — (3) says the animal's free energy has been low, and (4) quietly upgrades that to a claim that it acts in order to keep it low, turning a description of survivors into a law that guides them. But I can't tell whether rejecting that move guts the framework or just trims it. And I gather some of the proponents cheerfully concede the principle was built to be necessarily true, in which case they'd reject (5) instead of (4) — so I'd also like to hear what a person actually gives up when they say of their favorite theory that it can't be empirically tested and that this is a feature. That last move sounds like theology to my old ears, and I'd genuinely like to be told why it isn't.
