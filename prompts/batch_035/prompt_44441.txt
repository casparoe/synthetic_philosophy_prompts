I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm at work on a feature about typeface design and the psychology of reading. At a design conference last month, a veteran type designer argued to me that letters are like biological species: each printed 'a' is a specimen, each typeface is something like a breed or local population, the alphabet is a genus, and there is no essence of 'a' — only family resemblance, just as there is no single visible trait shared by every member of a species. He leaned on this to license a strong claim: since there is no essence, legibility is pure habit, so any self-consistent shape can serve as an 'a', readers will adapt to even a fairly radical display face the way a population adapts to its environment, and the designer's obligations are therefore purely aesthetic. A vision scientist I interviewed afterward pushed back, muttering something about invariants and features, but our time ran out before she could elaborate.

I'd like a proper audit of the analogy. Please start with the correspondence table — what plays the role of specimen, species, breed, genus, selection, adaptation — and then lay out where the mapping breaks. One datum I keep chewing on: the 'a' in most serif text faces is a two-storey construction with an enclosed bowl, a stem, and a little hook over the bowl, while the 'a' most of us write by hand is basically a circle with a stick on its right side; geometrically these share almost nothing, yet both read effortlessly as 'a', and the bare circle without the stick reads as 'o'. Can the species picture account for that? Which features of biology does the analogy quietly import that letterforms lack — ancestry, gene flow, gradual change under selection versus deliberate, discontinuous redesign by a single mind — and are those disanalogies actually relevant to his legibility conclusion, or merely colorful? Finally, does the "pure habit, anything goes" claim survive once the breaks are on the table, or does family resemblance, examined closely, itself point toward some structural invariant that the species framing obscures — and if the picture fails, what analogy would you put in its place? Please keep the whole response under 400 words, state how confident you are in your verdict, and flag the single point you are least sure of.
