I write long-form features on science and ideas for a general readership, and I am at work on a piece about the modern debate over paternalism — the argument, familiar from behavioral economics, that people are predictably bad at choosing for themselves and that someone else should therefore arrange their choices for them, gently, in their own interest. My editor wants the piece to open with the granddaddy of the position, so I have arranged an interview with the Grand Inquisitor of Dostoevsky's parable: the cardinal of Seville who arrests the returned Christ, explains to him over a long night why the Church was right to refuse the three temptations — bread, miracle, and authority — and so relieve mankind of the unbearable burden of freedom, and is answered only with a kiss on his aged lips and a command to leave and not come back.

Please play the Inquisitor as my interview subject. Constraints: stay in character until I write "end of interview"; your replies are spoken, one to two paragraphs at a time, not essays; you are severe, weary, and completely sincere — you believe your correction of the prisoner's work is an act of love for the weak masses, and you never concede that central point, though you may grant me small secondary points when my questioning earns them. You know nothing after your own century; when I describe something modern, you may interrogate me about it as an inquisitor would cross-examine a witness, and you should answer it strictly from your own resources — the temptations, the crowd, the tower, the kiss — never by borrowing my century's vocabulary as your own.

My opening move, after thanking him for his time and describing, in a sentence or two, the modern nudge state — governments that tax sugar and design pension defaults, all in the name of what citizens would want if they were fully rational: "Your Eminence, your whole edifice rests on bread, miracle, and authority, and it took the Church to supply all three. But suppose I told you that five centuries on there would be institutions doing precisely what you describe — lifting freedom out of people's hands for the sake of their happiness — with no miracles, no hellfire, and no God in the structure at all. Does that count as your work completed at last, or does a paternalism that needs nothing above man strike even you as missing something your edifice never lacked? Either way, you will have to tell me what exactly the difference is, because my readers will want to know whether these people are your descendants or your rivals."
