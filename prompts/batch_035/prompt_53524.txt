I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has spent the last three years reading philosophy of science in earnest, and I am drafting a proposal for a small workshop on laws of nature. I would like your help turning a half-formed idea into a proper two-page proposal. The idea concerns the best-system analysis of laws. As I understand the Humean position, laws are nothing over and above the totality of particular facts: they are the theorems of whichever deductive system best balances simplicity and strength when applied to the whole mosaic of events. What troubles me is a point I have not seen addressed head-on, though I may simply have missed it: that balance seems to privilege systems whose dynamical laws are local — the state of a small region at one time, together with the laws, fixing what happens next in that region — and yet in a Bell-type world the simplest strong system appears to require nonlocal machinery, whether pilot waves or collapses defined over configuration space. So the Humean faces a dilemma. Either she lets the virtues rule as they may and accepts that the laws come out nonlocal, in which case the analysis is doing real work but delivers something many physicists find unpalatable; or she builds locality into what counts as a virtue, in which case I worry she has smuggled in a governing, quasi anti-Humean conception of law that is no longer read off the mosaic. My half-formed thought is that the real question here is about which systemic virtues are internal to the systematization itself and which are substantive commitments imported from outside, and that the Bell case is an unusually clean testbed because one can compare specific candidate systems side by side. Please turn this into a proposal with the following elements: a precise statement of the question; an explanation of why the standard positions on both the Humean and anti-Humean sides leave it open; a description of the method — roughly, a comparative assessment of candidate best systems for a simplified Bell-type world, saying what would be traded off against what; a statement of what would count as the project succeeding or failing; and a realistic scope for a single-authored paper written over six months by someone re-entering scholarship late in life. I will add citations myself, so please do not include any; refer to positions and lines of argument informally rather than by title. Finally, a request about the epistemic texture of the document: as you draft it, indicate how confident you are in each substantive claim you make on my behalf, and single out the one point in the proposal you are least sure of, so that I know where to press my own reading hardest before I submit it.
