In my political philosophy reading group we've been discussing the problem of dirty hands, and someone kept invoking the line "sometimes it is right to do the wrong thing" — associated in our discussion with the view, roughly Michael Walzer's, that a political leader may face situations where doing the right thing, all things considered, requires committing an act that is itself wrong, and that the leader who does it is nonetheless tainted rather than fully absolved. The line was used to defend a specific case: a candidate who, in a close election, spreads a damaging lie about her opponent in order to keep a much worse candidate out of office — doing wrong (lying) in order to do right (the electoral outcome). The claim was that since she did the right thing, she deserves no blame, and anyone who blames her has confused morality with politics.

I can see that the one-liner is doing a lot of work while remaining vague, and I suspect some readings of it are trivial, some substantive, and some outright false. Please lay out, as a menu of distinct precise claims, the different things "sometimes it is right to do the wrong thing" could mean. Consider at least: whether "right" means obligatory, merely permissible, or praiseworthy; whether "wrong" means pro tanto wrong, all-things-considered wrong, blameworthy, or such that guilt or apology is still owed (the idea of a "moral remainder" that survives even a justified choice); and whether the line, on its most defensible reading, just collapses into ordinary consequentialism — in which case it would be a relabeling rather than a paradox. For each reading, say whether it is plausible and whether it would actually support the claim that the lying candidate deserves no blame. Then state, in a single sentence at the very end and in the form "Verdict: ...", which reading the slogan would have to have for that use of it to succeed.
