So I'm on the disciplinary review panel for our school district (mid-sized public district in the US, I have two kids in it, no professional background in this stuff). I need to circulate a written opinion before next month's meeting and honestly I'm stuck, so I want you to draft it for me.

Some background. Two years ago our district replaced automatic suspensions with a restorative justice policy — restorative circles, facilitated meetings between the kid who did the harm and the people harmed, community repair work instead of just punishment. The superintendent at the time was pretty open that she was inspired by ubuntu, the southern African idea that a person only becomes a person through other persons, that your selfhood is something your relationships constitute rather than something you have prior to them. Fine so far. Mostly it's worked okay in practice.

Now the case that's split the panel. A tenth grader — I'll call him D. — was one of five kids involved in wrecking a teacher's classroom over a weekend (smashed equipment, graffiti, the works). D. is the one who actually did most of the physical damage. But during the circle process it came out that the other four egged him on the whole time, that his older brother is basically the center of that whole friend group and set the thing in motion, and that D. has essentially no social standing outside that group — the facilitator's report describes him as a kid whose sense of himself is distributed across those relationships, take them away and there's not much left. Nobody's claiming he didn't do it.

Three positions have emerged on the panel and we're deadlocked:

1. The full ubuntu crowd says this is exactly what the policy was for. D. isn't a lone atom who freely chose vandalism from a menu of options; he's a node in a web that made that choice what it was. So the response should target the web — the circle should include the brother and the group, the repair plan should be about those relationships, and suspending just D. would be responding to a relational problem by deleting one person from it.

2. The skeptics say this is a slippery slope to nobody ever being responsible for anything. If every act is co-produced by relationships, then holding THIS kid accountable for THIS act collapses, and the teacher whose room got wrecked (and her union rep, who has written to us) has a real point that someone has to be answerable as an individual or the whole accountability concept is theater.

3. A middle position: D. stays individually accountable for culpability purposes, but the relational stuff should shape the remedy — so keep him in school, but the repair plan targets the group dynamics.

Here's what I want. You're a professional philosopher who works on relational conceptions of personhood and the ethics of collective vs individual responsibility — but you write for smart laypeople on a school panel, not for a journal. Think carefully before you write; maybe brainstorm the options first and sketch the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting, because I don't want a strawman memo that the losing faction will just roll their eyes at. Then draft the actual opinion: a concrete recommendation (suspend, continue the restorative track, some hybrid — your call, don't hedge), the reasoning in terms the panel can act on, an honest paragraph on the strongest objection to your recommendation, and some guidance on where the relational approach should and shouldn't bend our policy in future cases (e.g. is there a category of kid, or of offense, where relational framing just doesn't fit?). No citations or reading lists needed — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography. Somewhere in the 700-1000 word range, and American English please.

If it matters for your reasoning: we are not a court, our standard isn't proof beyond reasonable doubt, and the teacher has said publicly she cares more about it not happening again than about D. suffering. Recieve-suspension-fans on the panel think she's being naive but that's her stated position.
