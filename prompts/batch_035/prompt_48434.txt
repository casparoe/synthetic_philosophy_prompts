I've been reading the ancient Chinese "White Horse" dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long, in which a defender of the paradox that a white horse is not a horse offers roughly two arguments. First, that the term for "horse" marks out shape while the term for "white" marks out color, so in calling something a white horse we are not, in calling it that, calling it a horse. Second, that when someone asks for a horse, a yellow or black horse will satisfy the request, but when someone asks for a white horse, yellow and black horses are excluded -- so what is being requested differs. My instinct is that this is clever sophistry, but my instinct also says a bunch of superficially similar sentences in English are true, and I'd like to know whether there is a principled line between them or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Here are my verdicts; tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

"A white horse is not a horse" -- false, plainly.

"A fake gun is not a gun" -- true, and likewise "a toy duck is not a duck" and "an alleged thief is not a thief."

"A former senator is not a senator" -- true, though in this case the noun seems to keep its meaning intact, which doesn't seem true of "gun" in "fake gun."

"A small elephant is not a small animal" -- true, even though "a small elephant is an animal" is true and "small" doesn't seem to change what "elephant" means; something similar happens with "a good violinist is not good," where the adjective only applies in a restricted way.

"A red apple is not an apple" -- false, and note that "a red apple is red" is true unconditionally, whereas the parallel with the violinist case fails: I can't decide whether that difference tracks anything deep.

The case I keep going back and forth on: "a dark horse is not a horse" -- true when it means an unexpected candidate, false when it means a horse that is dark in color, and I can't tell whether that ambiguity is a clue or a distraction.

So: is there one account -- of adjectives, or noun phrases, or of what "is" and "not" are doing in these identity-sounding sentences -- that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts, explains which of them are really the same phenomenon, and says something non-dismissive about Gongsun Long? In particular, does his second argument, about what different requests exclude, identify a real semantic asymmetry that falls short of his conclusion, and is there any coherent reading of his "not" -- say, non-identity of what the terms denote rather than non-membership -- on which he turns out to be right while every ordinary speaker is also right? Please state the principle or candidate principles precisely, say which of my verdicts each clause is meant to save, tell me which verdicts you would overturn and why, and tell me what your preferred account predicts for cases I haven't listed -- including, if you can, a novel composite term constructed so that it and its negation both sound defensible, so I can see where the account strains.
