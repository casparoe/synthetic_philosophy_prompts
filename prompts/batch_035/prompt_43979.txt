A guy in my book club, big Isaiah Berlin fan, gave me this run-down of republicanism in political philosophy after I mentioned I'd been reading about it: "Berlin basically settled the liberty debate in the 50s — there's negative liberty (nobody stops you) and positive liberty (self-mastery), and the second one leads straight to tyranny because it lets governments force you to be free for your own good. Then Pettit and Skinner came along in the 90s to rebrand negative liberty. Their idea is that you're only free when nobody interferes with you — same as Berlin's negative liberty, just stricter. Where Berlin counts only actual interference, they count potential interference too, so it's basically negative liberty plus a nervous disposition. The one thought experiment they run is the slave with a kindly master: because the master could interfere at any moment, the slave counts as unfree even on good days. But that just shows the whole thing is incoherent — a slave with a kind master is obviously better off than a wage worker with a vindictive boss, yet on their view the slave is the unfree one. And since republicans also say that laws somehow don't count as interference, the theory ends up saying freedom is whatever the laws permit, which is exactly the road to tyranny Berlin warned about. So it's either negative liberty with extra steps, or it collapses into the thing Berlin already refuted."

I'm inclined to think he's being too quick somewhere, but I honestly couldn't say where. How much of this would someone who actually works on this stuff accept? Go bit by bit: what's accurate, what's subtly off (I've heard people insist the domination vs interference distinction matters here but I don't really get it), and whether his "either/or" conclusion at the end survives once you fix the parts that are wrong. Don't be shy about telling me where he has a point either — the slave-vs-bad-boss comparison seems like it should bite somehow.
