I'm giving a talk at a psychiatry department's research seminar next week defending a Dennettian line: the self isn't a thing in the brain but a center of narrative gravity, a posit the interpretive system generates because it makes prediction and explanation of behavior tractable, sort of like a center of mass in physics. My audience is mostly clinicians and clinical researchers — two of them work with dissociative identity disorder patients and are themselves split on whether that's literally many selves or one confused self, one runs a lab on narrative identity in depression (she thinks the story-telling framing is basically right but that calling it a "posit" ruins it), and there's a neuroscientist who's already told me the whole thing is untestable fluff. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this crowd will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary not a philosopher's, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. I especially want to be ready for the DID question — my own line is that multiple centers of narrative gravity in one body is no stranger than multiple centers of mass in one object, but I suspect that reply cuts both ways once they start asking about which center should control treatment decisions. And include the one I'm hoping nobody asks: whether a merely posited self can meaningfully consent to treatment, or stand trial, or be lied to.
