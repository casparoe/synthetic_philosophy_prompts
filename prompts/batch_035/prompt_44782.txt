I teach a 200-level phil of science course at a state school, ~18 students, one 75-minute session, and next week we hit the interpretations of probability. I'm tired of lecturing this one — I want a game where they run into the problem before I name it. My idea: the students become bookmakers. They have to post odds on a list of events, and the other students can accept or refuse the bets, with points instead of money. The trick is the event list. I want it to mix repeatable setups (a die under a cup, a handful of change dumped on the table), one-off physical events you can't practically rerun (whether my coffee is cold by the end of class, whether the squeaky chair in row three squeaks when a specific student sits down), and then some genuinely unrepeatable things like rain on graduation day in 2047 or a specific bridge failing in the next decade. If the odds have to be posted before anything resolves, they should hit the moment where the frequency story quietly runs out — there's no long run for this coin, it gets flipped once — and they either lean on the physical setup or on their own willingness to bet, without knowing those are the three options. Please design the whole thing: rules, the actual event list (you can tweak mine, the more concrete the better), the scoring so it doesn't need real money but still rewards honest odds, a rough timeline, and the debrief. In the debrief I want the three positions to get named only at the end, and each named as an explanation of a thing the students already did in the room. Two extras. First, build in at least one event where the frequency answer and the propensity answer would actually come apart — I have a vague thought about a biased-looking physical device versus its observed track record, but I can't quite make it clean, so you do it. Second, tell me honestly what this kind of betting game cannot reproduce about the debate. My suspicion is it's the normative part — why degrees of belief *should* satisfy the probability axioms, Dutch books and so on — since nothing in the room punishes incoherent bookmakers unless you build that in, but tell me if I'm wrong or if the game can partially capture it. And predict what will go sideways the first time I run it — I'm guessing someone posts absurd odds on the one-off events for laughs and I need a way to absorb that. Assume no calculators and no actual gambling adminstrator skills on my part.
