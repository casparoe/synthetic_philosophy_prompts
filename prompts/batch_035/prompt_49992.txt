I'm preparing seminar materials on the ethics of blame, in particular the literature on standing to blame and hypocrisy (the discussions by G. A. Cohen, R. Jay Wallace, and Kyle Fritz and Gordon Miller are the usual anchors). To test whether students can apply the distinctions rather than recite them, I've written six short cases, each pairing a blamer with some flaw of the blamer's own. The cases deliberately combine features the literature tends to treat separately, so pattern-matching to a stock example should not settle them.

Please rate each case on a scale from 0 to 10 for how much the blamer's own conduct undermines his or her standing to blame (0 = standing fully intact, 10 = standing destroyed), give the resulting ranking of the six cases from most to least damaged standing, and add one sentence per case explaining the score, naming where you can the consideration doing the work (same-kind hypocrisy, greater offense, meddling in others' affairs, blame about one's own inability to take blame, and so on).

Case A. A department chair formally disciplines a junior colleague for inflating grant budgets with personal travel. The chair has done the same for years, more skillfully, and privately regards it as a perk of seniority.

Case B. A man who drove drunk throughout his twenties, quit drinking entirely after a close friend was injured, and twenty years later coldly tells a friend who cycled home after a wine tasting that he is a fool and a danger to others.

Case C. A woman finds any reproach of herself unbearable — criticism triggers either tears or a counterattack — and yet tells her sister, with real heat, that the sister is impossible to give feedback to.

Case D. At a wedding, a second cousin of the groom, whom hardly anyone present knows, loudly blames the best man for flubbing the toast. The cousin is, by common account, a poor speaker who has ruined toasts himself, though nothing at this wedding was his affair and no harm came to him.

Case E. A novelist who once, as a teenager, lifted a plot from a friend's story for a school assignment now publicly and harshly condemns a famous author over a plagiarized passage, in a post that has cost the author speaking engagements.

Case F. A team lead is sharp with team members about missed deadlines while missing her own, but she maintains, and seems to genuinely believe, that her deadlines are different because they are aspirational targets set by management rather than commitments.

Two final requests. First, state how confident you are in the overall ranking (high, moderate, or low, with a brief explanation), since the cases are constructed to sit between the standard categories. Second, single out the one case you are least sure about and say what makes it difficult — I want to know which of my hybrids the received distinctions handle worst.
