The exam question was:

State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem from his 1955 paper. Specify its assumptions: which expected-utility assumptions hold, on whose preferences, and over which lotteries; the Pareto condition that connects individual and social preferences; what the theorem concludes; and what further assumption is needed to obtain equal weights for all individuals. Distinguish the aggregation theorem from the impartial-observer argument given in the same paper. Finally, explain what the von Neumann–Morgenstern framework delivers and what it does not, and why critics hold that the theorem does not by itself establish utilitarianism as a moral doctrine.

The student's answer, in full:

Harsanyi's 1955 paper contains two arguments, but the aggregation theorem and the impartial-observer argument are really the same argument stated twice. The setup is this: society faces lotteries over social outcomes, and each individual's preferences over these lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms. The social planner also has vNM preferences over the same lotteries; since the planner is situated behind a veil of ignorance, with an equal chance of turning out to be any member of society, the theorem applies a Pareto condition: whenever every individual prefers lottery p to lottery q, the planner prefers p to q as well. From these assumptions it follows that social utility must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, the weights being strictly positive. Harsanyi then adds that the weights ought to be equal, and this yields the classical utilitarian sum. What makes the addition possible is that vNM utility is cardinal: because each person's utility is measured on a cardinal scale, everyone's utilities can be placed on a common scale and summed. The upshot is that the theorem establishes utilitarianism, and thereby refutes egalitarianism, since no egalitarian social welfare function could satisfy the vNM rationality axioms.
