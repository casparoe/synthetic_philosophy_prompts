i cover science and ideas for a magazine, and im working on a piece about how the definition of death quietly changed once ventilators made the old signs unreliable. basically everywhere in the West now, the law says you're dead when all functions of the whole brain, brainstem included, have irreversibly stopped -- even though on the ventilator the heart keeps beating, cells keep metabolizing, wounds keep healing. the body is, in a lot of ways, still biologically alive, and that's the window in which organ procurement happens. so what does this legal practice presuppose about what a person is and what death is? it looks functionalist: the organism counts as dead when the part that integrates the rest stops, as if the person is a certain kind of functional organization rather than a living body. but then it gets weird -- if integration is what matters, why does it matter that the brain is what realizes it? if machines someday held the body's integration together, would the patient still be dead? and if you say the person is really the brain, that sounds like the higher-brain view people say counts consciousness as what matters -- which is exactly what the whole-brain rule refuses to require. so: reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, tell me whether it's one coherent position or a compromise several theories could live with, and be upfront about where your reconstruction is most likely to fail -- what would actually change your mind about it? keep it at a level my general readers can follow, and flag it if you're leaning on a contested reading somewhere.
