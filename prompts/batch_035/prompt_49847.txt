For the last two years my brother-in-law and I have been having the same argument at every family gathering, and I've realized we are each leaning on a comparison that neither of us has ever actually examined. I have no background in philosophy — everything I know about this comes from arguing with him and from reading encyclopedia entries, so please correct my terminology where it is off. I gather our disagreement is roughly the one between communitarians and liberals, but the philosophical labels matter less to me than whether either comparison can carry the weight we're putting on it.

His comparison: a nation is like a family. No one chooses their family, membership is not up for renegotiation, obligations run deeper than any contract, the older generation legitimately shapes the younger generation's way of life, and loyalty matters more than tallying credits and debits. From this he concludes that liberal neutrality — the state standing impartially among its citizens' conceptions of the good life — is a fiction: a family that tried to be a neutral referee among its members' values would not be a better family but a failing one, and a nation whose state refuses to promote a shared way of life is not neutral but dying. On his picture individual rights survive, but as house rules: real, yet defeasible when the family's good requires it.

My counter-comparison: the political community is like an association — a club or a housing cooperative — whose members join (or are born into but can leave), whose purposes they negotiate, and whose officials are hired staff rather than parents. From this I conclude the state must be a neutral umpire among strangers, justifying its rules to each member on terms all could accept.

What I want is an audit of both. Then, separately, tell me what each comparison smuggles in without argument — the family seems to import hierarchy, unconditional obligation, and a shared good that outranks the members; the association seems to import consent and a real option of exit, both of which look doubtful for a membership acquired at birth — and whether his and my conclusions survive once the failed rows are deleted, or whether each comparison was doing all the work. Finally, my niece proposed a third comparison: that belonging to a nation is like having a mother tongue — inherited rather than chosen, and shaping what you can even think to say, yet dictating no particular sentence and owed no obedience. Assess whether that one maps better than either of ours, and if not, say what comparison you would put in its place.
