I've been reading about pain asymbolia, the condition where people report feeling pain (they can locate it, tell you how intense it is, and even flinch at the stimulus) but claim it doesn't bother them at all, and I've come to hold a fairly strong view about what it shows: that the badness of pain is not part of the sensation itself but lives entirely in a separable distress-reaction attached to it, so that pain minus the distress is no longer bad for you, and there is nothing left that is intrinsically worth avoiding.

What would count against this position? I'd like to know which observations, arguments, or possible cases it stands or falls with: for instance, what would have to be true about what asymbolics are actually reporting for the separability claim to fail, whether someone could show that the "indifferent" pain is bad in some way the asymbolics themselves can't register, and whether the view collapses if we imagine future drugs that remove distress while leaving the full sensation. Please distinguish objections that a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it from ones they would dismiss, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- and if it has, whether that is a real problem for a view of this kind or just what we should expect from a claim about how pain matters to us.
