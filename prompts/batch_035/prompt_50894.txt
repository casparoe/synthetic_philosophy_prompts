I serve as one of the trustees of a mid-sized charitable foundation — about $4 million under management, all of it donations from a few hundred small donors in our region — and I've been asked to write the written opinion our board will vote on next month. The case has turned into the hardest question I've ever chewed on, and I want the memo to take it seriously rather than route around it.

Our treasurer, who founded the foundation twenty years ago and is personally beloved, has spent two years telling donors in our annual letters and at every event that the endowment was "on solid ground" and that our grant-making was fully funded. The books now show otherwise: we have been spending down principal at about 8% a year, and by the time our new auditor caught it, roughly a third of the endowment was gone. Here is the part that has me up at night. The treasurer had the quarterly statements the whole time. Several board members recall him leaving meetings early on days the investment report was on the agenda, and one remembers him saying he "doesn't like to look at the portfolio when the markets are ugly." When confronted, he didn't deny the facts, and he didn't confess to hiding them. He said, with what looked to me like genuine anguish, that he had believed with everything in him that the endowment would recover, that he'd convinced himself the drawdown was temporary, and that he'd never once said a word he knew to be false. One trustee wants us to treat this as straightforward dishonesty — suspend him, refer the matter to our regulators, and tell donors he misled them. Another wants to treat it as an honest mistake by a fading man who couldn't bear the truth, accept his resignation with thanks, and move on. Neither side has done the work of saying what self-deception actually is or how much it excuses.

I have no philosophy training — the closest I've come is arguing about this at my own kitchen table — but I keep circling one puzzle and I'd like the memo to face it head on rather than bury it. If the treasurer genuinely believed the endowment was fine, then what was steering him away from the statements on the ugly days? Something in him must have known, or at least suspected, or he would have just opened the envelopes like the rest of us open ours. But if he knew, then his reassurances to donors weren't the innocent words of a man with a false belief — they were the words of a man managing his own knowledge. It seems like he has to both know and not know at the same time, and I can't decide whether that's possible, whether it's just a fancy name for ordinary lying that we've let ourselves be talked into, or whether the truth is something in between that neither side on our board has words for. And whichever it is, I need to know what follows for what we owe him, what we owe our donors, and what we say in the letter to them.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — I need specifics and time frames: what happens to the treasurer and his title, whether anything goes to our regulators, whether we treat any of the loss as his personal responsibility, and what our donors' letter says about how this happened — and the reasoning has to actually engage the self-deception question rather than just asserting a conclusion about his character. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side of the vote whatever its real weight; the trustees on that side will be reading to see whether they were answered or just outvoted.
