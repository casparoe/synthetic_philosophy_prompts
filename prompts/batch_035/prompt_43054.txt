I'm finishing a position memo for a behavioral genetics seminar — the assignment is to state what heritability does and does not measure, say where the concept gets abused, and commit ourselves on a few live issues — and before I submit I'd like an outside check on whether my commitments actually hang together. Here is the package I plan to defend: (1) Heritability is a property of populations, not of individuals: it is the proportion of trait variance attributable to genetic variance within one specific population living under one specific range of environments, and it is a category error to say that 70% of some particular person's IQ is due to their genes. (2) The twin and adoption literature is broadly trustworthy, and the best estimate is that IQ in contemporary Western populations is somewhere between 50% and 80% heritable. (3) If we genuinely equalized environments — universal adequate nutrition, decent schooling for everyone, no lead exposure, and so on — the heritability of IQ would climb toward 100%, because the environmental contribution to variance would shrink even as everyone's IQ rose. (4) All the same, high heritability should temper our expectations about interventions: a trait that is highly heritable in a population is, other things being equal, harder to shift with policy, so it makes sense to direct intervention resources toward traits with lower heritability. (5) The Flynn effect — the enormous IQ gains registered within two or three generations in the same countries — is among our strongest evidence that IQ is environmentally quite malleable after all. (6) Adoption studies indicate that the shared environment contributes almost nothing to adult IQ, so I'm skeptical that differences in school quality or parenting style within the normal range matter much, though I do accept that children raised under severe deprivation can suffer lasting cognitive deficits.

Please audit the package rather than lecture on heritability in general. Tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity, and for each genuine one say what would have to give and what conceding it would cost me elsewhere in the memo. Note that some of my phrasing includes escape hatches like other things being equal — flag whether those actually save me or just paper over the problem. Then identify the single cheapest revision, the one change that would clean up the most. Conclude with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the number of the one commitment I should revise first, or the word none if you judge the package consistent as it stands.
