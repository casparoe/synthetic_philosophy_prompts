I teach a graduate seminar called The Ethics of Agreement, and we spend the first half of the term on the question of why promises bind: the consent-based view that my present will can somehow legislate for my future self, Hume's treatment of promises as an artificial contrivance that gets its force from convention, Rawls's practice-based account in Two Concepts of Rules, Scanlon's assurance-based view in What We Owe to Each Other, and Kant's argument that a maxim of false promising somehow undermines itself. For the final session of the unit I want to assign The Merchant of Venice rather than another article, and I'd like your help thinking through how to teach it. My students know the arguments cold but tend to treat promising as a tidy puzzle, and I suspect the play could show them how strange and unstable the phenomenon actually is.

The play is saturated with promising. Antonio enters the pound-of-flesh bond in what Shylock explicitly frames as a joke, a piece of merry sport — a promise made in play, binding in deadly earnest once circumstances change. The suitors at Belmont must swear a strange conditional oath before choosing a casket: if they choose wrong, they may never marry at all and must never reveal which casket they picked, and Morocco and Arragon pay this forfeit without any enforcement mechanism in sight. The trial scene gives us Shylock insisting on strict performance of his bond while Portia dismantles that literalism with an even fiercer literalism — the pound of flesh is owed, but not one drop of blood — and then, having refused to revoke the bond's letter, the state promptly overrides its substance anyway, stripping Shylock of his goods and forcing his conversion. And the whole thing is wrapped in the ring plot: Bassanio swears to Portia that he will never part with her ring, then gives it away under the pressure of a debt he cannot honor otherwise, and is forgiven through a mercy whose theatricality Portia herself arranged.

Here is what I want the essay to do: read the play as a sustained argument about where promissory obligation comes from, testing each theory against particular scenes rather than against each other in the abstract. Does the trial scene vindicate the Hobbesian thought that covenants are only as strong as the sovereign who enforces them — or does it rather show that the letter of a promise and its obligatoriness can come apart, so that neither Shylock nor the Venetian court actually locates the moral core of the bond? Does the casket oath, honored by losers who no one could compel, fit Hume's conventionalism, which struggles to explain why anyone would keep such a promise when the practice itself is not at stake? Does Bassanio's broken ring-oath, forgiven and then weaponized in Portia's test, support Scanlon's idea that what matters is the assurance given to a particular person rather than fidelity to an institution? And is the play aware that the one character who treats a promise as absolutely binding, Shylock, is also the one whose word the Christian characters break whenever it inconveniences them? I'd like you to be honest about where the play resists a tidy reading — it may well dramatize several incompatible views at once — and to end by telling me what The Merchant of Venice adds to this debate that Hume, Kant, Rawls, and Scanlon do not, since that is what I will ask my students in the seminar's closing hour.
