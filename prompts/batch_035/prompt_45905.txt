I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade — long enough that "the map is not the territory" feels less like a slogan and more like a reflex, and long enough to have absorbed the community's standing suspicion of any theory of truth that isn't some form of correspondence. So I felt a certain satisfaction last month when a popular history of American philosophy I picked up dismissed the pragmatist theory of truth in a single paragraph, roughly as follows.

Pragmatism, the book explains, was invented by William James, who held that a belief is true when it works — when believing it pays off for the believer. Truth, on this account, is like the solvency of a business: it lasts exactly as long as the returns keep coming in. James's friend Charles Sanders Peirce extended the idea with a formula of his own — that truth is the belief which all who investigate would ultimately converge on — but the book assures us this amounts to the same thing, since what everyone eventually settles on is simply whatever works best. The upshot is that truth is relative to the individual: if faith in God comforts you and your life goes better for it, the belief is true for you. Bertrand Russell is then credited with ending the whole debate by asking whether a belief that your sweetheart loves you becomes true merely because it is pleasant to hold. Since useful beliefs can plainly be false, the theory was dead on arrival; even Peirce gave up on it, renaming his own position with a term so ugly, he explained, that no one would want to steal it, and turning to a more serious theory of reality.

The paragraph felt satisfying, which is precisely what makes me suspicious. In our circles James functions as a cautionary tale — the man who confused truth with wishful thinking — and this paragraph reads like the cached version of that tale. Before I file it away as confirmed, I'd like a proper audit. Please go claim by claim: which parts would James have accepted, which parts would Peirce have objected to, where exactly does the book run the two philosophers together, and does the relativist conclusion — true for you, not for me — actually follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate? I'd also like your judgment on the rhetorical move at the end: is "useful beliefs can be false" a refutation of the theory, or an objection the theory was built to answer, and how would a defender of the view reply? Finally, one thing that has been nagging me: Peirce's convergence formula sounds oddly like the rationalist picture of beliefs settling under accumulating evidence — is that a genuine precursor, or am I pattern-matching across a century and should know better?
