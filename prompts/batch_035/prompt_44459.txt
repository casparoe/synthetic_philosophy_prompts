I'm on the student ethics committee at my med school, and last week we discussed a case about a woman with advanced Alzheimer's whose advance directive from fifteen years earlier says no feeding tube under these circumstances, while the patient now, in her lucid-ish moments, seems content and occasionally says she wants to keep going. One of the attendings said this is exactly Ulysses and the sirens: she tied herself to the mast back when she could still think clearly, and the fact that she now says otherwise is just the sirens singing -- the whole point of tying yourself to the mast is that your later shouts dont count. Apparently psychiatric advance directives even get called Ulysses contracts in the literature, so the analogy has real currency and isnt some one-off rhetorical flourish. He wasn't using it casually either: it was doing actual argumentative work for the conclusion that the directive should be followed to the letter and that the family's reports of her current wishes should carry zero weight, not just reduced weight.

I want an audit of that analogy specifically, not a rerun of the general advance-directive debate. Lay out the correspondence table: who or what plays Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the sailors with wax in their ears, the sirens, and the voyage home. Then tell me which disanalogies are load-bearing and which are just noise. Some that occur to me: Ulysses knew precisely what temptation was coming and what he'd want once he was past it, while an advance-directive author is writing for a future self whose interests and even personality she can't fully anticipate; the sirens temporarily changed Ulysses's desires while leaving his underlying values intact, whereas dementia may erode the values themselves; and the sailors in the story had standing orders to ignore Ulysses no matter what, which seems to smuggle in the exact conclusion the attending wanted (the later self has no standing at all) rather than deriving it. There may also be a disanalogy running the other way worth flagging -- Ulysses presumably untied himself once the danger passed, so maybe the story implies some kind of sunset clause rather than permanent binding.

So, three deliverables: the mapping, the disanalogy triage (which ones actually matter for the use the attending put it to, and which ones I can concede without losing anything), and then the verdict -- once the breakdowns are on the table, does the mast conclusion survive, or does it only survive for a stripped-down version of the analogy where much less follows about ignoring the current patient completely? And if the Ulysses picture is the wrong frame, is there a better comparison hiding in the case -- like leaving instructions for a stranger who will later be standing in your place -- and what would that one imply about how much weight her present-day wishes should get versus the document? Please answer in American English.
