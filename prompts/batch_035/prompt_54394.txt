I'm a graduate student working through the free will literature for a paper I'm struggling to scope, and I'd like your help diagnosing why the compatibilism/incompatibilism debate seems to have reached a stable stalemate rather than a resolution. Here is the pattern I keep noticing, and I want you to tell me whether my reading of it is right. Frankfurt-style cases against the principle of alternate possibilities were supposed to settle something, but incompatibilists who accept them simply relocate the source of concern to the actual sequence — and when manipulation arguments (like Pereboom's series of cases from designed agents to ordinary determined ones) press the compatibilist back toward origination, the compatibilist camp splits rather than converts: one faction bites the bullet and says the manipulated agent acts freely, another hunts for a relevant difference between manipulation and ordinary determination. Nobody on either side seems to lose, and nobody seems to win. Both camps also claim the intuitions of ordinary people support them, and the experimental work I've seen waved around cuts both ways.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a single disagreement at all: the compatibilist is answering something like a normative question about when an agent is an appropriate target of the reactive attitudes and what makes blame and praise intelligible practices, while the incompatibilist is answering a metaphysical question about the origins of actions in a determined (or indetermined) world — and the phrase "free will" glues the two questions together in a way that makes each side's answers look like non sequiturs to the other. Alternatively, maybe it's a bedrock intuition about desert that neither side can argue for, or maybe it's a genuine substantive disagreement about the concept of agency that simply is hard. I'd like a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions — though you should engage the concrete moves I mentioned, the bullet-biting asymmetry on Frankfurt cases and manipulation cases especially, since that asymmetry strikes me as the most telling data point. I'd also like your honest view about what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a decisive thought experiment, empirical results about how agents actually deliberate, a change in the question, or nothing at all and why.

Please respond as a professional philosopher specializing in agency and moral psychology, and think carefully about the structure of the disagreement before committing to a diagnosis.
