I'm taking a graduate seminar on rational choice and distributive ethics, and I've come out of our unit on Harsanyi genuinely persuaded of what I gather is a minority position: that the aggregation theorem really does settle things in favor of utilitarianism, or something structurally very close to it. As I understand the theorem, if each individual's preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms over lotteries, the social ordering satisfies them as well, and Pareto holds, then the social ordering must be an increasing affine function of individual vNM utilities — a weighted sum. When I first encountered it I assumed there had to be a loophole, but every escape route we studied struck me as either desperate or as smuggling in an independent moral commitment and presenting it as a formal objection. Rejecting expected utility theory for the social observer seems to cost far too much elsewhere; allowing unequal weights seems to abandon impartiality, which is the whole point of the exercise; and denying that the individual vNM numbers represent anything morally significant — saying they track attitudes to risk rather than intensities of well-being — strikes me as wordplay, since the theorem never needed them to be well-being in the first place. So I now hold, roughly, that consistency under risk plus Pareto plus rational benevolence forces additive aggregation, and that the burden falls on egalitarians and priorititarians to explain which premise fails and why we should care.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want the potential defeaters laid out and ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward: the Diamond-style fairness cases where a social planner must choose between two identically situated people and utilitarianism is indifferent between flipping a coin and just picking, which seems wrong in a way that matters; the charge that vNM utilities, being unique only up to positive affine transformation, are the wrong kind of quantity to be summing across persons in the first place; the possibility of relaxing completeness or continuity for the social ordering rather than accepting the full vNM package; the prioritarian move of making social welfare a concave transform of individual utilities, which seems to require giving up expected utility at the social level and might or might not be able to survive that. For each, I want to know whether a committed defender of the theorem's significance would concede it as a genuine cost or brush it aside, and where the honest dividing line falls. Finally, tell me candidly whether the position I've ended up with has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — if every counterexample can be met by retreating to a weaker reading of what the theorem establishes, does the thesis still have content, and is that insulation a worse problem for a formal result deployed in ethics than it would be for the same result inside pure decision theory?
