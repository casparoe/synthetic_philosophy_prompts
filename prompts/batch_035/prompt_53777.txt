The Cartesian theater is the old idea, going back to Descartes and his claim that the mind meets the body in the pineal gland, that there has to be some one place in the brain where all the signals arrive and consciousness officially happens. Dennett's point is that neuroscience has shown there's no such place: processing is distributed and parallel, nothing gets watched by an inner spectator. Instead, the brain is constantly writing and rewriting drafts of experience, and when it needs to, it goes back and edits the record after the fact. That's what the famous experiment with the two flashing dots shows — when one red dot and one green dot are flashed in quick succession, people see a single dot moving across and changing color halfway through, even though there was never a dot in the middle. So the brain clearly rewrites the tape to make experience seem smooth, which means consciousness lags behind reality, maybe by up to half a second like those famous timing experiments showed, and what we're conscious of is always the edited version. And that's why Dennett concludes that consciousness, as we ordinarily think of it, doesn't exist — the inner show was an illusion all along, so we're basically running on autopilot and there's nobody home; there's no fact of the matter about what anyone experienced.
