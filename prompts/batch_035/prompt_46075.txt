For a philosophy of mind seminar I'm writing on Dennett's account of the self, I'd like a close reading of Christopher Nolan's *Memento* (2000) through that account — specifically the idea that the self is a "center of narrative gravity": not a thing in the brain but an abstract, posited center, like a center of mass, around which the brain's narratives organize themselves, and whose reality consists in the explanatory work the posit does. The fit seems almost too good: Leonard Shelby has lost the ability to form new declarative memories, so his self has to be sustained entirely by external narrative scaffolding — Polaroids with captions, notes, tattoos, a police file. It looks like the film experimentally isolates the narrative self from everything else and shows it continuing to run.

But I want the reading to be honest in both directions, so please take the complications seriously rather than treating the film as an illustration. Several places where I suspect it resists:

1. Dennett's line of thought includes the claim that for the most part we don't spin our tales; they spin us — the narrative self is mostly not self-authored. Leonard is the reverse: because he can choose what to tattoo and what caption to write on a Polaroid, his center of narrative gravity is deliberately authored, moment by moment, by someone who will not remember authoring it. Is that a vindication of Dennett (the self really is just a narrative construction, and here you can watch it being constructed) or a counterexample (a self can be engineered, which suggests something doing the engineering that isn't itself just narrative)? When Leonard, in the final scene, plants a clue he knows is misleading so that his future self will have a mission, who is lying to whom, and is there any fact of the matter?

2. The film suggests that Leonard's conditioning and emotional memory survive even where his declarative memory fails — his body remembers his wife in ways his story can't accommodate, and the Sammy Jankis story may be a narrative vehicle for a trauma he cannot narrate as his own. If some of what matters to Leonard persists outside the narrative, doesn't that push back on a purely narrative account of the self, or does Dennett have room for this in his model of parallel, multi-draft processing with no single privileged stream?

3. Teddy's taunt that Leonard invents the story of Sammy to make himself feel better, and the shot of Leonard himself in the place his memory assigns to Sammy, raise the question of whether Leonard's pre-injury self and post-injury self are the same center of gravity at all. Dennett's account is supposed to make personal identity questions tractable in terms of narrative continuity; does the film's twist structure (played in reverse, so the audience reconstructs Leonard's self the way he does — from artifacts backwards) actually support that, or does it leave the identity question genuinely open?
