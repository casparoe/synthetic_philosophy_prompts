I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital. Next month I am giving the opening address at a day-long workshop our committee has convened on a pattern our staff have started calling the "contented patient problem": a patient executes an advance directive while fully lucid — often shortly after watching a parent decline with dementia — instructing that certain treatments be withheld if she ever reaches a specified stage of impairment, and then, having reached that stage, appears settled and even content, taking pleasure in small daily routines and mildly accepting the very treatments the directive forbids. Our nurses and palliative-care physicians tend to think that the patient's evident present comfort should count heavily against enforcing the document; our lawyer regards the directive as a binding exercise of autonomy that the hospital ignores at its legal peril; and two philosophers in attendance will want to press the thought, familiar from Dworkin, that the directive's authority rests on the patient's interest in her life going a certain way as a whole rather than on her moment-to-moment experience. In the address I want to develop a framing of my own: that the earlier self is best understood neither as a sovereign whose written word must be obeyed whatever happens, nor as a mere adviser whose instructions evaporate on contact with the patient's present contentment, but as something like a fiduciary — a trustee whose standing instructions deserve great weight precisely because they were adopted from a position of overview, yet remain open to reconsideration when the beneficiary's circumstances change in ways the settlor could not have foreseen. If that framing works, it should let the committee say both that the directive matters a great deal and that it is not the end of the conversation — which is, frankly, the position we already occupy without a theory to justify it. Please write the address, roughly five minutes when spoken, in British English. It should be comprehensible to the clinicians without condescending to the philosophers, it should not pretend there is an easy reconciliation where our committee in fact faces a hard choice, and it should leave the audience with one or two concrete questions to carry into the small-group sessions that follow.
