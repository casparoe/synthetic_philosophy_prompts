Write an essay on the following puzzle, which I've been turning over for some time. The standard view seems to be that a disposition is just a matter of what would happen in the right circumstances: a glass is fragile because it would break if struck. But I've come across two kinds of cases that seem to break this simple analysis. In one kind, something outside the object intervenes when the circumstances arise: a vase would be struck, but before it can shatter, a sorcerer (or a protective coating, or a guardian angel) steps in and either makes the vase temporarily sturdy or absorbs the blow. In the other kind, the object has the disposition, and the circumstances for its manifestation are present, but something independent blocks the manifestation from occurring, like an antidote blocking a poison that is genuinely present in someone's bloodstream. My question is whether these cases really show that the "would" analysis of dispositions fails. Please weigh the argument that the analysis can be rescued, perhaps by hedging it (e.g., saying the object would break if struck and nothing interferes), against the argument that hedging is circular or vacuous, since the "nothing interferes" clause seems to smuggle in the very disposition concept we were trying to analyze. You might also consider whether the two kinds of case are importantly different: in the first, the object arguably stops having the disposition before the manifestation could occur, whereas in the second, the object plausibly has the disposition all along even though the manifestation never happens. Reach a definite conclusion: does the simple conditional analysis of dispositions survive, does it need a principled repair, or should it be abandoned?
