My study group in a biostatistics course spent last week on Simpson's paradox, using a dataset our instructor made up: a new drug, clotrase, versus standard care for a fictional condition, where clotrase looks better in aggregate until you stratify by severity — then standard care wins in both the mild and severe strata, and the aggregate edge was just an artifact of the mild cases being channeled toward the drug. Standard stuff. But one member of the group, a philosophy student who tags along for the free pizza, left unhappy and came back this week with an argument he says he got from a skeptical epidemiologist blogger, and I can't find the seam. It goes: (1) Simpson reversals show that the sign of any association between two variables depends on whether, and how, one aggregates over a third. (2) For any dataset there are indefinitely many candidate partitions — measured covariates, unmeasured covariates, even silly ones like surnames or birth months — and for all we know the association we observe reverses under some partition we haven't tried, including partitions by variables we never measured and never will. (3) So from the data alone we can never rule out that the association actually runs the other way. (4) If we can't rule that out, then acting on the observed association — prescribing the drug, say — is unjustified. (5) Therefore no observational association should ever guide a treatment or policy decision, no matter how large the study.

What bothers me is that the argument seems to prove far too much, and in a way that should worry him more than it worries me. The disaggregated tables that display the paradox — clotrase better within each severity stratum — are themselves aggregates over finer partitions (severity plus sex, severity plus site, severity plus sex plus site, and so on), so his step (2) applies to them with exactly the force it applies to the pooled table. Run consistently, the argument doesn't just discredit observational causal inference; it discredits every contingency table in existence, including the ones the paradox itself is exhibited in, and leaves us unable to say anything about anything from data. He says fine, so much the worse for statistics, and adds that the point reaches randomized trials too: randomization balances covariates only in expectation, and pooled and subgroup estimates from trials are still aggregates, so meta-analyses fall with the rest. I have a suspicion the trick is somewhere between (2) and (4) — that there's a slide from "there exists some partition under which the sign flips" to "we could be wrong about which way the association runs," as if a descriptive reversal under an irrelevant partition were the same thing as evidence pointing the other way — but every time I try to say what the difference is, he asks me how I know which partitions are irrelevant, and I hear my answer relying on things that are not in the data, which is just what he predicted. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs — and please be honest about whether my instinct about the slide between (2) and (4) is right or whether the real flaw is earlier. For what it's worth, I'd like to keep (2) if I can: it feels like plain honesty about unmeasured confounders, and if the only way out is denying it, I want to know exactly what I'm giving up.
