I've read enough LessWrong-adjacent philosophy-of-language discussion that the Frege material is cached: "a = a" is available to anyone who grasps the sentence with no empirical work, while a true "a = b" can extend knowledge — Hesperus and Phosphorus, Cicero and Tully. My complaint is that every case in circulation runs the same direction: two expressions whose co-reference is news to the subject, so of course the identity is informative. I want a case engineered in the opposite direction, and my own attempts keep quietly collapsing back into the standard setup, so I'm outsourcing the construction. The point of the case is to pressure-test one specific account of the puzzle: the proposal that the informativeness of an identity statement just is the information that the two expressions flanking it co-refer. A scenario in which informativeness survives the total absence of metalinguistic news would leave that account with nothing to say, which is exactly the kind of gear-level stress I'm after rather than another round of pointing at Venus.

Build a scenario meeting all of the following. A subject S, on a single occasion, comes to accept a true identity "a = b", and the acceptance genuinely extends her knowledge: afterwards she can do something concrete she could not do before — settle a bet, make a decision, spend money. But the statement must carry no metalinguistic news whatsoever: S already believes, and if asked point-blank would immediately and sincerely assert, that the two expressions pick out the same thing. Constraints: mundane contemporary life; no amnesia, brainwashing, secret identities, or other genre devices; and the knowledge-extension must fall out of the scenario rather than be stipulated — tacking on "and this taught her something" is showing's failure mode, stipulation. One seed that may help: there's a familiar gap between knowing something well enough to use it and having it integrated where you need it — you can dial a phone number you would fail to recite.
