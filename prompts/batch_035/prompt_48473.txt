I'm on the committee at my university that administers a couple of small internal prizes for early-career researchers, and drafting our rules got me thinking about what the whole prize economy in science actually assumes. Look at the template we're all copying from, the Nobels: no more than three names can share a prize, the honored contribution is one discrete discovery per person (Einstein got it for the photoelectric effect, not relativity), the recipient generally has to be alive when it's announced — Ralph Steinman's 2011 medicine prize stood only because he died three days before the announcement and the committee hadn't known — and the grad students, postdocs, and people who built the instruments never show up on the citation. Meanwhile everyone on my committee knows that a lot of famous discoveries were made more or less simultaneously by different people: calculus, natural selection, and honestly most of the stuff in our own field, where three groups published basically the same method within about a year of each other. So what view of discovery and credit does prize-giving presuppose? Is it the lone-genius picture, where a discovery is the property of one particular mind and everyone else is a runner-up? Could you run the exact same prize practice on a view where discoveries are, to a first approximation, inevitable — "when the time is ripe somebody finds it" — and credit is just a coordination convention, like whose name goes on the street sign? Or does the practice need the individualist view somewhere deep in its guts?

There's a second layer that confuses me. Prizes also have a frankly utilitarian justification: they steer young researchers toward important problems, publicize science to the public, give people role models. That justification might be totally neutral about whether discoveries are inevitable or individual — it only needs prizes to *work* as incentives. So I genuinely can't tell whether the individualist bits of the practice are load-bearing or just inherited decoration that nobody's bothered to scrape off, and the Franklin case makes it feel like more than decoration: people still argue that she lost her shot at the 1962 DNA prize partly because she died in 1958 and the prize can't go to the dead, as if being alive at the award ceremony is part of what *earning* credit means. That mix of "it's about what you did" and "it's about who you are when we find out" is the apparent inconsistency I'd want untangled.

Reconstruct the implicit theory behind prize practice for me, and clearly separate what it strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Then work through the counterfactual: if we took the inevitability thesis seriously — discoveries as things the scientific community would have produced anyway, with individual credit as a convention we adopt for coordination reasons — what would have to change in how prizes are structured? Would the three-person cap still make sense, would we honor discoveries or persons, would the alive-at-announcement rule survive, and does the incentive argument get stronger or weaker?
