I've spent a week circling an argument my flatmate (a mathematics PhD student with finitist sympathies) laid out after our philosophy of mathematics seminar, and I can neither accept its conclusion nor find the step that betrays me. Here it is as he states it:

1. To count as a quantity, a property of collections must be additive: when A and B are disjoint, the quantity of A∪B equals the quantity of A plus the quantity of B, and adding a nonempty collection to another always increases the total.
2. The natural numbers can be partitioned into the even numbers and the odd numbers, two disjoint nonempty parts.
3. On Cantor's criterion of sameness of number — one-to-one correspondence — the naturals have the same number as the evens alone, since pairing each n with 2n matches them exactly.
4. So the number of the naturals equals the number of the evens, and hence also equals the number of the evens plus the number of the odds, even though the odds form a nonempty collection.
5. A notion of number on which adding something nonempty never increases the total is not additive in the sense demanded by (1).
6. So the natural numbers, though infinite, have no number that is a quantity; and since every infinite collection is equinumerous with some proper part of itself, the same holds of any actually infinite collection whatever.
7. If actual infinities exist, the points of a line segment, the moments of an infinite past, and the field values over any region of space are all such collections.
8. So if actual infinities exist, physics assigns quantities to domains that have none, and a physics that reports measurements where there is nothing to measure is incoherent.
9. Physics is coherent; therefore, no actual infinities exist.

My immediate worry is that the argument proves too much: a two-meter rod is equinumerous with one of its halves (pair each point with the point halfway toward one end), so by steps (4) through (6) a rod has no length either. When I pressed him on this, he said he accepts the consequence — the rod has a length because measure theory assigns it one, but measure theory does this by treating the rod as an actually infinite set of points, so if (9) is right, the length facts will have to be recovered some other way, and he regards that as a research program rather than a refutation. He also points out that I cannot simply retreat to saying the naturals "have a size but not a quantity," since step (6) then does no work and his conclusion follows anyway from the pairing in (3).

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I would like to keep the antecedent of (9) if I can — I want actual infinities, if only for the mathematics of analysis — and I also want to keep the intuition behind (1), that a genuine quantity does not stay flat when you add to it. If those two cannot both be held, tell me which to surrender and why it is the cheaper loss. And if some term is doing two different jobs across the chain, I want to know which term, and at exactly which step the switch happens.
