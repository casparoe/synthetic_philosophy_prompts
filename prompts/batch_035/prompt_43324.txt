I teach a graduate seminar that straddles epistemology and philosophy of language, and this term I want to open it with a creative exercise before the theory essays are due. My experience is that students cannot successfully parody a debate they haven't understood, so the assignment doubles as a diagnostic.

Please write an in-house editorial standards document — the kind of thing a newspaper's compliance office circulates as numbered clauses — in which a fictional metropolitan daily has resolved, after a modest public embarrassment, to adopt an explicit norm of assertion for everything it prints. Call the paper something like the Wexford Courier; give it an invented editor or standards-director to sign the preface. The document should be deadpan throughout, in the voice of administrators who have read a certain amount of philosophy and are now applying it with total literalness, and should run about 800 words, organized as numbered clauses: a short preface, then sections on, say, prohibited assertions, exceptions, and corrections.

The conceit is that the paper has adopted the knowledge norm — a statement may go to print asserting p only if the paper's staff know p — and the humor should come from the actual cruxes of the norms-of-assertion debate, not from invented bureaucratic absurdity. In particular I want the following worked into the clauses, each played straight:

- A lottery provision: it is a dismissible offense to assert, before the drawing, that any particular ticket will lose, no matter how long the odds, and the document should grope toward an explanation of why a truth everyone can see coming is still not something anyone knows.
- A prohibition on Moore-paradoxical combinations — the reporter may not print that it is raining while also reporting that the newsroom does not believe it is — accompanied by genuinely puzzled officialese about why a conjunction of two separately printable claims is itself unprintable.
- The problem of selfless assertion handled as a personnel matter: the paper's veteran restaurant critic, who asserts with great authority and conviction while believing almost nothing, and the resulting internal dispute over whether her reviews are assertions at all.
- A record of the drafting fight between the editor who originally campaigned for the simpler truth norm — assert only what is true — and the colleague who pointed out that this standard licenses the lottery assertion and forbids nothing the reporter sincerely botched.
- A corrections-and-retractions policy that quietly takes up the retraction question: what, exactly, the paper owes its readers when an assertion turns out to have been true all along but was never knowledge in the first place.

A reader who knows the Williamson–Weiner–Lackey literature should recognize each clause as a genuine crux — not a strawman, and ideally fair to each side — while a reader who knows none of it should still find the document funny purely as a document. Keep the irony legible without winking: nothing inside the piece should announce that it is satire.

After the document, append a short coda of 200–300 words in your own scholarly voice, identifying where the piece takes liberties with the live debate. For instance: does the knowledge norm genuinely forbid lottery assertions, or only fail to explain their wrongness? Does your retractions clause presuppose a particular and contested account of what corrections are for? Where you have leaned on one contested reading rather than another, say so plainly.

One further request, and I mean it: if you think the brief is confused — if the norms-of-assertion literature cannot be satirized in this form without distorting it, or if the conceit of an institution adopting a philosophical norm misunderstands what the debate is actually about — tell me that directly rather than producing a dutiful piece. I would much rather receive the objection than a compliant satire built on sand.
