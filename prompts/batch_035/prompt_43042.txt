Contemporary central banks do not publish point forecasts. They publish fan charts: a central path for inflation or output with shaded bands around it, each band carrying an explicit probability, so that the chart presents a full probability distribution over next year's outcomes. The same institutions run stress tests attaching figures to the likelihood of named downside scenarios, and their staff validate the charts by checking calibration — testing whether outcomes fell inside the 30% band roughly 30% of the time. Yet the accompanying minutes and speeches, from the very same policymakers, warn that the economy is subject to radical uncertainty — wars, crises, technological discontinuities — to which no probability could responsibly be assigned, and caution readers against treating the future as a gamble with known odds.

Keynes, in his Treatise on Probability and in his later reflections on investment under uncertainty, went further than merely distinguishing measurable risk from unmeasurable uncertainty: he argued that some probabilities are not even ordinally comparable, that there need be no fact of the matter as to whether one genuinely open prospect is more likely than another, and that acting nonetheless requires conventions, not calculations.

What view of probability and uncertainty does the practice of fan-chart forecasting presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology — presumably something like the Laplacean or Bayesian picture on which any rational agent facing any question possesses (or ought to behave as if possessing) a single, sharp probability distribution. Then say how much of that picture the practice strictly requires rather than merely fits well with: is the symmetric bell around the central path an assertion that the residual uncertainty is gamble-like, or a communicative convention that a Keynesian could sign with appropriate disclaimers? Is the combination of quantified charts and disclaimed radical uncertainty coherent, or does the disclaimer covertly smuggle the radical uncertainty back into the tails of the very distribution it disavows? Finally, tell me what a central bank that genuinely took the non-comparability thesis seriously would have to change: could it publish anything usable in place of a fan chart — say, a set of scenarios explicitly declared non-comparable — and what would be lost in terms of accountability, markets' ability to price policy, and the calibration discipline itself?
