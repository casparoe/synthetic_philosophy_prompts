I lead a small monthly discussion group — twelve adults, mostly retirees and parents, with no academics among us — and this month we tackled a summary of Garrett Hardin's 1974 argument that rich nations are lifeboats (his example: a boat with fifty people aboard, capacity for ten more, and a hundred swimmers in the water) and that taking swimmers aboard dooms everyone. The room split in a strange way: several members agreed on every concrete case but still fought over the labels, and one member kept switching sides without noticing. For next month's session I want to hand out a written quiz that sorts us by what we actually believe, not by what we call ourselves, and I'd like your help designing it.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in applied ethics. Before writing anything, think through the logical space: first list the questions on which the positions in this debate genuinely come apart, then place the actual positions in that space, and only after that write the quiz, so that every question earns its place by separating two positions that would otherwise land together.

The quiz should have ten questions, each a single concrete case with two or three answer options. Every option should be an action or a judgment ("we take one swimmer aboard and hold the others off", "we dump the cargo and take as many as fit") — never a label — and no question may simply ask about a position ("do you support open borders?" would be useless for my purposes). Keep all ten questions on the water, in Hardin-style boats, so that members answer about cases rather than about politics, but vary the premises across cases in ways that split the real positions. At minimum, include:

1. a case where the boat has spare seats, and a case where it is genuinely full;
2. a swimmer who is going under within minutes, versus one treading water comfortably;
3. a case where taking on swimmers makes the boat better off (they can row, or fish) rather than worse;
4. a case where the only way to help is for a passenger to give up their own seat;
5. a case where the boat carries luxury cargo that could be dumped to make room;
6. a case where the boat was built, long ago, with timber the swimmers themselves provided and never got to keep.

After the questions, give a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions: the strict Hardin position (the boat is full, there is no duty of rescue, those aboard come first absolutely); the Singer-style rescue duty view (if you can save someone at modest cost to yourself, you must); the "charity begins at home" position, which prioritizes those aboard without Hardin's harshness; and the position that rejects the metaphor's premises outright — the boat isn't really full, the swimmers aren't really drowning, and "carrying capacity" is a claim about how we choose to distribute things, not about physical seats. Finally, add a short section titled "what this quiz cannot tell you": identify any pair of positions your questions fail to separate (someone who always chooses rescue, for instance, might hold the Singer view or might simply believe the boat is never really full — if your cases can't tell those apart, say so plainly rather than papering over it), and flag any answer pattern that no named position occupies, with a sentence on whether you think nobody holds it because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Take your time and think carefully before committing to the questions. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — the quiz will be used in a room where nobody can look anything up, so it must stand on its own — and write everything in plain language a bright fifteen-year-old could follow. The point is diagnosis, not vocabulary.
