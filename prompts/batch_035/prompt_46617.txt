I am a retired spectroscopist who spent the better part of four decades in a physical chemistry laboratory, and since retiring I have been working through classical Indian philosophy, largely because a friend who studied Sanskrit keeps insisting that the Nyāya tradition anticipated much of what we would now call evidence theory and philosophy of science. I have read enough now to know the basic shape of the system: the Nyāya thinkers recognize four pramāṇas — instruments of knowledge — namely perception, inference, comparison, and reliable testimony, and they insist that a genuine cognition must be generated by one of these.

What I cannot make sense of is their treatment of memory. As far as I can tell, the Naiyāyikas deliberately excluded memory from the list, and this strikes me as an obvious omission, perhaps the central defect of the whole framework. In scientific practice, hardly anything is known "on the spot": I know last year's measurement only through my recollection of it, and the lab notebook functions as evidence only because I remember what the entries mean. Memory can even be a source of knowledge in the stronger sense: there are cases where one recalls a datum one had not registered as significant at the time of observation, as when a witness suddenly recovers a crucial detail. The Nyāya authors were hardly careless thinkers — they famously distinguished indeterminate from determinate perception and argued over the reliability of testimony in ways that anticipate modern epistemology — so why would they overlook something so elementary? I gather that some later schools, particularly certain Mīmāṃsā authors, did expand their own lists with additional pramāṇas, which suggests the lists were treated as revisable in principle.

My question, then, is this: is the exclusion of memory simply a premodern blind spot that later Indian philosophers should have repaired by adding a fifth pramāṇa, or did they have some principled reason for the exclusion? And if there is such a reason, does it survive the fact that in real epistemic practice — in a laboratory as much as a courtroom — remembered observations plainly function as evidence? If the reason cannot accommodate that, I am inclined to conclude that pramāṇa theory, however sophisticated, ultimately fails as a general theory of knowledge.
