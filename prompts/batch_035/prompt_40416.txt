At a month-long residency I'm attending, two of the other residents have fallen into a running argument about analogy, and last night it came to a head over dinner. The occasion was a set of letter-string puzzles of the sort Douglas Hofstadter's old Copycat work was built around: given that the string abc changes to abd, what should ijk change to? Nearly everyone, including both of them, answered ijl — take the rightmost letter and replace it with its successor — and that was the end of the easy part. The harder case was: given that abc changes to abd, what should xyz change to? Here z has no successor, and human answers scatter: a popular one is wya, arrived at by letting every concept in the rule slip — the alphabet is treated as circular, so the right edge becomes the left edge, successor slips into predecessor, and the "rightmost letter" becomes the leftmost one; other people insist on xyd or simply refuse the question. Ines, a literary translator with formalist instincts, and Dov, an essayist who writes about cognition, then went at it in four moves. (1) Ines argued that the puzzles reveal analogy as rule-following: ijl comes from an explicit rule, and even the strange answers to xyz are generated by explicit replacement rules with priorities; where human answers cluster there are regularities, and regularities are rules, so "fluidity" is a romantic name for a weighted preference structure. (2) Dov replied that the interesting cases are exactly the ones where rules collide: in xyz the instruction "increment the rightmost letter" runs off the edge of the alphabet, and nothing local tells the solver whether to abandon it, bend the alphabet, or shift the whole frame — that judgment is global, it concerns which reconstruction makes the two strings feel most alike, and it has to be made before any rule can be applied, because the concepts the rules would use (rightmost, successor, letter) are themselves what is being renegotiated. (3) Ines answered that this is still mechanizable: build a system of many small competing pressures — some toward alphabetic order, some toward attachment to an edge — capable of re-describing the string under stress, and you will find that the bending is itself regular; to call it unruled, she said, is just to decline to look inside. (4) Dov's rejoinder was that a mechanism which can rebuild its own concepts under pressure, and whose output is not fixed in advance, has already conceded the only point that matters: the mapping is constructed on the fly, so "rule" has been stretched until it covers whatever wins, and a rule that covers whatever wins says nothing.

I stayed out of it throughout — I have no view to push, and honestly I couldn't tell who was winning. But I would like the exchange adjudicated properly, move by move as the four numbered claims were actually made.
