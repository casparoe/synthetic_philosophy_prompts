I am a postdoc in a quantum-foundations group, and I am trying to predict how our most senior colleague — I shall call her Dr Amadi — will rule on two questions coming up at our seminar. She has never written a position paper; her views exist only as a long string of verdicts delivered from her usual seat, and I have kept notes.

Her track record, in roughly chronological order:

1. The CHSH inequality violations are genuine; local hidden-variable theories are refuted; and the closure of the detection and locality loopholes in the modern loophole-free experiments settles the matter. She has no patience for detector-artefact rescue attempts.
2. The superdeterminist escape — saving locality by denying that measurement settings are statistically independent of the hidden variables — she rejects as an unfalsifiable conspiracy, and she becomes visibly impatient when it is introduced as though it were a new idea rather than a loophole.
3. Retrocausal escapes fare no better with her.
4. The slogan that Bell's theorem refutes local realism she considers sloppy, and she grows testy when colleagues announce that realism alone was the casualty and locality has survived. Yet she has never been pinned down on which conjunct she herself would give up.
5. Bohmian mechanics she regards as a genuine theory — precise, empirically adequate, respectable — and she supervises a student working on it.
6. GRW-style spontaneous collapse she also regards as a genuine theory, and one she praises for being, in principle, empirically distinguishable from quantum mechanics.
7. Copenhagen-style instrumentalism she dismisses as not an interpretation but a decision not to interpret. She is a realist: there is a fact about what the world is like, including when no one is looking.
8. When the no-signalling theorem is raised as a comfort, she replies that the impossibility of sending messages with the correlations is a fact about the formalism, not a consolation, and does nothing to make the world local.
9. She has conceded, reluctantly, that she could live with a theory that privileges a foliation of spacetime, if that is the price of saying precisely what there is and when a measurement has an outcome.
10. On many-worlds she has been conspicuously silent, which everyone has noticed, because she is otherwise silent about nothing.

The two new cases:

(a) A colleague has applied for our group's internal grant: an interferometer for progressively more massive particles — large molecules, then levitated nanoparticles — designed to test whether macroscopic superpositions degrade in the way spontaneous-collapse models predict. It is up against a perfectly respectable rival proposal, and there is funding for only one. What will Dr Amadi do: fund the interferometer or not? And what result will she predict it finds?
(b) A visiting speaker will argue that many-worlds dissolves the nonlocality: no collapse, no influence, only branching plus decoherence, so nothing ever acts on anything at spacelike separation and locality is preserved. How will she respond?

What I want from you: predicted verdicts on (a) and (b); the candidate rules she might be running, given the track record — for instance, that outcomes are fixed in advance by deterministic nonlocal beables; or that there is a genuinely stochastic collapse; or a more pluralist realism on which only local beables have been ruled out and determinism remains open; or perhaps, despite everything, some way of holding on to locality — with an account of which parts of her record each rule explains naturally and which it strains at. Say where the rules agree, where they diverge on my two cases, give your best guess with your reasons, and — most usefully — tell me what further case, put to her at the right moment, would best discriminate between the rules. She is far too sharp to be caught contradicting herself, so the discriminating case must be one she has not yet ruled on, and it should be chosen so that her answer costs each candidate rule something.
