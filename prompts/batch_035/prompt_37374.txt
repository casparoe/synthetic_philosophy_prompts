My roommate and a friend of his got into it at dinner over where to donate this year, and I couldn't follow who was winning. Some background: my roommate gives to a malaria charity every year and was trying to talk his friend into doing the same. His friend refused on grounds I hadn't heard before. Roughly how it went:

(1) The friend said the whole pitch rests on the charity being the most cost-effective way to do good, but nobody can actually know that. The immediate effects of a bed net are visible enough, but the long-run effects of any act ripple out in ways no one can trace, and over decades those ripples can swamp the direct effect. A kid who doesn't die of malaria grows up and does things — some good, some bad — and you have no idea how that nets out. So the "cost-effective" ranking is a guess dressed up as a calculation.

(2) My roommate said that only follows if you assume the unknown ripples are symmetric — that they're just as likely to be good as bad, so they cancel and leave the direct effect standing. But nothing justifies that symmetry. If anything, a child surviving in a decent household is more likely to lead to more good than harm, the same way we reasonably assume a random stranger we rescue is more likely to be decent than terrible. Without an argument for symmetry, uncertainty doesn't erase the direct effects, it just adds noise around them.

(3) The friend replied that my roommate has it backwards: even granting a tilt toward good ripples, the point is about magnitudes. The direct effect — one life saved per few thousand dollars — is fixed and modest. The ripple effects are unbounded: the kid could be a future warlord, or the person who later saves a million lives. Once unbounded possibilities are on the table, even tiny probabilities of huge outcomes can dominate the expected value, and completely reverse the ranking of charities. The modest, "solid" part of the calculation is exactly the part that matters least. So it's not that the ripples cancel, it's that they're so much bigger than what your confident about that the confident part is basically a rounding error.

(4) My roommate said this proves too much. If uncertainty about ripple effects is disqualifying, it disqualifies every choice anyone makes, not just donations — choosing a job, having kids, voting. Whatever the friend does with his money instead also has unknowable long-run consequences. So cluelessness can't be a reason to withhold from the malaria charity specifically; at most it's a reason to be humble about everything, and it leaves the ranking of options exactly where it was. Given that, the medium-run knowledge we do have (nets prevent deaths) is the only handle available, so it's rational to grab it.

(5) The friend said that's where he disagrees: it's not about disqualifying every choice, it's about what conclusions you're entitled to draw. Fine, withhold and donate are both risky. But my roommate wants to go further and say the malaria charity is *the best* option, with confidence that passes down to donors choosing between charities, and that's the confidence cluelessness undermines. If you can't compare ripple effects, you can't compare charities that differ mainly in their long-run stories, and you certainly can't tell someone their money does the most good there. What he'd be doing, donating on the strength of the visible effect, might be fine — but he should admit he's going on the felt clarity of the direct effect, not on a comparison of total outcomes.

(6) My roommate's last word was that "felt clarity" is doing sneaky work there: the direct effects aren't just vivid, they're the best-evidenced part of the whole picture, so leaning on the best-evidenced part isn't a bias, it's epistemology. And he noted that if his friend were right that uncertainty reverses rankings, the friend should be able to say at least one case where donating to the malaria charity is worse than some alternative — but the friend never named one, he just gestures at the space of possibilities.

At that point they noticed I'd gone quiet and asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Can you go through the exchange step by step and say who's right at each stage — not just overall? And if there's a move both of them are missing, I'd like to know that too.
