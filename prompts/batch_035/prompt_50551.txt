You are a social choice theorist with strong philosophical instincts. Before committing to anything, brainstorm how a defender of individual liberty over one's personal sphere might try to systematize my verdicts, then give your considered answer in at most 700 words.

I believe people should be decisive over their own personal sphere — Sen's minimal liberalism, roughly. But my gut verdicts on cases don't obviously add up to any workable version of that, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. Here they are:

1. My homeowners' association votes 40–3 to forbid me repainting my house the shade of purple they all despise. I think the vote should have no force: the color of my own house is my business.
2. The same town votes to remove a book from the public library; every voter could simply not read it. Illegitimate for the same reason as case 1.
3. But a vote silencing my neighbor's midnight-to-4 a.m. drum practice is perfectly legitimate.
4. My other neighbor's enormous garden gnome collection, visible from the street and hated by everyone else, may be ordered removed by majority vote. I'm fine with that, even though structurally it's close to case 1 — the neighbors just have to look at it.
5. Six friends each privately want to end their annual Christmas-gift exchange, but no one dares propose it unilaterally. They take a vote and unanimously agree to stop. The vote is not merely legitimate; it's what voting is for.
6. A town of self-described lazy citizens votes to fine anyone who skips the communal park cleanup, having each admitted they would otherwise free-ride. Fine.

My question: is there a principle — some version of "each person is decisive over a protected personal sphere, defined by X" — that gets all six? Cases 5 and 6 are what trouble me most. They are majority decisions about what each individual does with their own time, money, and effort, and Sen's own argument seems to show that if each person is individually decisive over their own participation, the vote is either impotent or lands you in a cycle with Pareto. So does my willingness to allow those votes commit me to shrinking the protected sphere whenever there's a collective action problem? And if the sphere shrinks that easily, what is left of my verdict in case 1 when the neighbors argue that my purple house triggers a one-upmanship cycle in exterior paint that each of them would rather not be in?

Please state the best candidate principle precisely, clause by clause, telling me which case motivates each clause. Then apply it to two cases I haven't listed: a vote to fluoridate the water supply over the objection of the one resident who hates the idea, and a unanimous vote by members of a naturist club resolving that everyone may go unclothed only if everyone does. Finally, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, whether the cost of saving them all is a baroque or gerrymandered definition of the protected sphere, and whether you think Sen's result shows that my kind of liberalism is simply inconsistent with the Pareto principle I also endorse. Keep the whole answer under 700 words.
