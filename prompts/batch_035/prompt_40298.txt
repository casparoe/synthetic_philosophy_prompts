My neighbor and I have argued for two years about what it means to call a glass fragile, and he has an argument I cannot answer, though its conclusion cannot be right.

(1) To call a glass fragile is just to say that if it were struck, it would break — that is the whole content of the word, beyond describing shape and material. (2) Now take a glass packed tight in bubble wrap: strike it and it survives, though the glass itself is unchanged by the wrapping. (3) Or a glass with a vigilant guardian who snatches it away the instant anything touches it: struck, never broken, glass unchanged. (4) And it runs the other way too: a block of steel attended by a hidden helper who smashes it with a sledgehammer whenever it is struck — struck, and broken, every time — was never fragile. (5) So whether "struck, then broken" holds is settled by the surroundings, not by the object. (6) So there is nothing about the glass itself that makes it fragile; if fragility exists anywhere, it belongs to whole set-ups — glass plus bubble wrap, steel plus helper. (7) And the same must hold for every power we credit to things: solubility, flammability, the cutting power of a knife. Nothing has powers of its own; the powers all belong to situations. (8) But bleach really is poisonous, and it is the bleach, not my kitchen, that would do the killing.

He also has an answer to the obvious patch — say a glass is fragile if it would break when struck under normal conditions. Either "normal" means "no bubble wrap, no guardians, no hidden helpers," in which case we have defined fragility by ruling out in advance whatever would refute it, which he calls cheating; or "normal" means statistically typical, in which case how glasses are generally treated decides what my particular glass is, which seems worse.

Which step should I refuse, and what does refusing it cost me? I would most like to keep (1), because anything weaker seems to turn "fragile" into a mystery word.
