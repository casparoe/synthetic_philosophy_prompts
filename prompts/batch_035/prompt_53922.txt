My roommate is a decision theory person and thinks he's proved that rational nuclear deterrence is impossible, and I can't find the flaw. (1) After you've absorbed a first strike, retaliating is pointless at best: your own cities are already gone, launching back saves no one, and it just adds millions of deaths on the other side, most of them people who had no say in the decision. (2) A rational agent does not genuinely intend, at any point, to do something that will be pointless and terrible when the time comes. (3) Deterrence only works if the other side believes you would actually push the button after a first strike — not bluster about it, actually do it. (4) The other side knows your leadership is rational and knows (1) as well as you do. (5) So they can deduce in advance that you won't retaliate, so the threat is never credible, so two rational adversaries cannot deter each other at all. (6) So the only deterrence that can actually work runs on irrationality — the president's temper, bunker mythology, honor culture — and every step we take toward making nuclear decision-making more cool-headed and deliberate makes war more likely, not less.

He also says the argument generalizes: any threat whose execution would, once the moment arrives, serve no purpose and hurt everyone — a union threatening a strike that's worse for its members than the offer, a parent threatening something they'd hate to actually carry out — is equally unable to deter a rational opponent. That conclusion seems way too strong to me, but each step looks fine on its own. My instinct is that the trouble is somewhere around (2), because it feels like the argument is grading one and the same intention at two different times and getting different answers, but I can't make that precise without just hand-waving. He keeps gesturing at some puzzle about a toxin that he refuses to explain to me until I figure the deterrence thing out myself, which is annoying.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1), since it seems like the one thing everyone on all sides agrees about, and I'd rather not end up committed to (6) either, since "thank god our leaders aren't fully rational" is not a place I want to live. If the fix involves distinguishing something like an intention from a policy or a disposition, tell me what exactly the distinction is doing, because right now it looks like relabeling to me.
