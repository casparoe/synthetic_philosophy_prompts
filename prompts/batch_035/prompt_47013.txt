Next month I'm giving a forty-minute talk at a small workshop on observation selection effects — roughly twenty attendees: philosophers of science who know the Carter–Leslie doomsday literature well, two cosmologists who take fine-tuning arguments seriously, one Bayesian statistician who has publicly dismissed the whole doomsday tradition as a confusion about sampling procedures, and a handful of graduate students. My thesis is contrarian in both directions. On the one hand I defend the doomsday argument against its standard objections: I think the birth-rank reasoning is sound, that the self-indication objection is a mistake, and that the frequent complaint about there being "no random draw from the set of all observers" misses the point of Bayesian conditioning. On the other hand I argue that everyone has misidentified the conclusion. The reference class for the birth-rank update cannot sensibly include all observers or even all humans; it is restricted to observers who actually possess and deploy the argument — who are in a position to run it, not merely to be counted by it. But then what the argument predicts is not the extinction of humanity but the extinction of the practice of anthropic reasoning itself. Doom, in the argument's own terms, is the end of doomsday arguments. This is why the practical upshot is so oddly modest: an argument whose evidence base can only ever tell us something about the size of its own audience, and which, the more widely it is credited, the more it shrinks the class it counts.

I know the standard battle lines, but I want to be ready for this particular room, not for the abstract literature. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this audience, in the order they are likely to arrive, each phrased the way a philosopher of science or a working statistician in that room would put it, with a two-minute reply, a note on what I should concede up front rather than fight, and a flag on where the reply is soft enough that a sharp follow-up could open it up. Order the objections by how much damage they would do to the talk's central claim, not by how well known they are — the reference-class gerrymandering worry from the Carter–Leslie purists, the self-indication reply from anyone sympathetic to it, the statistician's complaint about sampling frames and priors-in-disguise, and whatever the fine-tuning people will want to know about whether my restricted reference class poisons their uses of observer-selection reasoning. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that takes my own claim that the argument only predicts the fate of the anthropic-reasoning tradition and turns it into an uncomfortable prediction about how much longer anyone, anywhere, will be doing this kind of philosophy at all — including the people nodding along in the front row.
