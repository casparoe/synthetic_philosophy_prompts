I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a corner I now need to think my way out of. The last act of my current book centers on a woman in her second trimester who suffers a catastrophic hemorrhage and is declared brain-dead, but whose body is kept on a ventilator at her husband's insistence because the fetus is still viable — a situation hospitals have actually managed, in documented cases, for well over a hundred days, some ending in the delivery of a living child. My narrator, the hospital's attorney, must eventually tell the family what is lying in that bed: a patient, or a corpse with a functioning placenta. I have decided the novel cannot end in ambiguity; it will take a position, and I would like it to be one I can defend when interviewers ask me what I meant. So, before I draft the final chapters, I need the underlying question settled to my own satisfaction: write an essay weighing the case for the neurological criterion of death against the circulatory one, and reach a verdict I can stand behind.

Ground the essay in what each criterion forces my book to say. If my character died when her brain did, then what exactly has been gestating the fetus these many weeks, and what does it mean for the ventilator to have been sustaining something — circulation, temperature, hormones — in a body that is supposed to be past sustaining anything? If she dies only when her heart is finally allowed to stop, then what was she during the interval, and can that position live comfortably with what happens two beds over in my ICU, where a man is declared dead within minutes of his heart stopping so that his organs can be procured, even though hearts have on occasion been observed to resume beating on their own shortly after? Consider what each criterion ultimately hangs on — whether death is the loss of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, the loss of the capacity for consciousness, or the loss of the person — and which of these the novel should endorse. And do consider the ordinary reader's intuition: a warm body with a beating heart and a growing fetus inside it does not look like a dead one, and my book will be judged partly by whether the reader's intuition survives the ending.

Do not end on a non-committal note; a novel that hedges in its final chapter is a novel that has failed, and an essay that does the same is of no use to me. Reach a verdict about which criterion the book should commit to, and say plainly what that verdict obliges the narrator to tell the family. Please write in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long, careful letter.
