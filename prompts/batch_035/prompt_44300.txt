The trouble is I graded these questions when I took the course myself years ago and I know my own understanding of some of this material has drifted. One question on the exam read:

"Philosophers sometimes talk as if Hume's is-ought gap and Moore's naturalistic fallacy are two labels for the same problem. Are they? Explain each, explain how they relate, and note where the two ideas come apart if anywhere."

Here is one student's answer, transcribed more or less as written:

"Hume observed that writers on morals tend to slide from describing how things are (is-statements) into prescribing how things ought to be (ought-statements) without ever explaining how they made that transition. That's the is-ought gap: no valid inference from purely descriptive premises to a normative conclusion. Moore later gave the same basic idea a sharper formulation as the naturalistic fallacy. In Principia Ethica he argued that whenever someone defines 'good' in natural terms — identifying it with pleasure, or with what evolution selects for, or with whatever a society approves of — they commit a fallacy, because good is a simple, non-natural property that can't be analyzed into anything more basic. His open question argument is supposed to establish this: for any proposed natural definition, it stays an open question whether the natural property picked out is really good, and that wouldn't be so if the definition were correct. So both thinkers are attacking the same target, namely the attempt to ground ethics in facts about the world. And on both views, any argument that appeals to what is natural is thereby fallacious — for instance, arguing that something must be good because it's natural, the way people say natural foods are better for you, commits the naturalistic fallacy. In the end the gap and the fallacy both show that moral judgments cannot be derived from factual judgments at all, so ethics must stand on its own non-natural footing, and indeed both Hume and Moore ended up as non-naturalist moral realists about such properties."
