I've been cataloguing my own verdicts on insects, pain, and what to do when you multiply a tiny probability of suffering by an enormous number of sufferers, and I want to know whether they compress into a principle or whether I'm running several inconsistent ones. My verdicts so far:

Swatting a mosquito that is biting me: obviously fine; I don't owe it a moment's deliberation. Pulling the wings off a fly purely because I'm bored: wrong, and I notice I wouldn't accept they probably can't feel it anyway as an excuse from anyone who did it. Driving at dusk with a windshield that ends the trip crusted with insects: I don't give it a thought, and I think that's fine. Flicking a spider out of the bath instead of washing it down the drain: worth the two seconds, and I'd feel a bit bad about the drain. Dropping live mussels into boiling water: mild pause, then do it. Dropping a live crab in: I'd stun or chill it first if that were easy, and I think failing to is a small wrong, not merely a forgivable one.

Now the ones I can't easily reconcile. A charity that lobbies agricultural ministries to approve a faster-acting pesticide, which on its own numbers would prevent what it estimates as an astronomical quantity of insect suffering for a few thousand dollars a year: I feel no obligation to donate, and even a donation strikes me as a nice extra rather than a duty — even though by any expected-value calculation this dwarfs everything else on the list combined. And a stranger who tells me that unless I hand over my wallet there is a one-in-a-quadrillion chance he will arrange for a vast number of beings to be tortured: obviously refuse, and the tininess of the probability seems to do all the work.

So: is there one principle that delivers all of these? I can see at least two candidates — a view on which low credence in sentience is discounted so heavily that no number of insects can outweigh even small human or practical costs, versus a view on which deliberate cruelty is wrong for reasons about the agent that bypass the arithmetic entirely, so that intention does the work in verdict two while aggregation never generates obligations at all — and I suspect they come apart somewhere. Please state the principle (or the two or three) precisely, say which clause each of my verdicts is evidence for, tell me what it predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on — after a rain, the sidewalk outside my building is covered with worms that will dry out and die, and carrying a hundred of them back to the grass costs me ten minutes — and be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, your preferred principle forces me to surrender.

Open with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
