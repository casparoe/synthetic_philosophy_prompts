I'm a backend engineer by training and I help run a small internal reading group on the foundations of trustworthy computing — about fifteen people, split roughly half and half between formal verification engineers (the Coq, Lean, and SAT/SMT types) and ML engineers who work on training and evaluating large models. Next week it's my turn to present, and I want to argue something I actually care about: that the Löbian obstacle is not, as half the group seems to assume, just Gödel's second incompleteness theorem wearing a costume, and also not just a curiosity about proof-based agents that gradient-trained systems make irrelevant. My line is that it's the sharp edge of a general phenomenon: a verification regime cannot certify its own trustworthiness from the inside. Löb's theorem is one rigorous instance of that — if a formal system could reason "whatever is provable in me is true" it could prove anything, which is why a proof-based agent can't verify a successor that reasons in the same system, and the standard escapes (trusting only proofs shorter than your own, parametric polymorphism in the tiling-agents tradition, probabilistic reflection and so on) relocate the trust rather than dissolving it. But I want to claim the same shape shows up in software everyone in that room touches: a test suite that fully exercised the test framework it runs on, a bootstrapped compiler compiling itself, Ken Thompson's old trick of hiding a backdoor in a compiler so it survives recompilation from clean source. My stronger claim, and the one I expect to get killed on, is that it will bite learned verifiers too — any setup where the thing doing the checking is substantially built from the same substrate as the thing checked.

The audience matters for what I get asked about. The verification people are the ones who will say this is Gödel with extra steps, that Löb is just the mechanism and the content is the second incompleteness theorem, and that nothing I've said is news since 1931. The ML people will say proof-theoretic agents are a strawman no serious lab builds, that in their world trust comes from benchmarks and held-out evaluation, and that a theorem about provability has nothing to do with systems that don't prove anything. Both camps have a manager in the room with them who will ask, in so many words, what decision this changes for us next quarter.

Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly: the objections I'll actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they'll arrive and ranked by how much damage they'd do to my position, each phrased the way that camp would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply I can actually deliver and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front before anyone makes me. And include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks — I suspect it's something about whether my test-suite and compiler analogies are genuinely isomorphic to the Löbian setup or merely evocative, since the formal obstacle lives in a system's ability to prove things about its own proofs and my examples involve something weaker than that, but if there's a worse one lurking, I want to know it before Thursday.
