Economist here who's ended up doing cost-benefit analysis at a transport agency, and I keep bumping into something about the old interpersonal utility comparision fight that bugs me. Back in the thirties Robbins basically declared that saying one person gets more utility from something than another person would is unscientific — you can observe choices all you want, but the comparison across heads is a value judgment, not an inference, so it has no place in economics proper. The ordinalist crowd took that and ran with it: utility isn't even a substance you could pour into two people and compare, it's just a device for ordering one person's preferences, so the comparability question doesn't even arise. And then the whole field does this weird dance: Arrow builds social choice while scrupulously never comparing across persons, Kaldor-Hicks compensation criteria get invented as a supposedly comparison-free workaround, Robbins-era methodological orthodoxy gets enshrined in the textbooks... and meanwhile everyone in applied welfare economics just quietly starts comparing anyway. Willingness to pay gets summed across households, QALYs get added up across patients, distributional weights show up in official guidance. Nobody ever won the argument as far as I can tell, we just stopped having it.

So my question is what kind of disagreement that actually was, and why it stalled into this truce instead of resolving one way or the other. My working theory — and I present it as just a theory — is that the two sides were never disagreeing about the same thing. Robbins was making a point about the limits of inference from behavior, an epistemology point, while the people who kept comparing utilities were making a point about what you need in order to make policy decisions at all, and each side's conclusion doesn't even touch the other's premises, which would explain why the debate could go nowhere. Is that right? Or was there a genuine substantive disagreement in there, maybe about whether utility is a real quantity that exists in people at all, or maybe a semantic one about what the word 'utility' is even doing? And here's the part that matters to me practically: given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress? Does Harsanyi's extended-preferences construction count as actually solving something or just relabeling the problem? Does the fact that hedonic psychology nowadays claims to measure affect in interpersonally comparable units touch Robbins's argument at all, or does his point survive that completely intact? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a history lesson — and if your diagnosis is that the question was ill-posed from the start, then I want to hear why the applied people are nonetheless getting away with summing QALYs without anyone objecting on Robbinsian grounds anymore.
