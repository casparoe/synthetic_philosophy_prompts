My book club has been fighting for a week about what it takes for two people to do something *together*, and I've lost the thread, so I need a referee.

It started when Dana and Marco co-wrote and performed a two-person show. Someone asked them how they pulled it off and Dana said "we intended to do this show from the start," and Marco (who is doing a philosophy degree and can't help himself) said that's really just shorthand -- there's no "we" that intends anything, there's Dana intending to write her scenes, Marco intending to write his, each intending it partly *because* the other is in, and both knowing that about each other. He said once you lay out all those individual intentions plus the mutual knowledge, you've said everything there is to say, and positing a further group-level intention is like positing a group mind hovering over them, which is obviously not a thing.

Dana pushed back with a case she swears she read somewhere: imagine two pianists in adjacent soundproof rooms, each rehearsing one half of a duet, each intending to play their part if the other plays theirs, and each knowing the other has the same conditional intention. Marco's recipe seems to be satisfied -- interlocking intentions, mutual knowledge, the works -- but they're not playing a duet together in any sense that matters. They're two soloists with matching files. So something got left out.

Marco came back that the missing ingredient is responsiveness: in real joint action each person is adjusting to the other in real time, and once you add that, the reduction survives. Dana then flipped to the opposite case: two session musicians who can't stand each other, hired to play a piece they've both played a thousand times, executing it exactly as scripted with essentially zero mutual adjustment, each just doing their job and collecting the check. Nobody would hesitate to say they *performed together* even though the responsiveness is basically nil. Her point was that whatever condition Marco keeps adding to patch the reduction -- common knowledge, responsiveness, commitments to each other -- each patch is itself a piece of togetherness, so the "reduction" ends up as a checklist of jointness-constituting facts rather than a debunking of jointness. The we-intent isn't explained, it's just been itemized.

Marco's latest move is to say Dana is equivocating between two questions: whether there's an extra *fact* on top of the individual facts, and whether there's an extra *mental state* of a new kind. He's happy to grant the first and deny the second -- the "we intend" isn't a separate act in anyone's head, it's the whole interlocking structure seen from a certain angle, the way "the crowd is loud" is true but not because of a loud thing over and above the people. He says Dana is assuming that anything irreducible must be spooky, and that she's welcome to keep saying "we intended" as long as she knows what the cash value is.

They've each asked me privately who's winning and I honestly flip depending on the day. Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what -- including whether the soundproof room case and the grumpy session musicians case actually cut the way each of them thinks -- and whether there's a disagreement left at the end or just two ways of talking? And if they're both missing something, what is it? (Dana says the whole fight might secretly be about whether the joint performance is *one action with two agents* or *two actions that happen to add up*, but Marco says that's a seperate issue. Is it?)
