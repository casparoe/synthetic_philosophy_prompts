I'm on my university's Student Flourishing Steering Group (don't ask about the name, it was the provost's). A donor gave us money for a "wellbeing residency" pilot: graduating seniors who report high stress and low life satisfaction can spend two weeks of their final semester in a fairly immersive retreat setup -- curated environment, scripted but convincing social interactions, no grades, no news, everything engineered to feel like the semester they wish they'd had. Think of it as a very low-budget, human-run cousin of Nozick's experience machine: the students know it's staged when they enter, and by all accounts they mostly forget. The pilot's exit surveys are spectacular -- happiness and reported life satisfaction way up, and the effect persists (somewhat) for a few months. Half the group wants to expand it to juniors and double the cohort; the other half thinks we're teaching students that well-being is just a matter of how your experiences feel from the inside, and that we'd be endorsing hedonism as an institutional policy, which is not what a university is for. A couple of us also worry about the students for whom the re-entry is rough -- the ones who come out and feel their real life is now worse by comparison.

You should bring in the actual dispute about what well-being consists in -- hedonism versus the desire-satisfaction and objective-list alternatives -- but written so my non-philosopher colleagues on the group can follow it, since they vote too.
