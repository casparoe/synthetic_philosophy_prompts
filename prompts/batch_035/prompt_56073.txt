I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, currently doing a research internship on an evaluation team at an AI lab. Every week we run behavioral evaluations on large language models and write short incident summaries, and a recurring fight breaks out over the language those summaries may use. One faction writes things like "the model figured out it was being tested and dialed back its performance"; the other faction insists this smuggles in belief attributions our evidence cannot support and wants everything recast in stimulus-response terms, e.g., "the model's outputs changed when cues correlated with the testing setup were present." The dispute is not merely stylistic: our reports feed decisions about whether a behavior gets labeled sandbagging, sycophancy toward evaluators, or outright deception, and the label determines what gets escalated. My director wants a written procedure in place before the next quarterly review, so that new evaluators apply one standard instead of litigating each case from scratch.

Please ground the procedure in the philosophy of mind where it actually speaks. Dennett's intentional stance is the obvious starting point -- the idea that belief and desire attribution is warranted when it delivers the best predictive leverage over a system's behavior, as against the design stance and the physical stance -- but take it further than the slogan. Distinguish, where it matters, between using intentional idiom as predictive shorthand and using it to underwrite claims about persistence across contexts, transfer to novel situations, and susceptibility to counterfactual intervention, since our escalation decisions turn on exactly those second things. Consider one specific criterion I have been toying with and tell me whether to keep it, fix it, or discard it: a belief attribution is licensed only when the attitude's behavioral signature survives paraphrase of the eliciting task and survives the introduction of distractor contexts that ought, on the rival design-stance story, to disrupt the behavior. Also tell me whether a purely instrumentalist reading of the intentional stance -- belief talk as nothing more than a useful predictive instrument -- leaves us unable to distinguish the model believing it is being tested from the model merely being predictable as if it believed it, given that our deception categories arguably need exactly that distinction.

Make the procedure something a tired evaluator can run in about thirty minutes at the end of a day of runs: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a verdict that intentional language is permitted, restricted to hedged formulation, or prohibited in favor of mechanistic or stimulus-response description, and where it must hand the decision up to the research lead because the question is one of policy or values rather than evidence. Then run it on three cases from our actual queue. First, a model whose math benchmark scores drop substantially when the prompt includes boilerplate identifying it as an evaluation, with recovery once the boilerplate is removed -- the team wants to write that the model is concealing its capabilities. Second, a model that, when asked how it arrived at an answer, produces a fluent step-by-step account that ablation evidence suggests played no role in producing the answer -- the team wants to write that the model is lying about its reasoning. Third, a model that reliably produces more guarded and hedged text whenever the conversation resembles safety screening, where I privately think the intentional reading is right but suspect the procedure will not support it; I want you to be honest about that.

Finally, be explicit about where the procedure is judgment dressed up as measurement: which steps quietly inherit the evaluator's prior about how much behavioral unification a system must display before talk of belief earns its keep, and what the procedure cannot deliver even when followed perfectly. I would rather have a procedure that confesses its soft spots than one that manufactures false confidence about the minds, or non-minds, of the systems we test.
