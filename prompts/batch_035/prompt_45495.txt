I've been keeping a list of cases where someone has first-order evidence for some proposition and then gets higher-order evidence about that very evidence, and I've noticed my verdicts don't obviously hang together. I'd like help figuring out whether there's a single principle behind them, or whether some of my judgments have to go. Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A radiologist reads a scan as showing a hairline fracture. She then receives a memo — false, as it happens — saying the machine's calibration was off all morning. She keeps believing there's a fracture on the strength of what she saw, while also believing her belief probably isn't warranted. I think she's rational to keep the belief. The akratic combination she's in feels uncomfortable but not irrational.

2. A student works through a problem, gets an answer she can defend line by line, and then a classmate she considers generally reliable tells her the answer key says otherwise. She keeps her answer while believing she ought probably to revise. Here I have the opposite reaction: I think holding on with full confidence is irrational, and she should at least substantially lower her confidence, even though her own worked-through reasoning is arguably the stronger evidence.

3. A coffee buyer tasting samples before she's fully awake knows her palate is unreliable in the morning. She takes a sip, believes confidently that this is the Ethiopian lot, and simultaneously believes she shouldn't be confident about that. This seems fine to me — even virtuous. She believes with reduced confidence, and her belief that she shouldn't be more confident is itself exactly right.

4. A poker player knows he plays badly when tilted and knows he's tilted now. Mid-hand he becomes convinced the villain is bluffing, while also believing his tilt makes that conviction untrustworthy. I think he's irrational if he acts on full confidence, and roughly the more so the more vivid the conviction feels — the felt certainty is itself part of what his higher-order evidence is about.

5. Someone learns of a study showing that people in her exact epistemic position — first-year graduate students assessing whether an argument is valid — are right only about half the time even when they feel certain. I think she should stay fully confident in conclusions she reached by checking a proof step by step, but should heavily discount her holistic sense that some informal argument "looks valid." Same study, same person, two different verdicts about what it defeats.

Cases 1 and 2 look structurally alike — strong first-order evidence, misleading higher-order defeater — and I give them opposite verdicts, which bothers me. My tentative repair is something like: higher-order evidence only defeats when the first-order evidence can't be re-verified at will. The radiologist can look again; the student can re-derive her answer, though, so maybe that principle actually forces me to overturn my verdict on case 2. And it gets messy with case 3, since the taste fades with each second that passes.

So, my requests. State, as precisely as you can, one or at most two candidate principles about when higher-order evidence defeats first-order justification — I don't care whether the principle appeals to re-verifiability, to the distinction between rebutting and undercutting defeat, to whether the higher-order evidence is itself supported by evidence the subject can inspect, or to something else entirely. Show me which clause each of my verdicts motivates, and where each clause applies. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and make the case for overturning that one rather than complicating the principle. Then apply your preferred principle to two cases I haven't listed: first, a mathematician who has carefully verified a proof but then learns that every colleague this week who "verified" a proof later found a flaw — some subtle environmental factor is affecting exactly the people who feel fine; and second, someone who can re-verify her first-order evidence at will, but where each act of re-verification is itself subject to the same higher-order worry, so that re-checking never bottoms out.

One further request about how you frame the answer. Please keep separate what is settled among people who work on this — where, for instance, it's common ground that higher-order evidence can be genuine evidence — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether akratic combinations are ever rational, or how a subject should handle evidence about her own evidence when the two levels disagree. Where my cases force a choice on a contested question, say so explicitly rather than presenting your preferred resolution as the consensus view, and note what the live alternatives would say about my cases. If some of my verdicts are ones informed people would reject for reasons specific to one of those live alternatives, I'd like to know that too.
