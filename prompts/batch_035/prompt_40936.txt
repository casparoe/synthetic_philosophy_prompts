A bit of background, since the question won't make sense without it. I model algae blooms in drinking-water reservoirs. Last month my lab's director asked me, in a funding meeting, which of our two rival models is "closer to reality," because we can only afford to develop one line for the next two years. Model A is ambitious: it represents the whole mechanism in detail and spits out predictions about timing, severity, which species will dominate, spatial spread — and it gets most of that roughly right, but we now know a couple of its structural claims (something about nutrient uptake and how the water column mixes) are flat wrong, and those errors propagate everywhere. Model B is cautious: it only forecasts next-week chlorophyll levels, says nothing about anything else, and what it says is nearly always nearly right. Neither model is true. Both are useful. And when my director said "closer to the truth" like it's obvious, I realized I have no principled answer.

I minored in philosophy of science and mostly forgot it, so I went digging. Verisimilitude is a mess. Popper's original definition apparently collapses — every false theory ends up equally far from the truth on it, if I understood the Tichý/Miller objections right — and since then there's a zoo of measures, similarity-to-the-actual-world ones and content-ordering ones, and they disagree about exactly my case: the cautious model looks great on some and terrible on others. Before I pick any measure, or tell my director it can't be done, I want the spec.

So write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of "closer to the truth" has to deliver. Fix the purpose as this: good enough to defensibly order rival false theories like my two models. I don't need cardinal numbers, just an ordering we could stand behind in a funding meeting. A few things I half-want on the list — do with them what you think is right, don't just include them because I said so: it has to rank false theories against each other, not just against the truth; bolting on true but irrelevant claims shouldn't count as progress (otherwise you get closer to the truth by appending trivia); it has to take a position on my actual trade-off, where one model is stronger and more wrong and the other weaker and more right; and — the one I'm least sure about — does it have to be usable when we don't know the truth? We're comparing models of a system we're still learning. I suspect that last one is where the whole thing dies, but don't let me steer you.

Format: a table with exactly these columns — (1) criterion, (2) what it demands, in plain words, (3) the case that forces it, from my two models or a classic one, (4) negotiable?, a straight yes or no plus one sentence of why, (5) which main existing approach fails it. Then a short paragraph on joint satisfiability: if some subset of the criteria can't all be met, name the subset and commit — no "some have argued that." Same energy on the negotiable column: take positions, I'll push back where I disagree. Finish with the single criterion that does the most work, and your actual answer to my director's question given all that.
