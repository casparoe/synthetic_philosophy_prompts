I teach philosophy of history, mostly the dead theorists who thought the past had a shape — Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee, that crowd. Lately I've been poking at a case study my students actually live inside: the default financial advice baked into the retirement industry. If you're 25 in the anglosphere and enrolled in a workplace pension, the default fund will be mostly equities, the glidepath will shift you toward bonds as you age, and the entire scheme only works if equities deliver positive real returns over a forty-year horizon. Which, I keep telling my students, is a bet on the shape of history. So here's what I want worked out: what view of history does this practice actually presuppose? Is it the full linear-progressive thing — the Whig story, each generation materially better off than the last — or would a cyclical view also license it, on the theory that cycles are shorter than a working life, so you just ride them out and the peaks average out? And where exactly does the practice stop being neutral between the two views — what specific part of it (the glidepath? the forty-year horizon? the assumption that the index itself survives to pay you out) would a Spenglerian, or an Ibn Khaldun-style theorist with whole civilizations rising and dissolving on century timescales, refuse to sign off on? Finally, be honest with me about whether the whole thing is just underdetermined: maybe it's compatible with nearly any theory of history so long as one holds the much vaguer belief that there will be no permanent civilizational ruin within forty years, in which case it presupposes far less than it looks like it does from the outside. I don't want the boilerplate declinism lecture, I want the actual commitments, and the specific points where they'd bite.

One aside I can't resist, fold it in or skip it as you see fit: the same industry that runs on this assumption loves to point out that markets have recovered from every crisis so far, which sounds like solid induction right up until you notice that every past investor also had only the past to go on. If that observation does real work in the presupposition question — say, about whether the practice smuggles in a claim about the future being like the past in a stronger sense than usual — use it; if it's just a flourish, cut it.
