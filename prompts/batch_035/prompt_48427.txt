I just got out of a two-hour arguement with my Dutch friend that ended with her half-jokingly saying Americans have no idea what "liberal" means. It started because I described myself as a liberal — I'm for abortion rights, a bigger welfare state, higher taxes on the rich. She said that in the Netherlands "liberal" means roughly her own party — the one English-language news always describes as the Dutch liberals — free markets, lower taxes, smaller government, and that by her lights my politics are social-democratic, not liberal at all. Then it got messy: we checked that her party sits in the same European Parliament group as the German free-market FDP, while in America "liberal" basically means the left wing of the Democratic party. But when we asked each other what the word USED to mean, we gave the same answer: individual rights, toleration, constitutional government, free trade. So the weird part is that the old core seems intact everywhere, and yet in Europe the word lands center-right and in America center-left. She ended up saying the two uses are just homonyms that happen to share a history, like "pole" the rod and "pole" the Polish person, and I ended up saying that can't be right because both of us could still recognize a third country's liberal party as liberal on sight. Don't just tell me the word is ambiguous and leave it there — take a definite side: is there one principled meaning under which both her usage and mine fit, and if so, what exactly flipped between Europe and America to move the same tradition to opposite sides? And if you can, untangle the bonus round that broke our arguement: why does "neoliberal" get used as an insult by the left even though it has "liberal" right in it, when "liberal" itself is, in America, a word the left uses about itself.
