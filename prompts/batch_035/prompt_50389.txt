I have noticed a recurring structure and cannot find a name for it, and I would like to know whether one exists. Some statements are such that, in the very act of sincerely asserting them, one guarantees their falsity. The clearest instance is the old Cartesian one: a person who says, with understanding, "I am not thinking anything right now" makes the statement false by making it. And the structure is not confined to thinking. "I am not saying anything" and "I do not exist" seem to work the same way: each could easily have been true — during dreamless sleep the first and third plausibly are — so there is no contradiction in the content taken by itself; the trouble lies entirely in the relation between the content and the act of asserting it.

What strikes me is that this is different from two neighboring things. First, it is not the liar paradox or a statement like "every generalization is false": in those cases the content itself is internally unstable, and its truth value churns depending on how it is read. Here the content is perfectly ordinary and could have obtained; the sentence would have been true if read off a recording made an hour earlier. It is falsified only when asserted now, by its author. Second, it is not the same as oddities like "it is raining, but I don't believe it is," where there is no contradiction at all, merely something one cannot sincerely say. That statement can be true while being asserted, however awkwardly, whereas mine cannot.

Descartes is the obvious landmark, and I take it his famous conclusion is an instance: whatever else it establishes, the thought behind it is presumably that any attempt to doubt it confirms it. But I am not sure the famous inference is the same as my phenomenon. If it is an inference, it needs a premise — that thinking is occurring — and it is not clear how that premise gets established except by this very reflexive route. And the route seems to guarantee only that thinking is happening, or that an assertion is being made, not that there is a stable subject doing the thinking. Whether the phenomenon underwrites the agent as well as the act looks exactly like the sort of thing a proper carving of it should help keep straight.

So: is there an established term for statements whose sincere assertion guarantees their own falsity, while their contents remain contingent and could perfectly well have been true of the world? If there is, I want to know how the term draws its boundaries: how it separates these cases from self-referential paradoxes, from the merely unsayable, from assertions that merely presuppose what they deny (as with "I have completely forgotten this conversation," which strikes me as nearby but different, since it seems possibly true), and whether it extends to cases where the disconfirmation comes not from asserting but from, say, wondering, doubting, or perceiving. If no established term covers it, coin one, give a definition precise enough to decide the hard cases, and tell me what it would take for a member of this family to function as a proof rather than as a mere embarrassment.
