I help run an online philosophy of mind reading group that has been going for about a year, and it has basically split into two entrenched camps. The funny thing is they agree on a lot: everyone in the group is a dualist about consciousness in some sense, they all think Mary learns something new when she leaves the black-and-white room, and they all take the explanatory gap seriously. But one camp is epiphenomenalist (qualia are real but causally inert, the physical world is causally closed and does all the work) and the other is interactionist (qualia actually reach down and affect neural processing somehow). Their endless argument always circles back to what our group has nicknamed the "synchronized reels" problem, which I gather is basically what philosophers call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my experience of the redness of red does literally nothing, why can I open my mouth and produce sentences about it? But the interactionists never explain where the causal gap in physics is supposed to be, since every muscle twitch in my speech seems to trace back to prior physical causes with no leftover room. Recently one of them proposed a thought experiment that broke the group apart: imagine a neuroscientist with a complete scan of your brain who can predict every word you will ever say about your experiences, in advance, purely from the physical state. The epiphenomenalists said that's just obvious and even helpful for their view; the interactionists said it's compatible with interactionism as long as the physical causes only screen off the reports statistically rather than excluding the phenomenal contribution outright, and then everyone started shouting about overdetermination. I think they've been arguing past each other in a few places and I want to force a reckoning.

So here's what I'm asking you to do: act as a professional philosopher of mind who has been hired to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between these two camps, and draft the joint statement they should be able to produce after a year of good-faith engagement. Please think carefully before you write, and do some quiet brainstorming first about where the genuine cruxes are versus where the disagreement is just words (e.g., whether "causal" is being used in a productive/physical sense by one side and a broader dependence sense by the other -- if any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which part and why). The document should have: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, which should include the non-trivial agreements, not just banal ones -- things about the explanatory gap, about causal closure being at minimum an extremely well-confirmed empirical generalization, about the fact that specific experiences are correlated with specific reports and that this correlation demands some kind of explanation; (2) a table of disagreements where each row states one side's position in wording that side would actually endorse, and I want you to be strict about this, no strawman summaries; (3) the cruxes -- I suspect at least one is whether causal closure is a metaphysical necessity or just a provisional finding, and another is whether evolution could ever select for phenomenal richness if qualia are inert, since selection only sees things that make a causal difference -- and for each crux, describe the argument, case, or kind of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts it would show. Please also address the synchronized reels problem head on: what exactly is each camp's story about how our phenomenal judgments get to be about qualia, and is either story really worse off than the other? The epiphenomenalists in my group tend to hand-wave about "psychophysical laws" and the interactionists tend to hand-wave about "some causal slack in the brain," so if either move is doing less work than its users think, call that out in the statement. Aim for something neither camp could dismiss as rigged. There's no rush, take the space you need.
