For my medical law and ethics module I've got to write a case analysis, and I want to do the Manchester conjoined twins case from 2000 (usually cited as Re A). Quick recap of the facts so we're on the same page: the twins were born joined at the lower abdomen; one of them (the court called her Mary) had almost no heart function, no working lungs and severe brain damage, and was alive only because her sister Jodie's heart pumped blood to her through a shared artery. Left conjoined, doctors said both would die within roughly six months. Separation meant Jodie would very likely survive and Mary would certainly die. The parents were devout Catholics from Malta and refused the operation, the hospital went to court, and the Court of Appeal authorised the surgery, which went ahead in November — Mary died on the table, Jodie survived and went home.

What I want is to run this through the doing/allowing and killing/letting die literature rather than through the law's own reasoning, and to have it run in both directions. First the mapping: is the surgeon's intervention best described as a positive act that kills Mary, as withdrawing the life support Jodie's body was unknowingly providing, as redirecting a lethal process away from one patient onto another, or as none of the above? Then say where each side of the debate comes down — the view that the bare difference between doing and allowing is morally irrelevant (Rachels' line), and the view that the difference tracks something real, like who initiates a fatal process versus who merely declines to stop one. And then the reverse direction: what does the case do to the theory? My hunch is it's an anomaly the distinction was never built for — Mary isn't a patient on a machine whose treatment can simply be withdrawn, Jodie never agreed to sustain her and is in no position to decide for anyone, the fatal outcome is a certainty rather than a risk, and the three judges reached the same result by three different routes (one of them even framed it in terms of self-defence, which feels like a confession that the act/omission line had stopped doing any work).
