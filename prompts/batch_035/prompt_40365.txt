I cover science and ideas for a living, so I spend alot of time around people who measure things, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about Goodhart's law — the idea that when a measure becomes a target it stops measuring the thing. Here are my gut verdicts on cases I've either covered or read up on, and I'd like to know if there's a principle behind them.

The Soviet factory with a nail quota in tons that made a few giant nails: obviously gaming, obviously bad. Colonial Hanoi paying a bounty per rat tail, so people clipped tails and let the rats go on breeding: gaming, bad. My old newsroom switching to judging stories by pageviews and everyone pivoting to slideshows: bad, I watched it happen in real time. Journals gaming impact factor, whole citation-cartels-worth of it: bad. Schools teaching to a standardized test, with scores going up but kids learning no more: bad — and this one even when I think the test itself is pretty honest about measuring the thing we want.

Now the ones that make me uncomfortable. My current editor tracks completed reads — how many people who open a piece actually finish it — and I have absolutely gamed that metric: tighter intros, shorter paragraphs, no showboating. But I think the pieces got better, and I don't feel like a rogue nail factory. A university near me runs a bike-to-work challenge counted by swipe-ins, and some people swipe without biking; everyone calls that gaming, but it strikes me as barely bad at all, wierdly mild. And I'm genuinely torn on GDP: governments optimizing GDP feels like textbook Goodhart to me, even though GDP is honestly a decent proxy for things we care about — a much better proxy than completed reads is of 'good article', and I gave that one a pass.

So the closeness-of-proxy idea can't be doing all the work, and neither can whether people are faking versus actually changing behavior. Is there one principle that captures the whole list? My hunch is it has something to do with how much pressure gets applied or how much of the real goal the metric leaves on the floor, but I can't make both the completed-reads case and the GDP case come out right with either. Tell me what principle you'd offer, what it predicts on a case I haven't mentioned — say, a city that starts grading its police on case clearance rates — and which of my verdicts you'd tell me to just give up on.
