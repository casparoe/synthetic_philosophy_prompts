My friends Iris and Theo built a scheduling app together on nights and weekends, and a company just offered them $100k for the whole thing. One catch: they have to agree on how to split it, or the buyer walks. And if the buyer walks, it says it'll just license Iris's calendar-sync component from her alone for $30k — so Iris is guaranteed $30k no matter what, and Theo gets nothing without a deal. They had this arguement at my birthday dinner and made me write it all down because they want an outside opinion.

(1) Iris went first: she wants 65/35, on the grounds that each of them should come out equally far ahead of walking away. She'd gain $35k over her guaranteed $30k, and Theo would gain $35k over his zero. She says she remembers from a game theory MOOC that this is the actual Nash bargaining solution, so it's not just her opinion.

(2) Theo said equal absolute gains is rigged toward whoever starts ahead. His dad did union negotiations for 30 years and always pushed a rule he called proportional fairness: each side should end up with the same fraction of their best possible outcome. Theo's best case is $100k (if Iris took nothing), and Iris's best-case *gain* is only $70k (100 minus her guaranteed 30), so equalizing the fractions gets Theo roughly $41k of the 100. He looked it up later and says this is a real thing called the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, so it's not just his dad's opinion either.

(3) Iris came back with something that happened last month: a different buyer had floated paying $130k for a version of the app built on just Theo's design, contingent on Iris signing away her component rights for a flat $30k. That deal lapsed and was never happening anyway — but if you feed it into Theo's fractional rule, his share jumps to about $45k, even though the deal would have left Iris exactly where her fallback puts her regardless. Her point: a rule that moves the split because of an option nobody was ever going to take is broken, and her rule (the Nash one) doesn't budge no matter what dead options are lying around, as long as they're worse than what you actually agree on.

(4) Theo answered that her rule has the mirror-image problem. Suppose the licensing offer for Iris's component had come in at $50k instead of $30k. Nothing about his work or the deal itself changes, but her equal-gains split becomes 75/25 — he loses $10k purely because her outside options improved. A fair rule, he says, should be monotonic: when one side's options get better, the other side shouldn't end up worse off.

They asked me to referee, and honestly the math made my eyes glaze over. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about each point, flag anything they're both missing, and check whether the numbers they were quoting (65/35, about 59/41, about 45, and 75/25) actually come out the way they said? And if you had to recomend a split yourself, what would it be and why?
