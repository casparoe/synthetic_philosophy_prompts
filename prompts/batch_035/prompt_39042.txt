I co-teach a graduate seminar on machine minds, and I'm responsible for this year's take-home final. We've spent three weeks on reinforcement learning and sentience, and last year's exam produced a stack of essays that all made the same two moves: either functionalism is true, so RL agents could in principle suffer, or suffering requires biology, so they could not. I want one essay question that makes that recitation impossible.

The question should be answerable in about 1200 words over a 48-hour take-home. It must include, inside the question itself, a comparison of two agent designs: (i) a standard model-free agent whose policy was shaped by a reward signal during training but is now frozen and deployed, with reward playing no causal role in its ongoing behavior; and (ii) an agent equipped with a self-monitoring module that, at deployment, continuously estimates its own predicted return and adjusts its behavior so as to keep that estimate high. Students must argue which of the two, if either, is the better candidate for suffering — which forces them to decide whether suffering would attach to the historical process that shaped a system or to occurrent states the system is in now.

Two further requirements on the question's content. First, it should require students to use the phenomenon of pain asymbolia — patients who report feeling pain but are not bothered by it — to see that suffering may decompose into a feeling-bad component and a minding-that-feeling component, and to say which of these, if either, a reward signal could be. Second, it should require them to confront the wireheading case: if reward simply is valence, then an agent that has hijacked its own reward channel is in maximal bliss, and students must say whether that is a reductio of identifying reward with valence or a consequence the view can absorb.

Alongside the question, write a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme worth 25 points total. The marking scheme should name the four or five moves an A answer must contain, with point allocations. And — this is the part I care most about — for at least two of those moves, it should state explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer. My expectation, which you should feel free to confirm or correct, is that a B answer will treat the observation that reward is just a number as if it settled the matter, and will declare the frozen agent obviously mindless without explaining what exactly changed between training and deployment. An A answer has to do real work with that change.
