I'm teaching a mid-level undergrad seminar on distributive justice — 16 students, one 80-minute session. They've had some Rawls but haven't hit the "equality of what?" debate yet (welfare vs resources vs capabilities), and I'd rather not lecture it at them. Design an in-class activity where they have to actually divide something up and run into the problem themselves: they'll think they're arguing about the right answer to one question, but they should slowly discover they can't even agree what it would MEAN for the distribution to be fair, because no one ever specified equal in terms of what.

Here's the wrinkle I definitely want built in: after they settle on a first distribution, open a trading phase where the recipients can exchange what they got, and then re-vote on whether the post-trade outcome still counts as fair. Some students will feel the trades wash out the original equalization (so equality of resources is unstable once you respect choice), others will say respecting the trades is the whole point of fairness — and I want the class stuck there, because that's the actual fight.

Also, somewhere in there I want a playable version of an envy-test style rule — anyone can propose a full bundle swap and the group sees who objects — so they feel what that rule does and doesn't settle.

Practical requirements: character profiles that pull the metrics apart (a recipient with expensive needs or tastes, one who can't convert the goods into anything they value, one who's content with very little, etc.) — but please make them non-obvious, not the standard textbook examples, and tell me if any of the profiles quietly bake in one side's assumptions, because I don't want the activity rigged. Rules, a timeline that fits 80 minutes, a way to run payoffs without real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual positions in the debate without me lecturing more than ~10 minutes at the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which parts of the debate the activity just cannot reproduce no matter how well it goes.

One more thing, and this matters to me: in the debrief notes, keep what's settled clearly separated from what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature — e.g. flag where you're just giving me a standard gloss vs where scholars actually still disagree (whether the capabilities metric can even be operationalized is I think one of these but you'd know better). I don't want to accidentally teach a contested reading as the consensus. If your not sure on something, say so rather than papering over it.
