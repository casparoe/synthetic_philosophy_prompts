At a reunion of the clinical research unit where I spent my career before retiring, two former colleagues fell into an argument I couldn't referee, and I've since gotten interested enough in the philosophy of it to want a careful verdict. The occasion was a retrospective database our unit helped assemble: a drug given for a common cardiovascular condition, compared against standard care across two hospitals. At St. Ambrose, the tertiary referral center that gets the sickest patients, the drug arm died at 15% (90 of 600) versus 20% on standard care (40 of 200). At the rural regional hospital, the drug arm died at 1.5% (6 of 400) versus 2% on standard care (100 of 5,000). Pooled across both hospitals, the drug arm died at about 9.6% versus about 2.7% for standard care — a spectacular reversal, since the drug was used far more at the sick hospital and far less at the healthy one.

The argument ran as follows. (1) Hanna, our biostatistician, said the pooled comparison is a statistical artifact, full stop: the drug lowers mortality in both hospitals, and any colleague who read only the aggregate table would be moved to withhold a beneficial treatment; the "real effect" is the one that holds patient mix fixed. (2) Jonas, the trial physician, replied that the pooled table isn't an error at all — it answers a different and perfectly genuine question. Treated patients in this system died at nearly four times the rate of untreated ones, and if you're a patient who doesn't get to choose whether your region is served by a tertiary center, that's a fact about the world you actually live in. (3) Hanna said it's a fact about how the drug is allocated, not about the drug, and the whole point of analysis is to separate those. (4) Jonas countered that for a health system deciding whether to roll the drug out, the allocation pattern isn't separable from the drug's effect — you don't get the molecule without the prescribing culture that comes with it. (5) Hanna conceded the roll-out question was fair but said that just shows the statistic you report must be chosen using causal judgment about who gets the drug and why, which is knowledge no table contains; statistics without that judgment is blind, and that's the real lesson of the reversal. (6) Jonas took the concession further than Hanna intended: if the choice between the pooled and stratified tables isn't settled by the data but by which question you bring to it, then "does the drug work?" has no context-free answer at all, and the famous "paradox" is no paradox — just two questions whose answers happen to disagree.

They left it there. Now that I've retired and have been reading philosophy of science, I'd like a move-by-move adjudication of their six moves: rate each as clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partly right and say precisely what the error or insight in it is. Along the way, please tell me whether Hanna's claim in (5) — that the lesson is the need for extra-statistical causal judgment — and Jonas's claim in (6) — that the lesson is the question-relativity of "the effect" — are genuinely rival conclusions or two sides of the same coin, and what, if anything, both of them missed.
