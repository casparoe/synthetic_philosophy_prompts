I lead a three-person technical due-diligence group at a venture fund that backs startups whose engineering rests on young science — quantum hardware, compact fusion, synthetic biology. Before each investment the team produces a one-page reality memo for every load-bearing scientific posit in a company's plan, classifying it as either bedrock (safe to underwrite the investment on), scaffolding (a working device that could be revised without the company's core value proposition dying), or load-bearing-and-fragile (the plan dies if the posit falls). We have about two weeks per deal, and the investment committee wants a definite verdict on each posit, not a survey of options.

One colleague of mine takes the pessimistic meta-induction seriously — the observation that many posits of past science that were empirically successful (the ether, caloric) were later discarded, so some of ours presumably will be too — and has derived from it what he calls the depreciation heuristic: treat confidence in a posit like a depreciating asset that decays with time since the posit's last novel predictive success, where a novel prediction means one that risked failure, not a fit to data the posit was invented to accommodate. Another colleague counters with something like entity realism: if engineers have to treat the posit as a real, manipulable thing in order to make instruments and processes work at all, treat it as bedrock regardless of how long it has been since anyone wrung a risky prediction out of it.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run on a single posit within a day: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with bedrock, where with scaffolding, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values — how much risk we are willing to concentrate on one fragile posit — rather than of fact. The procedure should incorporate, amend, or explicitly reject the depreciation heuristic, and you must take a definite position on which of those three to do and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to us. Then run the procedure on two hypothetical posits: (a) a startup whose plasma-confinement scheme rests on a mechanism with a twenty-year record of fitting data but no genuinely risky predictions, which the engineers nevertheless manipulate daily in building their hardware; and (b) a startup resting on a posit that made one startling novel prediction last year, since confirmed, but which no one can yet manipulate or use to intervene in anything. At least one of these should come out in a way my team will find unwelcome — say which, and why the procedure nonetheless yields that answer. Finally, tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
