I've been reading a lot of the old adaptationism fights lately — the fallout from the spandrels paper and the decades of argument that followed it — and I keep running into a configuration I don't have a proper name for. Whenever you actually pin down what the two sides disagreed about, it wasn't the facts. Everyone in that debate concedes that selection can't do everything, that drift, developmental constraints and mere byproducts are real, and even the most gung-ho adaptationist will admit, if pressed, that some traits are spandrels. And the critics conceede that selection is the only thing that even comes close to explaining complex function. So on paper it looks like there was nothing left to fight about, and people periodically announced that the whole thing was a verbal dispute that's now settled. But it obviously wasn't settled, and here's what I think the actual disagreement was: over what you should assume first when you meet a trait with no story on the table either way. The adaptationist wants "it's an adaptation" to be the starting point that has to be overturned, and the critic wants there to be no starting point at all until someone brings evidence. Same facts, opposite defaults. I've since noticed the same shape elsewere — the nativism debates about language and mind seem to run on it too: nobody on either side claims learning or innate structure don't exist, the fight is entirely about which one you presuppose when the evidence runs out.

Is there an established concept for this exact configuration? "Burden of proof" is the nearest phrase I know, but it doesn't feel like it captures it, because burden of proof is usually about one side having to argue for a contested claim, whereas here both sides agree on every claim in the neighborhood — what they disagree about is the null setting, the thing you revert to when the evidence is silent. It's also not a mere verbal dispute, since which default you pick ends up determining what gets studied and what gets hand-waved, which is a pretty real difference. If philosophy of science or argumentation theory or anywhere else has a standard term for it, I'd love to hear it, but I won't be satisfied with a bare label — what I really want is the carving: what exactly is the disagreement in, if not in facts (they share those) and not in meanings (they mostly share those too)? Is it just a disagreement about priors, or is there something more structural that distinguishes it from any old priority dispute? And if nothing in the literature fits, I'd want you to coin a term and give it a definition with actual edges, so I can tell when I'm looking at this thing and when I'm not.
