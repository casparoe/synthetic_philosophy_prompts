My housemate is the kind of person who reads longtermism stuff at breakfast, and last night he tried a new move on me: he said the simulation argument should make him *less* fanatical about the far future, not more, and by the end I half believed him and half thought the argument proves way too much. It went like this. (1) The fanaticism comes from the astronomical size of the far future — the thought that ours might be the century that determines whether trillions upon trillions of future lives ever happen. (2) But the simulation argument gives us serious credence that we're inside an ancestor simulation rather than at the base level of reality. (3) If we're simulated, then for a start, the "far future" only extends as far as our simulators keep the lights on, and there's no reason to expect they run us for a billion years — machines get switched off, grants expire, researchers lose interest. (4) And even if they do run us long, it's the simulators' world that's real while ours is a story on their hard drive, so our descendants' flourishing is at best derivative and maybe doesn't count for much at all. (5) So the expected value of shaping the far future has to be discounted by our credence that we're simulated, which is at least non-trivial, and that guts the astronomically large payoff that fanaticism feeds on. (6) So he should actually shift effort back toward people who exist now.

Here's why I don't trust it: swap in "giant asteroid" or "vacuum decay" for "simulators shut us off" and you get the same shape of argument, but longtermists already fold extinction risk into their expected value — a small probability of the far future still carries astronomical weight, that's literally the fanaticism thing. And the "our world isn't the real one" move would prove that nothing long-term is worth doing — saving for retirement, planting trees, writing books — for anyone with any credence in brains in vats, which describes basically everyone, yet nobody draws that conclusion. But when I pushed him he had an answer I couldn't fully dismiss: extinction risks like asteroids are more or less constant background hazards, whereas ancestor simulations are plausibly run *because* of the interesting decision points, and shut down once the researchers' question is answered — so the hazard isn't spread evenly, it concentrates exactly where the far future is supposed to begin. Which would make it disanalogous to ordinary extinction risk in a way that actually matters for the math.

So: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Is the shutoff-concentration point a genuine difference from asteroid risk, and if it is, what does the fanatic have to give up to absorb it? Or is one of his premises just false or way overpriced — e.g. the claim that simulated value doesn't count, given that the simulation argument itself needs simulated people to be numerous and real-ish to get going? If you think the argument is valid all the way through, tell me which premise is least costly to deny and what denying it costs.
