A few weeks ago I read a popular article — I think in a weekend supplement, and I no longer have it to hand — that summarised the Knobe effect for lay readers. The gist of the passage, as best I can reconstruct it, was this: 'Joshua Knobe's famous experiments showed that our moral judgements determine whether we say someone acted intentionally. In the original study, the chairman of a company is told that a new policy will increase profits but also harm the environment; he replies that he doesn't care about the environment and only wants the profit, and approves it anyway. Nearly everyone asked said that he had intentionally harmed the environment, even though the harm was a foreseen side effect rather than something he was aiming at. Knobe took this to prove that whether a side effect counts as intentional depends on whether we disapprove of it, and philosophers now agree that "intentionally" is an essentially moral word: to say that someone did something on purpose is really just a disguised way of blaming them. The same article noted a companion finding: when the policy instead happens to help the environment, and the chairman again says he only cares about profit, most people say that he did not intentionally help the environment — because, the article explained, we tend to give people credit only for what they actually aim at. So when a driver who has caused a crash says "I didn't do it on purpose", they are not really describing their state of mind at all; they are simply refusing to accept blame, and we should hear such denials accordingly.'

I came away feeling I understood something important, and the story does have a satisfying shape. But two things nag at me. First, the paragraph's explanation of the helping case — that we credit people only for what they aim at — seems to pull in a rather different direction from the earlier claim that our moral judgements determine what we call intentional; I can't work out whether these are the same mechanism or two different ones being quietly run together. Second, the closing remark about the driver feels like it is using a finding about how people use a word in surveys to say something about what people mean when they speak in earnest, and I'm not sure that leap is licensed. I have no training in philosophy of action or experimental philosophy, so I would be grateful for a careful audit: please go through the passage more or less sentence by sentence, saying what a working experimental philosopher would accept, what they would treat as a simplification, and what is simply inaccurate — for instance, how settled the interpretation actually is, and how far the headline finding really generalises. Then tell me whether the article's final claim about denials like 'I didn't do it on purpose' follows from the parts of the summary that survive the audit, and if not, what a more defensible version of that closing thought would be.
