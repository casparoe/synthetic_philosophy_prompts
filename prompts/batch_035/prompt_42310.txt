I run partnerships at a small foundation that fiscally sponsors advocacy nonprofits, and more and more of my week goes to evaluating proposals where two groups with genuinely different moral views want to do something together. A friend who did a philosophy PhD told me what I'm doing has a name in the literature, moral trade, and the gist as she explained it is: if you and I disagree about what matters morally, we can often each make a concession that's cheap by our own lights but valuable by the other's lights, so we both come out ahead by our own standards. The problem is that right now I'm evaluating these on vibes, and the vibes are not scaling. We get maybe one or two of these proposals a month, they typically involve somewhere between ten and fifty thousand dollars plus staff time, and I have about a week to make a recommendation, with the board having to sign off on anything over a hundred grand. Nobody on my team (me included) has any philosophy training, so whatever my friend told me over dinner doesn't survive contact with an actual decision memo.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run on one of these cross-values proposals: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it can't decide and has to hand things off to the humans because the question is one of values or trust rather than facts. I care about it being honest about where it will misfire — my friend kept saying things like the hard part is that both sides have to be able to verify the other actually delivered, and that sometimes what looks like a trade is really one side converting the other, which I guess is not the same thing at all. So please mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three real-ish proposals from my inbox this quarter. First: an animal advocacy group would publicly back a public health charity's school deworming program (they care about it basically zero) in exchange for the health charity endorsing plant-based lunch options in the same schools. Second: a conservation org wants to restore a big forest corridor on land we hold, and a small wild-animal-focused group (their view is that most wild animals live short lives full of suffering, so more animals can be worse) has offered to co-fund it if the conservation org switches to restoring a coastal wetland they beleive supports fewer sentient creatures per acre. I'll admit I love this one, forests are my thing, so flag it honestly if the procedure comes out against me. Third: a faith-based homeless shelter and a secular harm-reduction group want to co-run a program where each promotes the other's services, but the shelter's people think needle exchange is morally wrong even while conceding it saves lives, and I genuinely can't tell if that kills the trade or not. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like a long email — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — since it's going into a memo template that strips all that out anyway.
