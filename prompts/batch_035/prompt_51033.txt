I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my position on epistemic injustice ahead of our reading group's final session on Fricker, so the others can press on it. Before I send it around, I'd like a consistency audit. Here is the position as it currently stands:

(a) Miranda Fricker's taxonomy is correct: the two central forms are testimonial injustice (a credibility judgment deflated by prejudice in the hearer) and hermeneutical injustice (a gap in shared interpretive resources that leaves someone unable to make sense of an experience), and both are wrongs to a person specifically in her capacity as a knower.

(b) Hermeneutical injustice is structural through and through. Where the collective pool simply lacks the concepts, no individual is at fault, however regrettable the gap; blaming individuals for pre-conceptual gaps is a category mistake.

(c) Testimonial injustice, by contrast, is paradigmatically an individual failing — prejudice in the hearer — and its primary remedy is the virtue of testimonial justice: a corrective habit in individual hearers, whereby they check their credibility judgments against the operation of prejudice in themselves.

(d) Willful hermeneutical ignorance is nonetheless real and blameworthy: once interpretive concepts are publicly available, an agent's motivated refusal to take them up can be culpable, even though the original gap was nobody's fault.

(e) I resist identity-based shortcuts in credibility: no one's group membership by itself confers epistemic authority, and credibility should track evidence of reliability rather than demographic position. At the same time, I accept a deflated standpoint thesis: people who are systematically marginalized tend, under favorable conditions, to understand the workings of the social world better than the privileged do, because their position forces them to learn the dominant world while the dominant need not learn theirs.

(f) Finally, a case verdict I'm fairly confident of: a physician who discounts a woman's self-reported symptoms partly on the basis of diagnostic statistics that are themselves an artifact of decades of under-study of women's health commits testimonial injustice — and does so even if his statistical inference is locally impeccable and he holds no prejudice at all.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, ranking the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set; for each merely apparent one, show how it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I honestly don't know where the problems lie, so please don't pull your punches on any of (a)–(f), including the ones I sound most confident about.
