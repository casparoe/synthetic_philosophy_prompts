Plant and animal breeders, who developed the heritability coefficient, treat it as a strictly local fact. They estimate it afresh for every herd, field and flock; they expect it to change when feeding, housing or husbandry changes; and they would never apply a coefficient estimated in one environment to a very different one, still less treat it as saying anything about how far the trait could be moved by improving conditions for everyone. In public debate, the same statistic has a very different career. Ministers, officials and columnists quote twin-study figures as though they were stable properties of the traits themselves: intelligence is said to be 50 to 80 per cent heritable, so compensatory schooling can achieve little; body weight is said to be substantially heritable, so taxes on sugary drinks cannot much change a population's weight — and this despite the populations in question having grown several centimetres taller and considerably heavier within a few generations, while heritability within each generation stayed high. What view of heritability does this debating practice presuppose: that the coefficient measures the depth at which genes fix a trait, that it bounds the reach of environmental policy, or something else again? Is either reading actually required to license citing the figures, or does the practice sit comfortably with several interpretations, some defensible and some confused? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change in how such figures are used if the breeders' local interpretation were put in charge.
