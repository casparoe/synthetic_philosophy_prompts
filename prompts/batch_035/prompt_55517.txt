Until this term I had never thought about vagueness with anything at stake. I sit on my university's student hardship fund committee, and this year we were told to stop deciding cases one by one and write a rule instead: applicants with savings below a fixed figure qualify automatically, applicants above it go to a discretionary panel. Writing that number down bothered me in a way I'm still trying to articulate, and it has made me realize that the views on vagueness I've carried around since a philosophy of language course years ago may not be a package I could actually defend if pressed. Here is the set:

(1) Vagueness is not in the world. There are grains of sand, hair follicles, and students with bank balances; reality itself is fully determinate, and terms like "heap," "bald," and "in hardship" are vague only because our concepts are somehow incomplete. There is no fact out there that we are failing to detect.

(2) I am not giving up classical logic. The deviant approaches — truth-value gaps, degrees of truth, and so on — strike me as paying far too much for the puzzle. Excluded middle and modus ponens stay.

(3) There is no sharp boundary. There is no number n such that n grains is a heap and n+1 is not, and no figure such that £X is severe hardship and £X+1 is not. Any theory that entails such a boundary has gone wrong somewhere.

(4) The endpoints are completely fine. Ten thousand grains properly arranged is a heap; a single grain is not. A student with no income and nothing in the bank is in hardship. I would assert these without hesitation, and I don't regard that as negotiable.

(5) My official line on the sorites is that some premise must be false: I refuse to accept that one grain is a heap, and I refuse to give up the induction or the inferences, so a conditional somewhere along the chain has to fail. But I also find the tolerance thought — that one grain can never make the difference — as obvious as anything in this area. And I cannot say which conditional fails; I'm inclined to think there's no fact of that sort to be found.

(6) Epistemicism seems to me a reductio of itself. The picture on which there is a real boundary that we are somehow constitutively unable to know — I take that to be a failed theory, not a live option.

(7) Here is what the committee work added. Once our rule was in force, I became convinced that it was genuinely sharp: it is now simply true that an applicant with £2,000 in savings qualifies and that one with £8,000 is referred. Yet the vagueness did not go away; it reappeared just above and below the line, in cases that were clearly close in a way the clearly distant ones were not. So I now hold both that our stipulation created a real boundary and that the borderline phenomenon survived the stipulation intact.

What I would like is an audit rather than a survey. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, which only appear to be, and how severe is each conflict? For the real ones, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost elsewhere in the set — I want the trade-offs made explicit, not just the incompatibilities. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting me with a list of equally weighted worries. And since I can't tell whether the committee experience is actually doing any philosophical work here: does (7) bear on the heap case the way it feels like it does, or is it a red herring that has distracted me from whatever the real problem in the list is?
