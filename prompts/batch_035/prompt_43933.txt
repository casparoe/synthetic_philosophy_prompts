I recently joined the three-person grants committee of a small foundation that funds work in global health, family planning, and policies affecting future generations. My background is in economics, not philosophy, but I've discovered I need the philosophy anyway: last month the committee deadlocked over two family-planning programs — one that would bring fewer people into existence with comfortably good lives, one that would bring more people into existence with lives only barely above any level we'd consider decent — and two of us found the answer obvious in opposite directions. Both sides eventually appealed, loosely, to population ethics: one side to something like the repugnant conclusion and its relatives, the other to the thought that people who never exist can't be harmed or benefited. We could vote, but I'd rather understand the structure of our disagreement than win it.

Please respond as a senior population ethicist advising a funder, and start with a short brainstorm: lay out the candidate research questions in population axiology you would even consider putting on our list, so I can see the space before you rank it. Then give me a defended ordering of the top three or so. Candidates you might weigh include: whether the impossibility results — the apparent fact that no theory satisfies all of our core intuitions about population — mean the most useful work is now about deciding which intuitions to abandon rather than constructing new theories; whether person-affecting views can be made coherent enough to actually guide policy, or whether their well-known technical problems doom them to decorative status; whether the most valuable work is instead on decision-making under moral uncertainty, i.e., what to do when reasonable population views conflict; and whether anything else belongs on the list that I haven't thought of.

For each item in your ranking, defend the position: say why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for philosophers rather than economists or demographers, how neglected it currently is, and what would count as evidence that it should move up or down the list over the next few years. Throughout, keep our practical constraint in view — we care about answers that could plausibly change grantmaking within a decade, not just theoretical elegance. But be honest with me if the truth is that nothing in population axiology is yet decision-relevant at our scale, and our committee should stop arguing about Parfit over coffee and go back to the cost-effectiveness spreadsheets; I'd rather hear that now than in five years.
