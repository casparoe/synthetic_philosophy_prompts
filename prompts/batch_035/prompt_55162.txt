I'm TAing an intermediate game theory course and grading final exams right now, and I want a second opinion on one script before I commit to a mark. The question was:

"Consider the following centipede-style game of perfect information. Players 1 and 2 alternate moves; at each of three decision nodes, the mover chooses either Take (ending the game immediately, with payoffs as listed) or Pass (moving the game to the next node). At node 1, Player 1 moves: Take yields payoffs (4, 1), Pass continues. At node 2, Player 2 moves: Take yields (2, 8), Pass continues. At node 3, Player 1 moves: Take yields (16, 4), Pass ends the game with payoffs (8, 32). (a) Find the subgame perfect equilibrium via backward induction and state the equilibrium path payoffs. (b) State precisely what the backward induction argument assumes about the players. (c) The conclusion of this argument is widely regarded as paradoxical — explain what the puzzle is supposed to be. (d) In laboratory experiments, subjects routinely pass several times before anyone takes. Does this show the argument is wrong?"

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) Work backwards through the tree. At node 3, Player 1 compares Take (16, 4) with Pass (8, 32) and takes, since 16 > 8. At node 2, Player 2 anticipates this and compares Take (2, 8) with the expected outcome of passing, (16, 4); since 4 < 8, she takes. At node 1, Player 1 compares Take (4, 1) with the outcome of passing, (2, 8), and takes, since 4 > 2. So the unique subgame perfect equilibrium is Take at every decision node, and the game ends immediately with payoffs (4, 1). Notice that taking at node 1 is Player 1's dominant strategy, which is why this outcome is so robust.

(b) The argument needs both players to be rational, i.e. to maximize expected payoff given their beliefs, and each to know that the other is rational, so Player 1 can predict Player 2's choice at node 2 and Player 2 can predict Player 1's choice at node 3. Since the game has perfect information, no further assumptions are required — everything is out in the open.

(c) The paradox is that both players end up worse off than they could have: if they had both passed to the end of the tree they would have gotten (8, 32) instead of (4, 1). It's essentially the prisoner's dilemma in sequential form — individually rational play produces a collectively irrational result, and neither player can gain by deviating on their own.

(d) The experiments show that the prediction fails in practice but they don't refute the backward induction logic, which is valid as a piece of reasoning. What fails is the rationality assumption: subjects who pass are just not rational, or don't understand the game. Once you drop rationality, passing can make sense, but then we've left the domain of game theory entirely."
