I'd like some help adjudicating an argument that played out at our workplace over several days and that I, as the note-taker, was ultimately asked to referee — a job I don't feel equal to.

The context: our team is designing a performance dashboard for a mid-sized logistics company, combining on-time delivery rates, customer satisfaction scores, and cost-per-shipment figures. During the design meetings, two colleagues — I'll call them Dana and Farid — kept returning to the same clash about Goodhart's law (the observation that when a measure becomes a target, it tends to stop functioning well as a measure of what you actually care about).

Round one: Dana opened by claiming that the law is essentially a death sentence for the whole project. Any metric we install will eventually be gamed — drivers will hit the on-time number by cutting corners elsewhere, or the satisfaction score will be farmed with discount coupons. So instead of a dashboard, we should go back to experienced managers exercising holistic judgment.

Round two: Farid accused Dana of overgeneralizing. A thermostat optimizes against a thermometer all day long, and the thermometer stays perfectly accurate, because nothing in the system has an incentive to fool it. Goodhart's law only bites, Farid argued, when a measure is a *proxy* — tracking the goal imperfectly, through a causal chain that agents can exploit — rather than *constitutive* of the goal. On-time delivery, he suggested, is partly constitutive of good logistics: you can't fully game it without failing at the very thing it's part of. So the task is to prefer constitutive or tightly-coupled measures, not to give up on measurement.

Round three: Dana said the thermostat example smuggles in a load-bearing assumption: that the measured system contains no strategic agents. Replace the molecules with drivers, dispatchers, and warehouse staff, and every measure becomes a proxy, because now something in the system *cares* about the reading. As for "constitutive," Dana called it a sleight of hand: on-time delivery is constitutive only because we quietly define customer satisfaction so as to include it. You can always gerrymander the goal to match your preferred metric, so the constitutive/proxy distinction does no real work — it just launders whatever metric you were going to pick anyway.

Round four: Farid conceded that agents change the game, but denied that this licenses despair. Gaming, he argued, requires two things: slack between the proxy and the goal, and an incentive gradient pointed at the proxy. Both are engineering variables. You can shrink the slack by combining metrics so that gaming one degrades another (cutting corners hurts the satisfaction score; discount farming hurts cost-per-shipment), by auditing, and by rotating or fuzzing measures so no fixed target exists long enough to be exploited. The right conclusion from Goodhart's law is that metric design is a permanent, iterative discipline — not that we should replace it with something that has exactly the same vulnerabilities and none of the transparency.

Round five: Dana closed with two points. First, Farid's program is whack-a-mole: every patch spawns new exploits, and the resources consumed by that arms race are themselves a cost — call it the Goodhart tax — which has to be weighed against the dashboard's benefits and may well exceed them. Second, holistic judgment has a structural advantage no metric can match: because it isn't a fixed, legible target, it can't be optimized against. Managers can weigh context and reweight what they attend to, which is precisely what a number on a screen can never do.

I confess I can see force in almost every move. Please adjudicate the exchange round by round: say who is right about which specific point (including where each of them makes a decent point poorly, or a bad point decorously), flag anything both of them are missing, and render an overall verdict on what our team should actually conclude about the dashboard.
