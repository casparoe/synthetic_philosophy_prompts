I'm making a handout for a philosophy of mind reading group on the color phi phenomenon — two dots flash in sequence, different colors, and subjects report seeing a single dot that moves and changes color partway across, even though the color info for the second dot isn't available until after the first "moving" percept would've been formed. People in the group throw around "Orwellian" and "Stalinesque" like they're the only two options, and I want to show the logical space is actually bigger.

Can you build a decision tree that takes someone from accepting the basic experimental result to a position on the timing of experience? Branch nodes should be questions a person can answer without knowing which label it leads them to — things like: is there a fact of the matter about *when* the subject experienced the color change, or only about what they'll say and remember? does conscious experience have to be assembled somewhere in particular in the brain before it "plays"? can the content of an experience depend on input that arrives later without that being either a memory edit or an illusion? Try to build the tree from those kinds of questions rather than from a list of named views.

Leaves should include the Orwellian view (the experience happened mid-motion and memory gets revised after the fact), the Stalinesque view (the brain holds things back and edits before the show is broadcast), Dennett's line that there's no fact of the matter about when the experience happened, and anything else you think really occupies the space — maybe a view on which retroactive content-filling is just how temporal representation normally works, with no editing anywhere. For each combination of answers that nobody actually holds, say whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or possibly unexplored. And flag any two leaves the tree can't genuinely pull apart with questions like these. Maybe 6 or 7 nodes max — it's a one-page handout, not a monograph.
