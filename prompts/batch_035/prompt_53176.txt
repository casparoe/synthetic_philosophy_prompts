Something I keep chewing over: Augustine says lying is always wrong, no exceptions, not even to save someone from a murderer, and he more or less defines a lie by whether your words match what's actually in your head — the wrongness lives in the mismatch between speech and the speaker's mind. Fast forward a thousandish years and you get the Catholic doctrine of mental reservation: a priest hiding from Elizabethan pursuers could answer a question with words that were secretly true in his own head — like saying he hasn't seen a man when he means he hasn't seen him in the last five seconds, or hasn't seen him in his dreams — and count that as not lying, even though the entire point was that the pursuer takes the words the opposite way. So my question: what does the practice of mental reservation actually presuppose about what makes lying wrong?

It looks like it buys Augustine's definition wholesale — if there's no speech-contrary-to-mind, there's no lie, so the trick is clever and internally coherent — while quietly dumping the rest of his view, the part where lying is bad because you deceive your neighbor and that's bad for them and for you. Which feels like keeping the letter while torching the spirit. Or maybe the practice doesn't presuppose anything about harm at all, and the real commitment is something like: you may mislead men, but you may not utter a falsehood, period, because of what speech owes to God rather than to the hearer. Sort that out for me.

Specifically, I want you to reconstruct what the doctrine only makes sense under, which rival views of lying it quietly rules out (a Kantian-ish view where the wrong is in treating the hearer as a thing to be manipulated, a consequentialist view where it's all about bad outcomes for the deceived person, a view where the wrong is located in the deception itself — inducing a false belief), and whether the pieces of the practice presuppose consistent things. Then tell me what happens if you swap in the view that the wrong of lying is the deception, not the word-mind mismatch: does mental reservation collapse into plain lying with extra steps, or does it survive as something genuinely different? And last thing — there's an everyday version of this, like answering 'not here' about a friend hiding in your attic, or a teenager saying 'I've been out' while hiding in the house. Does that everyday practice need the same heavy theological commitments, or can it be defended on much cheaper grounds? I suspect the two cases come apart but I can't pin down where.
