I spent most of my career in physical chemistry, where a sentence like "water is H2O" is about as close to a necessary truth as anything gets, so I have been trying, in retirement, to understand what all the philosophical fuss over that sentence is about. A popular book I have been reading explains it roughly as follows: Kripke and Putnam showed that natural-kind terms like "water" are rigid designators, refuting the old descriptive theory of reference; two-dimensional semantics was then invented to sneak the descriptive view back in, holding that a term like "water" secretly carries a second meaning alongside the real one, something like the watery stuff found in our oceans and rivers, and that this second meaning fixes what the term picks out in each possible situation. Because this second meaning is available to speakers prior to any empirical inquiry, the book concludes that two-dimensional semantics establishes that every necessary truth is knowable a priori. It then adds that David Chalmers used the same apparatus to demonstrate that zombies -- beings physically identical to us but lacking consciousness -- are genuinely possible, and that this refutes physicalism about the mind.

The presentation intrigues me, but I cannot tell whether I am being told about a technical semantic framework for distinguishing different ways of evaluating sentences at possible worlds, or about a bundle of metaphysical theses, and the book slides freely between the two. Could you audit the summary for me? Which parts would a philosopher working in this area accept, which are harmless simplifications, and which are genuine conflations -- in particular, does two-dimensional semantics really claim that "water" means the watery stuff, or does it do something else with that function, and is the step from conceivability to possibility a premise of the zombie argument or something the argument establishes? I would also like to know whether the main proponents -- Stalnaker, Jackson, Chalmers -- actually agree that all necessity is a priori, or whether the book has merged distinct and partly opposed views into a single doctrine. Finally, please tell me whether the book's last claim, that physicalism about consciousness has been refuted, follows from whichever parts of the summary turn out to be accurate. Please answer in American English.
