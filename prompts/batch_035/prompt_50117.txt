I produce a methods-focused podcast for working scientists, and our next guest is a philosopher of statistics whose new book argues that the replication crisis vindicates, rather than discredits, frequentist error control. In his telling, the crisis came from abandoning error probabilities — optional stopping, post hoc switching of hypotheses, trying analyses until something cleared the bar — while the discipline drifted toward Bayesian measures that, by the likelihood principle, cannot register how a dataset was actually produced. He defends a severity account of evidence: a result supports a hypothesis only insofar as the procedure had a low probability of producing so favorable a result had the hypothesis been false. Draft exactly five interview questions for him, as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion; each item should be one or two sentences, the question itself plus, where useful, a single compressed follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. Collectively the questions must cover: the case of two experimenters who end up with identical data and hence the same likelihood function, one having fixed the sample size in advance and the other having sampled until a nominal significance threshold was crossed, so that only frequentist machinery treats them differently — he must say which one is reasoning badly and why; the Lindley-style clash in which a very large study yields both a conventional rejection of the null and a posterior that strongly favors the null, and which answer a working scientist should act on; what severity genuinely adds to ordinary power analysis, given that flexible analysis looks like exactly the ability to satisfy any nominal severity requirement several different ways; and the pragmatist's complaint that preregistration polices process while saying nothing about the one thing anyone in the lab actually wants to know, namely whether this particular effect is real. Use the remaining question to press him toward a concession — a case or consideration that might make him give ground — rather than another invitation to restate the book.
