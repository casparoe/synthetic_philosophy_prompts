I retired from a career in theoretical physics a few years ago, and since then I have been working through philosophy of science and decision theory as a devoted amateur. I recently encountered the so-called Pasadena game, introduced by Harris Nover and Alan Hájek in Mind in 2004. As I understand it, the original construction has a fair coin tossed until it first lands heads, with payoffs that grow without bound while alternating between reward and penalty, so that the expectation becomes a conditionally convergent series whose sum depends on the order of summation. To keep the mathematics clean, consider a cruder variant in the same spirit: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, on toss n; if it does, you receive (−2) to the power n dollars, a reward when n is even and a penalty when n is odd, with probability 2 to the power minus n. Each term of the expectation is then exactly (−1) to the power n, so the would-be expected value is the series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + …, which diverges by oscillation. Philosophers seem to conclude that such games simply have no expected value at all, and that rational choice theory must somehow limp along without one.

I confess my physicist's instincts rebel against this defeatism. In quantum field theory we confronted badly divergent quantities for half a century and extracted sensible answers anyway, by introducing a regulator, computing the quantity of interest, and then carefully removing the regulator; renormalisation, for all the abuse it took in its youth, underwrites the most precisely confirmed predictions in the history of science. And our toy series above has a distinguished candidate value of one half, obtained alike by Abel summation and by evaluating the geometric series at the boundary of its disc of convergence. It is tempting to conclude that decision theorists simply lack our toolkit: introduce a suitable summability or regularisation convention, let the Pasadena-style game acquire the value one half, and rational choice proceeds exactly as before.

Please argue against this temptation of mine. That is, argue that the physicist's machinery of regularisation and summability cannot legitimately be transplanted into decision theory to restore well-defined expected values for games like the one I described. I would like you to press on wherever you see the genuine disanalogy between assigning a value to a divergent series as an act of formal bookkeeping and using that value to guide a choice — for instance, whether any convention can earn the sort of choice-relevant authority that symmetry principles or experimental success earn in physics, and what becomes of the convention when two natural regularisation schemes disagree about the same game. Please respond in British English, and in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would compose a careful letter to a colleague.
