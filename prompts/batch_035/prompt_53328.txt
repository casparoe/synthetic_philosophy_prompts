In my phil language seminar the deflationists have this move where they just write out the equivalence schema — 'snow is white' is true iff snow is white — and act like that settles whatever we were discussing. Most recently someone used it to wave off a question about what makes it the case that 'water is wet' is true, like the schema shows there's nothing there to ask about. I get that the schema itself is hard to deny, but I don't see how it gets them from there to the dismissal, and I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs at once. Can you untangle what the schema is actually supposed to be claiming here — e.g. is it just the trivial biconditional that everyone including correspondence theorists accepts, is it that the truth predicate is semantically redundant once you can just say the sentence, is it that the predicate exists mainly so we can generalize over claims we haven't enumerated ('everything in the report is true'), is it the metaphysical claim that truth isn't a real property, or something about there being nothing left to explain once you have all the instances? Lay these out as a menu, say for each whether its plausible and whether it actually supports treating questions like mine as pseudo-questions, and tell me which reading the dismissal move in class is really leaning on (and whether it holds up).
