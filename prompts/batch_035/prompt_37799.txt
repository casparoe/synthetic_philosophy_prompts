**Exam question:** In the opening chapters of Capital, Volume I, Marx argues that the exchange between capitalist and wage laborer is, in a precise sense, fair: the worker sells labor-power, a commodity like any other, and is paid its value. Explain how surplus value is nevertheless extracted under conditions of equivalent exchange. Then explain the account of alienation developed in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, and assess whether the mature theory of surplus value presupposes, replaces, or merely coexists with the earlier theory of alienation.

**Student's answer:** Marx's central insight is the distinction between labor and labor-power. The worker does not sell labor; she sells labor-power, her capacity to work, and this commodity has a value determined, like any other, by the socially necessary labor time required to reproduce it — roughly, the cost of keeping the worker alive and working. The worker is doubly free, in Marx's famous phrase: free to sell her capacity, and free of any independent means of production that would let her decline to. Now, the value of labor-power is less than the value the worker's labor creates over the working day, and the difference — surplus value — accrues to the capitalist. So although the exchange is nominally at value, it is in substance unequal: the worker is paid the value of her labor-power but receives in return less than the value her labor produces, which means exploitation is, at bottom, a form of unequal exchange, and Marx's claim that the exchange is fair is best read as a piece of rhetoric designed to show that the injustice runs deeper than any cheating at the margins. On alienation: in the 1844 Manuscripts Marx argues, drawing on the notion of species-being, that humans are distinguished by free, conscious, productive activity. Capitalist production alienates the worker from the product of her activity, from the activity itself, from her species-being, and from other workers. Alienation is thus the subjective experience that accompanies exploitation: the worker feels estranged from her work because she knows, however dimly, that surplus is being extracted from her. This makes the two critiques related but independent. Exploitation is a structural feature of exchange under private ownership of the means of production; alienation is the psychological toll of that structure. It follows that a reformed capitalism could, in principle, eliminate both: if wages rose to the point where workers received the full value they create, and if work were reorganized to be varied and self-directed, surplus extraction would cease and alienation with it. Marx resists this conclusion only because he doubts capitalists would ever concede the full product of labor to the worker.
