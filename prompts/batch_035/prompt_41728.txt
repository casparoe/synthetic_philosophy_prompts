I'm a software engineer with zero formal philosophy background, but I've been going down a rabbit hole on Daniel Dennett after a coworker recommended his stuff. The bit that stuck with me is his claim that Darwin and Turing each pulled off what he calls a strange inversion of reasoning. Before Darwin, people assumed that obvious purpose in nature had to come from a designing mind, and he inverted it: you get all this competent-looking design with no comprehension behind it at all. Turing did something similar for computation — instead of needing an intelligent clerk who actually understands arithmetic in order to get reliable calculation, you get a mindless mechanism grinding through steps. Dennett's shorthand for the shared lesson is something like competence without comprehension. Anyway, a friend on my team and I have been arguing about whether these are really the same kind of inversion or only sorta look alike. His point is this: Darwin's inversion has no designer anywhere in the loop, ever, while Turing's still presupposes people who comprehend things — somebody had to design the machine, write the program, build the compiler. So Turing buys you competence-without-comprehension at runtime, but comprehension is still sitting upstream. My counter was that its just one level up: the designers doing the comprehending are themselves products of Darwin's blind process, so the comprehension in Turing's story is itself borrowed competence all the way down. My friend wasn't convinced — he says that move makes the comparison vacuous, because then literally everything traces back to blind evolution and the analogy stops doing any explanatory work.

So here's what I want from you: compare Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion as Dennett presents them, and tell me who's right in our argument. Does the designer-upstream difference mean the two inversions differ in kind, or is my levels move enough to keep Dennett's unified story intact? If you think my friend has a point, say exactly where the analogy breaks. If you think I'm right, say what the analogy actually explains that wouldn't be obvious without it. We're both programmers, so feel free to lean on computing examples (compilers, bytecode, whatever), but please don't assume I know any philosophy jargon beyond the stuff I've already mentioned.

Please write your answer in American English, as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
