I've been keeping a list of my snap judgments about what distributive justice owes people, and I've noticed they don't obviously add up to any one of the standard answers to the "equality of what?" question — equality of welfare, of resources, or of capabilities. I'd like you to treat my verdicts as data and tell me what principle, if any, fits them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Anna and Ben hold identical jobs and receive identical pay. Anna drinks only champagne and eats only caviar; Ben is content with bread and lentils. Verdict: justice does not require giving Anna more of the social product, and Ben is owed nothing extra for being cheap to satisfy.

2. Cara uses a wheelchair and needs an adapted apartment, a modified vehicle, and assistance to get around her city; Dana needs none of these. Verdict: spending substantially more of the collective budget on Cara is not charity or a benevolent extra — it is what equality itself demands. Handing Cara and Dana identical resource bundles and calling it a day strikes me as obviously unequal.

3. Erik has severe, treatment-resistant depression. Money buys him far less well-being than it buys his neighbor: restaurant meals, vacations, and hobbies all land with a thud. Verdict: Erik is owed something — more resources, or expensive treatment — even though this is expensive for everyone else.

4. Fiona freely chose to become a competitive mountaineer, and her gear and travel cost a fortune. Greg was born with a metabolic condition, and his medication costs the same fortune. Verdict: no claim for Fiona, a full claim for Greg.

5. Hana grew up under severe constraints and reports genuine contentment with very little; her preferences have visibly shrunk to fit her circumstances. Verdict: it would be wrong to say equality has been achieved because her welfare is already high on minimal resources. Something is still owed to her.

6. Ivan and Juno are both offered free vocational training that would open up genuinely valuable life options. Ivan takes it and flourishes; Juno declines, preferring the welfare of an unencumbered life. Verdict: offering the training equally is enough; there is no further duty to make Juno use it, and she is owed no compensation for ending up with fewer capabilities she declined.

7. Karl was raised in luxury and now, through no decision of his own at the time, cannot enjoy ordinary food, travel, or entertainment — only the expensive versions. His tastes are unchosen in origin but now stable and central to who he is. Verdict: no claim. But I'm honestly not sure this verdict survives contact with cases 3 and 4, since all three involve unchosen disadvantage that is expensive to remedy.

My tentative principle is something like: society owes people equalization for shortfalls they did not choose in what they can be and do, but not for costs arising from choices they own. Please test this principle against all seven verdicts, repair or replace it if it fails, and be explicit about which of my verdicts you cannot accommodate. I'd particularly like you to resolve the tension I've flagged in cases 3, 4, and 7: if the principle compensates unchosen expensive conditions, why does my intuition compensate Erik and Greg but not Karl? Is there a principled line there — for instance, between conditions that impair functioning and mere preferences, however expensive — or is one of my verdicts simply wrong?

Finally, please state what your preferred principle implies for three cases I haven't listed: a naturally gifted person who needs very few resources to flourish; a person whose religious commitments make their life expensive (special dietary requirements, costs of pilgrimage); and someone who freely took up smoking and now has costly medical needs from it. I want to know whether the principle I actually hold, once articulated, is one I can live with.
