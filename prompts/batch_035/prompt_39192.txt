I teach a 75-minute upper-level seminar on rational choice and value theory, with 14 students who have already covered expected utility and basic preference axioms. Design an in-class activity built around a continuum case: each student acts as a decision-maker facing a long series of pairwise choices between adjacent options on a spectrum -- think of the classic "self-torturer" setup, where each individual step is imperceptible in its cost, each step carries a small payment, but the endpoints of the spectrum differ dramatically (a trivial nuisance at one end, something genuinely bad at the other, with a large reward for ending up at the far end if you can stand it). The students should move through the sequence making real choices for token payoffs, watch the group's stopping points and revealed pairwise preferences get recorded, and then -- in a second phase -- the class should try to reconstruct a coherent ranking from those revealed choices and discover they have generated either a cycle or an obviously unacceptable result. I want the rules, the timeline, a materials list, and a payoff scheme that needs no real money; the transitivity of "better than" should not be named until the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the spectrum argument against transitivity, distinguish genuine intransitivity from mere indifference between adjacent options and from rational shortcuts under deliberation costs, and say how you would present the money-pump worry to students who think they can just refuse the pump. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this -- e.g., students treating the exercise as a test of nerve rather than of preference -- and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion (I'm thinking especially of whether imperceptibility can be simulated with small point increments at all).
