Write an essay about Death in Damascus — the puzzle where you run into Death in the Damascus market and he tells you he has an appointment with you tomorrow, so you flee to Aleppo, but it turns out Death is a near-perfect predictor of your decisions, so the very fact that you chose Aleppo means that's where you're fated to die. The standard philosophy version drops the story: you must choose Damascus or Aleppo, you know Death has correctly predicted your choice and placed himself in whichever city you'll pick, so deciding to go to Damascus is evidence Damascus is fatal, and vice versa. Causal decision theory chases it's own tail here — concluding you'll stay makes fleeing look better, which makes staying look better, forever. Evidential decision theory is sometimes said to handle it more gracefully, though it's not obvious it gives an answer either, just "you're doomed in both." Write an essay that actually takes a side on what a rational agent should do. It should cover: (1) whether the infinite loop is a genuine defect of causal decision theory or an artifact of treating your own future action as evidence to update on; (2) whether flipping a coin is a way out — if Death can also foresee the coin, randomizing gets you nothing, but if he can't, does it buy you a real 50/50, and what does that tell us about how decision theories should handle mixed strategies; and (3) whether the case even teaches us anything about decision theory at all, given that a predictor who is right about whatever you do is arguably not giving you ordinary evidence. Reach a conclusion and defend it — something like "coin-flipping works iff the predictor can't see the coin" is an acceptable verdict if you actually argue for it.
