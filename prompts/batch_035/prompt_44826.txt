I have no background in philosophy or computer science — my working life has been in insurance claims — but a few months ago I read Alan Turing's 1950 paper in Mind, the one where he sets up his question-and-answer game in place of the question of whether machines can think, and I have kept returning to it ever since. What I would like is a prediction about its author, and I should warn you that the prediction interests me less than the reasoning behind it.

From my repeated reading, his verdicts on the earlier cases seem to run as follows:

1. On mistakes: he would not hold a machine's errors against it. He thought the demand for an error-free machine was misplaced, and that a machine which occasionally errs, and which has enough of a random element to try things and learn from them, is closer to a mind than one which never errs.

2. On the objection from Lady Lovelace — that a machine never originates anything and does only what we order it to do — he answered that in fact machines take him by surprise all the time, and that human originality, when you trace it, comes from much the same sources: what we have been taught and the effects of chance.

3. On the objection that a machine might behave intelligently while having no inner life at all, he answered that if we refused to grant thinking to anything whose feelings we cannot inspect, we should each be left sure of no mind but our own — a solipsist's position which nobody actually holds — and that in practice we grant thinking to anyone whose conduct makes it reasonable to do so.

4. On the objection that a machine could never do this or that particular thing — appreciate strawberries and cream, fall in love, write a sonnet — he held that this way of listing disabilities is unfair, since no single human can do everything either, and that the question should be confined to what shows in conversation: whether the machine, at the other end of the written exchange, can pass for a person.

5. On how such a machine should come to exist, he was fairly definite. He did not want engineers to write down an adult's knowledge and abilities and program them in directly. He proposed instead beginning with the simplest possible child-mind, so simple that it can be easily programmed, and then educating that child machine by something like the ordinary course of teaching and correction, involving rewards and punishments, carried out by teachers who would be largely ignorant of exactly what was going on inside it.

Now for the new case. The programs that answer questions and converse with people today were not built the way Turing's engineers would have built an adult mind: nobody wrote their knowledge into them as rules or entries. But they were not exactly educated one utterance at a time, as his child machine was to be, either. They were instead given an enormous body of writing produced by human beings, and left to absorb, by sheer repetition, the patterns in it; only afterwards were people brought in to grade a selection of their answers and nudge them toward giving better ones. On top of that, unlike his confined game, they are built entirely from what other people have already written — their whole substance is second-hand in a way his child, out in the world bumping into things and being told off, was not.

Given his five earlier verdicts, would Turing say that such a program thinks?

As I say, the yes or no is the least of it. What I want is the rule he seems to me to be running, because I can see at least three that fit his record equally well, and they disagree here. On the plain behavioral rule, nothing matters but the conversation at the other end of the wire, and the manner of manufacture is beside the point entirely — a program that could hold up its end convincingly would qualify however it came by the ability. On the developmental rule, what mattered to him was that a mind be grown rather than assembled: started simple and built up through its own mistakes, under correction, and my language program's strange diet of other people's finished sentences, with correction coming only at the end and from the outside, may or may not satisfy that. And on a third rule, closest to his reply to Lady Lovelace, what matters is origin: a thing whose entire stock comes from what human beings have already written has nothing of its own to say, however well it converses, though it may still surprise us now and then.

So: which of these, or which other rule, do you think he is actually running across the five verdicts? What does each rule predict for my new case? Give me your best guess at his verdict, with your reasons for preferring that guess over the others. And then tell me this: what further case — some other machine we could put to him — would most cleanly separate these rules from one another, and what would each rule have him say about it?
