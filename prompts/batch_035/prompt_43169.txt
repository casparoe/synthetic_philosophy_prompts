I'm a novelist working on a book set in DC, told from the point of view of a health-industry lobbyist, and before I can write her convincingly I've realized I need to figure out what I actually think counts as corruption in politics, since the whole plot turns on which of her practices the other characters (and I, on her behalf) treat as shameful. So I've been listing scenarios and jotting down my gut reactions. A PAC giving the legal maximum to a senator's campaign: not corrupt, that's just politics as everyone knows it. A lobbyist paying for the same senator's luxury golf weekend in Scotland: corrupt. A think tank funded by pharma flying a senate staffer to a nice conference in Aspen, all disclosed: not corrupt exactly, but I wince. Promising a bank regulator a seven-figure consulting gig for after she leaves office: corrupt. Hiring a senator's mediocre son at 400k a year to "consult": corrupt. Hiring the senator's genuinely brilliant daughter at market rate for a real job she's qualified for: fine. A bundler collecting two hundred maxed-out checks from executives and handing them over in one photo op: fine, somehow, even though it's five times the golf trip in money. And paying an ex-senator two million a year to lobby his old collegues the year after he leaves: corrupt, or at least gross enough that my heroine should feel bad about it, which in a novel is basicaly the same thing.

The problem is I can't find the line, and I suspect no line exists. Is it that the benefit has to go to the officeholder personally (but then the ex-senator case goes the wrong way, since he's out of office when the money arrives, and so does the staffer's flight)? Is it about converting political power into private gain whenever it happens (but then the ordinary voter who backs a candidate because he promised her a tax cut should count as corrupt too, and she obviously doesn't)? Is it just circumvention of whatever the contribution limits happen to be, which seems to make corruption depend on where a legislature set a number? And there's one case I keep going back and forth on, which I think is my novel's whole moral in miniature: my lobbyist has an old college friend who is now a senator, and over a purely social dinner she mentions, no ask, just mentions, that her client is getting killed by some regulation, and the senator quietly helps. No money, nothing illegal, just friendship doing what money can't. Corrupt or not? Can you find a principle that gets all or nearly all my verdicts, tell me honestly which ones it can't save and which of mine I ought to give up, and say what it predicts about the dinner scene? Please no headings or bullet points or anything, just continuous prose like a long email, I'll be reading it on my phone between chapters.
