My philosophy reading group has been stuck for two weeks on anthropic reasoning, and the last meeting ended with two members, Dana and Marcus, still going at it over coffee afterwards. The trigger was Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" scenario. The setup as they were using it: two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, agree on every observation anyone has ever made, but T2 posits vastly more observers in the universe — say a trillion observers on T1 versus a trillion trillion on T2 — and the scientific community assigns them equal credence on the evidence. Dana declared she could settle the question from her armchair: the fact that she exists is far more probable on T2 (where there are a trillion times as many people who could have been her), so her existence is powerful evidence for T2, and she'd bet on it at enormous odds without waiting for any experiment. Marcus was appalled: no armchair reflection on one's own existence should let a philosopher overrule cosmologists who haven't even collected the relevant data yet, and he suspected Dana of treating herself as a soul that might have failed to be born, when all T2 guarantees is that *someone* would be around having this thought.

Dana answered with a simpler case she called the coin-toss universe: a fair coin is flipped; on heads one observer is created in a white room, on tails a million observers are created in identical white rooms, none of whom can communicate or compare notes. Before observing anything, what should your credence be that the coin landed heads? She said it's obviously about one in a million and one, and that anyone who says one half should be willing to bet on tails at even stakes every time the experiment runs, which she claimed is a money pump. Marcus replied that from anyone's point of view half the runs of that experiment contain just one person, and that Dana's answer works by counting the million observers' indistinguishable opinions as a million separate pieces of evidence for the same fact — a factory that stamps out more observers will trivially contain more people wondering which factory they're in, and that tells you nothing about which factory exists. Dana's last move was to point out that Marcus's kind of view carries its own famous embarrassment, the Doomsday argument: if you reason the way he does, your birth rank among all humans who will ever live is evidence that humanity ends soon, and if he's willing to swallow that, armchair conclusions shouldn't shock him. Marcus conceded he has to either accept a doomsday-ish conclusion or get picky about the "reference class" of observers you count yourself among, but said that a modest, adjustable flaw like that beats Dana's position, which in principle lets a priori reflection about how many people there are *overturn* particle physics data.
