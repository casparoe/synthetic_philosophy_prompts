My friend took a game theory course last semester, and when I asked him about dominance he explained it from his notes, roughly like this: a dominated strategy is one that some other strategy beats no matter what the other players do, and since a rational player would never choose a dominated strategy, you can just delete them from the game. He definately stressed that the dominating strategy has to be an actual, pure strategy — his line was that if the only thing that beats you is some random mix of two other strategies, that's not really domination. You haven't been beaten by a real strategy, just by an average, so a rational player might still reasonably pick you. He also said the nice thing about iterating is that once you delete the dominated strategies, new ones become dominated in the smaller game, and you keep going until nothing is dominated anymore, and this process never costs you anything important: every Nash equilibrium of the original game survives, so you're just cleaning up the game without losing any solutions. He wrapped up by saying this is why common knowledge of rationality has teeth — once it's out in the open that nobody plays dominated strategies, the iterated deletions are basically bookkeeping, and in practice alot of games collapse down to a small set of strategies that people then actually play.

I mostly believed him, but the mixing part bugged me a little. If randomizing is allowed as something players can do, why wouldn't it be allowed as something that dominates you? But I don't know the material well enough to say where he's right and where he's cutting corners, and he's the one who took the course, so.

Can you go through his explanation piece by piece and tell me which parts a game theorist would just sign off on, which are sloppy but fixable, and which are flat out wrong? I'm especially interested in (1) whether his "pure strategies only" rule for domination is correct, (2) whether his "every Nash equilibrium survives" claim is true as he stated it — he never said anything about the difference between strict and weak dominance, and I have a feeling that distinction is where things bite, and (3) whether his last claim, about real players converging on what's left, actually follows from the math or is him sneaking in an empirical claim. Feel free to flag anything else he got subtly wrong that I didn't even notice to ask about.
