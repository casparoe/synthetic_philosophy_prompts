My cousin, who did a philosophy MA years ago and never lets anyone forget it, tried to explain Frankfurt cases to me at dinner and I want to check whether he was being fair. Here's roughly what he said: back in the sixties Harry Frankfurt invented these thought experiments to destroy the idea that moral responsibility requires being able to do otherwise. In the classic version there's a guy, Jones, who decides on his own to shoot Smith. Unbeknownst to Jones, a mad scientist named Black has implanted a chip in his brain: if Jones had been about to decide NOT to shoot, the chip would have kicked in and made him decide to shoot anyway. As it happens Jones decides to shoot all by himself, so Black's device never activates. So Jones is responsible for the killing even though he literally could not have done otherwise, and this refutes the principle of alternative possibilities once and for all. And, my cousin added, this is also basically a proof that free will is compatible with determinism, because in a deterministic world nobody can ever do otherwise and Frankfurt showed that doesn't matter for responsibilty. He said the only pushback comes from people who insist that right before the chip would have triggered, there was some tiny flicker of freedom, and that philosophers regard that as a pretty desperate move.

I mostly followed him but two things felt off to me. One is the jump from one weird case with a brain chip to "therefore determinism is fine" — that seems too fast. And the part where he said the flicker objection is desperate and nobody takes it seriously sounded suspiciously like a debater's move rather than a report. But honestly I don't know the literature, so maybe he's right. Can you go through his summary as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no intro or conclusion?
