So I've been reading about Hofstadter's Luring Lottery from back in the 80s and I want an actual careful analysis of it, not the usual "and then people submitted googolplexes, how funny" retelling that every rationality blog does.

Quick recap of the facts so we're on the same page: in his Scientific American column, Hofstadter ran a real contest. To enter, you sent in a postcard with a positive integer on it, and that integer counted as the number of entries you were submitting -- so you could enter a million times with one card. The prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received (the exact mechanics of who gets which slice don't matter much for what I want). He had spent the preceding columns working out "superrationality" -- the idea that perfectly rational agents facing the same symmetric problem will all arrive at the same answer, so you should pick the strategy that's best on the assumption everyone like you picks it too. By his lights, the superrational move was to roll a die with as many sides as the expected number of entrants and only send a card if you rolled a 1, so that the whole readership collectively simulates a single randomized entry. He'd even done a warmup version, the Platonia dilemma, where a fortune goes to a person only if exactly one of twenty people responds. In the actual lottery, of course, the readership did not roll giant dice: he got an avalanche of entries, some people wrote down a googolplex, others filled the whole postcard with mathematical expressions engineered to evaluate to the largest number that would physically fit, and the prize dissolved into a fraction of a cent, with no way to even determine a winner.

What I want: treat this as a genuine experimental test of superrationality and work it in both directions. First, map the game properly -- who the players are, what the strategy space really is, what Hofstadter's die-rolling scheme is actually doing (why is randomization doing the work that pure sameness can't?), and what a Nash-style analysis predicts versus the superrational one. Second, where does superrationality break as a model of what happened -- and be precise about *which* assumption failed. Is it that the readers weren't superrational, or that the game wasn't symmetric, or that entry into the pool of "reasoners like me" is self-selecting, or something else? The googolplex people are interesting here: some of them clearly understood the superrational argument and defected anyway, which is different from not getting it. Third, flip it around: does the outcome show superrationality is wrong, or just that it was never meant as a description of Scientific American subscribers, and is there any reading under which the episode is evidence *for* something in the neighborhood (like the claim that mixed superrational strategies are the unique sensible ones in games with unbounded strategy spaces)?

Also, if you know, I'm curious how the die-rolling solution relates to the mixed-strategy stuff in standard game theory -- is superrationality just a rederivation of some equilibrium concept with extra philosophical decoration, or is there a real mathematical difference in what it recommends here? I've seen people claim both and I don't have the background to referee it. Dont worry about exact dates and dollar amounts, the structure is what I care about.
