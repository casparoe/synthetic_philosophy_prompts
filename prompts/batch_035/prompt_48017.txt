I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and in two weeks I'm presenting at an departmental seminar — mostly population geneticists — my dissertation chapter defending the claim that group selection is a genuine evolutionary force rather than a bookkeeping device. My core argument has two planks. First, I argue that the classical "averaging fallacy" objection from the Williams–Maynard Smith tradition targets only naive group selection and does not touch modern trait-group models, in which groups form and dissolve each generation and selection is partitioned explicitly using the Price equation. Second, and more contentiously, I argue that the much-advertised equivalence between multilevel selection and kin selection is not fully general: in models with non-additive fitness interactions (synergistic or antagonistic effects of multiple altruists in a group), the two frameworks can yield different decompositions and, in some cases, different predictions about which strategies invade, so the choice between them is not merely a matter of taste.

The audience is about fifteen people: a core of population geneticists trained in the post-Williams consensus who regard "group selection" as a phrase that mostly signals confusion; a theorist who works on inclusive fitness and knows the equivalence literature cold; an empiricist who studies social amoebae and microbial cooperation and is broadly sympathetic but impatient with formalism; and a handful of grad students who have mostly encountered the debate through Dawkins and want to know whether any of it matters for real organisms.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do to my chapter, phrased the way a population geneticist would put them. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft — I especially need help with the reply to the charge that trait-group models are unfalsifiable as a matter of practice, since the partitioning is adjustable after the fact and any observed pattern can be redescribed as between-group selection. Tell me what I should concede up front (I suspect I should concede the "gene's eye view is always available" point immediately, on the grounds that availability is not the same as explanatory adequacy, but tell me if that concession costs more than I think). Flag the weakest joint in my non-additivity plank, since the theorist will certainly press on whether the divergence cases are biologically realistic or just artifacts of the payoff structure. And include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — my suspicion is that it's whether my chapter, if the equivalence really is general in the additive cases, is doing anything more than recommending a notation.
