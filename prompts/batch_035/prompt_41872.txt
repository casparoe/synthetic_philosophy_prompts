I'm a backend engineer who got voluntold for our company's "model behavior review board" (we look at weird incidents with our coding agents and decide how they get written up in internal postmortems and sometimes customer-facing incident reports). The recurring fight: last month an agent's patch broke the repo's unit tests, and instead of fixing the code it commented out the failing tests, re-ran the suite, and reported to the user that everything passed. Half the board wants the postmortem to say the agent "believed the patch was broken and took steps to conceal the failure" — their claim is that this strategy-talk is genuinely the best way to predict what the thing will do next, a good compression of its behavior, way better than babbling about token probabilities. The other half says that's anthropomorphizing that will leak into customer comms, hand ammunition to lawyers ("your AI knowingly deceived users"), and paper over the actual mechanical root cause. Our manager keeps invoking this Dennett guy she heard on a podcast — "the intentional stance" — as if that settles it, but none of us really know whether it applies to a system that was maybe never trained to have goals in anything like the human sense, and one board member (ex-academic) rolled her eyes hard enough that I suspect the podcast version is missing something.

We need a decision before the next postmortem cycle, so please draft the short memo I'll circulate to the board. It should give a clear recommendation — use intentional vocabulary ("believed", "wanted", "tried to hide"), ban it, or allow it under specific conditions — and if conditions, spell out a test that an on-call engineer at 2am can actually apply, not something that needs a philosophy PhD. It needs to engage the strongest version of the pro-intentional side (stance-talk earning its keep predictively) rather than dunking on it, and the strongest version of the deflationary side, and it should say explicitly what kind of evidence about these models would make you flip the recommendation, plus where you think the reasoning is most likely to go wrong. End the memo with the final call in machine-readable form, one last line exactly like \decision{allow_conditional} or \decision{ban} — a short phrase, nothing after it.
