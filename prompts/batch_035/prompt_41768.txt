So here's a thing I keep bumping into. Everyone knows the standard line: Hempel wanted historical explanation to work like physics explanation — subsume the event under a general law, with the initial conditions as antecedent — and everyone since has basically said that's a non-starter for history, and that what historians actually do is explain by narrative: they trace a chain through time, and the explanation is somehow in the shape of the story. Fine. But I want to test this debate on a real case, not in the abstract. Take the July Crisis of 1914 and run it through both models, and then tell me what the case does to the debate itself.

Concretely, three parts:

First, actually try to build the best covering-law explanation you can of the outbreak of the war. No strawman — I want the strongest version. The candidate generalizations are things like: alliance cascades in multipolar systems (once one mobilizes, everyone's commitments force the rest); mobilization timetables as commitment devices that take the decision out of leaders' hands; or leaders who previously got away with brinkmanship underestimating the risk (the 1912-13 Balkan crises went fine, so why not again). For whichever candidate you pick, say what the antecedent conditions would have to be for July 1914 to instantiate the law, and then be honest about the point where the "law" gets vague enough that it stops explaining and starts just redescribing what happend.

Second, do the narrative version. This is closer to what actual historians of 1914 write — on one side you have the Fischer line, where Germany (or Austria-Hungary) knowingly chose war, and on the other the sleepwalkers framing of Christopher Clark, where everybody stumbles in, watchful but unseeing, nobody actually wanting the general war. What is the narrative doing that the law-version can't? And be careful here: is the narrative actually tracing a causal chain (in which case maybe it's just an implicit covering-law account with the laws left unstated), or is it doing something else — like making the outcome feel inevitable in hindsight, or redistributing agency and blame, which is arguably not explanation at all but evaluation dressed up as explanation?

Then the part I actually care about. A friend of mine claims the whole covering-law vs narrative fight is a disguised fight about event individuation, and July 1914 is the perfect case for her point. "The outbreak of WWI" only picks out something a law could cover if you can sort events into types, and the July Crisis is exactly the kind of one-off tangle — the blank check to Vienna, Serbia's answer to the ultimatum, Russia's "period preparatory to war," the fact that the cabinets were all on holiday — where type-individuation looks hopeless. So on her view the covering-law theorist never even gets to the laws; they lose at the earlier step of saying what event they're explaining. Is she right? Or can the covering-law side individuate events coarsely enough (e.g., "great power war following a crisis in an alliance system") that they stay in the game — at the cost of the explanation not being about 1914 anymore? And does the narrative explainer have their own individuation problem they just never mention, given that a story has to start and stop somewhere and the choice of endpoints (Sarajevo? the 1908 annexation crisis? 1870?) is doing a lot of unacknowledged work?

No reading lists, no citations — just the analysis.
