"Singer's argument starts from utilitarianism: what makes a being morally considerable is its capacity to suffer, not its rationality or personhood. Since animals can suffer, their interests count, and giving them less weight just because they aren't human is arbitrary in the same way racism and sexism are arbitrary — hence the term speciesism, which Singer coined. The argument from marginal cases sharpens this: many humans (infants, people with severe cognitive disabilities) lack the higher cognitive capacities we usually cite to distinguish ourselves from animals, but we still think they have full moral status. So if these humans count, the capacities can't be what grounds moral status, and by the same token species membership can't either; if they don't count, we're commited to accepting that infants can be used in ways we would never accept. Since marginal-case humans clearly do have moral rights, animals must have moral rights too. Singer's conclusion is that animals are entitled to equal treatment with humans — just as we can't favor a human's interest over a pig's when the two interests are equally strong, we also can't ever treat the two differently in practice. A defender of species-based distinctions can reply with a contractualist story: morality is a system of cooperation among beings who can reciprocate, and while animals fall outside it, marginal-case humans are inside because they are members of our own species and we care about them. I find Singer's side stronger, because the contractualist reply smuggles in sentiment for marginal-case humans rather than offering a principled line."
