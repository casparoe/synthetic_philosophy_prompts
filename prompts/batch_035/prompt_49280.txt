So this term I'm teaching a philosophy of history seminar, and right before we get into Butterfield's The Whig Interpretation of History I want to screen the opening "Dawn of Man" section of 2001 plus the last twenty minutes, the Star Gate and the Star Child. My working idea, which I want to stress-test: 2001 might be the most fully committed piece of Whig history ever filmed, except with the destination moved from the present to some post-human future. The whole of everything that happens between two milestones is compressed into one cut — bone thrown in the air becomes an orbiting satelite — as if all intervening history is just filler, which is pretty much exactly the move Butterfield complained about, where the past only earns its keep as a step on the way to us. And the plot structure is straight providence: monolith shows up, evolution leaps forward, repeat, and it terminates in the Star Child looking down on the earth. Even the music is a one-line thesis — Zarathustra, man as a bridge to something beyond man, teleology you can hum.

But I keep worrying that the film is ironizing this more than asserting it. The bone is a weapon, and the thing it cuts to is (in Clarke's novel, anyway; the film keeps it ambiguous) a nuclear weapons platform, so the one thing carried across the cut is our capacity to kill at ever increasing scale — progress re-advertised as escalation. And the whole middle chunk, the HAL section, reads like the progress story breaking down in the machinery room: the most sophisticated product of the leap forward is the thing that very nearly ends the trip.

So here's what I want from you: read 2001 as philosophy of history. Where does it genuinely commit the Whig vice — valuing the past only from its endpoint, treating history as a road with lanes painted on it, the rest — and where does it resist or subvert that reading? Second question, the one I actually can't decide: does the external intervention by the monoliths make it the opposite of the Whig story, since progress here isn't the past unfolding from within toward us but something imposed from outside, or is that just providence with better special effects — and does it matter for the historiography point? Third, Butterfield's whole target was histories that culminate in the writer's present. The Star Child is a future, not a present, and not even a future we're shown as a society, just a fetus and a planet. Does relocating the terminus like that change the moral of the Whig critique in any way worth teaching, or is it cosmetically different? I'd also take suggestions on which specific scenes to actually show the students, and what to warn them about so they don't just read cosmic destiny off a match cut. Kubrick was famously cagey about meaning, and I don't want to hand my seminar a tidy thesis the film itself refuses to sign. (Also — if you think I'm wrong that the ending even functions as a terminus, that the Star Gate sequence undoes narrative altogether rather than completing it, I'd genuinely like to hear that case.)
