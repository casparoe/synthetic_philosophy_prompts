Consider the absent-minded driver problem in its standard form, as it originates in Piccione and Rubinstein's work on imperfect recall. A driver leaves a bar at midnight and drives home along a route with two identical intersections, X then Y. He is so absent-minded that at an intersection he cannot remember whether he has already passed one, and the intersections are indistinguishable. At each he can CONTINUE or EXIT. Exiting at X leads to a hazardous road (payoff 0); exiting at Y leads to a hotel (payoff 1); continuing past both leads home (payoff 4). The ex ante optimal policy, fixed before departure while his memory is intact, is to CONTINUE at each intersection with probability 2/3, for expected payoff 4/3 — better than any deterministic plan. The interesting question is what is rational for the driver standing at an intersection, judged only from the information state he actually occupies there.

Give me your credence in the following claim: there is no genuine conflict between the planning perspective and the action perspective in this problem — the rational action at the intersection is exactly what the ex ante optimal plan prescribes, and the appearance of paradox dissolves once the driver's situation is analyzed correctly. I want a number, not a survey of positions, and a decomposition into at least these terms: (a) the probability that the driver's self-locating credence — his probability of being at X rather than Y, given that he is at some intersection — is well-defined at all, given that it appears to depend on the very policy he adopts (a fixed-point worry); (b) the probability that, if well-defined, that credence should be 1/2 by symmetry rather than something derived from his own policy, such as 3/5 if he continues with probability 2/3; (c) the probability that the correct action-level principle is a mutual-consistency requirement between policy and self-locating belief rather than straightforward expected-utility maximization at the information set; and (d) any further terms you think belong, e.g. whether the variant in which the randomizing coin is flipped before departure and its outcome is known to the driver at the intersection tells against the claim. Assign each term a number with one line of justification, show how they combine into your overall credence, and then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or variant would move it the most.
