I'm a science journalist who has spent a lot of copy over the years on the boundary between physics and the special sciences, and I've noticed that I keep issuing the same verdicts on reduction and emergence without being able to say what unifies them. I'd like your help extracting the principle, if one exists. Here are my considered verdicts.

First, temperature reduces to mean molecular kinetic energy, and nothing of scientific importance is lost in the reduction. That case seems easy to me.

Second, liquidity: no single molecule is liquid, and "being liquid" is not a property any microstate has, but I regard this as harmless. It's just molecules in bulk; I'd call it weak emergence and insist nothing new exists in the world when enough of them get together.

Third, phantom traffic jams that propagate backward down a highway, or gliders in the Game of Life: predictable in practice only by simulation, never in general from the update rule, yet again I say there is nothing genuinely new there. Novel behavior without efficient derivability still doesn't impress me as emergence in a strong sense.

Fourth, and here I get uncomfortable. Sharp phase transitions, like boiling or the onset of superconductivity, are only truly sharp in the thermodynamic limit — an idealization involving infinitely many particles. A finite system, strictly speaking, never boils; it crosses over smoothly. So there are textbook facts, like the existence of a definite boiling point, that have no exact counterpart at the microlevel at all. My verdict is that this is real emergence, more than the liquidity case, and yet I also hold that nothing happens that isn't just the molecules obeying the Schrödinger equation. I cannot fully reconcile those two commitments, and I want to know whether I must give one up.

Fifth, multiple realizability: pain can be realized by very different substrates in an octopus and in me, which I take to make the special sciences autonomous in practice, while still granting that a Laplacean demon working from the microphysics could track everything. Autonomy, for me, is epistemic.

Sixth, my one confession: I suspect phenomenal consciousness does not reduce, while distrusting my own suspicion, since it may be nothing more than a failure of imagination on my part. But if I allow strong emergence there, I can't see what stops me from allowing it for liquidity too, and I don't want to.

Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts, or must some go? If you offer candidate principles, state each one precisely, tie every clause to the case that motivates it, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: whether phonons — the quasiparticles solid-state physicists treat as particles but which are collective vibrations of a lattice — count as real on my considered view, and whether thermodynamic irreversibility is emergent given that the micro-laws are time-symmetric. Please also tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save. And throughout, indicate how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of — I'd rather know where the ground is soft than be handed a finished principle as though it were settled.
