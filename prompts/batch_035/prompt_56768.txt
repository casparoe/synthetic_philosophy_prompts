I have to write up a position statement for my cog sci department's language-and-thought reading group — before the semester starts everyone has to put their cards on the table so we can poke at each other. Here's my hand:

1. I think the strong Whorfian claim — that you literally cannot entertain a thought your language has no way to express — is dead, and I'm fine with that. I get tip-of-the-tongue experiences all the time where the thought is clearly there and the words just aren't, and I take that at face value as evidence.

2. At the same time I'm sold on the weaker stuff: communities that use absolute/cardinal directions for everything ("the cup is north of the plate") apparently stay oriented in ways I never could, and I read that as language reshaping habitual cognition, not just vocabulary differences.

3. Underneath it all I'm a Fodor guy: there's a language of thought, it's innate, and its basic conceptual inventory is the same across the species. Natural languages are more like an interface on top of that.

4. I take the Russian two-blues result (speakers discriminating faster across the siniy/goluboy boundary) as showing language is causally online in perception itself, not just labeling things after the fact.

5. I'm pretty dissmissive of untranslatability claims in anthropology — schadenfreude, hygge, whatever — those always turn out to be paraphrasable, it just takes more words.

6. I also hold that bilinguals are one thinker, not two — switching languages doesn't switch the underlying person.

Before I send this to the organizer I want to know: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? For each genuine tension tell me how bad it is and what the cheapest fix would be, and at the end say whether there's one revision that would clean up the most. Keep it under 600 words, I don't want a treatise.
