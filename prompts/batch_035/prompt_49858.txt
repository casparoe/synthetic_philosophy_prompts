I am a doctoral student in counseling psychology, currently on practicum at a large university's counseling center, where I co-facilitate a weekly psychoeducation group on stress and perfectionism for graduate students (about ten to twelve clients per session, fifty minutes, CBT-informed but not individual therapy). A striking proportion of the group's members arrive already fluent in pop-Stoicism — a meditation app, a daily-quote newsletter, a bestselling modern manual — and the dichotomy of control is the idea they reach for most often. In its classical form, it partitions everything into what is up to us (on the standard reading, our judgments, intentions, and impulses) and what is not (bodily states, other people, reputations, and outcomes generally), and counsels confining one's concern to the first class. The mass-market versions vary: some preserve the strict dichotomy; the most influential popular revision, due to William Irvine, softens it into a trichotomy — things fully in one's control, things partially in one's control, things entirely outside it — with the advice that for the middle category one should set internal goals (do your best in the performance rather than aim at winning). The Stoics themselves, of course, said more than the slogan suggests: externals were classed as "indifferents," but as preferred or dispreferred ones, and the tradition plainly counsels undertaking what are usually called appropriate actions toward externals even while withholding one's happiness from their outcomes. My clients, understandably, have none of this background, and the doctrine they actually hold is whatever their app taught them on Tuesday.

My problem is that in the moment — a group member says the app told her that her anxiety about the academic job market is irrational because the outcome is not up to her, so she should let it go — I have no principled basis for deciding when this framing is a genuinely useful cognitive reframing and when it is quietly harmful. Sometimes it functions like textbook decatastrophizing and relabeling of a worry as outside one's agency; sometimes it rationalizes disengagement from preparation the person could perfectly well do; and sometimes, most troublingly, it becomes a tool a client deploys against herself, as with one group member whose partner tells her she cannot control his behavior and should therefore work on her own reactions, a usage I suspect of licensing passivity in a deteriorating relationship. My supervisor, a cognitive-behavioral clinician with no patience for ancient philosophy, has asked me to produce something she can sign off on, and my own philosophical reading (largely of the popular literature, plus some Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius in translation) is not yet systematic enough to generate it.

Please write a decision procedure I could actually run, in a few minutes, on an instance of a client deploying dichotomy-of-control reasoning — whether it arises in group, in a brief post-session check-in, or in the psychoeducational materials we distribute. The procedure should do the following, in whatever order you think is defensible: distinguish the situations in which the classical dichotomy, the Irvine-style trichotomy, or neither does the work the client needs; identify the characteristic misfires of the doctrine as popularly absorbed (e.g., the collapse of "not fully in your control" into "not worth acting on," the leap from an outcome's being indifferent to one's *happiness* to its being indifferent simpliciter, the slide from accepting a dispreferred outcome to not working to prevent it); specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; and mark clearly the points where the procedure stops being conceptual triage and hands the decision to a clinician or to the client's own values — including the point at which it flags a safety issue rather than a philosophical one. Be honest about where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as a rule, and about what it cannot do: I am not asking for a defense of Stoic therapy, and the procedure should say candidly whether it is delivering Stoicism, CBT wearing a toga, or something in between.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my actual group. First, the client mentioned above who interpreted "the interview outcome is not up to you" as a reason to stop preparing for campus visits, and who now feels virtuously detached and badly prepared. Second, a client with climate anxiety who is deciding whether to keep organizing a departmental sustainability campaign, who says the dichotomy has finally given her permission to stop — and I should disclose that I find the doctrine's application here most troubling, since she is one of the few people whose disengagement is a real loss. Third, the client whose partner uses the dichotomy as described above, where I suspect the procedure's verdict and my instinct to intervene will conflict. One of these cases must yield an answer I will not like — if the procedure always returns my preferred verdict, it is not a procedure but a mirror. Finally, state what you had to decide about the underlying philosophy in order to make the procedure usable at all: whether you kept the strict dichotomy, adopted the trichotomy, or replaced both with something like an effort-allocation heuristic; which distinctions from the Stoic texts you preserved, which you dropped, and what that choice costs.
