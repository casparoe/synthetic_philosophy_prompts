I play bass in a five-piece band that's been together for six years. At a rehearsal last month, our singer came in with a chorus melody, and over the next two hours it turned into a full song: I wrote my bassline, the guitarist found the bridge riff on the spot, the drummer worked out the arrangement, and the singer kept adjusting her melody to sit on top of what we were building underneath her. Nobody ever said "let's write a song together," but nobody thought of it as anything else either — when the drummer flubbed a fill we said "we lost the thread there," not "Dave lost the thread." Now the band is breaking up badly, and the singer has registered the song under her name alone. Her argument is that the vocal melody is the song and everything else is accompaniment, so she wrote it and we were just doing our jobs as instrumentalists; the rest of us feel that the band wrote the song, and the guitarist in particular keeps saying that as though it's a plain fact. I can't tell whether it is one, or just something comforting to say.

I'd like you to help me think this through carefully, as a philosopher of action or social ontology would — please reason your way through it rather than just telling me what's customary, and feel free to brainstorm out loud before concluding. There are really three tangled questions. First, the descriptive one: was there ever a "we" that did anything that night, or just five individuals each doing their own thing in the same room that happened to interlock? If it's only the second, my singer seems hard to refute — the melody is hers, my bassline is mine, and nothing remains. But it also feels obviously false that what happened was five parallel solo performances; something happened that night that none of us did or could have done alone, and I want to know whether that intuition survives scrutiny and, if so, what it would take for it to — what conditions have to hold for a group, rather than several people, to author something. Second, the normative one: the guitarist insists the singer is wronging us, not just disappointing us, and I notice I feel entitled to be consulted about the song's fate even where I have no complaint about anyone reusing their own bassline. Are those feelings tracking a real obligation, or inventing one? And third, the practical one: does anything you'd say about the first two actually change what she's entitled to here, or does authorship by a "we" that no longer exists dissolve into nothing the moment the group does? Please take the questions seriously on their own terms and don't just fall back on what music-industry convention happens to be.
