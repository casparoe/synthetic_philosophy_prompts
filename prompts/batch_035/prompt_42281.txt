I'm the deputy country director for a humanitarian NGO in a country where, roughly monthly, we face decisions that feel morally off but seem necessary: paying a "registration fee" at checkpoints to get food through, hiring the minister's brother-in-law's trucking company so our convoy permits keep getting approved, quietly taking names off a beneficiary list after intelligence services ask who we're helping, sharing (inaccurate but plausible) aid statistics with a donor government that's a party to the conflict. Our existing guidance is useless -- it says comply with all laws and never do anything that compromises humanitarian principles, which is not a decision, it's a wish. I've been reading about the "dirty hands" tradition in political ethics (the Walzer essay, the idea that a good leader sometimes has to do wrong and should feel the wrongness of it, arguments about dirtying hands for the lesser evil), and I want that operationalized. Please write a decision procedure my team could actually run in a 48-hour window when these things come up: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it just gives an answer with a moral cost attached rather than a clean bill of health, and where it hands the decision to a named person because the question is one of judgment rather than something you can check. I'd also want the procedure to include whatever the dirty hands literature says about aftermath -- recording, guilt, accountability, not letting one dirty decision quietly become policy -- since that seems like half the point of the literature and none of it is in our guidance. Then run it on three cases: the checkpoint fees, the brother-in-law's trucks, and the doctored statistics for the donor. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't do (I suspect the doctored statistics are beyond any procedure, but I want that argued, not just asserted). Assume whoever runs it has no philosophy background, 15 minutes to read the procedure, and a boss who will ask "so is this allowed or not" at the end.
