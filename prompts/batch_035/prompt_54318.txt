The homework question was:

"In Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, what is sympathy, and how does the impartial spectator figure in his account of moral judgement? In your answer, comment on whether sympathy operates the same way with grief as with resentment."

The student wrote:

"For Smith, sympathy is our capacity to share the feelings of another person. When someone near us is consumed by grief, their emotion is contagious, and we come to feel the same sorrow ourselves; sympathy is thus essentially automatic, a kind of emotional infection that spreads from the sufferer to the bystander. Smith calls conduct proper when the spectator's emotion matches the agent's in strength, so propriety is really a matter of equal intensity on both sides. Since grief is the most contagious emotion, Smith says we always find it easier to sympathise with sorrow than with joy. The impartial spectator is Smith's name for the opinion of society as a whole: when we wonder whether our conduct is acceptable, we are effectively polling the actual people around us and taking their average reaction as the verdict. This is why Smith thinks moral judgement is fundamentally social — right and wrong are whatever the crowd of spectators settles on. With resentment, the spectator shares the injured person's anger directly, which is why Smith treats resentment as a reliable guide to injustice, needing no correction before we act on it."
