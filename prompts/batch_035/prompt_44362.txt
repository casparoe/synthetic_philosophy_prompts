I am a retired geophysicist who spent most of a career on postings in Japan, Germany, and the United States, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy systematically — at present the literature on conventions, starting from Lewis's Convention and working outward. A pattern I first noticed decades ago, long before I had any vocabulary for it, has kept resurfacing in that reading, and I cannot find an established name for it, so I am appealing to anyone who knows the terrain better than I do.

Here is the pattern. A local convention — tipping, queueing, removing one's shoes, not jaywalking — is arbitrary in content; everyone in the locality would agree it could have been otherwise, and nobody regards the rule itself as a moral law. Yet what a violation of it means depends entirely on what else the violator is plausibly known to know. When my wife and I first arrived in Japan, our blunders over shoes and business cards were read by our hosts as ignorance, and met with patient, cheerful correction; years later I watched a fluent, decade-long resident decline, once again, to remove his shoes in a host's house, and the hosts were visibly wounded — not because shoe-removal had become a moral matter in the interval, but because by then the same act could no longer be read as anything but a statement about how much he cared. In Germany I have seen strangers rebuke an assimilated resident for jaywalking while a small child was watching, while shrugging good-naturedly at tourists doing the same thing twenty meters away; in the United States, a long-term resident who never tips is treated by waitstaff with a coldness that a fresh-off-the-boat non-tipper never encounters. The mirror image also appears to hold: outsiders who follow the local convention receive a warmth that mere correctness of behavior would not by itself explain, as though adherence were being read as a token of regard rather than as compliance. The convention, in short, seems to function as a test of attitude precisely once the hypothesis of ignorance has been excluded.

The nearest things I have located do not quite fit. Lewis's conventions are characterized by arbitrariness, common knowledge, and coordination, and he has little to say about how one and the same violation is interpreted differently depending on the violator; the psychological literature on how practices gradually acquire a moral character describes a change in the practice itself, whereas what I am describing is a change in the interpretation of a single act, produced not by any shift in the practice but by background evidence about the agent. So I have two questions. Does this configuration have an established name, and if so, where is it discussed? If it does not, I would like you to say so plainly and coin a term: give a definition that distinguishes it from ordinary convention-breaking on one side and from the moralization of a practice on the other, and address the edge cases — the resident who knows the convention but claims sincere objection to it; the tourist who happens to comply by accident; the local who violates it and is read as merely eccentric. I am less interested in the label than in the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
