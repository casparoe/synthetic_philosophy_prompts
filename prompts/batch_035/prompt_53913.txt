I'm a retired neurophysiologist -- thirty-odd years recording from visual cortex, the last ten of them on neural correlates of consciousness projects -- and in retirement I've finally had time to read philosophy properly instead of skimming it for grant proposals. Somewhat to my own surprise I've ended up defending a fairly thoroughgoing epiphenomenalism: that phenomenal experience is real but causally inert, a kind of exhaust of neural processing rather than a driver of it. My sketch of the argument is basically: decades of looking at NCC data and finding correlation everywhere and downward causation nowhere, plus the thought that if the causal story about my motor cortex is complete, there's simply nothing left for the feeling itself to do.

Next month I'm presenting this at my local philosophy of mind reading group. The room is mostly analytic philosophers: a couple of hardcore functionalists who think the whole qualia business is confused from the ground up, one person who takes integrated information theory very seriously, a couple of grad students who just wrote papers on physicalism, and a retired GP who is the most stubborn emergentist you've ever met. Nobody there will be impressed by the fact that I held a pipette for a living.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in roughly the order they'll arrive, phrased the way these people would actually put it (not the way a physicist would). For each one give me a short reply I can deliver in two minutes, and be honest about where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should just concede parts of up front. And yes, I know what the worst one is -- the one about how, if the qualia do nothing, I'm apparently telling a room full of people about them right now. Tell me how bad that one really is, what my best available answer is, and whether I'd be smarter to raise it myself before someone else does. I'd rather limp out of there than get flattened in the first five minutes.

Please write everything in British English, spellings and idiom included -- I've lived outside the UK too long and my written English has gone transatlantic, which the group teases me about.
