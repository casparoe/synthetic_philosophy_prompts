I'm a PhD student in development economics, and I edit a small blog read mainly by graduate students and early-career researchers who are deciding where to direct their donations, research topics, and working hours. Nearly all of them say they choose causes using the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework, but when I press them with concrete cases, their judgments rarely track the arithmetic their labels imply: some let sheer scale override everything else, some quietly discard the three factors and ask what an extra dollar buys, some treat a crowded field as disqualifying for reasons that have nothing to do with the framework's logic. I want a diagnostic quiz for the blog, tentatively titled something like "Which cause prioritizer are you actually?", that sorts readers by what they say about cases rather than by the framework they claim to follow.

Please take the role of a decision theorist and experienced survey designer. Before committing to any questions, brainstorm several candidates and discard those whose answer options don't actually separate the positions I describe below, because a question on which every reasonable reader picks the same option is dead weight. Then produce the quiz.

I have in mind eight to ten questions. Each should be a fully specified concrete case, with two or three answer options phrased so that a reader can answer without knowing which label her answer supports. Between them, the cases must pull apart the following axes, which I care about far more than the standard textbook treatment of the framework. First, whether importance is treated as the absolute scale of a problem, or as scale already discounted for what others would do anyway: a case where one cause is enormous but well-funded and another modest but abandoned should expose this. Second, whether the respondent genuinely combines the three factors multiplicatively, or lets the largest number dominate lexicographically: a case where one cause scores highest on a single dimension but lowest on the product of all three should expose this. Third, what work neglectedness is doing in her reasoning: whether a crowded field puts her off because the next dollar buys less there, or because she thinks being one voice among many in a fashionable area is itself pointless — these are different views wearing the same word, and a case contrasting a cause crowded with money against one crowded only with sympathizers should expose them. Fourth, whether tractability can ever be low enough to knock an enormous cause out of the top rank entirely: a case involving something like biological aging, vast in scale and stubbornly unsolved, should expose this. Where useful, include one case about whether the sheer scale of risks to people who don't yet exist can count toward importance, since that split runs through my readership.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto leaves with these names: strict multiplicative ITN; scale-first, where importance is lexically prior; marginal cost-effectiveness first, where the respondent bypasses the three-factor scaffold and asks directly what the next dollar or hour buys; neglectedness-as-advocacy, where crowdedness matters through voice and coordination rather than returns; and satisficing, where the respondent's real stance is to pick one problem and throw resources at it until it is solved, regardless of comparative arithmetic. If a combination of answers corresponds to no recognized position, say so plainly and mark the leaf unnamed rather than inventing a label. Flag honestly any pair of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate, and say what additional case would be needed to separate them, rather than papering over the gap.

One format requirement, which is unusual for a quiz, so take it seriously: my blog publishes long-form essays, not listicles, so write the entire deliverable in continuous prose. Fold each question and its options into flowing paragraphs, narrate the scoring key as connected text, and use no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables of any kind — the piece should read the way one would write a detailed letter to a colleague.
