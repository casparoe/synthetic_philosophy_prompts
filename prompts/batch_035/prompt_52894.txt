Australia fines you if you don't show up on election day, but the secret ballot means nobody can check what you actually did in the booth — handing back a blank or deliberately scribbled ballot is perfectly safe. And yet if you do fill it out, a ballot for the House of Representatives only counts if you number every single box in order of preference: you can't just vote 1 for your favourite and stop, you have to rank the entire field, lesser evils and all. So the state forces participation, stays neutral on expression, but insists that any expression be a complete ranking. What's the implicit theory of voting here? Is the duty apparently one of showing up rather than choosing, and if so what does that say about how the system conceives of the vote — as a civic ritual of presence, or as a genuine choice? And does requiring full rankings institutionalize the lesser-evil argument, treating "I genuinely don't prefer either of the last two left standing" as incoherent, or is it just a technical device for translating preferences into a winner? It seems like the only honest option left for the voter who thinks all the candidates are equally bad is spoiling the ballot, which the system officially registers as a non-vote. I'd like a reconstruction of what this whole arrangement presupposes about citizens, preferences, and what a vote even is, whether the pieces fit together coherently, and what would have to change if we instead made the duty one of actual choice, or dropped the full-ranking requirement and let people stop after their first preference.
