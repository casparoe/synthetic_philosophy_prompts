I'm part of a reading group on machine ethics, and last week we discussed whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer. One member pressed an analogy that has stuck with me: the reward signal, she said, is the machine analogue of pleasure and pain. It is what the agent works to obtain and to avoid losing, it arrives or fails to arrive as the world surprises the agent, and it is the only thing, in an RL system, that plays the functional role that valence plays in us. Her conclusion was that once an RL agent's policy is general enough and its environment rich enough, the default assumption should be that something it is like to receive or be denied reward — and hence that sufficiently advanced RL agents belong within our moral consideration, perhaps with some already close to the line. A sceptic in the group fired back with a rival analogy: reward is to the agent what fitness is to the organism — an explanatory scalar that accounts for why a behaviour exists without anyone inside the system feeling anything at all. Fitness explains the plant's phototropism, he said, without suggesting the plant is chasing the sun for the thrill of it; why isn't reward just the same sort of bookkeeping for a policy rather than a phenotype?

I'd like a proper audit of both analogies, because I suspect each is doing more work than it can carry. For the pleasure-and-pain side, please set out the correspondence table explicitly: reward mapped to valence, negative reward to pain, the temporal-difference error (the gap between predicted and obtained reward) to something like hope or disappointment, the discount factor to some analogue of prudence or impulsiveness, reward shaping to externally rigged incentives, and whatever else genuinely maps. Then show where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I've noticed but can't evaluate: reward is a training-time quantity that can be disconnected from the deployed agent entirely (a policy can be executed without the reward channel even being computed), whereas valence is plausibly constitutive of experience rather than a wire attached to it; reward is designer-set and revisable in a way pain is not; and the fact that RL algorithms were inspired by the dopamine prediction-error story in neuroscience may make the borrowing look tighter than it is. For the fitness analogy, the table is easier but the disanalogies are less obvious to me: an organism's fitness is not computed or represented anywhere, while reward is an actual signal entering an actual learning mechanism, which seems to break the sceptic's mapping at the crucial joint — or does it?

Finally, please separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended moral use from the ones that don't. It may be that the fitness analogy refutes a naive identification of reward with valence while leaving open a subtler route to machine suffering (perhaps one keyed to the agent's internal states rather than to reward as such), and it may be that my friend's conclusion survives even though her analogy does not. If both analogies fail as arguments, I'd like to know what a better one would look like — what comparison, if any, could genuinely settle or even illuminate whether an RL agent can suffer, and what an answer would have to establish about the system beyond what we can read off the training loop. Please assume no access to the current literature; argue from the concepts and the mechanics of RL themselves.
