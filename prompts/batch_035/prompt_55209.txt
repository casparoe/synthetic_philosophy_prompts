Over the holidays my brother-in-law and I got into our now-annual argument about whether individual choices matter. His position is the simple one: since one person's emissions, purchases, or ballot are each a rounding error on the aggregate, none of us is doing anything wrong so long as we're not egregious about it — and my "every little bit helps" rejoinder struck even me as pretty thin. So I did what I probably should have done years ago: wrote down my actual gut verdicts on a list of cases, and discovered that they fit neither his view nor mine. Here is the list. Not voting in a national election on the grounds that my vote won't decide it: I think that's a bad excuse, and I'd quietly blame a friend who stayed home out of pure laziness, even knowing the chance of my vote being decisive is something like one in many millions. Buying a chicken sandwich at the airport: I've come to think this is a small wrong, even though I'm confident one sandwich doesn't add a single bird to production in any way I could trace. Taking one long-haul flight a year to see my sister abroad: this feels basically fine to me, at worst a modest indulgence — even though the flight emits more in a few hours than the rest of my year put together. Flicking a wrapper out the car window: obviously wrong, and I'd be embarrassed to be seen doing it, though the harm is a rounding error on a rounding error. Letting the tap run while brushing my teeth during a drought: feels mildly wrong, but weakly — I do it half-asleep without much guilt. Buying a gas car instead of an electric one: feels like a genuine contribution to harm, spread over years of driving, in a way the single flight somehow doesn't.

The embarrassments are not hard to spot. My verdicts on the flight and the wrapper run exactly opposite to the magnitudes involved: the enormous contribution is fine and the microscopic one is shameful. The sandwich and the flight have the same structure — one tiny addition to an aggregate harm — and I judge them differently, apparently because I've recently had feelings about the one and not the other. And voting feels near-obligatory while the tap feels like etiquette, though neither is efficacious in the way we usually demand of wrong-making. I keep reaching for a principle of my own, something like: my act is wrong when I take a benefit that exists only because others refrain — a free-riding idea. But it doesn't seem to fit: the gas car isn't obviously a free ride on anyone's restraint, the wrapper isn't a benefit at all, and voting is a cost rather than a benefit, yet it's the verdict I feel most strongly about. Either the principle is hopeless or my verdicts are.

So that is what I'm asking for. Give me a principle, or a short menu of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to decide cases — each clause tied to the case that motivates it — rather than a mood like "participation in collective harm." Tell me what the winning principle says about cases I haven't listed: a single burger on my birthday after a year of vegetarianism; a billionaire's private flight, which is enormous in magnitude but singular in structure; voting in a seat so safe that the decisiveness odds are even more remote than usual; and one more I keep going back and forth on, whether my small donation to a large relief fund is more like my vote (worthwhile though ineffectual) or like the tap water (nice, but not owed). And be honest with me about the choice the list forces: I'd rather know whether the only way to save all six verdicts is a principle with a separate clause per case — at which point it explains nothing — and which of my verdicts the simpler principle should be allowed to overturn, and why those rather than the others. Please don't just tell me my verdicts are inconsistent; I know. Tell me which ones to surrender and what I'm left holding.
