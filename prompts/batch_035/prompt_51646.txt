I've read LessWrong and the rationalist blogosphere for long enough that I should have written this essay years ago: a reading of Slaughterhouse-Five through the presentism–eternalism debate. The Tralfamadorians are textbook eternalists — they see in four dimensions, regard all moments as equally real, and treat the thought that time passes as an Earthling confusion — and their ethics seems to follow straight from the metaphysics: since no moment ever goes away, grief is a category error, and Billy should simply attend to the pleasant moments and let the rest alone. I want the reading to press on two suspected slides. First, does the claim that all moments are equally real entail anything at all about which moments matter or how one should grieve — or is the novel's consolation really fatalism in eternalist dress, such that presentism plus a fully deterministic physics would deliver exactly the same comfort, leaving the metaphysics doing no work? Second, Billy's coming unstuck gives the reader a moving now gliding along the block, which is closer to the moving spotlight theory than to anything the Tralfamadorians profess; I suspect the novel cannot narrate eternalism without covertly reinstating a privileged present, and I would like that suspicion tested rather than assumed. Please tell me where Vonnegut genuinely dramatises the block universe, where he trades on a slide from ontology to consolation, and whether the dark reading holds — that Billy's Tralfamadorian serenity is a symptom of his trauma rather than a philosophy he has adopted. Paraphrase scenes rather than quoting the text, and please write your response in British English.
