I teach an upper-level seminar on memory culture and how different societies deal with a bad past -- Germany and the whole Vergangenheitsbewältigung thing, Japan, Turkey, the American South and its monuments. 14 students, one 2.5-hour session, room with movable tables. I've tried running the Germany-vs-Japan comparison as a discussion twice and it keeps going wrong: students just perform the positions they read about the night before, like they're reciting for the seminar rather than feeling why any of this is hard. So I want to flip the order: they build a memory politics from scratch, under pressure, and only meet the real cases afterwards.

Design me a simulation for this. The setup: a fictional country (make one up, give it a one-page brief you also draft -- keep the factual seed minimal, maybe a fifth of what you're tempted to include) about 25 years out from the fall of a 20-year military dictatorship. The thing I want them to run into themselves, before anything gets named, is that "dealing with the past" sounds like one thing but is really four or five incompatible things -- prosecuting, archiving, memorializing, teaching, indemnifying -- each with different price tags, different timelines, and different constituencies, and that a coalition can vote unanimously for the slogan while agreeing on almost nothing underneath it. So: a fixed city budget and a menu of memory projects priced so they can't have all of them (a memorial at the old labor camp site, digitizing and opening the secret police files, renaming the big avenue vs. putting up contextual plaques, a school curriculum unit, pensions for victims' families, a museum), and role cards for a divided city -- victims' association, an old-regime party that's still a third of the vote, a youth faction tired of hearing about it, a business faction that wants tourism money, the historians. Some private objectives, a points scheme that doesn't need real money, hard decision points on a timeline.

Then a second round set 15 years later: half the chairs go to players' in-game children plus a foreign investor delegation, and they have to decide what to renew, revise, or let quietly lapse -- I want to see whether the round-one consensus survives its own success. For the debrief, map what actually happened onto the concepts (monumentalizing vs. working-through, renaming vs. contextualizing, why the second generation's boredom is doing real political work), and only then connect it to the cases we've read. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I assume some faction will try to win instead of negotiate, and students who already know the German case will treat it as the answer key -- and how to handle that. And be honest about what the activity structurally can't reproduce about real memory culture: the fear is obviously gone, but I'd like to know if the timescale problem is partially fixable with the second round, and what isn't fixable at all and I'll just have to supply in the debrief.
