I'd come across the game before in a course on game theory, and while most of the chapter sounded right, a couple of its claims sat oddly with me, so I'd like a careful audit rather than my own hunch. Here is the relevant passage, condensed slightly:

"To see how rational people end up paying two dollars for a dollar, the economist Martin Shubik devised the dollar auction in 1971 as a laboratory model of conflicts like Vietnam, which had a way of escalating far past any prize worth winning. The rules are simple: the highest bidder takes the dollar, but the second-highest bidder must also pay whatever was bid, and gets nothing. Once bids pass a dollar, the bidders are no longer trying to win; they are trying not to lose, and every further bid is a textbook case of the sunk cost fallacy — throwing good money after bad. Shubik showed that even intelligent, well-informed players reliably bid well past a dollar, and the game gave psychologists the idea for 'escalation of commitment', Barry Staw's finding that people double down on failing projects to justify the money already spent. The lesson is clear: never enter a contest where the loser pays, and once a project runs over budget, the rational move is to cut your losses immediately."
