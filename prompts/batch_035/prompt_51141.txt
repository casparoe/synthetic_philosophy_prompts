I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, and in two weeks I'm presenting my dissertation proposal to our department's consciousness seminar. The room is unusually split: roughly half experimentalists who work on attention, masking, and binocular rivalry and treat subjects' verbal reports as just another dependent variable, and the other half a mix that includes two phenomenologists trained in the Husserlian tradition who think any third-person method presupposes what it was supposed to investigate, plus one analytic philosopher of mind who knows the Dennett literature cold. My thesis is that heterophenomenology is the right methodological backbone for a science of consciousness, but in its classic form it leaves unused a resource it could legitimately draw on: reports from highly trained observers. I want to defend what I'm calling calibrated heterophenomenology. Psychophysics has always given extra weight to practiced observers without embarrassment, and my proposal is to treat first-person report reliability as itself an empirical, measurable quantity: a subject's report enters the data stream with a weight that depends on how well that subject's reports have tracked established measures in the past, so that a long-practiced meditator's or trained phenomenologist's report is neither dismissed as mere behavior with no special standing, nor swallowed whole as incorrigible first-person data, but is treated like an instrument whose calibration we can study and improve. The neutrality that makes heterophenomenology methodologically clean, I want to argue, is compatible with taking training seriously.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way the relevant subgroup would phrase it — the phenomenologists' worry about losing the first-person perspective in their vocabulary, the experimentalists' suspicion that I'm overcomplicating their data collection, and whatever the analytic philosopher of mind will raise about the intentional stance and the status of the subject's "texts." For each: a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and whether I should concede the point up front or fight it. I'm especially anxious about one thing, and I'd like your judgment on how exposed it really is: it seems possible that my calibration story is quietly self-undermining, since if training only counts as improving a subject's calibration to the extent that their reports converge with third-person measures, then the reports never add anything beyond those measures, and the meditators' distinctive claims — the ones this whole apparatus was supposed to let in — are exactly the ones that come out with no weight. If that objection lands, I'm not sure what I have left, but I also suspect there's a reply involving treating agreement-with-instruments as a bootstrap rather than the whole of calibration.
