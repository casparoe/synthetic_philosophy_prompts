And here's my classmate's answer:

"The lottery paradox, due to Kyburg, starts from a fair lottery with, say, a million tickets and exactly one winner. Each ticket has a probability of 0.999999 of losing, which is very high, and there's a plausible principle saying that if something is sufficiently probable, it's rational to believe it. So it's rational to believe of ticket 1 that it will lose, rational to believe of ticket 2 that it will lose, and so on. But it's also rational to believe that at least one ticket will win, since that's certain given the setup. So if belief is closed under conjunction, we can believe 'every ticket will lose,' which contradicts the thing we're certain of. Hence paradox.

Kyburg himself solved this by insisting that rational belief requires probability 1, so strictly speaking you shouldn't believe any ticket will lose — you should suspend judgment on all of them. This preserves consistency but at a heavy cost, since we rationally believe all sorts of things that fall short of certainty.

The preface paradox, due to Quine, is basically the same problem in a different setting. An author carefully checks each chapter of her book and so has excellent grounds for believing each chapter is error-free. Yet in the preface she sincerely asserts that the book 'surely contains at least one error,' because her past books and every other carefully checked book she knows of have contained errors. Believing every chapter is true, plus believing the book contains an error, is again inconsistent.

So the two paradoxes are really one paradox wearing two hats. In both cases you have a large set of individually justified beliefs, plus a well-justified belief that at least one member of the set is false. The only structural difference is that the lottery case spells out the probabilities explicitly while the preface case leaves them vague, which is a cosmetic difference, not a structural one.

Since the preface author is clearly rational, we have to accept that a rational agent's set of beliefs can be inconsistent. And this shows the probabilist is wrong too: the Bayesian demand that degrees of belief be consistent (coherent) can't be a requirement of rationality, since the author's credences are inconsistent in the same way. The lesson is that rationality just doesn't care about consistency, either for flat-out belief or for credences."
