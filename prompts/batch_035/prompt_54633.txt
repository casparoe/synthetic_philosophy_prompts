A graduate student of mine, working at the intersection of philosophy and machine learning, wants to use the coherence arguments as the framing for her thesis, and she has forwarded me a summary from an AI-safety newsletter that she proposes to rely on. It reads, in substance, as follows: "Coherence arguments show that rationality leaves an agent no freedom about its basic decision procedure. If your preferences are inconsistent — say, intransitive — a trader can cycle you through a series of trades that leaves you strictly worse off, and by repetition pump away all of your resources, so incoherent agents are eliminated by selection. Von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that any agent with consistent preferences is representable as an expected utility maximizer. Dutch book arguments do the same for beliefs: unless your credences obey the probability axioms, a bookie can offer you a set of bets, each of which you'll accept, that guarantees you a loss. So coherence is sufficient for expected utility maximization, and since markets and evolution punish incoherence, any sufficiently intelligent or competitively selected agent will be an expected utility maximizer — which is why alignment research is entitled to take that as its fixed starting point." I suspect this compresses too much, but I would like your help locating exactly where. Please audit it: which claims a careful decision theorist would accept as stated, which are conflations or overstatements — in particular, whether "consistent preferences" does the work the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem actually requires, and whether the probabilism and preference arguments are really running in parallel the way the summary implies — and whether the step from local coherence to "any sufficiently intelligent agent will be an expected utility maximizer" follows from anything true in the passage. I am inclined to think the framing survives with repairs — my student's instinct — but tell me honestly whether the concluding claim about alignment is something she can keep.
