For eleven years I have been the secretary of a small housing cooperative — thirty-one units, a shared tool shed, a rotating mowing roster, a seed library, a common-room fund nobody ever audited because nobody ever needed to. Three months ago the members voted, nearly unanimously and with some tears, to sell the building to a developer. The closing date is fixed and public: the thirtieth of June. Since then I have watched our cooperation die in almost textbook order, and I find this both galling and, I have to admit, grimly funny. The mowing roster quietly emptied within a fortnight of the vote. The seed library was "borrowed from" wholesale by a member who has, I am told, a thriving stall at the weekend market. Last week a padlock appeared on the tool shed, along with a note about "transition-period asset stewardship." Two households have stopped contributing to the common-room fund on the stated reasoning that there will soon be no common room. It is the backward-induction unraveling, performed across raised garden beds, and I have decided that if I cannot stop it I can at least memorialize it.

Write a satirical news article for our final newsletter — headline plus roughly 450 to 550 words — reporting on the situation in the breezy style of a local paper's human-interest story. The piece must be deadpan throughout: it reports each act of defection with the same cheerful tone it uses for the community's history of goodwill, and it never editorializes, never calls anyone selfish, and never winks at the reader. The irony should be legible entirely through the juxtaposition.

The humor must come from getting the game theory right, so that someone who teaches this material would nod rather than wince. In particular, the article should reflect, without lecturing, the actual mechanics of the collapse: cooperation in an indefinitely repeated game being sustained by the shadow of the future, and its evaporation once the end date is common knowledge, with the defection spreading backwards from the final weeks as everyone anticipates everyone else's anticipation; the difference between how the same people treated strangers (one-shot logic) and neighbors (iterated logic) back when the horizon was open; the fate of the two or three residents who are still playing cooperative strategies out of habit or principle and are now being steadily exploited; and the small tragedy of misread signals — at least one feud in the article should have begun with a genuine mistake, someone who truly forgot a roster duty, and then escalated through retaliatory rounds, so that the noise problem appears alongside the horizon problem. The padlock note may be quoted; a payoff matrix may be alluded to as though it were a piece of community ephemera, like a flyer for the summer fair.

Include at least three quoted voices, all invented: a resident who defends his withdrawals strictly on the grounds of the known terminal date, and is puzzled that anyone finds this antisocial, since under the new conditions it is simply the rational thing; a resident who keeps cooperating first and keeps losing, and who insists she would do the same again, though she can no longer quite say why; and an academic who has moved in across the street, or a resident who has taken an evening class, and who now describes the shed, the roster, and the fund in the vocabulary of strategy names, to the visible irritation of everyone else.

The article should close with something forward-looking and hollow — the annual picnic, or the question of who will take the communal rhododendrons — and it should leave a reader who knows nothing of game theory with the sense of a real and ordinary loss, and a reader who knows it well with the uncomfortable recognition that the model is not a caricature of these people but a description of them.
