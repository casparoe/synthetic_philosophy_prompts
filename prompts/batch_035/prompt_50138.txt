For roughly two years I have been turning over Newton's bucket argument on my own — I have no philosophical training, just a cheap paperback edition of the Principia and a good many evenings — and I have now been invited to give a twenty-five-minute talk on it at the philosophy evening class run by my local library. The audience will be curious adults with no physics beyond school; I get a whiteboard and nothing else. I am not asking you to summarise the argument for me; I want a plan for how to deliver it.

A few things I have worked out for myself, which the plan should take into account. First, everyone in that room will have stood on a playground roundabout at some point, and their memory of being flung outwards is the strongest thing they will bring with them. I suspect it is also the root of the worst misunderstanding they will form — that there is a real outward force pushing things away from the centre — and that if this is left alone, people will end up thinking Newton has shown something about forces, when the argument is actually about what any motion is relative to. Second, my instinct is that the water's surface, not the bucket, should carry the whole talk: the bucket's walls can be at rest or spinning and it makes no difference to the water, but the surface is flat at the beginning and hollow at the end, and the changeover happens when the water is spinning relative to — what, exactly? It is that question I want the room to feel as a genuine puzzle rather than as a rhetorical flourish. Third, I keep wondering whether to close with the case Newton himself raised of two globes joined by a cord, in a universe containing nothing else: there the cord's tension is the only sign that anything is rotating at all, and there is nothing else for the rotation to be relative to. It unsettles me more than the bucket does, because the obvious reply people make to the bucket — that early on the water is moving relative to the bucket, and later it is not — has no analogue there. But I cannot judge whether an audience this fresh can take it, or whether it belongs on the cutting-room floor.

So please plan the session for me. I want the order in which the pieces must come and what depends on what; whether the roundabout or the bucket should go first, and which of them should carry the argument; the misconceptions the audience will form, in the order they will surface, together with the point in the sequence at which each is best headed off — I have already guessed at the outward-force one, but I also expect someone to say that Einstein settled the whole matter, and someone else to say the question answers itself because the water is plainly moving relative to the Earth, so tell me where those two will land and how the sequence should absorb them. I also want to know what I should openly decline to go into at this level — my own guess is the mathematics of curved spacetime, and probably Mach as well, but correct me if I am wrong. And finally, one question I could put to the room at the end whose answers would show me whether they have genuinely grasped that the argument is about what makes it true that something is rotating, rather than about whether rotation has visible effects.
