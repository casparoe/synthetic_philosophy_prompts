Background, since you'll need to know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired physicist (thirty-odd years in satellite geodesy, which mostly taught me to distrust elegant models of messy systems), and since retiring I've been sitting in on graduate seminars at the university near me -- statistics first, then decision theory, and now I've got the bug properly. Next month I'm presenting to their rational agency working group on the absent-minded driver problem, and after how my last outing went, I want a real prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the paper itself. Quick statement of the setup so we're aligned: the driver passes identical intersections, can't tell which one he's at, and forms no memory of earlier choices. Exit at the first intersection pays 1, exit at the second pays 4, sailing past both pays 0. The standard planning answer is to continue at each intersection with independent probability 3/8, for an expected 25/16. But from inside the driver's seat -- "I'm at some intersection" -- that plan looks suboptimal, and optimizing from the seat breaks the plan. That tension is the paradox. My thesis is that the problem is mislabeled: it isn't a puzzle for decision theory (evidential versus causal versus whatever else), it's a coordination problem between two instances of the same driver with perfectly aligned interests and no way to communicate, so the machinery that actually applies is the machinery of symmetric games -- focal points, correlated strategies, maybe team reasoning -- and most of the literature is rummaging in the wrong drawer.

The audience matters here, so let me describe them. The economists and game theorists treat this as home turf -- it came out of their own literature in the 90s -- and they'll push hardest on the claim that there's nothing left to explain once you set it up as a game of imperfect recall; the philosophers of action will ask whether "two instances of the same person" is doing any real work or whether it's just one agent with amnesia wearing a costume; and there's a CS guy who will say that any driver recomputing at every intersection with the same information does the same thing every time, so he wants to know where the indeterminacy is even supposed to come from. Same thesis, very different incoming fire.
