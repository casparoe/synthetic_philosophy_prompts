I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar (twelve students, all comfortable with elementary propositional logic) and I'm writing the take-home final, to be completed over 48 hours. I want one question on Moore's paradox that cannot be answered by reciting the textbook line that assertion expresses belief. The question should force students to explain a pattern of data, not just the famous sentence, and the pattern should be printed on the exam paper itself so that students who never encountered the variants still have everything they need.

Here is the data set I want the question built around, reproduced verbatim as items (1)–(5):

(1) "It's raining, but I don't believe it" — absurd to assert, yet plainly consistent: there are situations in which it is raining and the speaker does not believe it (on rainy nights, for instance, the speaker is asleep).
(2) "It's raining, but John doesn't believe it" — perfectly assertible; often just a useful piece of gossip.
(3) "It was raining yesterday, but I didn't believe it" — assertible; a natural thing to say when recounting a mistake.
(4) "It's raining, but no one believes it" — strange to assert, though presumably true whenever rain begins to fall over an empty valley.
(5) The diary case: a person writes "It's raining, but I don't believe it" in a private diary that no one will ever read, and never says it aloud.

The question should ask: what account of the relation between assertion and belief predicts exactly this distribution — defective in (1), fine in (2) and (3), strange again in (4), and whatever the student judges about (5)? Students must commit on whether the defect in (1) is semantic (the sentence, as asserted, is a contradiction in some extended sense — the Wittgensteinian line) or pragmatic (a violation of a norm governing assertion), and their account must earn its keep across all five items: a story that explains (1) while remaining silent on why (2) through (4) behave differently is not an answer. On (4) I especially want them to notice the difference between a defect that is speaker-relative, as in (1), and one that is not — if you assert (4) and believe yourself, you already believe it is raining, which falsifies the second conjunct, and the same trap awaits anyone who tries to accept your assertion. On (5) I want them to decide whether the absurdity attaches to the act of asserting or to the state of believing one does not believe, and to see that their answer determines whether Moore's paradox is ultimately about the philosophy of language or about self-knowledge.

Please respond in three sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths:

1. "Exam question" (250–350 words): the question exactly as it will appear on the paper, including the data set above, phrased for students, with any word limit and the credit weighting stated.
2. "Model answer" (about 600 words): written as an actual answer a top student would submit, not an outline or a commentary on what such an answer would say.
3. "Marking scheme" (about 450 words): allocate the 20 marks across exactly five must-hit points, each with its mark value and one line on how B-grade answers typically miss it; then close with a short list of moves that should lose marks even though they sound sophisticated — e.g. gesturing at "pragmatic contradiction" without ever saying what would make a norm of assertion true rather than false. The scheme should make it possible for a grader to distinguish an A answer, which handles the tense asymmetry in (3) and the speaker-relativity contrast between (1) and (4), from a B answer that explains (1) alone.
