Something I've never gotten a straight answer on: what does discounting in government cost-benefit analysis actually presuppose about the value of future people's lives?

So the practice, as I understand it: when an agency appraises a project with long-run effects — a flood barrier, a nuclear waste facility, climate policy — it discounts future costs and benefits. A benefit arriving in 2100 gets multiplied down by some rate per year, so a death prevented in 2100 counts for a fraction of a death prevented today. The stated justification for the rate is usually a mix of things: money now can be invested, so a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later; future people will be richer, so an extra dollar means less to them; and then usually a leftover chunk called something like the "pure" rate of time preference, which is just... future welfare counts less because it's later. The famous fight a while back over climate economics was basically about whether that pure chunk should be zero or a few percent per year, and the choice swung the whole appraisal of climate policy.

What I find puzzling is that the practice seems internally messy in ways nobody acknowledges. Some guidance uses a rate that declines over the horizon — high for the next few decades, then stepping down — which is hard to square with any single story about why we discount at all. Health effects are sometimes discounted at a different rate than the money costs of producing them. And the same governments publish sustainability language saying we owe future generations consideration, while the arithmetic of appraisal steadily shrinks whatever they'd get out of our projects.

So here's my question: if you take the practice seriously as a philosophical object rather than a technical convention, what theory of value does it presuppose? Is the discount meant to operate on welfare itself — later people just matter less — or only on units like money and health-years, via their wealth, in which case anything whose value doesn't track wealth (species survival? avoiding extinction?) arguably shouldn't be discounted at all, or maybe should be discounted negatively since future people will be richer but not more numerous? Can the declining-rate schedules be derived from any coherent view, or are they best read as a political compromise between an ethical camp (pure preference is indefensible) and an economic one (opportunity cost is real), stitched together so nobody has to win the argument? And the part I most want: if a government genuinely adopted a zero pure time preference, how would appraisal actually change — would far-century benefits just dominate everything, and would that be a reductio of the ethics or of the method? Please distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to fit with; I suspect alot of the discounting machinery is held together by ambiguity between "money is worth less to the rich" and "later lives are worth less, period," and I want to know whether that ambiguity is load-bearing.
