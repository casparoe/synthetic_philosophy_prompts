I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and my editor wants a piece pushing back on the recent "consciousness is basically being figured out" hype. My half-baked thesis: global workspace theory, and the experiments people cite for it (the kind where a stimulus either "ignites" the fronto-parietal network or doesn't, and subjects report which), are really a theory of conscious *access* — the stuff that gets broadcast and becomes available for reporting, reasoning, remembering — and everyone keeps sliding from that to phenomenal experience, the what-it's-like part. The twist I want as the centerpiece: even the newer "no-report" paradigms, where experimenters try to measure awareness without asking the subject, still define the conscious moment by what the subject *would have* reported — so they don't escape the circle, they just hide it better. Write the argument for me: a good chunk of current consciousness science is a science of access dressed up as a science of phenomenology, and the field should say so out loud. Keep it punchy, op-ed length, but don't dumb it down. And at the end tell me how confident you are in each of the main steps, and single out the one point you're least sure of — I don't want to print something that falls apart the first time an actual cognitive scientist reads it.
