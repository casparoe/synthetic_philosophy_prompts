I'm taking a political philosophy course and we just covered the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism — the Rawls point that utilitarianism basically treats a whole society as if it were one big person with one pool of utility, so it can't explain why it's wrong to chop up one guy for parts when the numbers work out. For my term paper I tried to sharpen the objection into an actual argument, and now it's proving way too much and I cant tell which piece to drop. My roommate, who is annoyingly good at this stuff, read it and just said "yeah, that conclusion is cooked, good luck" and went back to his problem set.

Here's the argument. (1) It's impermissible to impose a serious unconsented burden on one person for the sake of benefits to others — the organ harvesting case — and what explains this is not the size of the numbers but the fact that the burden and the benefits fall on different people. (2) And why does that matter? Because a tradeoff can only be justified from the standpoint of someone for whom the trade is net positive: there has to be a single subject whose life contains both the loss and the gain, so the gain can count as compensating the loss. (3) If there is no such subject — no one for whom the outcome is better — then the outcome is not better, full stop. "Better" just is "better for someone"; there's no further impersonal sense in which gains in one life can outweigh losses in another. (4) In the transplant case there's obviously no one for whom the outcome is better, so it's not merely wrong to do it, it's not even an improvement over leaving things alone. Fine so far, I can live with that. But (5) the same holds whenever benefits and burdens land on different people, which is basically every policy ever. A dam that floods one valley to irrigate another: no single subject both gains and loses, so on this view building it isn't better than not building it. Not worse, not better, just not comparable. (6) And a war that kills a million to prevent a famine that would have killed ten million is not better than the famine. That can't be right — even the most hardcore anti-utilitarians in the class think some mixed-benefit policies, and maybe even some wars, are better than their alternatives, all things considered.

So something has to go, and I don't like any of the options. Dropping (1) or (2) basically gives up the separateness objection, which is suposed to be the whole paper. Dropping (3) means admitting there's an impersonal sense of "better" in which gains and losses can be summed across people — but isn't that just utilitarianism smuggled back in at the meta level, one step too late to do any work? Maybe there's a third option, like saying "better" can be indexed to something other than individual persons (groups? the whole world?) but that feels like it's cheating too. Which step should I reject, and what do I actually pay for rejecting it — like, what does my view then have to say about the dam case? Also, if you think my roommate is wrong and the conclusion is actually fine, I'd genuinely take that too, but you'd have to sell me on a world where nothing with mixed effects across different people is ever better or worse than anything else.
