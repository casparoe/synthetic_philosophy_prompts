I'm a PhD student in political theory, writing a dissertation chapter on how the formal theory of nuclear strategy was received, caricatured and absorbed by the wider culture of the early 1960s. My supervisor, who cut his teeth on Cold War security studies, has suggested I stop treating Stanley Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove (1964) as a period satire and instead treat it as an intervention in the deterrence literature itself — a comic objection to the theory being assembled at exactly that moment by Thomas Schelling and Herman Kahn (Kubrick consulted Kahn, who had seriously entertained proposals for doomsday machines). The fit does seem uncannily close: the film stages the credibility paradox (deterrence rests on a threat to do something that would be irrational to carry out), the commitment problem and its devices, brinkmanship as the deliberate manipulation of shared and uncontrolled risk, and the tension between making one's retaliation automatic and tamper-proof on the one hand and leaving it hostage to accident or a single deranged officer on the other.

What I would like is a close reading of the film through deterrence paradoxes and brinkmanship that takes both the film and the theory seriously, and that is faithful in both directions — neither reducing the film to an illustration of Schelling nor distorting the theory to fit the jokes. Three threads I particularly want pursued. First, the Soviet doomsday machine: an array of cobalt bombs, buried, unconditionally automatic, and programmed to detonate if anyone tries to disarm it — the perfect commitment device, which fails to deter for the sole reason that it was kept secret, with the premier intending to announce it at the Party Congress the following week. What precisely does this show about the knowledge and credibility conditions of deterrence, and is the film right that a machine solves the rationality problem only by relocating it into design, communication and concealment? Second, Plan R itself: a provision deliberately built to guarantee retaliation after a decapitation strike becomes the instrument of accidental war, which suggests the deeper and darker symmetry — that everything making deterrence robust against the enemy makes it fragile against one's own officers, procedures and lapses. Third, General Ripper: deterrence theory presumes a unitary rational actor, and the film's rejoinder is one paranoid man with a loophole and a fluoridation obsession. Should a Schelling-style theorist concede that the film exposes a genuine gap in the theory — the organisational and psychological failure modes that formal models abstract away — or does the satire depend on exaggerations (a lone general able to order a wing attack) that the command arrangements of the day wouldn't have permitted, so that an honest critic has to flag where the film cheats? Please also tell me where the film resists the reading: the war-room first-strike arithmetic, and the closing debate about repopulating from mineshafts, where the comedy comes from cold rational-choice talk rather than from madness, and where one might say the film shows deterrence logic surviving the apocalypse it failed to prevent.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of strategy who knows the film and the theory equally well, and think carefully before committing to verdicts — sketch the competing readings of each scene first, then adjudicate. Note that I cannot chase up references, so argue the theory from its content rather than by name-dropping papers. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the strongest one-sentence statement of what the film actually shows about deterrence — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
