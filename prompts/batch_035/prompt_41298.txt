My closest friend has, over the past three years, moved her gym membership four times: from gym A to gym B, then B to C, then C back to B, and finally B back to A, paying a cancellation or joining fee at each step. She is now exactly where she started, out roughly nine hundred dollars in fees, and with what she admits is no better equipment, location, or schedule than she had at the beginning. Her partner thinks this record is straightforwardly irrational — if you end where you began and paid for the round trip, something went wrong. My friend's defense is that every individual decision was well-grounded: gym A got overcrowded, gym B's hours changed after a management switch, gym C briefly raised its rates, and so on. The circumstances genuinely changed between decisions, she says, so there is no single moment anyone can point to as the mistake; evaluated one at a time, each choice was the right one given what she knew then. She adds that she has no interest in signing long contracts to lock in her current preferences, since treating her future self as someone to be bound and managed seems to her both paranoid and vaguely insulting. Her partner replies that the fact she wouldn't bind herself is part of the problem — that a person with stable, coherent preferences wouldn't need external binding, and that the gyms, who run these fee schedules openly and see people like her cycle through all the time, are effectively running a business model on the gap between her moment-to-moment judgment and her overall interests.

Who has the better of this? More precisely: is there anything genuinely irrational in my friend's record, and if so, where does the irrationality live — in the individual choices, in the sequence taken as a whole, or only in the counterfactual situation where someone deliberately arranged the options knowing her tendencies? Is it fair to say that the mere fact of being exploitable in principle shows something wrong with her preferences, even though no one ever actually exploited her, and the changes she cited were real? And is her refusal to precommit a legitimate position about how a person should relate to her future selves, or is it best understood as a rationalization that guarantees she'll keep paying for the same round trip? I'd appreciate a clear verdict along with your reasoning about each of these questions.
