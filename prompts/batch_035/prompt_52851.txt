Is there an established name for the following move, and if not I'd like you to coin one? I keep noticing that a lot of the rules governing my day aren't laws at all — they're physical. The trolleys at my local supermarket only release once you've put a pound coin in, which quietly does the job that a reward scheme or a "please return your trolley" notice would otherwise have to do. The benches outside the station have armrests every half-metre so nobody can lie down on them. The traffic calming near the school is a chicane instead of a 20 mph sign, because apparently the sign wasn't trusted on its own. My friend's block of flats has a spring-loaded gate that can't be propped open, which settles the standing argument about whether the gate should be left open, without anyone ever having to win that argument.

I've heard the bench kind of thing called hostile architecture, but that label seems to cover only design aimed at keeping certain kinds of people away, whereas what I'm noticing is broader: the moment when a regulation gets moved out of the rulebook and into the object, so the rule enforces itself. Nobody has to be present to apply it, nobody can be blamed for breaking it, and nobody gets any discretion — the chicane doesnt have a slow day, the gate makes no exception for someone struggling with a sofa. I was talking this through with my brother-in-law, who's an urban planner, and he just said "everything is regulation now" and couldn't name it either, so I'm asking you.

So: does this general move — building the rule into the artifact rather than announcing it — have a name of its own, and how does it relate to hostile architecture, which seems to me like one subtype rather than the whole category? If there is no single established term, I want more than a name; I want you to actually carve the concept. Which cases count and which dont? What's the difference, if any, between an object that makes a rule impossible to break and one that merely makes breaking it costly? And where's the boundary between this and ordinary ergonomics — a handle shaped to be pulled rather than pushed is doing *some* work of telling me what to do, so at what point does that become a kind of lawmaking? I'd also be curious whether the philosophers who argue about whether machines and structures can themselves embody politics would say this phenomenon is evidence for their side, but only if you can work that in without turning the answer into a lecture.
