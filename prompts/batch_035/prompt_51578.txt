I'm taking a course on justice and equality of opportunity, and I've become stuck on something about how our tax institutions actually treat inherited wealth. In the United States, an inheritance is not counted as income to the person who receives it — even though it arrives, from the recipient's point of view, as a windfall determined entirely by the accident of birth. The estate tax applies only to very large estates, transfers to a surviving spouse pass entirely untaxed, and when heirs sell inherited assets they pay capital gains tax only on appreciation since the death, not since the original purchase — a "step-up in basis" that quietly erases decades of unrealized gains. The gift tax exists almost entirely as a backstop, to stop people from dodging the estate tax while alive; meanwhile annual gifts below a modest threshold, payments of tuition and medical bills for relatives, and gifts to charity are all exempt without limit. Politicians defend the whole arrangement both ways at once: the estate tax is justified as a bulwark against hereditary aristocracy and a leveling force for equal opportunity, while its opponents denounce it as "double taxation" of wealth that already belonged, in some sense, to the family rather than to the deceased alone.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory this bundle of rules presupposes. Does the non-taxation of inheritances as income, together with the spousal and familial exemptions, presuppose a picture of the household as a unit of shared ownership — the idea that the heir was already a partial owner of the family's wealth all along, so that the "transfer" at death is in part a legal fiction? Or does it presuppose something about the moral distinctness of family transmission specifically, such that money earned by a stranger's labor is income when it comes to me but money earned by my parent's labor is not? Then there is the opposite pull: the estate tax itself, the anti-aristocracy rhetoric, and the generation-skipping rules all seem to presuppose that dynastic accumulation is a public harm and that equality of starting points matters. Can these two sets of presuppositions live together, or is the system philosophically incoherent rather than merely a political compromise? Finally, distinguish for me what the system strictly requires from what it merely accommodates — could a consistent meritocrat, a consistent defender of family autonomy, and a luck-egalitarian each sign off on some parts of this arrangement, and which parts would force each of them to demand a change? And if we rebuilt the system on a straightforwardly egalitarian theory — say, by treating inheritances above some threshold as ordinary income of the recipient — what in the current design would have to go beyond the estate tax itself?
