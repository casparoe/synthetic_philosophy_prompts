I'll be grateful for a careful, unhurried answer from someone who can speak as a professional philosopher of emotion, and I'd ask you to work through your reasoning before committing to conclusions rather than handing me a survey of positions.

Some background. My mother died in the spring. A few weeks after the funeral I found myself sobbing over a death scene in a novel — not a thin, aesthetic thrill, but the heavy bodily thing, the same creature that had sat through the funeral and felt it. What unsettled me was not the crying itself but that it coexisted, without strain, with my complete knowledge that the character never existed and the death never happened. I did not believe for a moment that anyone died, and I grieved anyway.

I studied philosophy as an undergraduate, so I know this is an old battleground. The standard shape of the problem, as I understand it: it seems we only fear what we believe to be real; we are genuinely moved by what happens to characters; and we never believe in the characters. Something has to go, and each of the exits has been on the table for decades — say the emotions aren't genuine but some mimicry; say the emotions are genuine but irrational, the price of a cognitive failure; say fear and pity don't require belief at all, only imagining or thinking-of; say the real object is something else entirely, the skill of the performer or the formal properties of the work. My reading over the past months suggests that every exit has a well-known rejoinder, every rejoinder a well-known counter-rejoinder, citations accumulate, and nobody has moved in forty years. That stall is what I want diagnosed, not the paradox itself answered.

Here is what I've noticed that makes me suspect the stall is structural rather than a matter of anyone being obtuse, and I want you to take these as test cases any diagnosis must accommodate rather than as curiosities. First, I cried at the novel, but I also tear up at reruns of news footage I know is decades old, where everything was once fully real; and I have woken from a nightmare genuinely afraid of something I already knew, while dreaming, was a dream. Neither is fiction in the strict sense, yet both look like the same phenomenon. Second, dramatic irony: watching a tragedy, I dread a disaster for a character precisely because I know it is coming and she does not — my fear seems to require my knowledge of the fictional fate, which is odd if fear is supposed to be a response to apparent present danger. Third, and this is the one I find most perplexing: earlier this year I made a deliberately cruel choice in a video game, one no one existed to suffer from, and felt something uncomfortably close to guilt about it for days — though guilt normally tracks something I actually did to someone. And fourth, the ambiguity of my own case: when I sobbed at that death scene, was I moved by the fictional death, or by the actual fact that a living author had managed to construct something that does this to readers — a real object if ever there was one? I genuinely cannot tell these apart from the inside.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the disagreement may be partly verbal, because the two camps mean different things by belief — one treating it as occurrent endorsement of a proposition, the other as any cognitive state that can feed an emotion — so that each side can concede the other's point without noticing it. Or perhaps the sides are answering two different questions under one name: whether fiction-directed emotions occur, which seems settled, and whether they are fitting or rational, which may be a normative question that no description of our psychology could settle. But I also suspect something else: the third leg of the paradox, the claim that we cannot be moved by what we take to be unreal, may function as bedrock for one side — an intuition about what emotion is for that neither side can argue the other out of. I don't know which of these, if any, is right.

So here is what I'm asking. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what the participants actually claim: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of two questions under one heading; is a hidden question about what emotions require as input doing the work; is the impasse a sign that the original question was ill-posed? Your diagnosis must earn its keep against my four cases — it should tell me which of them belong to the same phenomenon and why, and whether the video-game case, where I seem to be the author of the harm, breaks the pattern or exposes something the standard formulations miss. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a finding about emotion, a change in how the question is posed — or give me an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. I am not looking for the name of a winning theory. I want to understand what kind of thing the disagreement is.
