My brother and I have been arguing ever since a documentary about welfare policy sent us down a rabbit hole, and I've realized I actually don't know what I mean when I say two people are "equally well off." I've been testing myself on cases, and my gut verdicts seem to point in different directions, so I want to lay them out and see if there's a single principle underneath, or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are the verdicts. If someone is paralyzed and needs an accessible apartment and a wheelchair, I think society clearly owes her the difference — it's not fair that she's worse off through no choice of hers, and money or services should make it up. Same for someone born with a condition that requires constant medication. But if someone simply has really expensive tastes — he genuinely suffers unless he can drink vintage wine, feels plain bread as an insult — I don't feel we owe him anything, even though by his own lights he's just as badly off as the paralyzed woman. Then there's the guy who could work but surfs all day and is poor: no compensation, obviously. Yet an artist who deliberately takes a badly paid job she finds deeply fulfilling — I feel almost the same about her, no compensation, but not quite as confidently, and I can't say why. Two more that trouble me: a woman in grinding poverty who has genuinely cheerful, modest expectations and reports being perfectly content — I feel she's still badly off, even though by her own happiness she's fine. And a man who's had a hard life and adjusted his hopes down until he no longer wants anything he can't have — something about that bothers me even more, as if the contentment itself were the injury.

So my question is: is there one currency of equality that gets all or nearly all of these right — is it about resources (money and goods in your hands), welfare (how well your life actually goes for you, by your own lights), or something in between, like what you're genuinely able to do and be regardless of whether you end up wanting to use it? I can see that welfare handles the poverty-but-cheerful case badly, and that resources handles the wheelchair case badly too (the same money buys different lives), but I don't want to just bolt the two together with no story. Please tell me what principle best captures my verdicts, which verdicts it can't accommodate and I should probably give up, and — this is the part I most want — what it says about two cases I haven't decided: someone who freely takes a huge gamble, knows the odds, and loses everything; and someone who would be miserable in every life available to her, not because anything external is wrong, but because of how her mind is put together.
