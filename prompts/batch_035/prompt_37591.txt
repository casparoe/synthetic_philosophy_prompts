I run a small ML team at a company that sells vision systems for factory quality inspection, and I'm the guy who has to sign off every time we ship a model to a customer line. It's a few release decisions a month, each one has real money attached (a bad model means missed defects that reach the customer's customer, or a flood of false rejects that stops the line), and after signing I can't easily walk it back — the models run inside the plant, offline, for months.

Here's my recurring problem, and why I'm asking you. Our models look great on our curated test suites and then reality drifts: a customer changes the alloy, the lighting rig gets swapped, a new line has a slightly different camera angle, and performance quietly degrades. My engineers respond by adding more test data; the sales team responds by quoting the benchmark numbers; I'm stuck in the middle guessing. I know enough to know this is an old problem wearing new clothes — something like the no-free-lunch results (no learner can beat any other averaged over all possible worlds, so any advantage has to come from assumptions matching the actual problem) and the classic problem of induction (past performance on a set of cases is only evidence to the extent the future keeps being the same kind of thing, which you can't verify from inside). I don't want a philosophy lecture though. I want a release-gate procedure my team can actually run.

Concretely: something two engineers can execute in an afternoon, no PhD in learning theory required, producing a short report I can hand to a non-technical director. It should be an ordered list of questions about a candidate model, with (a) what counts as a sufficient answer to each, (b) where the procedure outputs a hard "no, hold this release" and what to do instead (collect what data, run what check), and (c) where it refuses to decide and hands the question to a named human, because what's left is a bet about the future or a values question, not a facts question. I specifically want it to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up in procedure clothing — I've been burned by checklists that pretend to be objective.

Where I think the theory should bite: the procedure can't treat benchmark numbers as evidence by default. It has to force us to say what we're assuming about the deployment distribution relative to the training/testing distribution, what would have to be true of the world for the benchmark to predict the line, and who's actually vouching for that match. And it should exploit the flip side of no-free-lunch too — if a model is doing suspiciously well everywhere, that itself is information that it's fit to something other than the problem we care about.

Then run it on two cases so I can see how it behaves:

1. A new defect-detection model that beats our incumbent by six points on the full standard suite, trained on our biggest pooled dataset — the sales team is already quoting the six points, and the customer deployment is a single plant that contributed almost none of that data.

2. A candidate that only matches the incumbent on the suite — no headline number, nothing for marketing — but was trained with a month of data pulled from that specific plant, including a period where they ran a different supplier's material.

I want at least one of these to come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire — what kind of real-world shift it will wave through, or what kind of genuinely safe release it will stall. And be clear about what the procedure fundamentally cannot do: I assume there's no way for it to prove the future will cooperate, so where exactly does it bottom out in somebody signing their name to a guess?
