I spent the better part of four decades doing radiochemistry — isotope ratios in soil cores, mostly — and I have come to philosophy only in retirement, working through the philosophy of language and of science in whatever order the secondhand bookshop supplies. The question that has me stuck is truth. The deflationary line, as I understand it, says there is nothing more to truth than the equivalence between asserting a sentence and asserting that it is true, and that the concept earns its keep mainly as a device of generalization: it lets me say that every reading in the log is true without reciting the log. Part of me finds this exactly right; it matches how the word behaves in my lab notebooks. Another part suspects it is true in the way a map with no roads on it is accurate: correct, and useless for driving.

Before I take sides, I want a specification, because I have noticed that arguments over deflationism are usually arguments over what a theory of truth is *for*. So please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of truth must meet if it is to serve the practice of empirical science specifically — that is the standard of adequacy I want to fix in advance. Among the uses I would want covered, consider at least these, and add whatever I have missed: licensing the inference from "it is true that the sample contains cesium-137" to the sample containing cesium-137, and back; expressing infinite or open-ended conjunctions ("every entry in this data table is accurate" — where "accurate" seems to be doing truth's work without being truth's word); serving as the norm of assertion and belief, if it is one; explaining, or explaining away, the intuition that the success of our best instruments and models needs truth as an explanatory posit, as some scientific realists claim; and meshing with the formal results a scientist of my vintage at least knows of secondhand — Tarski's undefinability theorem and the liar paradoxes. There is also a consideration I want on the table and want you to judge fairly rather than humor: in instrument work we never compare a reading with reality directly; we calibrate against other instruments, which were calibrated against others, and the whole edifice is only anchored if there is a point at which the readings are true of the world rather than merely consistent with one another. Call this the calibration problem if you like. I do not know whether it is a genuine constraint on a theory of truth or a confusion the deflationist can diagnose, and I would like to know which.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and classify it as non-negotiable, a preference of a particular camp (say which), or suspect — a demand masquerading as a constraint. Then flag any pair or subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, if there is one; that is the part I most want to see done honestly. Only after that, and briefly, score the redundancy theory, Quinean disquotationalism, Horwich's minimalism, and the prosentential theory against the list — the criteria are the substance, not the scorecard.

Please give the whole thing as a single JSON object, structured as follows. A field "purpose": one sentence stating the standard of adequacy fixed above. A field "desiderata": an array of objects, each with "id" (d1, d2, ...), "name", "statement", "motivating_case", "status" (one of "non_negotiable", "camp_preference", "suspect"), and "status_note" explaining the classification and, for camp preferences, naming the camp. A field "joint_satisfiability": an object with "verdict" and, if unsatisfiable subsets exist, a "conflicting_sets" array listing the ids and the tension in a sentence. A field "most_excluding": the id of the desideratum that knocks out the most candidates, with a reason. A field "scorecard": an array of objects with "theory", "meets" and "fails" (arrays of ids), and "one_line_verdict". Finally, a field "what_would_change_my_mind": an array of the two or three specific arguments or considerations that would flip your verdict on the deflationary side, and a field "weakest_link" naming where in your own reasoning you think a mistake is most likely to be hiding — I have been wrong about "obvious" points often enough in this retirement project to want that stated plainly, not as a formality.
