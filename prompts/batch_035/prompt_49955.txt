I took a decision theory course last semester and we spent a week on Ellsberg's urn experiments and ambiguity aversion — the idea that people treat unknown probabilities differently from known ones in a way plain expected utility theory can't capture. Ever since, I keep seeing the concept everywhere, and I want to test it against a real case: the collapse of the terrorism insurance market after 9/11. The way I understand the basic story: after the attacks, reinsurers (who you'd think are in the business of taking on exactly this kind of risk) pretty much immediately refused to write new terrorism coverage, primary insurers followed by excluding terrorism from commercial policies, and real estate and construction deals that needed coverage started stalling. Congress eventually stepped in with the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act in late 2002, which required insurers to make terrorism coverage available but put the government on the hook for the bulk of losses above a deductible. So here's my question: analyze this episode through the lens of ambiguity aversion. Map the actors — reinsurers, primary insurers, property owners, lenders, the government — and say what refusing to write coverage at basically any price looks like if you model it as an Ellsberg-style choice between bets with known odds and bets with unknown odds.

Where I especially want the careful work is in both directions. First, what does the Ellsberg/ambiguity frame actually explain here — was the market failing because insurers couldn't *estimate* a probability (ambiguous) versus estimating one fine but not liking it (risky but terrible), and does that distinction do any real explanatory work? Second, where does the frame misdescribe or miss things — like fear of correlated losses and outright ruin, capital and rating-agency constraints, or legal exposure, any of which could have sunk the insurers even if someone had handed them a decent probability estimate. And third, does the episode test the theory back — e.g., does the fact that the government could restore the market just by acting as a backstop suggest that "ambiguity aversion" was really rational wariness about tail risk and ruin rather than a preference quirk, the way the original Ellsberg experiments seem to show?

Please be upfront about how confident you are in each part of the analysis and single out the point you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the mapping is shaky than get a polished story. And don't worry too much about legislative details or exact dates; I care about the conceptual mapping, not the fine print of the statute. Also happy if you flag places where the theory is just silent (like maybe it says nothing about why reinsurance specifically collapsed first) instead of forcing it to say something.
