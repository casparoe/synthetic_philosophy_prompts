I'm the engineer responsible for observability at a company that runs a large distributed system, and a design review last month left me unable to think about much else. Our monitoring stack — the thing that is supposed to tell us when the service is degrading — is itself a distributed system, deployed by the same automated rollout process as everything else, running on the same hardware, upgraded by the very pipeline it is meant to keep an eye on. When an auditor asked how we know the monitoring works, the honest answer was that we look at its track record: when it has flagged incidents, we investigated (using, mostly, other parts of the monitoring stack) and it turned out to be right; when it was silent, we inferred all was well. A colleague called this bootstrapping, and I've since found that epistemologists circle the same structure under the labels bootstrapping and easy knowledge. Rather than reach for whichever answer is nearest, I want the specification first.

So: write the desiderata — what any adequate account of how a source earns credibility in its own reliability has to deliver. That means the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the practices it must not indict wholesale, and the formal or statistical facts it must be consistent with, each with the motivation behind it. State which requirements you regard as load-bearing and which you suspect are prejudices of one camp rather than genuine constraints, and if you believe some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and identify which single requirement you would surrender first.

Cases I can already see the theory has to handle, offered in case they help you find ones I've missed:

The bathroom scale: I weigh myself five mornings running on a scale I have no independent reason to trust, accept each reading as it comes, and conclude the scale is reliable. If the theory says I know that, credibility is far too cheap; if it says no track record argument of this shape ever works, it seems to condemn vast stretches of ordinary and scientific practice in one stroke.

Cross-checking against a sibling: I test scale A against scale B and they agree — but they came from the same factory, and a common defect would explain the agreement just as well as accuracy would. Our monitoring has exactly this structure: the putatively independent check runs on the same hardware and shares the same configuration database. The theory has to say whether shared origins destroy the evidential value of agreement, and why.

The self-updating instrument: our rollout pipeline redeploys the monitoring stack itself, so the track record I actually have is a record of previous versions. Whatever the theory says about a stable source tested against its own past, it has to say something about a source that keeps becoming a new source.

Testimony: on any view where a source must be certified before its outputs can count, a child's knowledge gained from parents looks impossible, since no child can bootstrap trust in testimony by independent means. The theory must not have this consequence, or must own it.

Calibration chains: instrumented science bottoms out somewhere — at some point, a device is trusted without external validation. The theory must be consistent with the fact that this works.

Keep the weight on the criteria and the motivating cases rather than on adjudicating known positions; I'll do the scoring myself. But do include one closing paragraph on how you'd expect the two obvious strategies to fare against your list — the one that accepts the track-record reasoning and grants knowledge of reliability cheaply, and the one that rejects it and insists credibility in one's own sources must have some other provenance entirely. Finally, since I will be making real design decisions on the back of this: for each requirement you mark as load-bearing, tell me what would change your mind about it — what a counterexample would have to look like, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
