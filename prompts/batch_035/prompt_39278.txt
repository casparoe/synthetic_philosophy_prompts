I teach an introductory course on philosophy of mind and free will, and next term I'm screening Chaplin's Modern Times (1936) alongside our unit on determinism and agency. I'd like to frame the discussion with Daniel Dennett's notion of "sphexishness" — his coinage for behavior that looks intelligent but turns out to be rigid, situation-insensitive routine, named after the digger wasp in the old Fabre anecdote: the wasp drags her paralyzed prey to the edge of the burrow, leaves it, goes below to inspect the nest, comes back up, and only then hauls the prey inside. But if an experimenter nudges the prey a few inches aside while she's below, she drags it back to the edge and re-descends to inspect again — and she will loop through this indefinitely, never noticing that the inspection is pointless. For Dennett, the opposite of sphexishness — flexible, context-sensitive responsiveness to the actual situation — is roughly what free will worth wanting amounts to, at least on his compatibilist picture.

Here's what I want the students to chew on: read Modern Times as a film about the attempted sphexification of a human being. The assembly line, the unseen president ordering the belt sped up, the automatic feeding machine demonstrated by a salesman with the explicit pitch that it will let workers eat lunch without stopping work — all of these are attempts to replace flexible behavior with rigid, optimized routine. And the nervous-breakdown sequence, where the Tramp physically cannot stop making the bolt-tightening motion and chases anything button-like down the street, seems to literalize sphexishness perfectly: the movement has become decoupled from its context and replays regardless of the situation, exactly like the wasp's inspection loop. That scene alone should make the concept vivid in a way the wasp never could.

But I don't want to hand the class a tidy illustration, so I need help seeing where the film resists this reading. The Tramp is arguably the least sphexish character in cinema — his whole comedy is improvisation, repurposing objects, seizing the accidental occasion (picking up the fallen red flag and instantly becoming a revolutionary). And there's a deeper complication I'm unsure how to teach: Chaplin's physical comedy was famously rehearsed to the point of obsession, so a flawlessly executed gag is itself a kind of machine precision. Is the film about escaping sphexishness, or does it quietly show that flexibility is itself a trained, scripted capacity — that the opposite of the wasp is not spontaneous freedom but overlearned skill? Which scenes would you point the students to on each side, where does the reading genuinely strain, and does the film, taken seriously, add anything to the Dennett-style claim that freedom just is non-sphexish responsiveness? I'd like a reading that doesn't merely decorate the philosophy with film clips, and that's honest about what the film can't do for the argument.
