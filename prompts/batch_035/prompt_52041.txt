I'm taking a graduate seminar called something like "Culture as Cause," built around Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and after three weeks of it I've started keeping a private list of my gut reactions whenever someone explains something economically or politically by appealing to values, norms, or a "culture." The problem is that I can't tell whether my reactions track anything principled, and I'd like help extracting whatever principle, if one exists, is behind them.

Here are my verdicts, case by case. (1) The Weberian claim itself: that certain Reformation-era beliefs — predestination, the anxiety about one's election, the search for reassurance through disciplined worldly labor — contributed causally to the emergence of modern capitalism in northwest Europe. I accept this as the right kind of causal claim to make, whatever its truth value. (2) The old "amoral familism" explanation of southern Italian backwardness — villages trapped in poverty because families trust nobody outside the household and so cannot sustain public goods — I distrust, even though structurally it looks like the same shape as (1): values shaping economic behavior across generations. (3) Cross-national differences in tax compliance or bribery explained by norms of fairness and generalized trust: I accept. (4) Explaining one identifiable person's behavior culturally — my grandfather's obsessive saving as a residue of Depression-era scarcity — I accept, but it feels like a different species of explanation from (1) and (3), closer to ordinary belief-desire psychology that happens to have cultural content, and I'm not sure whether that difference is real or just a change of scale. (5) "Culture of poverty" explanations of persistent inner-city poverty: I reject, and my rejection is partly political, which worries me — I suspect (2) and (5) get rejected because they look like blaming the victim while (1) gets a pass because its targets are respectable dead Protestants. (6) An economist's rival to (1) — that proto-industrial regions with dispersed property holdings, navigable rivers, and political fragmentation would have developed capitalism regardless of confession — I find convincing too, and I don't think accepting it requires rejecting (1), but I can't say why not without sounding like I'll let two rival explanations of the same event both count as causes whenever I happen to like both. (7) The case I keep going back and forth on: the claim that Japan's postwar growth was abetted by cultural norms of corporate loyalty and lifetime employment. It was formulated mid-growth, then quietly abandoned when the economy stagnated and "culture" was redeployed to explain stagnation. Something about that smells, yet the Weberian story was also assembled after capitalist development was already underway, so I can't just say the smell is retrodiction.

My tentative hunch is that the distinction has something to do with whether the values are attested independently of the outcomes they are invoked to explain — sermons, catechisms, and diaries on one side, versus inferring the values from the very behavior they are supposed to cause on the other — and with whether a specified psychological or institutional mechanism connects the value to the behavior. But I would like that hunch taken apart rather than flattered. Please state the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, precisely; say which clause of it each of my cases is supposed to motivate; test it against my list honestly, telling me which of my verdicts it cannot save and what I should give up; and tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't mentioned, such as the claim that high-trust Scandinavian culture explains the durability of the Nordic welfare states, or the claim that a sudden religious revival should change household savings behavior within a single generation. If it turns out no principled line can be drawn, I'd rather know that than be handed a principle gerrymandered to let me keep my favorites.
