I write long-form features on science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and next month I'm sitting down with a behavioral ecologist who has spent her career defending a strict reading of the handicap principle — the claim, associated with Amotz Zahavi, that honest signaling between animals is normally maintained by the signals being costly to produce. Her new book argues that the field's drift over recent decades toward pluralism about honesty — indexes, receiver punishment, common interest, and other mechanisms supposed to keep signals reliable without production costs — was a mistake, or at least a retreat dressed up as sophistication. She is an experienced media guest, quick with the peacock and the gazelle, and her signature deflection, when pressed on any particular mechanism, is to say that the mechanism is a detail and the principle is what matters. I want to get past that.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for the feature. They should be phrased accessibly enough for an educated general reader but turn on the actual content of her position. Specifically:

1. At least one question on the empirical embarrassment at the heart of her paradigm case: the work in recent decades suggesting that peahens may not prefer the most elaborate trains, and that other textbook handicaps look shaky when measured carefully. Include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give — probably something about measurement, context-dependent preferences, or trait multifunctionality — and a second follow-up for the case where she bites the bullet and accepts that the classic examples are weaker than advertised.

2. At least one question on the distinction between a signal being costly to produce and being costly to fake — the idea that what matters is not that the peacock's tail is expensive, but that it is much more expensive for a low-quality bird to grow than for a high-quality one, so that the cost scales with the very quality being advertised. Critics say this concedes everything the "strict" reading was supposed to protect. Ask whether her version of the principle survives the concession that most real handicaps work this way, and follow up by asking what, if she does concede it, still distinguishes the handicap principle from ordinary signaling theory.

3. At least one question on index signals — signals kept honest by physical constraint rather than by expenditure, like a deep croak being hard for a small animal to produce. Her likely line is that an index is just a limiting case of a handicap, one where the cost of faking is effectively infinite. Follow up on whether stretching "cost" to cover that case leaves the concept with any distinct content, or whether the strict reading is being saved by relabeling.

4. At least one question on maintenance rather than origin: if a handicap is an equilibrium between signaler and receiver, what keeps the receiving side paying attention when the signal becomes rare or the selection regime relaxes? Feral and domesticated animals that retain extravagant ornaments without obvious mate choice are a natural case to put to her.

5. At least one question on the waste problem, turned up a notch: the standard reply to "the handicap principle makes nature look absurdly wasteful" is that waste is the price of honesty. But if honesty can often be had more cheaply through constraints or enforcement, then a world in which costly signaling is the norm is a world with more waste than a pluralist one implies. Does her strict reading commit her to the claim that nature is more wasteful than it needs to be, and is that a prediction or an embarrassment?

6. At least one question on the human traffic in the idea — luxury goods, academic credentials, public philanthropy, the casual use of "virtue signaling" as an insult. Ask whether the principle licenses any of these applications, whether the popularization has helped or damaged the idea, and follow up on where she would draw the line between an analogy she endorses and one she resents.

7. A closing question on falsification: what observation, in the field or the lab, would force her to give up the strict reading rather than reinterpret it — and how would she tell the difference between a world where costly signaling is the norm and one where it is a special case?

Please construct the sequence so that the "mechanism versus principle" deflection gets progressively harder to use: early questions should let her establish the view on her own terms, later ones should pin specific mechanisms to specific predictions, so that answering each with the deflection costs her something visible to a lay reader. No citations or reading lists — just the questions, the follow-ups, and one line each on why you expect the reply you anticipate.
