I'm auditing my notes from a graduate seminar on contractualism, taught in broadly Scanlonian terms: every case is framed as a choice someone must make, and the question is always which principles nobody could reasonably reject, where complaints belong to individuals and burdens are compared person-to-person rather than summed. Over the semester the professor has handed down a series of verdicts, and I want to predict where he will land on the case scheduled for next week. His record so far:

(1) A surgeon could harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five dying patients. Impermissible, no matter how many are saved: the five patients' complaints cannot be added up to outweigh the one patient's complaint.

(2) A firefighter can rescue either five people or one person from a burning ward, with the same effort and no side constraints. He says the five must be rescued: numbers count here.

(3) A station may interrupt a broadcast watched by millions, imposing a trivially small annoyance on each viewer, in order to warn one person of a lethal hazard. Permissible, apparently: tiny burdens can be aggregated.

(4) A broadcaster could instead impose a single large burden on one person, say conscripting one viewer's entire weekend, to prevent that same trivial annoyance spread over millions. Impermissible, which suggests the aggregation in (3) runs in one direction only, or only below some threshold of seriousness.

(5) In a tie case, five people each facing a ten percent risk of death versus one person facing the same ten percent risk, where we can protect either the group or the individual: he says we may protect the five. When individual complaints are exactly balanced, apparently, numbers break the tie.

Next week's case: a public health agency with a fixed budget must choose between Program A, which prevents the certain death of one identified patient, and Program B, which reduces each of five million people's annual risk of death from three in a million to one in a million, roughly ten expected deaths prevented. Each individual's additional risk under the second program is minuscule; the aggregate expected benefit is ten times the certain loss.

Predict the professor's verdict. I can see at least two ways of systematizing his record so far, one on which verdicts (1) through (5) trace to a two-level structure where small burdens aggregate while serious ones trigger the individualist restriction, and one on which numbers never do more than break exact ties, so that verdict (3) needs some separate explanation. There may be others, including views on which the key variable is probability rather than burden size. Please identify the candidate principles that fit the full record, say what each predicts about the identified-patient case and why the tiny per-person risk makes this case harder for some of them than for others, give your best guess at his verdict with your reasons for preferring it, and describe the further case, other than the one he will actually decide, that would most cleanly discriminate among the principles that survive.
