In the seminar I teach on philosophy of history, the debate between covering-law and narrative explanation has been running since Carl Hempel argued that a historical event is explained only by subsuming it under general laws, and it now strikes me as completely stalled: the Hempelians concede that historians never actually state the laws and insist they are implicitly law-governed anyway, while the narrativists (following William Dray and, later, Louis Mink) reply that narratives explain by rendering events intelligible — by showing how agents' reasons, circumstances, and contingencies fit together — and that this is not substandard science but explanation of a different kind. Both camps publish, neither converts. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not disagreeing about the same explanandum at all: the covering-law side is explaining why events of a type occur, while the narrativist side is explaining why this particular event, embedded in this particular course of events, took the shape it did, and the word "explanation" is doing double duty. Diagnose the disagreement: is my hypothesis right, or is there a real substantive dispute about what explanation must be, or a hidden empirical question about whether history in fact has stable enough regularities for laws to cover anything? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a concrete case where the two models yield rival verdicts, or nothing at all, and why. Open with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, without preamble or restating the question.
