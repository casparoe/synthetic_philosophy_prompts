I'm taking an intermediate game theory course, and we've just reached the centipede game. Setup as we had it: two players alternate moves; at each turn a player can "take," ending the game and collecting a larger share of the pot, or "pass," which roughly doubles the pot before the next turn. The pot is largest if both pass through all one hundred nodes, but at every node taking gives the taker slightly more than they could get by passing once more. Backward induction delivers a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium: the first mover takes immediately, and the whole grand pot evaporates.

What really struck me, though, was a remark my professor dropped at the end of lecture, almost as an aside: that the notorious feature of this reasoning — that its conclusion destroys the premises needed to derive it — is structurally the same puzzle as the surprise examination paradox (the teacher announces an exam for next week that will be a surprise; the students reason it can't be on the last day, since they'd anticipate it, then can't be on the second-to-last day by the same logic, and so eliminate every day — whereupon the exam on Wednesday surprises them). In the centipede case the parallel is this: the induction seems to require common knowledge of rationality, but if player 2 ever gets to move at all, that very fact is evidence against the common knowledge, so the inductive step at that node loses its footing; and yet the argument concludes that the game never gets there.

Please write a dialogue between two students from my seminar. One — call her Ana — defends the backward induction argument as sound: she takes it to be a perfectly valid proof of a conditional (if there were common knowledge of rationality, the first mover would take at once), and she holds that the "paradox" is just a confusion between the soundness of an argument and its applicability to agents whose rationality isn't actually common knowledge. The other — call him Theo — thinks the reasoning is self-undermining in the way the surprise exam is: the later-node steps of the induction are derived from premises that the earlier-node conclusions, if acted upon, would already have made untrustworthy, so the argument cannot be anyone's actual reason for taking early, and it's a mistake to dignify its output as "the" solution of the game.

Make both of them genuinely competent, and have them respond to each other's actual points. In particular, the dialogue should work through at least the following, rather than talking past each other:

- Theo's core complaint, spelled out precisely. At node 99, say, the claim "player 2 will take" is supported by the assumption that if node 99 were reached, common knowledge of rationality would still be in place. But if node 99 is in fact reached, that is precisely the situation in which the induction has already misfired somewhere — so what licenses using it there? His model is the surprise exam, where eliminating the last day is fine, but eliminating the second-to-last silently relies on the first elimination surviving the hypothetical that the exam hasn't happened by the second-to-last evening.
- Ana's reply, which should not be a strawman. Perhaps: the argument establishes what agents with common knowledge of rationality would do, and nothing about a hypothetical play reaching node 99 counts as evidence against it, since in that hypothetical the game simply hasn't ended yet; the "disconfirmation" idea smuggles in a causal or learning story about the agents that a theory of ideal rationality need not include. She might also press the distinction between the rationality of an agent and the reasonableness of a choice made under conditions the theory says can't arise, and compare the induction to proofs by mathematical induction in which no one ever runs the base case.
- A genuine exchange on whether they can converge on something like: backward induction is valid as a proof but idle as a deliberational guide. Or does Theo think even that concession is too much, because in the centipede game the players must actually decide at nodes whose reachability the argument declares impossible, so "idle" understates it? And can Ana accept some version of it without abandoning her view that subgame perfection is the right refinement?
- Whether the experimental fact — that real subjects typically pass for a while and both end up richer than under immediate taking — settles anything between them, or whether both should bracket it as irrelevant to the idealizing question. I'd like to see them disagree about this rather than agree to set it aside.

Give them distinct voices — Ana crisp and a little impatient with hand-waving, Theo more taken with structural analogies — and let each land at least one point the other genuinely has to answer. End with them stating precisely what, if anything, still divides them, rather than dissolving into a vague synthesis.
