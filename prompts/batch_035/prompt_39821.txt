I've been reading the decision-theory threads on rationalist blogs and forums since the days people first rediscovered Hofstadter's superrationality columns, and I still don't know what I actually believe about cooperating. So let me lay out my gut verdicts on some cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them — and which of my verdicts has to go.

First, the setup facts, so we're talking about the same thing. In Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma, a trillionaire tells twenty people that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars; if more than one, or none, arrives, nobody gets anything. No communication between them is allowed. His Luring Lottery was similar in spirit but ran for real in Scientific American: each entrant mailed in a postcard bearing a number of entries (one entry if you wrote "1," or as many as you liked by writing a bigger number), and the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, paid to the submitter of one randomly chosen entry. In the actual event, enough people submitted astronomically large numbers that the prize became a rounding error on nothing.

My verdicts. (1) One-shot prisoner's dilemma with a random stranger, no communication, never meeting again: defect, obviously. (2) Same game, but I've learned the stranger has spent twenty years reading the same decision-theory threads I have and, by every indication, reasons about these questions much as I do: cooperate, and defecting now feels like a kind of self-betrayal. (3) In Platonia, if I'm confident the other nineteen reason the way I do, I endorse the superrational move — sending the telegram with probability 1/20 — and would actually go through with the randomization. (4) In a fresh Luring Lottery, I want to submit exactly one entry, and I still want to submit one entry after learning that the vast majority of entrants have never heard of superrationality and will submit googol-scale numbers — "don't be part of the collapse" — even though I can't see how that impulse survives the arithmetic. (5) National election, chance of my vote being pivotal minuscule: vote. (6) My homeowners' association, forty voters I know personally and who are all out for themselves: I still vote, yet I would defect without hesitation on any one-shot cooperation problem with those same forty people. (7) Tip the waiter in a city I will never revisit: tip, and stiffing him feels worse, somehow, than defecting on the stranger in (1).

My tentative principle is evidential: cooperate when my cooperating is itself evidence that relevantly similar reasoners will cooperate too. It seems to deliver (2), (3), and (5). But it's silent on tipping, where the waiter isn't mirroring anything; it can't keep (1) at "defect" once I reflect that the random stranger is also a person reasoning about the game; and it pulls apart my two verdicts in (6) in a way I can't make sense of. So: is there one principle that saves all seven, or a small set of candidates? Please state each precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and report its verdict on cases I haven't listed — in particular, a one-shot dilemma against a reasoner who runs the very same reasoning I do but has opposed values, and a Platonia where I know one of the nineteen is a superrationalist and I know nothing about the rest. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate.

And please push back if the request is confused at the root. If superrationality from the beginning just muddles acting on evidence with causing outcomes, or if my seven verdicts are unrelated intuitions wearing one hat, say so rather than rescuing them for me — I'd rather learn that my list was never going to compress than get a principle welded onto it.
