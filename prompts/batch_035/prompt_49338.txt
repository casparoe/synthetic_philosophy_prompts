My school has a rule that every factual claim in a history essay must carry a citation, and we are told never to cite an encyclopedia but to follow it to the sources the encyclopedia cites. When I traced the chain for one topic, the books at the end turned out to cite one another, some in a circle, and beneath them sat a handful of primary documents — a diary entry, two letters, an inventory — that cite nothing and are simply consulted. This puzzles me, because the rule seems to say that every claim needs support, yet nothing supports the documents on which everything else rests. What theory of justification, if any, does the citation rule presuppose? Does the practice treat those primary documents as foundations requiring no support, depend instead on the mutual corroboration of the secondary literature, or make a demand for endless support that the practice in fact quietly abandons? Please tell me which of these the rule strictly presupposes, which it merely fits well with, and where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — and say what the rule would have to look like if a teacher insisted that the chain of support may never terminate.
