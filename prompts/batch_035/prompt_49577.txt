I'm enrolled in a graduate seminar on metaethics, and next week I have to defend moral generalism against a particularist in a graded debate exchange. I'd like to practice against a live opponent before then. Please play a committed moral particularist in the tradition of McDowell and Dancy: someone who holds that the moral valence of a consideration is not fixed in advance but is determined holistically by the details of the case, and who believes any acceptable moral theory must do without exceptionless moral principles without collapsing into unprincipled intuition. Be sharp and never a strawman: you may concede small points, but never the main one, and you should press hard on whatever seems weakest in my position. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so it feels like a real exchange, and stay in character until I write "stop." Here is my opening move: you particularists love to point out that the same consideration can change its moral character with context — that lying is wrong in one case and right in another when it protects someone from harm. Fine. But notice that whenever you show me such a flip, you explain it by pointing to some further feature of the case, and that suggests the valence is not floating free but is governed by a covering generalization we simply haven't articulated yet. So let me offer you "default generalism": every consideration carries a default moral valence that holds when its enabling conditions are met, and the flips you cite are just cases where those conditions fail — which is itself a matter of general fact. Doesn't your holism of reasons amount to nothing more than a complaint that we haven't yet finished writing down the generalizations?
