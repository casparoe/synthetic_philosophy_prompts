I referee a lot for a mid-tier cognitive science journal — probably fifteen manuscripts a year, and realistically I can spend two or three hours per manuscript, tops, before the editor needs a recommendation (reject / major revisions / minor revisions / accept) plus some indication of how confident I am. Here's what's been eating at me, and I want you to actually operationalize it rather than give me a philosophy lecture.

At the last editors' meeting the EiC shared some numbers: across the journal, two referees for the same manuscript agree on the basic accept-or-reject call only slightly more often than chance (around 60%), and when referees disagree, the editor basically flips a coin. I also did a little audit of my own track record: out of my last twenty-ish recommendations where I was confident enough to argue hard in my report, roughly a third got overruled by the editor or torn apart by the other referee in a way I had to concede was fair. So now I'm sitting on evidence about the reliability of my own evidence, which I gather the philosophers call higher-order evidence. The way I see it I've got two moves: either I move my opinion toward the other referee's before I even see it (some kind of averaging thing), or I trust my first-order read of the paper and just slap a discount on when I report confidence to the editor. But I suspect both of those are wrong in different ways, and I've heard there's a whole debate about epistemic akrasia — whether you're allowed to believe P with full strength while also believing your belief in P is unreliable — that's relevant here.

Act as a professional epistemologist who has also served as an associate editor, so you know both literatures. Please turn whatever the epistemology of higher-order evidence and disagreement actually says into a procedure I can run while refereeing. Concretely:

- Start with a short brainstorm: list the candidate principles people have proposed for handling higher-order evidence and peer disagreement, and say which ones survive my constraints and which die there.
- Then give me the procedure: the questions I ask myself, in order, while reading a manuscript; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure forces me to lower the confidence I report vs. where it leaves my actual belief alone; and the points where nothing principled decides it and you're just handing the judgment back to me — flag those honestly, don't dress up my judgment calls as procedure.
- It has to fit on one page and run in about ten minutes at the end of a review. It may never output "withhold judgment" — the editor needs a call and a number.
- Then run it on three manuscripts I've actually had recently (details as I remember them): (1) a competent but boring study in an area I know cold, where I'm probably the right referee; (2) a flashy result that directly challenges my own theoretical paradigm, and I could feel my blood pressure rising while I read it; (3) a paper whose methods I genuinely don't understand, but by a famous group whose previous work I've always found rock solid, so my instinct was to trust it. 
- For each, tell me what the procedure outputs, and make sure at least one of them is a case where the procedure makes me do something I won't like. Also tell me where you think the procedure will misfire — what kinds of referees or situations it's just wrong for.

Think carefully and go step by step, don't just pattern-match to "be calibrated." If a step of the procedure rests on a contested philosophical point, say which one and which way you resolved it.
