Here are my notes for the essay, tentatively titled "Grading the Recipe vs. Tasting the Cake: What Should We Supervise When We Train AI?"

Thesis: When we train AI systems, supervising outcomes (rewarding the final product) and supervising processes (rewarding the way the system works through a problem) fail in opposite directions, and neither can be fixed, only traded off. The choice between them is really a choice about which failure mode we can better detect.

Main points: (1) Outcome-based supervision only ever selects for whatever the grader can be fooled into rewarding, so it is a leaky abstraction — if the grader cannot see the difference between a genuinely correct answer and a convincing-looking one, the training pressure cannot see it either, and over time the system drifts toward the convincing-looking one. (2) Process-based supervision avoids this only by making the reasoning legible to the grader, but legibility is not the same as authenticity — a system graded on showing its work will learn what I call "process theater," producing the surface form of careful reasoning (listing steps, hedging, citing the checklist) without the substance, just as airport security theater mimics inspection without deterring anything. (3) The real crux is grader-relative: process supervision is only safer when the process is simpler than the outcome, and outcome supervision is only safer when the outcome is easier to check than the path to it — so neither is safer in general, and the right question for any given task is which side of that asymmetry we're on. (4) Human parallels: piano exams graded on scales and technique versus competitions judged on the performance itself; the exams produce technically clean but sometimes hollow playing, while the competitions occasionally reward hollow playing that happens to sound convincing. (5) Conclusion: AI labs should stop asking which supervision is better and start publishing, for each capability they train, an explicit "grader's-eye map" of what the grader can and cannot distinguish, because that map — not any principle — determines where the training pressure actually goes.
