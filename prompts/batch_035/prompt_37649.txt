Write a dialogue between two decision theorists: a champion of safe Pareto improvements and a skeptic of them. Background for the dialogue, so both characters can be precise: a safe Pareto improvement, as the champion uses the term, is a jointly accepted mechanism, installed before play, that maps each player's submitted action to a possibly different one, such that for every possible pair of actions the players might have chosen, the outcome the mechanism produces is weakly better for both players than the outcome those actions would have produced directly. The canonical example is a game of chicken with a pre-agreed mediator: if both drivers submit "go straight," the mediator flips a coin and forces one of them to swerve; every other pair of submissions is passed through unchanged. The champion also touts surrogate goals — publicly treating some trivial matter (a token strip of land, a ceremonial title) as vital, so that an opponent's threats can be "paid off" there at low real cost to either side.

The skeptic's case should rest on two contentions, and the champion must answer them head-on rather than dodge: (1) The safety guarantee is counterfactual, not behavioral. It secures that each possible pair of actions does weakly better, but installing the mechanism changes which actions are rational to choose. Once a mutual crash is impossible, swerving no longer protects the cautious driver, so in the modified game the aggressive can threaten more credibly than before — a cautious player can end up worse off than she would have fared in the original game, even though the mechanism is "safe" by the champion's own definition. (2) Such mechanisms subsidize recklessness: they pay out most to those whose counterfactual behavior was worst, systematically rewarding the stubborn and the threatening, with the prudent underwriting the insurance of the imprudent.

Both characters should be equally competent. The champion must give a substantive reply to each contention — for example, clarifying exactly what the guarantee does and does not claim, or arguing that the shifted incentives are part of the point rather than a flaw — and the skeptic should press follow-ups on the actual replies, not on weaker versions of them. The dialogue should end with both characters agreeing on at least one precise claim about when such mechanisms help and when they can backfire.

After the dialogue, append a short note addressing: which of the skeptic's two contentions you judge stronger, what argument or consideration would most plausibly reverse that judgment, and the single point in the dialogue where you were least confident that the character you were writing for was being fair.
