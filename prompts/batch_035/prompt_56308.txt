I help decide how a large pool of grant money is split across causes — global health, factory-farm animal welfare, existential risk, and a few others — and the tool we use is the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework: score each cause on importance (the good that would be done if the problem were fully solved), tractability (the share of the problem a fixed increase in resources is expected to remove), and neglectedness (a discount for crowdedness, on the thought that returns diminish as more money and attention arrive in an area); combine the three scores, and rank the causes. Before we lean on this any further — or replace it — I want the specification it is supposed to live up to, written out and justified, not a defense of the incumbent.

Please write out the desiderata a cause prioritization framework must meet to be adequate for our actual allocation decisions: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse — marginal versus average impact, cause-level versus intervention-level judgments, the difference between a problem being large and a solution to it being underfunded — and the facts about funding markets it must respect, such as diminishing returns, other funders adjusting when we move, and the possibility that an intervention does net harm rather than just less good. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it; mark which are non-negotiable and which are conveniences of our particular situation; flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied (I would especially like your view on whether a framework can be at once sensitive to diminishing returns and stable under our choosing where to draw the boundaries between causes); and say which single requirement excludes the most plausible rivals. Then, briefly, score the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework as we use it against the list: where it passes, where it fails, and which failure matters most. Please answer in American English.
