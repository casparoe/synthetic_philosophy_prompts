I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding forums since the late 2000s — long enough to remember when "the alignment problem" named one specific thing: specifying a goal that an optimizer much smarter than us could pursue safely, and the worry was that we couldn't do it. Fifteen years on, I sit on the grant committee of a safety-focused foundation, and the same phrase now gets applied to everything from RLHF fine-tuning to honesty probes to detecting sandbagging, and our committee is split on whether that's one problem wearing several hats or several problems sharing a name. This quarter we must allocate a $12M round. On the table: a five-year unified agenda that would absorb most of the budget, which amounts to learning a complete specification of what humans would endorse on reflection and optimizing against it; versus a split across mechanistic interpretability, methods for eliciting what a model actually believes rather than what it says, scalable oversight for tasks no unaided human can evaluate, and automated detection of deceptive alignment. A third faction argues that even that portfolio misses the real question — who is the principal? An individual operator, a lab, a public? On their view, a system perfectly obedient to the wrong principal isn't misaligned but is still a catastrophe, so they want a governance track carved out of the same envelope. Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before next month's vote. It should recommend a concrete allocation with a review point and time frame, take a position on whether these sub-problems are facets of a single problem or genuinely distinct — and show why that matters for how we fund, not just as a label — give reviewers criteria they can actually apply to the twelve pending proposals, and give the unified agenda's strongest argument, that a sufficiently capable optimizer defeats patches and that anything short of a complete value specification is just a way of losing slowly, an honest hearing before explaining why it should win or lose here.
