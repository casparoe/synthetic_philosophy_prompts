I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly at the intersection of animal welfare, ecology, and public health, and over years of reporting on this beat I've ended up with a set of positions on the ethics of insecticides and pest control that I'd like audited for coherence. Here is the package, roughly as I'd state it in my own notes:

1. Moral standing comes in degrees and tracks sentience. The expert reviews I've covered in recent years have convinced me that bees, and probably many other insects, are sentient, and that their interests therefore count for something — even if not for as much as a mammal's or a human's.

2. Death, as opposed to suffering, does not wrong an insect. Since insects lack the self-directed, future-oriented interests that make death a harm to the one who dies, what matters morally is how they are treated while alive, not whether they are killed. On this view, a genuinely painless killing wrongs no one.

3. Vector control is one of the great moral achievements of the past century, and the recurring resistance to insecticide-based malaria control — from the DDT era onward — has been a catastrophe measured in children's lives. I would accept an enormous number of dead mosquito larvae, killed by whatever means, before accepting one additional death from malaria.

4. The routine agricultural use of broad-spectrum insecticides, above all the neonicotinoids, is a moral scandal, and my outrage here is primarily about what they do to bees — the losses of foragers and colonies — not about residues on my own food. I want them restricted far more aggressively than current regulation does, even at some cost to yields.

5. I support banning glue traps, and I regard killing insects for entertainment as wrong in a way I'm confident does not bottom out in suffering: I would still condemn it if the killing were genuinely instantaneous and painless. There is something wrong with destroying these creatures for amusement, full stop.

6. Where the evidence on sentience is unsettled, I follow a precautionary rule of thumb: treat the animal as sentient when doing so is cheap, and don't demand certainty before extending protection.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how badly; which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on inspection; and, for each real one, what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd rather not abandon any of these — particularly not 3 or 4, which feel like the core of how I report on this beat — but what I want is an audit of where the package actually breaks, not a list of places where it merely looks strained. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious.
