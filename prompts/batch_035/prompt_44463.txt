I'm preparing a paper that promises to resolve Thomson's lamp rather than merely rehearse it, and before committing to any particular resolution I want the standard such a resolution would have to meet fixed in advance. Please write the desiderata: what an adequate resolution of the lamp puzzle must deliver — the points it must get on the table and settle, the distinctions it must respect, the neighboring cases it must handle without ad hoc machinery — with the motivating case behind each requirement. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp, and, most important to me, flag any subset of the requirements that you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable: if the list itself forces a choice — say, between grounding the final state in the machine's history and letting the final state be settled by the physics of the switch — then say so plainly, since that is a result in its own right. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to thin the field of candidate resolutions.

To fix the purpose: the resolution should be good enough to close the dispute among people who already know the puzzle, not to introduce it to students; it must tell them something they don't already get from the standard statements. And it should at least address the following, unless you think some of these are misconceived, in which case veto them with reasons:

1. Non-stipulation. The resolution cannot simply declare the lamp on or off at the finish; it owes an account of where that fact comes from, or else a principled explanation of why there is no such fact to be had.
2. Parity. The same treatment must extend to variants where the lamp is red/green rather than on/off, and to variants where a third state (broken, say) is in play; a resolution that handles these differently needs to say what licenses the difference.
3. The Benacerraf point. It must take seriously the observation that the complete history of the machine before the finish, together with its initial state, fails to entail anything about the state at the finish, and it must locate the moral: is the lamp story inconsistent, merely incomplete, or consistent with either outcome?
4. Neighbor cases. It must say something about the dichotomy paradox, where we seem to accept a completed supertask whenever a runner finishes a course, and about the elastic-collision supertasks in which time-reversing the setup appears to let energy emerge from a static configuration — either covering these under the same account or explaining where and why the boundary falls.
5. Formal versus physical. It must keep straight whether it is answering a question about the story as a formal object or about machines with real switches, hysteresis, and moving parts, and it must not slide between the two mid-argument.

Close with a brief scorecard — a few sentences each — of the three stances on offer: that the supertask is impossible outright; that the description is simply incomplete, so the lamp could be either and nothing paradoxical follows; and that the state at the finish is fixed, if anything fixes it, by the physics of the switch, making the question empirical.
