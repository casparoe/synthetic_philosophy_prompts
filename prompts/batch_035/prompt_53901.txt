A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the function of the heart. It started trivially — he offhandedly said the heart is there to pump blood — and by the second hour we had each dug into one of the two rival accounts of biological function. I have come out on the side of the etiological, selection-history account: a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected in the past, so that the heart's function is to pump blood precisely because pumping blood is what hearts were selected for, and this is what allows function talk to do genuine explanatory work, distinguishing the heart's function from mere side effects like producing the thump-thump sound. My friend ended up defending the causal-role account, on which a trait's function is whatever contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system — the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping is the heart's contribution to the circulatory system's capacity to circulate blood — and he thinks the etiological story ties function to a historical fact that is unnecessary for most of what biologists actually do, and hopeless for novel traits, engineered genes, or structurally novel traits that selection has not yet had time to act on.

I walked away unsatisfied, though, because neither of us really probed our own position's weak points; we just fired objections at each other's. So let me ask you about mine. What would genuinely count against the etiological account? I don't want the familiar volley of cases — I know the list my friend was working from (it includes the novel-trait problem, the plasticity of functional attributions between levels, the charge that selected effects are epistemically inaccessible so function claims become unfalsifiable, and so on), and I want you to go well beyond it. What I want is a map of the position's exposure: which observations, results, or theoretical developments would a fair-minded etiological theorist have to concede as counting against the view, and which are merely awkward? Which of these would its committed defenders themselves acknowledge, and which do they tend to wave away? Where does the view actually risk something — does it make any distinctive predictions about, say, how malfunction and dysfunction should be ascribed in medicine, or how functional talk should behave across biological subfields — and where has it instead been protected by rounds of qualification that leave it unable to lose? If the latter, tell me whether that insulation is a fatal flaw for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of a primarily reconstrualive proposal.

Please take a definite position throughout: say which challenges you judge fatal if they stand, which merely damaging, which survivable with the standard repairs, and say in the end whether you think the etiological account is, on balance, exposed enough that I should soften my new allegiance — or whether my friend got the worse end of last night's argument. Commit to a verdict rather than handing me a menu of options.
