I teach a historiography seminar, and for next term I'm compiling a set of adversarial-collaboration documents to show students how residual disagreement can be made exact. The one I want drafted concerns the Needham question and its economic descendant, the Great Divergence debate. The two parties are, on one side, a "late-divergence" economic historian in the vein of Kenneth Pomeranz's The Great Divergence (2000), who holds that as late as around 1750 the most developed parts of China — the Yangzi delta, say — were broadly comparable to the most developed parts of Europe in life expectancy, markets, and living standards, so the divergence demands a relatively late, conjunctural explanation (coal, colonies, New World resources); and, on the other, an "early-divergence" institutionalist who holds that by 1800 the divergence in scientific culture, institutions, and capabilities was already centuries old, so the interesting question is why Europe had drifted so far ahead by then — a question they think the late-divergence side dodges by picking a moment when the two regions still look similar on exactly those indicators that suit them.

The two have been arguing past each other for years: one side hears "why Britain and not China?" and answers "wrong question, wrong contrast class"; the other hears that as an evasion. Please act as a professional economic historian serving as the rapporteur of their collaboration. Before drafting, work through for yourself what the genuine cruxes are. I suspect at least one is methodological: whether the question "why Britain and not China?" is well posed at all — whether the contrast class {Britain, China} is a natural joint at which to carve history, or an artifact of treating Europe's path as the default baseline. Note that the question as standardly asked bundles two distinct contrasts — Britain rather than France or the Netherlands, and Europe rather than China — and that answers to the two need not even be of the same kind (a story about English coal answers the first but not obviously the second). A second likely crux is timing: when the decisive divergence began, and what would even count as evidence about it.

Then draft the joint statement itself: a numbered section of claims both sides could sign, including about the uncontroversial facts (China's technological leadership before roughly 1500, the approximate timing of British industrialization, the existence of quantitative evidence for both regions, and whatever else is genuinely agreed rather than merely bland); a table of the residual disagreements, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording; and, for each crux, a concrete piece of evidence both would agree bears on it — new real-wage or anthropometric comparisons for the Yangzi delta versus northwest Europe, say — with each side's prediction written down in advance. If any part of their dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, for instance over what "divergence" is supposed to range over (living standards, capabilities, or potentialities), say which part and why. The document should stand on its own: no citations or bibliography beyond the works and dates already mentioned here. Be disciplined — agreed claims must be things both sides really accept, not watered-down truisms. Keep the whole document, table included, to at most 1,000 words.
