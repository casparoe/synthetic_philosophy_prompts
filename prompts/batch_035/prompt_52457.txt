I run incident retros at a mid-size cloud hosting company -- we do maybe 60 a year, and every single one ends in the same fight about what goes in the "root cause" field. Half my facilitators are counterfactual people: the root cause is whatever the outage wouldn't have happened without, so they'll happily write "the intern's config push" even though three other safeguards also had to fail for that push to take down prod. The other half are intervention people: the root cause is the thing you'd actually go change to keep it from happening again, so they'd write "the approval step that silently skips when the reviewer is marked out-of-office" and leave the intern out entirely. I've read enough to know this maps onto a real fight in the philosophy of causation (Lewis's counterfactuals vs Pearl and Woodward's interventions, roughly), and I want something that operationalizes the difference instead of whoever talks loudest winning.

Write me a decision procedure my facilitators can actually run during a retro: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure stops and says "this is a prioritization call, hand it to engineering leadership," and where it has to admit both camps are right and we record two entries. It has to fit in the 90 minutes of a retro and be usable by facilitators with zero philosophy background -- they'll get a one-paragraph explanation of the counterfactual vs. interventionist thing, tops.

Then run it on three incidents:

1. An intern pushed a config change to prod on a Saturday. The approval step that should of caught it silently no-ops if the reviewer is marked out-of-office, and a caching layer meant to contain the blast radius had been misconfigured months earlier by someone who's since left. Two-hour outage, 40% of customers.
2. A DNS provider we depend on (single vendor, no backup) shipped a bad rollout, and we went down with them. Everyone agrees we can't make the vendor change anything.
3. A memory leak we've known about for a year and filed as "fix eventually" finally OOM'd the primary cluster during a traffic spike. Everyone knew this day would come.

For at least one of these, the procedure should land somewhere I won't like -- I'm on record that incident write-ups should name the human action, not the system gap, and I want to see whether the procedure survives that or just agrees with me. And be straight with me about what the procedure can't do: where the two camps will still be arguing when it's finished, and what a facilitator should do at that point.
