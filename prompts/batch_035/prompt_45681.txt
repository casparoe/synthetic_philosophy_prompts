I produce a small history-of-ideas podcast, and for our season finale we record a staged interview across time: a Keynes scholar who has agreed to answer entirely in character, drawing only on what Keynes actually wrote and did, so the questions need to be ones the historical record can answer. The guest, obviously, is John Maynard Keynes, and I want the interview to be about his conception of the good life, not his economics — the through-line running from his student-era conversion to G. E. Moore's ethics (with its doctrine that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are the ultimate goods, that right action is merely the means), through his memoir My Early Beliefs, delivered to the Memoir Club in 1938, where he looks back on the young Bloomsbury circle's creed, and on to his late essay Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren, with its prophecy that once the economic problem is solved mankind will face its permanent problem — how to live wisely and agreeably.

Please draft eight to ten questions for this interview. I want them to press where the texts themselves are tense, with a follow-up keyed to the most likely in-character reply. In particular I want at least one question on each of the following: first, the apparent contradiction between the early creed — as Keynes describes it in My Early Beliefs, the view that nothing ultimately mattered except states of mind, that these states were not associated with action, achievement, or consequences, and that value depended on an unanalysable organic whole — and the fact that Keynes then spent his life frantically engaged in exactly the world of action, achievement, and consequences, in the Treasury, at Versailles, and in the endless battles over policy; a natural follow-up there would ask whether his decades of public work were, on his own account, ultimately justified as a means to a state of mind rather than for their results. Second, the scene Keynes revisits in that same memoir: D. H. Lawrence's furious accusation that he and his Cambridge friends were emotional shells — little swarming selves, as Lawrence put it — and Keynes's late-life concession that the young circle had treated matters of the heart partly as a game of intellectual outlawry; I'd like a question pressing whether that concession doesn't give away the whole case for the Moorean good life he claims never to have abandoned. Third, the passage in Economic Possibilities where he calls the love of money as a possession a somewhat disgusting morbidity, a half-punishable crime, alongside the fact that Keynes himself speculated heavily on the markets and rather enjoyed it — there the follow-up could turn on whether money-making was for him an amusing game rather than an appetite, and whether that distinction can really carry the moral weight he puts on it.

The final question or two should look forward rather than back: the essay's prediction that a hundred years hence the economic problem may be solved, and that his grandchildren's real struggle will be to fill their days wisely when accumulation no longer has social value — with a follow-up asking him to say, in his own idiom of states of mind and organic wholes, what a Tuesday afternoon in that solved world would actually look like, since that is precisely where his early creed and his optimism about his grandchildren seem to pull in opposite directions. Please keep the register conversational but intellectually serious — the questions should be speakable aloud on radio, not footnoted — and phrase them so that a plausible in-character answer is possible for each one, rather than as gotchas he can only parry.
