I help run a monthly philosophy meetup at a bar (yes, it's exactly as pretentious as it sounds) and next month's topic is vagueness — heaps of sand, balding men, the whole classic mess. My problem is that whenever we do a topic like this, people just pick the school whose name sounds cool and argue for it, and nobody notices when their actual intuitions about cases point somewhere else entirely. So I want a "which theory of vagueness do you actually hold?" quiz I can hand out at the start of the session.

Requirements: eight or so questions, each a concrete case or forced choice, two or three answer options each, and no question may name a theory or a philosopher. Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions: epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree/fuzzy views, the gap views (borderline statements are truth-valueless), the glut views (borderline statements are both true and false), contextualism, and the deflationary "vague predicates are just incoherent and the sorites proves it" option — that last one should be a leaf even if nobody in the room ends up there.

Some cases I want included, because versions of them have caused actual arguements at the meetup:

- a sorites chain where the person has to say whether there's a single grain whose removal flips "heap" to "not heap", or whether that question is itself confused somehow
- a borderline guy (call him Gary, mid-balding) and what the truth status of "Gary is bald" is: true, false, neither, both, or "true to degree about 0.5" — with the further option of saying that last answer is not really a truth value at all
- whether "Gary is either bald or not bald" comes out true even if "Gary is bald" itself doesn't come out true or false (this one caused a small riot last time)
- higher-order vagueness: is there a sharp line between the definitely-bald and the borderline-bald, and can a theory even *allow* a non-sharp answer there without falling apart?
- the town-stipulation case: a mayor decrees an exact hair-count cutoff for a local ordinance — did that reveal what "bald" meant all along, or did it create something new? I want this one to split people who think vagueness lives in language from people who think it lives in the world.
- whether it even makes sense to say the world itself is vague (is there a vague number of mountains on Earth independent of how we talk?) or whether all vagueness is ours

After the scoring key, tell me honestly which pairs of leaves the quiz can't separate — I'd rather know my instrument is blind in places than pretend it isn't. Also note any consistent answer patterns that don't map onto any real position, with your guess at whether nobody holds that combo because it's incoherent or just because nobody's bothered.

Keep it readable for people whose last philosophy course was in college. Cases and labels only, no papers or citations needed. If some leaf only shows up for people who give weird mixed answers, say so in the key — I don't want people leaving the bar thinking they're epistemicists just because they misread question 4.
