I'm taking an upper-level metaphysics course and we just finished the persistence unit (endurantism vs perdurantism, temporal parts, temporary intrinsics, all that). My friend in the study group has an argument he claims destroys perdurantism, and honestly its been bugging me all week because the conclusion feels way too strong, but each premise looks fine on its own. He runs it like this:

(1) Perdurantism says a persisting thing is a four-dimensional whole made of temporal parts, and when a thing has a property at a time, that's because the temporal part located at that time has the property simpliciter. This is how it handles temporary intrinsics: I'm sitting now and was standing an hour ago, and on perdurantism it's not that I have some funny relativized property, it's that my-now part sits and my-then part stood. (2) My mug is red, and it could have been blue — if the factory had used a different glaze, the very same mug would have come out blue. (3) But if having a property at a time works by some part of the thing having that property, then by the same reasoning, having a property in a counterfactual situation works by a part having it too: there's a "modal part" of the mug located over in the blue-glaze world, and that part is what's blue. (4) So the actual red mug literally has blue parts of it scattered across other possible worlds, exactly parallel to the way I have sitting parts and standing parts scattered across times. (5) And nobody accepts this — even perdurantists who are full-on modal realists about concrete possible worlds say the guy at the other world is my counterpart, a distinct thing that resembles me, not a literal part of me. If they won't buy modal parts, nobody will. (6) So the perdurantist either swallows modal parts, which is insane, or admits the "having a property at a time = some part has it" move doesn't generalize beyond times — and if the principle can't generalize, it's unclear temporal parts were ever really earning their keep on temporary intrinsics either.

He swears (6) just follows from (5) plus the parity in (3). My gut says something slides between (3) and (4) — like the perdurantist only ever signed up to explain one object having incompatible properties at different times within the actual world, and my friend quietly promoted that into a thesis about what the theory owes us across worlds. But I can't pin down exactly where the analogy breaks, and my friend keeps saying that refusing to extend the principle to worlds is just special pleading — you liked parts when they solved your problem, you dislike them when they solve mine. Which step should he give up? Why did that step look so innocent in the first place? And what does rejecting it cost — does the perdurantist lose anything she cared about, or was the modal case never a case where her machinery was supposed to apply? I'd rather keep (5) than (2), for what it's worth, but I want a real diagnosis and not just a vibe.
