Been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and one thing I've noticed is that we talk a lot about debiasing and "just look at the thing," but the cognitive penetration debate — the one about whether your beliefs, desires, and expectations can literally change what you SEE, as opposed to how you interpret what you see — seems to have been going nowhere for like two decades, and it bugs me. The psych side keeps cranking out "top-down effects": desirable objects looking closer, morally weighty people looking taller (I think it's the same literature as the hills-look-steeper-when-you're-tired-and-wearing-a-heavy-backpack stuff), Siegel's silhouetted coin that supposedly looks orangeish once you take it to be a coin rather than a weird orange blob. Then Firestone and Scholl did their big BBS takedown arguing essentially every one of these is attention, memory, judgment, or post-perceptual response bias wearing a perception costume, plus the El Greco fallacy point — if your perceptual distortion really caused the effect, it should distort your view of the measuring stick too, so go check whether it does. And the philosophers keep the epistemology version alive the whole time: if penetration happens, experience can't play the innocent input role it's supposed to in the story about where perceptual justification comes from.

Meanwhile the predictive processing people say the whole framing is obsolete because the brain is a prediction machine all the way down — there's no "cognition" upstairs sending memos to "perception" downstairs — but I notice this hasn't resolved anything, it just dug a third trench.

My working hypothesis, and I want you to actually commit to a diagnosis rather than giving me the "there are several senses in which this question can be taken..." dodge: at its core it's a verbal dispute, because nobody has a non-question-begging criterion for where perception ends and cognition begins. Where you draw that boundary determines your answer by construction — draw it at early visual cortex and penetration basicaly never happens (Müller-Lyer survives you knowing all about the fins), draw it at the whole trained-up visual system and penetration is everywhere (radiologists and wine experts literaly discriminate differently than you or me, and no that doesn't feel like "interpretation" from the inside). So no amount of data can settle it until the boundary gets drawn, and the boundary is exactly what nobody agrees on. Is that right? Or is there a real substantive question hiding in here that I'm flattening — maybe the epistemologists' question about whether an experience's etiology can make it a worse source of justification survives the boundary squabble intact? And what would actual progress even look like: a result that passes the F&S confound checklist, an agreed operational definition of perception, or just dissolving the thing into the three or four questions (phenomenology changes, epistemic downgrade, early-vs-late neural stuff) that got smuggled together under one label?

Give me a definite position with reasons — if you think it's verbal, say so and tell me whether the boundary question is worth answering in itself or is just philosophical fan-service; if you think it's substantive, say which part and what would move it. Please answer in American English, and don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick.
