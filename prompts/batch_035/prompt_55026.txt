I teach a critical-thinking elective at a vocational college, and last weekend I had a long, frustrating argument with a close friend who has been reading up on Fomenko's New Chronology. His position wasn't the crude one that everything before AD 1000 is fabricated; it was the more defensible-seeming one that conventional chronology rests on a small number of interlocking sources, that duplicate-looking "patterns" in the historical record are dismissed too quickly by specialists, and that mainstream historians refuse to engage with anomaly-counting arguments because their careers depend on the timeline staying put. What unsettled me wasn't that I couldn't refute him point by point — I mostly could — but that I couldn't articulate, in a way a first-year student would feel, *where exactly* the reasoning goes wrong. I knew the demarcation vocabulary (unfalsifiability, ad hoc immunization, shifting the burden of proof), but reciting criteria did nothing against the lived experience of a story that hangs together. I want my students to hit that experience head-on before I give them the vocabulary for it.

Design me a 75-minute in-class activity for 18 first-year students, no prior knowledge of historiography or philosophy of science assumed, minimal materials (they have phones and I have a printer). The core mechanic I'm imagining: small groups are given a deliberately curated dossier of genuine, checkable historical facts — parallel dynasties, coincidentally similar reign lengths, repetitive wars, figures with suspiciously analogous biographies — and their task is to construct the most persuasive "compressed chronology" argument they can: a claim that two chunks of history are really one, with points awarded for explanatory boldness and for absorbing anomalies that the "orthodox" reading leaves dangling. They should work as motivated reasoners, not skeptics; the grading rubric should reward them for building a good conspiracy, so that at the halfway point they experience how satisfying it feels when everything clicks into place. Only then does the second phase reveal the mechanism. But I'm not wedded to this structure — you're the designer.

Please commit to a definite design rather than offering me a menu of options: exact rules, the actual categories of "anomalies" I should put in the dossier, how groups compete and how you score them, and the sequencing of the reveal. The debrief should map specific moves the students made onto specific moves in Fomenko-style arguments and onto named demarcation criteria, so the connection isn't left to serendipity. I'd also like you to give a firm verdict on which part of the demarcation problem this activity genuinely demonstrates versus which part it only simulates or smuggles in through my framing, and a concrete prediction of the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with fixes. Where you have to make a judgment call — for example, whether letting students argue in bad faith for thirty minutes risks crystallizing belief rather than inoculating against it — take a position and defend it rather than hedging; I'd rather run a design with a stated failure mode than an average of five safe ones.
