Quick question before I write it: the book club I'm in has voted to do Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle next month, and I've been voluntold to lead it because I'm the one who "did history" (one term of historiography at uni, whoop). I've also been having this running row with my brother-in-law, a proper Marxist, who insists the war's outcome was settled by industrial capacity and that counterfactual history is, in his words, bourgeois nostalgia for individual agency. So I'd like to come out of this with an actual reading, not just vibes — and some idea of where he's right.

What I want is the novel read through the contingency vs inevitability debate in history writing — the argument between event-driven, "great man" style history and the structural stuff (geography, economics, long-run forces), plus the methodenstreit over whether counterfactuals are an indispensible tool or a parlour game. Tolstoy's War and Peace, second epilogue, is the famous literary version of the anti-great-man side, and E.H. Carr had his bit about Cleopatra's nose, so there's form for doing this through novels.

Here's what interests me about PKD specifically: the counterfactual inside his counterfactual doesn't match our world either. The Grasshopper Lies Heavy imagines an Allied victory, but it's a different Allied victory, and then at the end Juliana consults the I Ching and gets told, if I'm remembering right, that the inner book is somehow the true one — that the world the characters live in is false. So is Dick taking a side on whether history could have gone otherwise, or is he saying something nastier about the epistemics: that any claim about the unactualised past is a fiction you consult like an oracle, and it mostly tells you about yourself? There's also Tagomi's whole crisis — the way small objects and small moral moments feel charged with significance in the Japanese-ruled half of the book, against the vast German machinery that flattens everything into structure. Maybe that's a thesis about small contingencies mattering? I'm hazy on plot details after twenty years, so keep me honest on the novel but don't invent scenes.

Which bits would you point the group at, and — honestly — where does the novel resist this reading? I don't want to do the thing where we treat the book as an illustration of a debate it doesn't care about. If it's really about the impossibility of ever validating a counterfactual, rather than about which side of the debate is correct, say that plainly. And if there's a position the book takes that neither my brother-in-law's structuralism nor the naive contingency view captures, that's exactly what I want for the discussion.
