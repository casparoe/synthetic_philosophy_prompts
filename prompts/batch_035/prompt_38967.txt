I teach an upper-level ethics seminar (14 students, 75 minutes) on whether the preference/bias distinction can be made principled. Design a "Preference Tribunal" activity: students sit as an appellate court reviewing rulings by a fictional Registrar who either certifies a citizen's stated attitude as a preference, entitled to standing in deliberation, or strikes it down as a bias. Provide 4–5 case vignettes written out in full (each under 80 words), each constructed to defeat a specific criterion the tribunal will be tempted to rely on — stability under reflection, being informed, non-instrumental endorsement, independence from conditions that produced it — but avoid the textbook stock examples (the worker who says she prefers the factory job, the adaptive preference within an oppressive marriage); invent fresh scenarios with a similar logical structure, and include at least one set in a value system unfamiliar to my students, so the debrief can show them importing their own norms to classify someone else's. Include the court's procedure and decision rule, a way of scoring rulings that doesn't use real money, timing, a debrief mapping each vignette to the criterion it was built to undermine, and an honest note on which feature of the underlying philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce. Close with a brief self-assessment: state how confident you are in the design and single out the element you are least sure of. Hard limit: 800 words in total.
