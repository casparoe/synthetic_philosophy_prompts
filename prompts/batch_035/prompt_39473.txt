Question 4: A gardener hired to water a neighbour's roses while she's away on holiday never gets around to it, and the roses die. Explain why this kind of case — causation by omission — creates a problem for the simple counterfactual theory of causation (c causes e iff, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred). Sketch one response that keeps counterfactual dependence at the centre, and state one objection to that response.

Student's answer: The simple theory has an obvious problem here. The only relevant counterfactual dependence is: had the gardener watered the roses, they wouldn't have died. But the gardener's not watering is not an event, and the theory's relata are events, so the theory can't even formulate the right counterfactual. Worse, millions of other people also didn't water the roses, and if any of them had, the roses would have survived too, so once the theory is repaired it will overgenerate causes. The best repair, which David Lewis himself proposed in Causation as Influence, is to say that an absence counts as a cause only when it is salient — when it violates some norm or expectation. The gardener was supposed to water, so his omission counts; the absence of watering by a random person in New Zealand doesn't. The standard objection is that this makes causation partly normative, relative to what we expect or care about, which seems inconsistent with the objectivity of causal claims in science. Cases of omission are really no different from double prevention anyway: the gardener's omission prevents the watering, and the watering would have prevented the death. So all omission cases are just double prevention cases in disguise and raise no new issues. Finally, since the counterfactuals can be restated over facts rather than events — the fact that the gardener didn't water caused the death — taking facts as the relata of causation solves the omission problem completely.
