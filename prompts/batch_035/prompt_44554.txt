second-year phd student in psycholinguistics here, writing my qualifying paper on relativity effects. my advisor, who's a moderately committed whorfian, keeps reaching for the tinted glasses analogy in our meetings: your native language is a pair of tinted glasses you got at birth and can never take off, and whatever tint it has, that's the world you see. speakers of different languages therefore never quite see the same facts — not because anyone is irrational, but because you can't step outside your lenses to compare. she uses it to defend the moderate position: no mystical worldview determinism, just an in-principle filter on attention and categorization that you can't audit from inside.

i keep feeling like the analogy is doing all the argumentative work, but i can't put my finger on exactly where it fails, and i need this for a chapter. can you audit it for me? like, what maps onto what — what's the glasses, what's the tint, what's the wearing, what would "taking them off" even be? then the disanalogies: a tint is uniform across the visual field and static over time, whereas documented language effects are patchy, task-dependent, and show up under attention load but wash out otherwise; you can be *told* about your tint and reason around it, which is basically what colorblind people do all day; bilinguals look less like people who can't remove glasses and more like people who swap prescriptions depending on who they're talking to. some of these probably sink her conclusion and some probably don't — i'd like you to separate those, because the chapter needs to be fair to her.

and then the real question: once you fix the mapping or throw the analogy out, does the moderate conclusion still stand on its own, or was the glasses picture quietly smuggling in the very claim (unremovable, uniform, speaker-transparent filter) that the empirical literature doesn't actually support? my instinct is the better picture is the old toolbox metaphor people trace to later wittgenstein — language as a set of instruments you reach for, where having a specialized tool makes some jobs easier without making other jobs impossible — but i worry that one is so weak it's compatible with anything. is there a middle analogy that captures what's real in the relativity findings without inheriting either picture's baggage?
