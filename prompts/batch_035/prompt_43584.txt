I'm a doctoral student in contract law, and a dissertation chapter on the moral force of promissory obligation has forced me to admit that my judgments about when promises bind are stitched together from doctrine rather than principle. I'd like your help bringing them to reflective equilibrium. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases; please tell me whether a single principle captures them and, if not, which verdicts have to go.

A promise extracted at knifepoint to pay the mugger a further sum the next day: not binding, and I feel no guilt at all about breaking it. The law says the same under the doctrine of duress, but I want the moral ground, not the doctrine: something about the promisee wronging me in the extraction seems to be what voids it. A promise induced by fraudulent misrepresentation — I pledged a large donation to what I was told was a school-building charity that is in fact a lobbying operation: not binding. A solemn promise to someone who dies before I can perform — I promised my grandfather on his deathbed to keep the family farm intact, and I now think I am bound, even though he can no longer rely on my performance, be harmed by my breach, or even know about it. A casual assurance at a party — "I'll be there Saturday, I promise" — uttered among friends in the usual tone of half-serious banter: barely binding, if at all, even though the host may genuinely plan around me. Conversely, an assurance that never uses promissory words but induces serious reliance — a parent who, by a steady course of conduct over years, leads her child to expect that she will fund her education: I think this binds as firmly as an explicit promise, which is roughly what promissory estoppel says, though again I want the principle underneath, not the doctrine. A promise to do something gravely immoral — I promised a friend I would help conceal his crime: I waver between thinking it binds and is overridden, and thinking it never binds at all; I would like the principle to decide this for me. A promise made by someone temporarily incompetent — drugged, or seven years old: not binding, its breach no worse than any other change of mind. A promise whose occasion silently disappears — I promised to look after your cat while you were away, and the trip was cancelled: nothing remains, and I notice I don't feel I have broken anything.

Two tensions I can already see. Whatever makes the deathbed promise bind cannot be any benefit to, reliance by, or harm to the promisee, since none of these is available to a dead man; yet whatever voids the knifepoint promise seems to be a fact about the promisee. So my verdicts appear to require the bindingness of a promise to be both promisee-independent and promisee-dependent. Separately, the banter case and the estoppel case pull in opposite directions on whether it is the utterance of the words or the induced reliance that does the work.

And here is the case I keep going back and forth on: a promise induced by a misrepresentation that made no difference to my decision. I agreed to give a keynote because I believed the organizer when she said the conference was on climate policy; it is in fact on tax law, but I would have accepted either topic — I simply like talking. Does the fraud void the promise, as it did in the charity case, or is the promise intact, as the thought that the deception changed nothing suggests? My gut delivers both verdicts at once, which I take as a sign that at least one is wrong.

Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely; motivate each clause by pointing to the case that demands it; say what the resulting principle predicts for cases I haven't listed — for instance, a promise made to an institution, or one extracted by a threat of doing something the threatener had no right to do but that I would have tolerated anyway; and be explicit about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot save. I would rather overturn a verdict quietly than keep a principle that needs a new epicycle for each case.
