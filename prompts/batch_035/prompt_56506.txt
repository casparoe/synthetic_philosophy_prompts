I'm an operations research engineer by training and a constraint-programming practitioner by trade: I spend my days building scheduling systems — hospital rosters, airline crew pairings, exam timetables — and I'm fluent in that vocabulary down to its fine grain: hard constraints versus soft constraints, feasible regions and their emptiness, infeasibility certificates, slack variables, weighted penalty objectives, lexicographic priority levels, over-constrained systems, Pareto fronts. When a timetable is over-constrained, I know exactly what to do: relax something, promote nothing to a hard constraint unless it truly is one, and let the solver minimize weighted violation. I've recently started reading ethics seriously, and I keep feeling that the philosophical debate about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible is the exact conversation my colleagues and I have about over-constrained systems, except that in philosophy nobody gets to open the solver log.

Here is the debate as I understand it, stated plainly so you can check my version before translating it. One side holds that genuine dilemmas are impossible: if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, and doing both is impossible, then — given the seemingly innocent step that if you ought to do A and ought to do B, you ought to do both — you would be obligated to do the impossible; and since ought implies can, at least one of the two original verdicts must have been wrong from the start, however much it felt right at the time. The other side, in the tradition associated with Bernard Williams, holds that some conflicts are fully real: both obligations stand, you must choose, and the one you don't act on leaves a remainder — an appropriateness of guilt or regret that is not a bookkeeping error, not a mistaken belief that you violated a real obligation, but the correct response to having actually done so even though every available option violated something. In between, some philosophers accept dilemmas but deny the remainder, treating all-things-considered judgment as the only judgment that matters once the conflict is resolved.

Please do two things. First, restate this debate, move by move, in the vocabulary I actually use: treat claims about obligations as claims about constraints, ask whether the anti-dilemma argument is an infeasibility proof, whether the agglomeration step (from two oughts to the joint ought) corresponds to anything in how we handle constraint systems, whether the "ought implies can" premise is a feasibility requirement on the constraint set, and whether the Williams-style remainder has any counterpart at all in what my tools record — penalty weights, violation counts, a solution's distance from the feasible region. Second, give me the residue report, and be honest rather than flattering: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, what has no expression in my vocabulary whatsoever, and where the temptation will be to smuggle in an assumption and call it translation — for instance the assumption that all obligations are constraints of the same type, or that there is a global objective function at all, or that resolving a conflict demotes the losing consideration to a soft constraint. I can already hear the obvious one-line answer — a moral dilemma is just an over-constrained system, and the anti-dilemma side is just insisting that every real system must be feasible — so I'd particularly like you to tell me whether that identification is a genuine translation or my vocabulary talking, and whether the residue that won't cross over points to something my framework cannot say or to something in the philosophers' debate that was never as clear as it looked.
