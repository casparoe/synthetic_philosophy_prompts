For the past two years I've been in a small decision-theory reading group with a friend I'll call Dana, and after enough arguments I've started keeping a ledger of her verdicts, because she insists she decides "case by case" and refuses to state a general principle. Her track record so far: she takes any fair coin flip offered at favorable odds; she buys insurance on her apartment despite acknowledging it has negative expected value, calling herself risk-averse about ruin-sized losses; she rejects Pascal's original wager on the ground that infinitely many rival deities could cancel the expected payoff; she refuses the classic mugging, where a stranger demands $5 and threatens to torture 3↑↑↑3 people in some other dimension unless paid; she refuses, with identical dismissiveness, the mirror-image offer in which a stranger claims that for $5 he will create 3↑↑↑3 blissful lives; she nonetheless donates a tenth of her income to an asteroid-detection nonprofit, citing expert estimates that the chance of her donation helping avert human extinction this century is about one in a hundred thousand, and calling it the best use of money she knows of; and she once declined a drunk stranger's offer of two-to-one odds on a die roll at a party, saying that odds that good from that source meant she was missing something.

Next month our group is discussing a new case, and I want to walk in already knowing her answer. The case: before a new particle collider is switched on, a team publishes an argument that each run carries a roughly one-in-fifty-million chance of triggering a transition to a true vacuum, which would propagate outward at light speed and eventually destroy not just Earth but every star in the observable universe. The calculation is built on real measured physics — though the relevant coupling constants have only been measured to limited precision — a couple of outside physicists endorse the order of magnitude, and most of the rest shrug. The collider would otherwise do excellent science. Should it be turned on? Predict Dana's verdict. Then tell me what rules her track record is actually consistent with — I can see at least three candidates (something about which probabilities she is willing to act on, something about how utility scales with the size of the payoff, maybe a flat cutoff below which she ignores tiny chances), and I'd like you to lay them out, say what each one predicts about the collider, and flag exactly where they diverge. Finally, give me your best guess with your reasons, and describe the further case — the one we should bring to a later session — that would best tell the surviving rules apart.
