I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums since about 2009 — long enough to have followed the whole arc of thought about indirect normativity, from the early LessWrong enthusiasm for coherent extrapolated volition, through the community's periodic rediscovery of Rawls, to the present fashion for deliberative alignment. What that discourse almost never does is test itself against a real, completed instance of idealized deliberation. There happens to be an unusually good one, and I would like you to analyse it.

The episode is the Irish Citizens' Assembly on the Eighth Amendment, 2016–18. Since the answering analysis should not turn on contested details, here are the salient facts. The Eighth Amendment to the Irish constitution, adopted by referendum in 1983, acknowledged the right to life of the unborn and placed it on an equal footing with the mother's, which in practice made abortion unlawful in almost all circumstances. After decades of controversy, galvanised in part by the death of Savita Halappanavar in 2012, the government established a Citizens' Assembly: ninety-nine members of the public, randomly selected and broadly representative by gender, age, region and social class, chaired by a former judge of the Supreme Court. Over successive weekend sessions from late 2016 into spring 2017, the Assembly heard legal, medical and ethical experts, advocacy groups on both sides, and personal testimony; in April 2017 it recommended that termination of pregnancy be lawful without restriction up to twelve weeks, alongside other grounds. A parliamentary committee refined the recommendations, the government called a referendum, and on 25 May 2018 roughly two-thirds of the electorate voted to repeal the amendment.

Please analyse this episode through the lens of indirect normativity and idealized deliberation, running the analysis in both directions.

Be disciplined about the mapping. Who or what counts as the "we" whose volition is being idealized — the ninety-nine, the electorate, the nation? What exactly does the idealization consist of, and how does it compare with Yudkowsky's formulation of what an idealized version of us would want, "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together"? Note that the Assembly operationalises some of these clauses and not others: curated expertise stands in for knowing more; protected time and enforced civility stand in for being better reasoners; but there is no analogue at all for thinking faster, and the shared-history clause is inherited rather than constructed — the Assembly deliberated inside a national memory of the 1983 amendment rather than transcending it. Also worth mapping: the old tension, familiar from the deliberative democracy literature, between a mini-public as microcosm (it mirrors us) and as enlightened sample (it improves us), since the Assembly was asked to be both at once.

Then I want your judgment on two structural features in particular. First, the episode is two-stage: the idealized process proposes and the unidealized electorate disposes. The Assembly's recommendation carried no legal force until a raw, un-deliberated majority ratified it — indeed, the referendum was arguably called precisely to supply the legitimacy that a random sample of ninety-nine could not possess on its own. Indirect normativity as usually formulated knows nothing of this: the theory's paradigm is a one-shot system handed the whole of normative authority. Is the Irish structure a corruption of the idea, or the discovery of something the theory needed all along — a division of labour between epistemic quality and democratic legitimacy, in which each stage launders the other's deficiencies? Second, the regress: someone idealizes the idealizers. The Assembly's agenda, its roster of witnesses and the framing of its ballots were curated by officials, the chair and a secretariat drawn from the civil service. In the rationalist vocabulary this is the problem of the training data for the deliberation — the layer the theory is silent about. How much of the outcome should be attributed to that unexamined layer, and does the episode give us any way to see inside it at all?

Finally, tell me honestly where the lens breaks. The obvious pressure point is causal: opinion polls had shown a majority for liberalisation for years before the Assembly sat, so a sceptic can claim the deliberation was epiphenomenal and the referendum would have gone the same way without it. If so, what was the Assembly actually doing — measuring a volition that was already there, or constituting one and reading it back as discovery? That distinction matters, because the entire appeal of extrapolation over ordinary polling rests on it. And give me a verdict on the theory: does the episode confirm indirect normativity, constitute an anomaly for it, or present a case it never anticipated — a partial, staged, human-scale idealization whose output is ratified rather than trusted? Where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak; and if you think the CEV comparison is ultimately a stretch, say that too, with reasons.

Please write your answer in British English.
