I'm on the steering committee of a 180-member housing cooperative, and we're kicking around the idea of replacing our elected board with a randomly selected one — every year the nine board seats would get filled by drawing names from the membership list, the way jury duty works. It started because our elections have become a farce: its basically the same 15 or so people who run every time, turnout hovers around 22%, and one faction has effectively captured agenda-setting. A few of us got excited after hearing about citizens' assemblies being used for constitutional questions in Ireland, though obviously our stakes are much smaller — we mostly argue about the roof fund and whether to allow subletting.

We've also got two wrinkles on the table from members. One guy proposes what he calls "graduated sortition": the draw would be weighted so that anyone who has served a term in the last five years has a lower chance of being picked, to spread the burden around and stop a lucky few from becoming a permanent board-by-lottery. Another member wants a hybrid board: five seats by election and four by lot, on the theory that elections at least give us a mechanism for rewarding people who've actually learned the job. And there's a lurking worry about a recall rule — if a randomly drawn board starts doing something genuinely crazy, there's no natural way to vote them out mid-term, since we never voted them in in the first place.

Could you describe the most important implications of moving to this system? Please structure your reply as follows: first a section "What changes overnight" (~150 words) on what is immediately different the day we switch; then "Legitimacy and competence" (~350 words) on the tradeoff between the democratic pedigree of the draw and the loss of skill and institutional memory on the board; then "The wrinkles" (~250 words) specifically on graduated sortition, the hybrid board, and the recall problem, saying for each what it does to the core case for sortition; and finally "Bottom line" (~100 words) with your honest take on whether a co-op our size should do it at all. Assume board members get only a small honorarium and that we'd definately keep a professional property manager for the day-to-day, in case that changes your picture of what the board actually does.
