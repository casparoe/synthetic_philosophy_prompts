I'm a PhD student in machine learning working on interpretability — specifically on what people in my field call the linear representation hypothesis: the idea that concepts are encoded as directions in activation space, so that something like a model's belief that Paris is the capital of France might show up as a feature direction that a linear probe can read off, and that can in principle be edited or steered by adding a vector. At a recent lab reading group we drifted into an argument about the old Churchland-style eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires — the view that propositional attitudes, as commonsense psychology conceives them, are a false and stagnant theory destined to be eliminated by mature neuroscience the way phlogiston or demonic possession were, rather than smoothly reduced to it. One labmate claims interpretability is quietly vindicating eliminativism: what we actually find when we open up a network is graded, superposed, polysemantic features with causal roles, not discrete, all-or-nothing beliefs standing in logical relations to one another. Another says the opposite — that probes and steering feel precisely like isolating a belief and an intervention on it. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of mind who also has a genuine working knowledge of interpretability research, and to restate eliminative materialism in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report.

By the residue report I mean the following, spelled out per claim: which of the eliminativists' claims about folk psychology translate cleanly into talk of features, circuits, probes, activation steering, and training distributions; which translate only with distortion — and note where the distortion is doing hidden work; and which claims have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I'd particularly like you to grapple with three specific worries I can't resolve myself. First, the distinction between eliminating a concept and retaining it as an instrumentally indispensable higher-level description: in my terms, is belief-talk like macrostate-talk in statistical mechanics (coarse-grained but faithful, and you'd be crazy to abandon it) or like a legacy debugging vocabulary we tolerate out of habit? Second, the normativity residue: the attitudes are things I can be criticized for — I can hold inconsistent beliefs, reason badly from them, be mocked for them. My features have magnitudes, directions, and causal effects, but nothing in the probe-and-circuit toolkit looks like inconsistency or bad inference. Does the normative vocabulary simply have no counterpart at the mechanistic level, and if so, does that support the eliminativist or their opponents? Third, the fact that my objects of study are neural networks rather than brains: does eliminativism about belief even make the same claim about artifacts that were trained to imitate belief-like behavior, or does the translation silently assume I'm talking about brains?

Please think carefully before writing. Start by brainstorming: list what you take the eliminativists' actual commitments to be (charitably — including the connectionist arguments they used, which are closest to my home turf), then list the core commitments of the interpretability vocabulary, and only then attempt the mapping, being explicit about which party's assumptions you're importing at each step. Don't smuggle in physicalism or functionalism and call it translation. I want the residue treated as the interesting part, and I want a final verdict on whether the residue points to something my framework cannot say or to something that was less clear than it looked in the original philosophical debate.
