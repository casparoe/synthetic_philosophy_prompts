Longtime reader of the Sequences-era personal identity threads here. I keep noticing a recurring gap in how those discussions handle a certain question, and I want to know whether the academic literature has a name for it.

Everyone in rationalist circles is comfortable with the patternist line — you are the pattern, so a faithful upload is you — and with Parfit-flavored moves on which psychological continuity, not identity, is what matters. But when the question is phrased as "could you have been conceived from a different sperm cell," people give a flat, confident no: not "someone very like you would exist," but "someone else would exist, full stop" — even though the resulting child would match you more closely than any upload ever could. Neither toolkit predicts this; identity seems to be hanging on the particular material origin rather than on anything downstream of it.

Probing the reflex with a ladder of cases makes it stranger. Same egg and sperm, but joined in a lab a week later: most people say that's still me. A later sibling with my exact genome, causally disconnected from my conception: almost nobody says that's me, not even the patternists, and I can't reconstruct their reason. And the case that breaks everything — the zygote that became me splits, one twin is implanted now, the other stays frozen for a decade. Both twins have literally the same origin, so either origin doesn't settle identity after all, or one twin is the other.

So, three questions. Does the view that your particular origin — the actual gametes, or the actual zygote — is non-negotiable for your identity have an established name, and how are its boundaries drawn? Specifically, is the shared-origin twin case a known embarrassment for it? And if the boundary question (origin as the specific cells, versus origin as the information they carry, versus "enough of the original matter") has no settled treatment, I'd settle for a well-drawn distinction: a definition of what this view is committed to, and where exactly it comes apart from patternism and from psychological-continuity views.
