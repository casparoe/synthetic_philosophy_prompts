I write novels for a living, which mostly means spending my days deciding what my characters notice and how precisely they notice it, and last month I attended a public lecture by a visiting cognitive scientist that has unsettled my working picture of what perception is. His core claim, delivered with great confidence, was that the brain is a prediction machine: it is constantly generating a full model of the scene in front of it, and the sensory signal never enters wholesale but only arrives as a thin stream of prediction errors, telling the brain where its guessed world needs revising. On this basis he described ordinary perception as a kind of controlled hallucination, and then drew the conclusion that seemed to interest him most: that we never actually see the world at all, that what we call seeing is a private fiction kept on the rails only by constant small corrections. When I asked afterward what this meant for the sensory scenes I spend weeks crafting, he said, cheerfully, that I was essentially documenting each character's hallucination and that there was no shared scene for any two of my characters to perceive -- which, if true, would quietly change how I write every room, every conversation, every landscape.

I am in no position to evaluate the science, but I know enough to distrust a good line doing philosophical work. So I would like you to take on the role of a philosopher of mind with a solid grounding in cognitive science, think carefully and systematically, and audit this analogy rather than merely report it. Begin with a bit of brainstorming about what the prediction-machine picture is actually claiming, then lay out the mapping explicitly -- what corresponds to the machine, the prediction, the error signal, the hallucination, and the control -- and say where the mapping holds and, crucially, where it breaks. I want to know which features of clinical hallucinations the phrase quietly imports that ordinary perception lacks: hallucinations are typically idiosyncratic rather than shared, resistant to correction rather than disciplined by it, and arrived at involuntarily. Then address the part I most care about: does the speaker's conclusion -- that we are cut off from the world, that there is no scene two people both perceive -- actually follow from the predictive story, or is it an artifact of the word "hallucination" carrying weight the machinery itself doesn't supply? Finally, tell me what the prediction-machine picture does get right that a passive-camera picture of perception gets wrong, and whether some better analogy is available that preserves those insights without the skeptical conclusion, since that is what I need to know before I revise my next chapter. Please reason step by step and be candid about where you think the speaker was overselling.
