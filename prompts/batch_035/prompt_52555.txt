I write about physics for a general readership, and I keep bumping into an editorially inconvenient pattern: when a theory is built to fit data that was already in hand, scientists discount it, but when a theory sticks its neck out on data no one has yet, success counts enormously. Cosmologists shrug at MOND because it was tuned to galaxy rotation curves that were already measured, yet they treat the 1919 light-bending expedition as one of general relativity's finest hours -- even though the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, which Einstein's equations also explained, was likewise old news when the theory arrived. So the same community, within the same field, sometimes counts old evidence heavily and sometimes barely at all. What view of confirmation does this normative practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit epistemology -- does the practice tacitly assume that a theory built to accommodate known data can be scored differently than one that predicts unseen data, and if so, on what grounds, given that both explain the same observations? Then tell me whether the norm is internally coherent or whether the Mercury-versus-MOND asymmetry reveals that something else (simplicity, unification, the theorist's prior track record) is doing the real work. Finally, what would have to change in how scientists and journalists talk about "evidence for" a theory if the community adopted a strictly Bayesian conditionalization view in which anything you already know with certainty can no longer move your credence?
