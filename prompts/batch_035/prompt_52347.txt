I'm putting together satire examples for a course unit on Kierkegaard's critique of the press and "the public" — the argument from the first part of *Two Ages* (1846), the long book review where he diagnoses his own age as one of reflection without passion. The pieces of that diagnosis I most want the satire to operationalize: leveling, the process by which the age of the collective quietly abolishes the distinction between the distinguished person and everyone else, so that no one stands out and no one has to; the public, a phantom or abstraction that no one can ever actually locate but that is constantly invoked as the authority; the press as the public's creature, the medium through which the phantom barks; passionlessness dressed up as balance, reflection, and above all chatter — the endless inconsequential talk about everything, from a position of total safety. And crucially the redistribution of responsibility: when everyone reads about everything together, no individual feels answerable for anything, because the whole public shares the blame and the whole public is nowhere.

Write the satire as a leaked internal editorial manual for the opinion section of a mid-sized newspaper — an onboarding document for new opinion writers, deadpan corporate voice, roughly 700 words, numbered sections with bureaucratic headings (e.g., "Section 4: Converting Convictions into Content"). The manual's rules should read like sensible editorial best practice while quietly implementing the whole Kierkegaardian diagnosis: passion is a firing offense on professionalism grounds; strong personal conviction is a workflow bottleneck and must be routed through the takes pipeline; the author of every article is ultimately the Public, which cannot be reached for comment and does not, strictly speaking, exist; the paper's proper stance toward anyone who actually does something is admiring coverage from a safe distance, since admiration is how the reflective age experiences action without the risk of acting; responsibility for any error is to be distributed across readership until it evaporates. Somewhere include a quote from a fictional departing columnist, terminated for having a conviction, and a short appendix with FAQ entries like "What if I believe something?" The manual's writers must not know they are in a satire.

The jokes have to come from getting Kierkegaard right rather than from zingers — a reader who knows *Two Ages* should find it fair and feel the diagnosis land harder for being dressed as workplace policy, and a reader who has never heard of Kierkegaard should still get a piece that functions as recognizable satire of modern media. Two constraints on fairness: don't strawman Kierkegaard as a crank who hated newspapers the way one hates a new technology — his target is the passionless reflection and the phantom public the press serves, not the printing press itself; and let the manual reveal, maybe in the appendix, the melancholy note that the leveling is not some villain's scheme but something we all carry out together, each of us grateful to the collective for making standing out — and therefore inward passion — unnecessary. If you want an in-joke, the paper's bitter rival can be a scurrilous scandal-sheet, but that's optional; the core requirement is the deadpan, in-universe, professionally reasonable voice throughout.
