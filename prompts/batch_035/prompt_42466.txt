I'm a science and ideas correspondent preparing a long-form interview for our magazine's ideas program, and I'd like your help drafting the questions. My guest is a cognitive scientist who has spent much of her career arguing that the classic split-brain literature greatly overstates the case for there being two subjects of experience in a patient after corpus callosotomy. Her position, as I understand it, has three strands: the dramatic dissociations show up only under artificial laboratory conditions — lateralized tachistoscopic presentation, chimeric figures — that never arise in ordinary life; in the months after surgery the two hemispheres develop mutual cueing and other workarounds that restore functionally integrated behavior; and the inference from a double dissociation in performance to a doubling of subjects is a non sequitur, since an intact brain likewise contains parallel processes that do not exhaustively communicate with one another.

Please draft ten to twelve interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the view. I want each of the following represented, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

(i) a question pressing the standard rejoinder that the left hemisphere's confabulatory narration — calmly inventing explanations for actions the speaking hemisphere did not itself initiate — is exactly what you would expect if the narrating system no longer had access to a single unified field, with a follow-up anticipating her likely reply that post-hoc rationalization is ubiquitous in intact brains as well;

(ii) a question on whether her view and the more cautious rival position — one subject, granted, but a consciousness that is genuinely and partially disunited — amount to a substantive disagreement or merely a preference between labels, with a follow-up pressing her to state a testable prediction that would separate the two;

(iii) a question on intermanual conflict, the occasional episodes in which one hand works at cross purposes with the other — reportedly sometimes resolved only when the patient physically restrains the offending hand — and whether her integration-by-workaround story can absorb this or must dismiss it as transient and unrepresentative;

(iv) and a question on individuals with agenesis of the corpus callosum, whose hemispheres never had a callosum to lose and who nonetheless show subtle integration deficits, asking whether that finding fits her framework or pressures it.

For every question, please append a note of one or two sentences indicating where the question presupposes a contested reading of the experimental record or a disputed characterization of her position, and what answer from her would reveal the question to have been badly framed. I'd rather not have any questions about how she came to the topic, her feelings about her critics, or her advice for young researchers; every question should turn on the substance of the dispute itself.
