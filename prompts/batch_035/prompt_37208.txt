I'm taking a political philosophy course in which we've just finished Popper on the open society and Rawls on toleration, and my study group has produced an argument that I find disturbingly hard to answer. It runs as follows. (1) Toleration is the refusal to suppress views and practices one disapproves of, in cases where one has the power to suppress them; the disposition is empty unless it operates precisely where the tolerating party dislikes what it is leaving alone. (2) A state is tolerant only if it extends this refusal across the board: it does not get to pick which disapproved views it will tolerate, since picking on the basis of its own disapproval is exactly what toleration forbids. (3) A liberal state disapproves of intolerant movements, and it plainly has the power to ban them, censor their advocacy, and bar them from office. (4) So a liberal state is tolerant only if it lets intolerant movements organize, campaign, and compete for power on equal terms with everyone else. (5) A movement that wins power can then use it to abolish the framework that allowed it to compete, and once it has won, tolerance cannot be restored by tolerant means. (6) Therefore a tolerant state must stand by while the intolerant abolish toleration: a consistent commitment to toleration commits the state to permitting its own abolition.

What makes this hard for me is that it seems to prove too much, in more than one direction. Run the same reasoning about free speech and you get that a regime committed to free speech must permit the censorship of speech, since the censor, too, just holds a view the free-speech regime disapproves of. Run it about promise-keeping and you get that a person committed to keeping promises must keep a promise to break all future promises. Yet I can't see which step in the chain is doing the damage. I'd most like to keep (1) and (5): (1) because I can't see how toleration survives as a distinctive virtue if it only covers things the state is indifferent to anyway, and (5) because history does not offer many examples of tolerant movements voting a tyranny back out of existence.

So here is what I'm asking for. Either the argument is valid, in which case tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what the alternatives each pay; or it fails somewhere, in which case name the step, explain why it looked fine, and show me what the repair costs the rest of the chain. My own tentative thought is that the answer must lie in rejecting or restricting (2), via something like the thought that toleration is a reciprocal convention and those who reject reciprocity forfeit its protection — but that move strikes me as too easy as stated, because it hands the state a discretionary license to decide who has "rejected" reciprocity, and states have historically found their dissenters to be the intolerant ones. So if you do send me to (2), I want to see how the line is drawn without collapsing into "we suppress whatever we call intolerant," and what the tolerant state pays in consistency by drawing it. And if you think the flaw is an equivocation or a slide somewhere in (1) through (4), I'd like you to spell out the two readings the offending step trades on, because I've stared at it and can't get them apart.
