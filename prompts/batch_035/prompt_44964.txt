I'm a retired spectroscopist who has spent the last couple of years reading about Bayesian confirmation theory, at the level of a serious amateur. There is a pattern I keep running into, and I want to know whether its parts have established names — and if not, I'd like help carving the phenomena up and naming them myself. I should say upfront that a bare label won't satisfy me; what I want is a statement of what each phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.

The pattern has two pieces, and I genuinely don't know whether they are one thing or two.

The first piece is a tension I can't get anyone to resolve. On the Bayesian way of thinking, as I understand it informally, evidence confirms a theory when the evidence is more to be expected given the theory than given its denial — the evidence raises the theory's probability. But many of the most celebrated confirmations in the history of science run backwards relative to this formula: the evidence was fully in hand, and regarded as settled, before the theory ever existed. The stock example is general relativity and the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, which had been measured decades before Einstein worked out the theory; when the theory reproduced the number, everyone counted it as a triumph, and I count it as one too. Yet if the precession was already certain, then its probability was already 1 — and so was its probability conditional on the theory. Nothing gets raised. So either the probability-raising formula is missing something that scientists are right to see in these cases, or scientists are responding to a kind of support that simply isn't probability-raising. I don't know which, and I'd like to know whether this puzzle has an established name.

The second piece, which may or may not be the same thing. When I ask myself when the retrodiction of known facts impresses me and when it doesn't, the answer seems to depend on what the theory's author had in view while building it. Two cases from my working life: one group at our lab built a correction model whose entire point was to reproduce a known seasonal artifact in our baselines, and nobody thought the model well supported merely because it matched — it was built to match. Another group built a model of detector noise for an unrelated purpose, and it turned out, unprompted, to land on that same seasonal artifact to good precision; everyone in the room trusted that model more afterwards, even though nothing about anyone's probabilities had changed in the meantime. Same data, same degree of numerical agreement, opposite reactions. My colleagues reach for words like overfitting or ad hoc for the unimpressive case, but those seem to me to name the practice of rescuing a theory after a failure, not the difference between aiming at a target and hitting one by accident. And note a wrinkle: in the impressive case the builders knew the artifact existed in general terms — they just hadn't used it. So the distinction isn't cleanly "knew about the evidence versus didn't."

So, my questions:

1. Does the first puzzle — certain evidence apparently confirming anyway — have an established name, and how is that concept actually defined?
2. Does the second distinction, the one that seems to turn on whether the evidence was in the builder's view, have a name of its own? And is it one phenomenon with the first, or two? If two, what exactly is the difference, and which one is doing the work in the Mercury case?
3. For anything without an established name, coin a term, define it, and say where its boundary cases fall — including the wrinkle above, the case where the builder knew the rough magnitude of a phenomenon but not its measured value.
