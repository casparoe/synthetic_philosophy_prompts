I convene an upper-level philosophy of language module at a British university and am drafting this summer's three-hour closed-book exam. I want one essay question on performative utterances and speech acts that cannot be answered by reciting Austin's felicity conditions, the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary trichotomy, or the constative/performative contrast. The crux should instead be what Austin called the etiolation of language: the fact that utterances on the stage, in a poem, in a soliloquy, or introduced by quotation seem to lose their performative force even when every classical felicity condition appears to be satisfied — the actor who says the marriage words before a real registrar would not thereby be marrying. The question should press students on whether etiolation is a unitary phenomenon with a single diagnosis (parasitism on serious use, the speaker's non-acceptance of conventional consequences, the absence of genuine uptake, or something else), or whether the etiolated cases come apart, and on whether the phenomenon is all-or-nothing or a matter of degree.

To keep the question from being answerable out of lecture notes, please build it around a concrete scenario that mixes the cases: a dinner-theatre event held in a deconsecrated chapel in which a paying audience watches an actor, playing a ship's sponsor, smash a bottle against a prop bow while the theatre's marketing copy promises every ticket-holder a "genuine christening", and, on the way out, a visitor's phone reads aloud a customer-service message generated by a language model: "I hereby promise you a full refund." Candidates should have to say, for each utterance, what act (if any) was performed, by whom, and what follows about which feature of the situation does the work — and then generalise to whether an artificial system's outputs can be illocutionary acts at all, or only locutionary events accompanied by perlocutionary effects. I'd like the question to be answerable in roughly seventy minutes, so keep it to a single question with a short preamble, not a menu of sub-parts that lets candidates dodge the hard comparison.

Please supply three things: the question as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite position rather than surveying options; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose them. The marking scheme should separate a First from a 2:1 by naming the specific moves only a First-class answer will make — for example, noticing that the refund case and the theatre case might demand opposite diagnoses, or testing a proposed criterion of etiolation against a case it over- or under-generates. Distractor-style credit should be handled explicitly: say which tempting but shallow moves (e.g. declaring the language model's output a performative because it has perlocutionary effects on the visitor) deserve only partial credit and why. Please write the question, model answer, and marking scheme entirely in British English, and do not attribute any specific quotations to Austin or Searle — the exam is closed-book, so the question should not presuppose verbatim knowledge of any text.
