My friend has this running thing about whether video-game characters can be wronged — and she's insistent that's a different question from whether the on-screen violence is bad for you, the player. Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them: mowing down pedestrians in a crime sandbox just to see it happen, wrong, but the wrong is to you — she'd never say you wronged the pedestrians. Killing the scripted story companion who pleads when you aim at her (same canned plea every playthrough, nothing behind it): wrong, and when I pushed her on who it's wrong TO, she said to her, a little, because the game models her not wanting to die, and that's as much of a not-wanting-to-die as exists in that world. The fake crying faces a mobile game slaps on every animal you tap, obviously bolted on to guilt you into spending: not wrong to the animals at all, that's just a marketing skin. Letting the villagers in her life-sim get hungrier and more miserable while the console is off: this one visibly bothers her, but she wouldnt commit to whether it's bad for the villagers or just bad that she installed a world where that happens. Letting her little sister's virtual pet — maybe four behaviors total, nothing inside it — starve because nobody pressed the feed button: wrong, but the wrong is to the sister, full stop, the pet is a sock puppet.

Next week we're doing new cases and I want to be ready. Case one: a heavily modded sandbox NPC with persistent goals, memory of what you've done to it, and something like a mood that lasts for in-game days — but it's a private build no one will ever see or hear about, she'd feel nothing doing it, and she's not the type to get coarsened by it. Case two: the same sister's same pet, except this time the sister is standing right there watching and laughing while it starves. Case three: a dungeon-master character whose dialogue is generated live by a language model, so its complaints about running the world for ungrateful adventurers are never twice the same — no persistent state underneath, just fresh text each time. For each case: what's she likely to say, what rule is she implicitly running, what other rules fit her track record just as well, and which of the three cases does the best job of splitting those rules apart? And is there a further case that would settle it for good? I care less about the right answers than about nailing down the rule she seems to be running and where it forks.
