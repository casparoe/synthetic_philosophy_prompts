A student of mine got a comment auto-removed on a big social platform last week because she quoted a slur — in a thread about, of all things, the semantics of slurs, quoting the word in order to mention it. I teach philosophy of language, and instead of just being annoyed I started wondering what the whole apparatus actually presupposes about how these words work.

Here's the practice as I understand it. The first layer is token-level auto-flagging: the string appears, the algorithm acts, with no regard for whether it was used or mentioned, quoted, negated, reported on, discussed. Then human review layers exceptions on top: quoting in an obviously journalistic or academic context sometimes gets restored, and some slurs are treated as permissible when used by members of the targeted group — the "who's saying it" rule, where the very same token is fine from one speaker and a violation from another. And notably the courts do something different again: in hostile-environment cases context, repetition, and who directed the word at whom all count, and quoting in the course of discussing an incident is treated differently from using it.

So what's the implicit theory? Does flagging on the token alone presuppose that a slur's derogatory content is semantic — encoded in the word itself, insensitive to context in a way ordinary descriptors aren't — or is the system just not in the meaning business at all, tracking what a token does (harms, offends, chills) rather than what it says? And then the in-group exception seems to pull the other way: if the same string is permissible or impermissible depending on the speaker's standing, does that presuppose some contextualist or indexicalist view? Can one institution presuppose a context-insensitive account for the automation and a context-sensitive one for the exceptions without being incoherent, or is that just two views badly bolted together?

Then the questions I actually care about. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: could a committed pragmatist about slurs — someone who thinks the derogation is implicature or some other contextual effect — still endorse token-flagging on error-cost grounds, the way you might ban all fireworks in a dry forest without any theory of combustion? Where exactly is the practice underdetermined between semanticist, pragmatist, and expressivist readings? And if the contextualists are right about how slurs actually work, what would moderation have to become — is the "who can say it" rule the thin edge of per-utterance interpretation, and is that even something an institution operating at scale can do? Be honest about whether the parts of this practice presuppose compatible things or whether one of them has to give.
