My friend Daniel and I spent most of last night arguing about quantum suicide, and before I write up the position I ended up defending, I want to know whether it actually hangs together. Here is what I claimed, roughly in the order I asserted or conceded it:

(1) The Everett interpretation is true: there is no collapse, and every outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized in some branch. This goes for ordinary macroscopic chances too — when I drive to work, there are branches in which I crash, and they are no less real than this one.

(2) What matters, both for rational decision-making and for what I should care about, is Born measure, not branch count. If a quantum event comes out one way across a great many physically distinct branches totaling 1% of the measure and the other way in a single branch totaling 99%, that is simply a 1% chance of the first outcome, and there is no further fact about how many branches go each way that rationality or ethics should track.

(3) Death is bad for the one who dies because it deprives them of the goods their life would otherwise have contained, not because dying is painful or frightening; that there is nothing it is like to be dead does not make death harmless.

(4) Personal identity over time consists in psychological continuity.

(5) The setup under dispute was the usual one: a device coupled to a quantum randomizer that fires with probability one-half each round, with the pot doubling every round it does not. Daniel insisted that since the only branches containing any future experience of mine are ones in which I survive and collect, I am guaranteed, from the inside, to walk away a winner, so refusing is irrational; he then went further and argued that the same reasoning applies to every hazard, so I should expect, conditioning on remaining conscious at all, to find myself increasingly old and improbably lucky — quantum immortality proper. I refused to play, and I refused the immortality conclusion. My stated reasons were that across most of the measure in which I survive the early rounds I survive badly injured rather than intact, and that Daniel was confusing there being surviving branches with there being safety.

(6) Late in the argument, worn down, I conceded that if I were already dying of an incurable disease, and the game were arranged so that the small-measure set of branches in which the device never fired for me was also the set in which the disease turned out to be cured, I might well play.

Does this set cohere? Which of these commitments pull against each other, which only appear to, and where does the cheapest repair lie? I will admit that I felt least sure of my footing somewhere around the identity claims — it was there that Daniel seemed to be gaining ground — but I would rather not rely on my own sense of where the trouble is. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning, say for each what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list, and tell me whether anything I said under (5) is really doing the work I claimed for it.
