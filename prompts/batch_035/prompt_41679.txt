I'm building a discussion section for an undergrad cog sci course on the self, and this week is Hofstadter's strange loop idea -- specifically the bit from I Am a Strange Loop where he argues an "I" isn't confined to one skull but is a pattern that gets partially instantiated in other people's brains. He works through it very personally after his wife Carol died in 1993, describing finding pieces of her still running in his own head. I want the students to actually have to apply the criterion instead of just nodding along.

So here's what I want from you. Please take on the persona of a philosopher of mind who has spent an unhealthy amount of time inside Hofstadter's books and generally thinks pattern talk about selves is right, and think carefully before you rank -- do a little brainstorming first about what "amount of self-pattern instantiated in a given brain" could even mean quantitatively, and whether Hofstadter's own remarks give us a usable metric (fidelity of the pattern? how often it's activated? how causally efficacious it is in the host's decisions?).

Then the actual task. Imagine a woman, Mira, who died last year at 62. Below are ten "carriers" -- brains and other things where her self-pattern might or might not live on. Rank them into tiers, from "that's a real chunk of Mira's loop, genuinely still running" down to "that's a footprint, not a foot -- traces but no self." For each carrier, a sentence or two of justification, and make sure you're applying Hofstadter's actual pattern-based criterion rather than just sentimental intuition (call yourself out when the two diverge).

1. Her husband of 34 years, who finishes her sentences and involuntarily uses her verbal tics.
2. Her adult daughter, who knew her deeply but has spent her whole life defining herself in opposition to her mother -- so the pattern is there but constantly running in negate mode.
3. Her writing collaborator of two decades, who says she still hears Mira's objections while drafting and adjusts her prose accordingly.
4. A large language model fine-tuned on 15 years of Mira's emails and journals, which can produce uncannily Mira-ish prose on demand but runs only when prompted.
5. Her published book, which has ~40,000 readers, none of whom knew her, each of whom carries some compressed and distorted fragment of her.
6. A neuroscience postdoc who never met her but has spent three years immersed in her archived lab notebooks and claims to know what Mira would have thought about an experimental design before checking.
7. Her small town, where she led the wetlands preservation campaign, the hiking trail is named after her, and schoolkids learn a two-paragraph version of her life.
8. Her estranged brother, who knew her intimately but only from ages 0 to 22 and still models her as that young woman, never updating the model in 40 years.
9. Her best friend, who actively avoids thinking about her because the grief is too much.
10. Her own brain, in the final year of her life, when a neurodegenerative condition had allegedly eroded much of what people who knew her called her personality.

Some extra things I want in there: (a) for the husband, address the question a sharp student will raise -- if big pieces of Mira's pattern run in his brain, does his own "I" get diluted, or crowded, or does two-patterns-one-brain even make sense on Hofstadter's picture? (b) tell me where you'd personally put the cutoff between "enough pattern to count" and "not enough," and whether that cutoff is sharp or sorites-style mushy. (c) flag the single carrier on the list where you think Hofstadter himself would most disagree with your placement, and why.

Length-wise, substantial but not a dissertation -- the tiers plus justifications are the core, don't let the brainstorm up front balloon. No need for citations or page numbers, I know the material, I just want the thinking. Also please dont sand down the weirdness: if the LLM carrier forces you to say something odd about substrate-independence, say the odd thing.
