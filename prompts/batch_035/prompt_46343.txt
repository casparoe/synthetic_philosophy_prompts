So, bit of background: I've been reading OB/LW-adjacent stuff since roughly the Aumann agreement threads were hot, and two of my friends have now been arguing about this for literally years. One of them — call her the Aumannite — thinks Aumann's 1976 result basically shows that persistent disagreement between rational people is a failure mode: if two people have common priors and their posteriors are common knowledge, they can't agree to disagree, so when two humans keep disagreeating after talking it out, at least one of them is being epistemically irrational somewhere (bias, motivated reasoning, whatever). The other one — the prior-divergence guy — thinks the common prior assumption is doing all the work and it's basically fantasy for humans: your "prior" is just your posteriors from before you could form memories, and there's no reason to expect those to line up across people with different life histories, different teachers, different genes, different everything. He thinks the theorem is true but almost vacuous as a criticism of actual human disagreement.

They finally agreed to do the adversarial-collaboration thing. Write the joint statement they should come out of it with. Specifically:

- A numbered section of claims both could sign, including about the theorem itself (it's valid math under its assumptions, nobody disputes the proof, and both sides agree about what the assumptions are). These claims should be substantive, not vacuous "we agree the theorem exists" filler.
- A disagreements section, ideally a table, where each disagreement is phrased in wording the relevant side would actually accept — so no "the other side just denies rationality" cheap shots from the Aumannite, and no "the other side lives in math-land and has never met a human" from the prior-divergence guy. Each side should be able to read its own row and nod.
- The cruxes. I suspect one is whether common priors are a defensible idealization for ideal agents or merely a modeling convention, and another is what "sharing evidence" even means once you count twenty years of life experience as evidence (can that stuff be transmitted at all?), and maybe a third about whether computation and communication costs — the fact that nobody can actually condition on the other person's full epistemic state — let you escape the conclusion in practice.
- For each crux, at least one test, argument, or case both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Think stuff like: whether forecasting groups that extensively share evidence converge harder than groups that don't (Good Judgment Project style data), whether prediction markets behave the way each of them would expect, whether making people bet their credences shrinks disagreement and whether it shrinks it toward accuracy.

And if you conclude that part of their fight is just verbal — like they're quietly meaning different things by "rational," or by "disagreement," or by "should" — say which part and why. They've earned an honest diagnosis, not diplomacy.
