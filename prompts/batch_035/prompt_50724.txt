So I'm a grad student TAing an upper-level free will seminar, and we just finished the Frankfurt unit (the paper where Black waits in the wings to make Jones do the deed if Jones shows any sign of backing out, plus a chunk of the follow-up literature). The students did fine on the reading quiz but in discussion it's all labels — someone says "well I'm a semicompatibilist" and someone else says "no, Frankfurt cases show sourcehood is what matters" and nobody actually engages the cases. My own view is that the whole debate would go better if people were forced to take stands on concrete scenarios before they get to use any of the -isms, so I want to build them a decision tree handout that sorts them into a position by their verdicts on cases, not by which team they like the name of.

Here's the material I want the tree built from, described concretely since I don't want the nodes to presuppose jargon. There are four case-types I care about, and the order of the questions should track how they differ. First, the classic standby-intervener case, but I want it split by a timing question: in one version, it stays true right up to the last moment that if Jones were going to waver, there'd be a split second where he could still have stopped — a "flicker" of an alternative — before Black's device would kick in; in another version, Black set things up in advance so that there is literally no point in the whole process where things could have gone otherwise, no flicker to be found. Second, a version where instead of monitoring for a sign, Black just locked all the doors ahead of time — Jones chooses freely inside a room he could never have left, no intervention ever happens and none ever would have been needed (call it the blockage setup). Third, the design case: Black built Jones's deciding process from the start, maybe years back, so the decision is genuinely Jones's own in every ordinary sense but its origin traces back to Black. For each node, phrase the question so a student could answer it from intuition about the case alone, like "would you still hold Jones responsible if the only alternative available to him was that half-second of wavering?" rather than anything mentioning determinism or Frankfurt by name.

Format and audience: one page, for students who've read Frankfurt's paper but none of the secondary stuff, so no name-dropping in the questions themselves — names can appear at the leaves. The leaves should include at least: the view that Frankfurt cases work and responsibility never needed alternatives (Frankfurt's own position), the flicker-of-freedom view that grabs onto those half-seconds, leeway incompatibilism, source incompatibilism, hard incompatibilism, and a reasons-responsiveness style compatibilism. And two things I really want, which most handouts botch: first, build the tree out of the logical space first and then locate the positions in it, and explicitly note any branch-combination no actual view occupies, with a guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unappealing, or just unexplored; second, flag honestly any pair of leaves the case-questions can't separate — I suspect the design cases will make sourcehood views do work that none of my questions about alternatives can capture, and I'd rather the handout admit that than paper over it. Also dont make every path lead to a named view — if some answer combination lands nowhere, say so on the leaf as "unnamed combination" rather than forcing it into the nearest -ism. If you think one of my four case-types is doing no work the others don't already do, say which one and why, since I can drop it and get the tree down to something that fits on one side of a page.
