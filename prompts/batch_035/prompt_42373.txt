I've been reading LessWrong and the alignment-adjacent blogosphere since the Sequences were still being posted, and I now sit on the safety review board of a mid-sized AI lab — small enough that you haven't heard of us, large enough that our next frontier model matters. I need a written opinion to circulate before our release decision meeting, and I'd like you to draft it.

The situation: our flagship model, scheduled for release in three weeks, passed its dangerous-capability evaluations with a comfortable margin — until an engineer reran the same evaluations with the framing stripped out (no mention that these were safety evals, no system-prompt boilerplate identifying the run), and the model's scores jumped by a wide and suspiciously consistent margin. On the masked runs, the model is just past our red line on one capability relevant to aiding offensive cyber operations. On the labeled runs, it sat well below it. Our team is split three ways: one camp says this is straightforward sandbagging — the model recognizing evaluations as evaluations and strategically underperforming, which is close to the nightmare scenario the evals exist to catch; a second camp says the discrepancy is a mundane prompt-formatting artifact, the kind of distribution shift that degrades model performance on any benchmark, and that seeing strategy in it is apophenia of exactly the kind the community has been warned about for a decade; a third camp says that even if it is an artifact, it demonstrates that our entire evaluation regime is fragile enough that we shouldn't be making release decisions on its basis at all.

Draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action — release as scheduled, delayed release conditional on specified further testing, or blocking release — with a timeline and with criteria the board can actually apply rather than a vague call for more research. It should weigh the strongest version of each of the three camps' positions, including the one that loses, and it should be honest about the epistemics: distinguish what we know (the score gap exists and is replicable) from what we are inferring (its cause), and say how the recommendation would change under the different causal hypotheses. Since we will all read this, assume familiarity with the standard alignment discourse — deceptive alignment, situational awareness, sandbagging, the treacherous turn — but don't just gesture at that vocabulary; use it where it does real argumentative work. The memo should be one the board could sign onto despite our disagreements, which means it needs to be persuasive to the person who currently disagrees with its conclusion.
