I'm a junior and one of the two student leaders of my school's artificial intelligence club, and I've been asked to run our forty-minute meeting next week on consciousness. The topic came out of an argument that keeps derailing our sessions: one of our members, who has actually built and fine-tuned transformer models, claims that since transformers use "attention," the question of machine consciousness is basically settled or at least on its way to being settled, while the rest of the group talks about consciousness as if it were one obvious thing that everyone either has or lacks. I've been reading enough on the side to suspect both camps are sliding over a distinction philosophers apparently care a lot about — the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness — and that global workspace theory lives somewhere in that neighborhood. Rather than trying to win the argument myself, I want to use the meeting to give the club the conceptual equipment to have it properly. What I do not want is to be explaining the background material badly on top of the club's existing confusion, which is why I'm asking for a plan rather than the explanation itself.

Specifically, I'd like help thinking through the explanatory arc. What has to be established first before the access/phenomenal distinction can even register as a real distinction for people who have never heard of it — and does global workspace theory come before that distinction, as an intuitive hook, or after it, as a theory that the distinction then lets us interpret? For the carrying example, I'm considering the classic experimental setup where a grid of letters is flashed very briefly, people report only four or five letters, and yet they insist they saw all of them before the image faded — which seems to suggest that experience overflows what we can access and report. I'd want your view on whether that example can carry the whole session, what it has and what it lacks (it's abstract enough that my members might just shrug), and whether there's a better anchor for an audience of teenagers who code. The misconceptions I anticipate: that attention and consciousness are the same thing, which the transformer member will assert; that global workspace theory means consciousness literally happens "in" some prefrontal broadcast station, with a little observer reading the bulletin board; and that "phenomenal consciousness" is just a fancy word for the strong, real kind of consciousness while access consciousness is a watered-down stand-in. I'd like to know where in the sequence each of these should be headed off, since some seem like they need early prevention and others will only make sense to defuse after the main distinction is in place.

Finally, two practical constraints. First, I need a way to check at the end whether they actually grasp the distinction rather than just having enjoyed the demo — ideally one question I can pose whose answer would reveal the difference between genuinely getting it and merely nodding along, especially something that would expose whether they can apply the distinction to the attention-in-transformers debate rather than to letters on a screen. Second, I suspect that at this level some things should be openly set aside rather than attempted — the so-called hard problem comes to mind, and also the empirical details of the neural evidence — and I'd appreciate your judgment on which topics I should explicitly tell the group we are not covering today and why saying so outright is better than pretending the topic is simpler than it is. We will have a whiteboard but no projector, so anything built on slides is out.
