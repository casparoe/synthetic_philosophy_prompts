I cover physics and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been drafting a long feature on how quantum experiments are actually described by the people who run them. Something keeps snagging me that I'd like your help thinking through. When an experimentalist reports preparing, say, a single-photon polarization state thousands of times in a row and then "verifying" it by state tomography, the abstract reads as though a stable physical property were being checked, the way a mint checks that every coin blank weighs the same. But the verification is destructive: each individual system is measured once, in one basis, and the state is reconstructed statistically from the pattern of outcomes across the whole ensemble of supposedly identical runs. So the practice simultaneously leans on there being a real, repeatable property carried by each individual system — and on a procedure that only ever touches the aggregate. Philosophers apparently split on whether the quantum state is a real physical thing out there (they call this the psi-ontic view) or a bookkeeping device that summarizes an agent's information about a system (psi-epistemic), and I want to know what the lab's way of talking quietly commits to.

There are a couple of cracks in the practice that make me suspect it's not neutral between the views. First, experimenters shift register depending on whether things are going well: a paper's abstract says a state was "prepared and verified with 99.9% fidelity," psi-ontic language if I understand the term; but when the same physicist is troubleshooting, she says the run had "bad state preparation" or a "contaminated ensemble" — language that treats the formal state as a summary of what we know, which drifted when drift was present in the hardware. Second, and this is the part I find sharpest: the formalism uses the very same density matrix for two situations the practice describes completely differently. When we say a photon is in a mixture because we simply don't know whether it's horizontally or vertically polarized, that's plainly ignorance — there is a fact we lack. But when a subsystem is entangled with its environment and we trace the environment out, we assign it the same kind of density matrix, even though on most psi-ontic views there is no fact of the matter about its pure state to be ignorant of. The practice never marks that distinction; it uses one tool for both and moves on.

So here is what I'd like from you: reconstruct the implicit view of the quantum state that the tomography-and-fidelity practice presupposes, if any. Does the statistical, many-copies character of verification fit better with an epistemic reading, or does the demand that all those runs be "identically prepared" presuppose that each individual system carries the state as a property? Does the practice's refusal to distinguish the two kinds of mixture quietly rule out one of the camps, or is it genuinely underdetermined, with both a psi-ontic and a psi-epistemic story able to license it as-is? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and then tell me concretely what would change — in how papers are worded, in what "certifying" a quantum processor could even mean, in whether tomography would be rebranded as something like calibration of expectations rather than measurement of a property — if a major lab adopted an explicitly psi-epistemic house style, or, for contrast, an explicitly Everettian one. I'd like to be able to explain to readers where the physics ends and the metaphysics, invisible but load-bearing, begins.
