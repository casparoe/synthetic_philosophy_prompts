Construct a thought experiment designed to separate three accounts of what makes pain bad for the person feeling it: (1) a felt-quality view, on which pain's badness is fixed by its sensory intensity, so that pain of a given intensity is equally bad for the sufferer no matter how they relate to it; (2) a desire-satisfaction view, on which relieving pain benefits the sufferer roughly in proportion to how strongly they want it gone; and (3) an aversive-component view, on which what is bad about pain is the distress and the urge to be rid of it — roughly the thing that is missing in pain asymbolia, where patients feel pain at full intensity and can rate it, yet report that it does not bother them.

The scenario should be built around a treatment decision that the felt-quality theorist and the desire theorist both count as a substantial benefit to the patient, while the aversive-component theorist counts it as a trivial one, on a par with granting a calm preference for a window seat. To engineer this you will need, within one realistic clinical paragraph, a dissociation between intensity and aversiveness, grounded in a documented condition such as pain asymbolia rather than in any drug with invented properties; and, combined with it, a patient preference for the pain's removal that is genuine and strong yet wholly free of distress — the patient wants the pain gone the way someone might want a scar removed while caring little about it. The verdicts must be generated by applying the three theories to the case, not built into the patient's psychology by fiat.

After the scenario, provide the walk-through: state what each view says about the case and why; identify which feature of the scenario is doing the work; explain what happens to the three-way split if that feature is removed, or if the preference is made even mildly aversive; and set out the strongest attack available to the theorist who comes out worst — in particular, whether they can argue that the calm preference, once spelled out carefully, turns out either not to be calm or not to be about the pain. The case is intended for a seminar on whether the badness of pain reduces to how pain feels, so a scenario that quietly stipulates its conclusions instead of deriving them from the theories fails the brief.
