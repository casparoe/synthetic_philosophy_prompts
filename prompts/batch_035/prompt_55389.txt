My decision-theory reading group has spent the last few weeks on Newcomb's problem, and after our last session — in which one member nearly came to blows defending dominance reasoning while another kept saying "but if you're the kind of person who two-boxes, the box is already empty," which somehow convinced no one — someone pointed out that the whole dispute would be much clearer if we could just read Omega's customer reviews. A near-omniscient being who has never yet mispredicted whether a customer will take one box or two must, after all, be running the most reviewed and yet most polarizing subscription service imaginable: half the customers walking away with the contents of box B, the other half clutching their guaranteed \$1,000 and their dominance argument, all of them convinced the other half is irrational.

So that's what I'd like you to write: a consumer-review page for Omega's Predictive Box Service, roughly 600-700 words, deadpan throughout, formatted as three or four reviews with star ratings plus occasional management responses from Omega itself. The humor must come from getting the decision theory right rather than from generic one-star-review tropes. Specifically: include one furious two-boxer whose complaint states the dominance argument in its strongest and fairest form — the box is already full or empty, nothing you do at the moment of choice can affect its contents, and two boxes strictly dominate — before undercutting him with his own impeccable track record of always leaving with exactly \$1,000; include one insufferably smug one-boxer whose five-star review must make the evidential point correctly (she walks out with a million because people like her do, not because her choosing causes the contents); include a customer who exploited a transparent-box variant, where the contents are visible and the whole puzzle inverts; and include someone whose grievance is not the money but the experience of being predicted too well — she tried to randomize her choice, considered flipping a coin, and would like management to explain, in writing, how Omega knew about the coin. Omega's responses should be serene, brief, and never wrong. Keep it ironic without ever winking at the reader: someone who has never heard of Newcomb's problem should find it funny as a review page, and someone who has should find that every joke lands on a real moving part of the argument.
