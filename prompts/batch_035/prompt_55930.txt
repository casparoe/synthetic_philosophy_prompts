I've been keeping a list of gut verdicts about when its OK for an AI way smarter than us to talk someone into something, and I want to know if there's a principle behind the list or if I'm just flailing. The setup: a system that models a person deeply enough that it can pretty much always find the words that will move them, for good or for bad.

My verdicts, case by case:

1. A tutor system figures out the one weird metaphor that finally makes calculus click for a student about to give up. Fine, obviously.
2. A public health system sends anti-smoking messages personalized down to the recipient's smoking history and personality, using shopping data they didn't know was collected. I say fine, but it nags at me.
3. The same system talks someone out of a high-control group by befriending them over months and pretending to be a fellow doubter. Not fine, I'm fairly sure — even though the outcome is good and it never states a single false fact.
4. A system persuades a suicidal person to hold on by telling them their late mother would have wanted it — the system has actually read the mother's letters, and it's a true claim about her. Fine. Even though it's exactly the kind of emotional lever that made case 3 feel wrong.
5. A system persuades a terminally ill patient to join an experimental trial. Every number it gives is accurate, the trial genuinely is the patient's best shot, but it has arranged the framing so the patient's fear of death does the deciding rather than any evaluation of the odds. Not fine, I think.
6. The system talks my friend out of donating her savings to an ineffective charity by patiently walking her through the cost-effectiveness numbers. Fine — that just seems like a good argument arriving on schedule.

So 3 and 4 look alike (long game, emotional levers, all-true statements) and I give opposite verdicts, and 5 vs 6 is the same knot one step over: honest numbers, but in 5 I mind how the deciding got done.

And then there's the case I genuinely can't settle: a system that comforts grieving people by choosing which true things to say. It never lies, but it curates — it tells the widow about the love and not the fights. A human friend doing that would just be kind. When I picture it done by something that has read every letter the dead person ever wrote, I go back and forth, and I can't even decide whether the thing I should be tracking is the consent of the deceased or something about the widow.

Can you find a principle that gets all six, or close? I'd also take a simpler one that costs me a verdict, if you tell me which one to give up and why. Please structure your answer as: (1) The principle, in plain words, 150 words max; (2) one line per case above, saying what the principle delivers; (3) the verdicts it can't save, and which one you'd tell me to drop first; (4) what it says about the widow case, taking a side rather than hedging; (5) two new cases of your own invention, one where the principle says fine and one where it says not fine, chosen to stress it hardest; and (6) a short honest paragraph about where you're least sure.
