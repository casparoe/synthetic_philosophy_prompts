I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns the measurability critique of effective altruism: the complaint that EA systematically favors interventions whose effects can be measured (deworming pills, bed nets) over structural or institutional change whose effects cannot, because only the former fit into cost-effectiveness estimates and randomized trials. The debate has stalled. Critics say this is a bias built into EA's method; defenders reply that preferring better evidence is not bias but epistemic responsibility, and that "unmeasurable" does not mean "better." I suspect both sides are conflating two different comparisons. The first is a comparison within a set of similarly well-measured interventions, where ranking by estimated effect seems fine. The second is a comparison across causes, where one side's estimates are far more precise than the other's; there, treating both point estimates as if they carried the same evidential weight does something else — it lets the precision of a measurement masquerade as a property of the intervention. My working name for this is "precision leakage." I also suspect a second, compounding distortion: even within well-measured causes, picking the top-ranked intervention out of many noisy estimates is a version of the winner's curse — the winners are partly the interventions whose measurement error happened to point upward. If this is right, the measurability critique stops being a vague cultural complaint about EA and becomes a claim about comparison procedures that can be stated precisely and, in principle, corrected, for example by shrinkage or precision-weighted allocation across causes.
