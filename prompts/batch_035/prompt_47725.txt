I'm a PhD student working on a dissertation at the intersection of philosophy of mathematics and epistemology, and my committee is split in a way that has made my prospectus meeting feel like a reenactment of a fifty-year-old standoff. On one side is my advisor, a fairly relaxed platonist who takes Benacerraf's 1973 dilemma -- the tension between a uniform, Tarski-style semantics for mathematical language, which seems to commit us to abstract objects, and a causal theory of knowledge on which we could never know about such objects -- to have been dissolved long ago: the causal constraint was always an artifact of a particular 1970s epistemology, and once we know about universals, unobservable physical magnitudes, or the past the same way, mathematical knowledge stops looking special. On the other side is a committee member sympathetic to Hartry Field's fictionalism, who thinks dropping the causal condition only hides the problem: the real challenge is explaining the reliability of our mathematical belief-forming methods when those methods never interact with what (if anything) the beliefs are about, and on this reading every platonist response -- full-blooded platonism, indispensability plus inference to the best explanation, the claim that mathematical entities figure indispensably in scientific explanations and so earn their epistemic keep -- is a new coat of paint on an unsolved problem.

What strikes me is that neither of them, nor the literature I've read around this (the easy-road versus hard-road responses to indispensability, the debate over whether the Fieldian challenge generalizes to any domain where we lack causal contact), has moved the other in any way I can detect. Everyone agrees on the mathematics, everyone agrees on the phenomenology of mathematical practice, everyone even agrees that we can't causally interact with numbers -- and yet nothing happens. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that this is not really a disagreement about mathematics at all: it is a disagreement about what epistemic access requires, and more specifically about whether the reliability of a belief-forming method needs an explanation that cites the objects the beliefs are about, or whether that demand -- which we plainly don't enforce for ordinary perceptual beliefs -- is being selectively weaponized against the platonist. But I'm also drawn to a deflationary reading on which the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one side is doing epistemology of mathematics, the other is doing something closer to a general theory of reference and truth, and the dilemma only looks like a single problem because Benacerraf's paper happened to put them on the same page.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on platonism versus nominalism and not a survey of the positions. Is the impasse substantive or verbal? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question -- about, say, what a theory of reliability must contain -- that is doing the real work? Are the sides disagreeing about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data (is the practice of working mathematicians evidence, or just the explanandum?), or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for without circularity? Is the persistence of the debate a sign that the original dilemma was ill-posed -- the causal condition a crude stand-in for a constraint no one has ever managed to state properly -- or just a sign that it is genuinely hard? And given your diagnosis, tell me concretely what would count as progress: a sharper formulation of the epistemic challenge, a distinction that splits the question, a formal account of acausal reliability, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. My advisor and committee member each have a shelf of moves they've been running since the 1990s, so I'd especially like to know whether the diagnosis you give would give either of them a reason to move, or whether I should expect the same meeting again next year.
