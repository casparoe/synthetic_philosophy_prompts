Before I dive into the secession literature I want the spec first, not another defense of one theory. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of secession and self-determination has to actually deliver. I'm thinking it has to give a determinate verdict on cases like Scotland 2014 (Edinburgh and London agreed on the referendum), Catalonia 2017 (no such agreement, referendum suppressed), and Quebec after the 1995 near-miss with its contested question wording -- plus at least one case where secession is clearly impermissable, like annexation by force dressed up as self-determination. It has to respect distinctions people tend to collapse, like between a group's being a "people" at all and what they're owed once they are one, or between internal self-determination (autonomy inside the state) and external (your own state). And it can't flout what I'd call stability constraints: it can't make every border infinitely contestable or hand every discontented minority a veto over every state, or the UN would have five thousand members. For each requirement say what case or consideration puts it on the list, mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the preferred emphasis of one camp, and flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable -- that tension is the part I care about most, since a list that can't all be satisfied at once is itself the interesting result. Then briefly, which single requirement does the most work of excluding the main contenders (pure plebiscite theories, remedial-right-only theories, nationalist views), and don't bother scoring them all, I'll do that later. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullets or tables -- like an email, I'll be reading it on my phone.
