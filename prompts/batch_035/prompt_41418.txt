I want to stress-test a classical Chinese debate against some modern developmental psychology, and I need an interlocutor with genuine backbone rather than a strawman. Please play Xunzi — the third-century-BCE Confucian who held that human nature is bad, that our raw dispositions lead to conflict and greed if left to themselves, and that goodness exists only through conscious activity, ritual, and the shaping force of teachers and exemplars, the way a potter shapes clay into a vessel rather than waiting for the clay to shape itself. Speak in the first person as Xunzi; restrict yourself to his conceptual repertoire and pre-Qin ways of thinking, though you may ask me factual questions about the modern findings if you need them answered, phrased as Xunzi would phrase them. You may concede small points when pressed, but never the core claim that moral goodness is achieved rather than given — and do not soften your position merely to be agreeable.

Now the findings I want you to reckon with. Developmental psychologists have found that children of roughly a year and a half — far too young for explicit moral instruction — will spontaneously help an unfamiliar adult in mild distress: retrieving objects the adult cannot reach, opening doors or barriers for them, and so on, without any request, reward, or praise. Something similar, though more rudimentary, has been observed in young chimpanzees. What is more, later experiments found that if toddlers are given material rewards for helping, they afterwards help less than children who were never rewarded — as though the reward crowded out something that had been operating on its own.

I am playing the Mencian in this exchange, and here is my opening move: these children display the sprout of compassion and of approving and disapproving long before ritual or teaching could have planted anything. The behavior is spontaneous, unrewarded, and appears at an age when your program of habituation and correction has not yet begun; it even flickers in beasts. Your entire system depends on goodness entering from outside — the potter, not the clay — yet the clay reaches for the dropped object all by itself. And your system cannot even appeal to reward, since reward, we now know, makes the sprout wither. Stay in character and give me your best reply.
