I'm taking a final-year seminar on theories of distributive justice, and we have each been asked to submit a short position statement to be workshopped in class. Before I submit mine, I would like to know whether it actually hangs together. The position is this:

(1) The levelling-down objection is fatal to telic egalitarianism: a change that makes some people worse off and no one better off cannot make the situation better in any respect, so equality is not intrinsically good.

(2) I accept the strong Pareto principle: whenever one person is better off and no one is worse off, the outcome is better.

(3) I am a prioritarian: the moral weight of a benefit depends only on how well off its recipient is — on their absolute level, not on their position relative to others — with benefits to the worse off counting for more. I also hold that the overall value of an outcome is exhausted by these weighted benefits.

(4) I nonetheless retain a residual fairness judgement: there is something bad, over and above how badly off they are, about someone's being far below others through no fault of their own — bad even when nothing can be done about it, as with someone struck down by illness in an otherwise flourishing society. I think of this as bad luck or unfairness rather than inequality, which is why I do not take (1) to touch it.

(5) In our seminar's calibration case, I judged that a two-person society in which each person is at 50 is in one respect better than one in which one person is at 50 and the other at 90, because of what it means for the first person to be so far behind. I am fairly confident in that verdict.

(6) A classmate objects that prioritarianism has a levelling-down problem of its own: with steep enough weights it can prefer a world in which nearly everyone is much worse off, so long as the worst-off person is slightly better off. My instinct is that this is not genuine levelling down, because someone really does gain, but I am not sure I can defend that.

My tutor has said that the statement sits uneasily with itself but would not say where. So: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look as though they are, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating every pair as equally bad, and tell me concretely which commitment has to give and what I lose by giving it up.
