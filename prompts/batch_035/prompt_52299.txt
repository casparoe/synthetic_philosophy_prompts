I teach ethics and public policy, and I'm drafting a short position statement on the ethics of discounting for a workshop on intergenerational justice. Before I circulate it, I want an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Here they are, numbered:

(1) Pure time preference is irrational, both within a single life and across generations: the mere fact that a benefit falls later, or a burden earlier, gives it no additional or reduced weight.
(2) It is nonetheless permissible to attach somewhat less weight to the concerns of one's own distant future self, on the ground that psychological connectedness declines over time — this is discounting by relation, not by date.
(3) Nothing of the sort is available for far-future strangers: their temporal distance from us licenses no discounting of their interests, so the near-zero pure-time-preference approach to climate cost-benefit analysis is correct in principle.
(4) A positive consumption discount rate in official cost-benefit analysis is nonetheless defensible, since future people are expected to be materially better off and a marginal unit of consumption will do them less good — and I take this to be fully consistent with (1) and (3).
(5) Where there is a real probability that the recipients will never exist — extinction risk above all — discounting the far future is legitimate, and this is not time preference in disguise.
(6) The order in which goods and evils arrive within a life matters to how well that life goes: an improving life is better than a declining one, other things being equal. I regard this as a claim about the shape of a life rather than a temporal preference, so I do not think (1) is threatened.

Please tell me which pairs or larger subsets of these commitments are in genuine tension and how badly; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection; and what the cheapest single revision would be, together with what adopting it would cost me. Present the audit as a JSON object with the following schema: a field "tensions" containing an array of objects, each with the fields "commitments" (the numbers involved), "severity" ("mild", "moderate", or "serious"), "diagnosis" (two or three sentences), and "cheapest_fix" (what would have to give, and at what cost); a field "apparent_only" containing an array of objects, each with the fields "commitments" and "why_it_dissolves"; and a top-level field "verdict" (one or two sentences identifying the single most productive revision and why). Order the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and do not soften a tension merely because the commitment in question is widely held.
