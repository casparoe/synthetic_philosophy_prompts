Write an essay on the backward induction paradox in the centipede game, and defend a verdict about what the paradox shows about the idealized conception of rationality that generates it.

Begin by laying out the game and the induction argument in your own terms: a finite alternating-move game with a growing pot, in which each player may at each turn take the larger share or pass, and in which backward induction from the final node implies that a rational pair ends the game at the very first move — even though both players would come away richer had they passed along. Then take up the paradox proper: the induction requires beliefs about what a rational player would do at nodes that, if all the players are rational, are never reached, and it is far from obvious that those counterfactuals are coherent. Consider whether the best response is (a) Aumann's, that common knowledge of rationality, properly understood, entails the backward-induction outcome and the appearance of paradox comes from conflating what players know on and off the equilibrium path; (b) a revisionist one, on which rationality at unreached nodes is idle or ill-defined, so that rational play can sustain cooperation for a stretch; or (c) a deflationary one, on which the paradox is mostly a staging ground for unresolved worries about counterfactuals and common knowledge that working game theory can route around.

Weigh two further considerations. First, the experimental record: McKelvey and Palfrey's centipede experiments found that subjects typically pass at early nodes and take in the middle rounds, with play drifting toward the induction prediction only as payoffs were scaled up. Say what this pattern is evidence of — bounded rationality, strategic uncertainty about the other player, or a genuine counterexample to backward induction as a description of what even ideal agents would do. Second, the following line of argument, which I want taken seriously even if you end up rejecting it: in a centipede where mutual passing to the end leaves each player only marginally better off than defection at the second-to-last node, a rational player can reason that even a very small probability of facing a cooperative type makes passing optimal, and common knowledge of *that* reasoning may itself suffice to sustain early passing — so the induction conclusion may depend on an unexamined assumption that rational agents can never rationally accept strategic risk about each other's type. Assess whether this dissolves the paradox, merely relocates it to the question of how probabilities of types enter the common knowledge base, or conflates rationality with something else entirely.

Reach a definite conclusion: is the backward-induction outcome a theorem about ideal rationality, an artifact of idealizations that ideal agents themselves could not coherently act on, or neither of these?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Somewhere in the essay, identify the point at which your reasoning is most vulnerable — which argument, if repaired or answered more forcefully, would move you to a different verdict.
