Okay so next month I'm giving a talk at a joint departmental colloquim — roughly half analytic metaethicists, a couple of historians of Victorian science, and one grumpy Kantian who thinks history of philosophy is a waste of everyone's time — defending this thesis: Sidgwick's whole involvement with the Society for Psychical Research (the presidency, the telepathy experiments, sitting with Leonora Piper, the Census of Hallucinations, all of it) wasn't a Victorian eccentricity bolted onto a serious philosopher, and it wasn't just guilt-free fun either. It was the Methods of Ethics continued by other means. The dualism of practical reason — the place in book three-ish where he concludes that if egoism and utilitarianism can conflict, and nothing in the universe guarantees they won't, then his whole system has a hole in the middle of it — was for him not the end of an argument but the start of an empirical research program. If you could find out whether consciousness survives death, whether there's any cosmic order that rewards sacrifice, the hole either gets patched or the system definitively collapses. So the seances were applied normative philosophy, not a hobby.

Either the standards were right and the whole thing was for nothing, or the standards were a performance he got to feel good inside. I don't have an answer to that and it scares me.
