Last night I argued until well past midnight with my closest friend, who designs choice architecture for a government behavioral science unit, about whether the nudges she builds are manipulative. I ended up defending a sharper position than I had planned to take: that an influence attempt is manipulative when it operates by exploiting a vulnerability in the target's decision-making rather than by giving her considerations she could weigh as reasons; that bias-harnessing nudges — defaults, framing, salience manipulation — therefore count as manipulation even when fully disclosed and aimed at the target's own good; and that this doesn't settle anything against them, since manipulation can sometimes be permissible, but we should call it what it is. She pushed back that transparency dissolves the charge, that manipulation requires either deception or acting against the target's interests, and that my criterion would condemn ordinary teaching, good advertising, and even a well-placed word from a friend.

In three weeks I present this position to her team: a dozen behavioral scientists and policy staff who are proud of their transparency standards, plus a legal scholar who thinks "manipulation" should remain a term of art for deception-plus-exploitation and dislikes philosophers stretching it. Prepare my objection-prep sheet: the eight questions I'm most likely to face, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased in the vocabulary that room actually uses (choice architecture, System 1, informed choice, autonomy, consumer protection), each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should concede up front rather than fight, and identify the single question that would do the most damage if I flub it under pressure.
