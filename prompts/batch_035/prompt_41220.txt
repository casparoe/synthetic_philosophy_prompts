I help run a small book-and-film club that this winter read only tragedies, and every year the same row breaks out about why on earth we do it. I'd like to settle it by finding out what each member actually believes rather than which label they like, so please write me a ten-question sorting quiz.

Each question should be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased in plain language so a member with no philosophy background can answer it without knowing where it leads. These cases must appear somewhere among the questions: (a) a pill that removes the sadness of watching a tragedy while leaving you fully able to follow and remember the story, and whether you'd take it; (b) a film so clumsy and manipulative that it still reliably makes you cry, and whether you'd rewatch it; (c) a documentary about a real disaster that you know will affect you exactly as a fiction would; (d) a person whose own life closely mirrors the tragedy, watching it for the first time; (e) choosing between a guaranteed good cry and a pleasant, undisturbed evening.

The quiz should sort members into positions on why we enjoy sad art — roughly: that the sadness is simply traded off against something else and we value the whole package; that it's the safety and unreality of art that makes the sadness enjoyable at all; that what we enjoy is an emotion about the emotion, i.e. being moved as such; that we enjoy discharging or working through the sadness, so it pays off afterwards; and the deflationary view that there's nothing much here in need of explanation. Finish with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto those positions, flag any pair of positions the quiz can't genuinely separate, and note any answer pattern no one is likely to hold — with a sentence on whether you think that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
