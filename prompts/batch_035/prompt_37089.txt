I manage collections care at a maritime museum, and my work keeps forcing a distinction on me that I cannot find a name for. It concerns the conditions under which a thing counts as the same thing after its parts have been replaced — but not, I think, the usual version of that question. What I keep noticing is that those conditions seem to differ systematically by category of thing, and to be settled in practice by communities of people rather than by anything in the objects themselves.

Concretely: when we plan a major restoration, the curatorial team debates what proportion of original fabric a vessel must retain to be exhibited under its historic name rather than labeled a reconstruction, and the number we can live with differs between a wooden hull, a riveted iron hull, and a small craft where the lines matter more to us than the material. Meanwhile, the volunteer fire brigade in my town still refers to its personnel carrier as the 1972 engine even though nothing of 1972 remains but the registration plate, and nobody considers that fraudulent; but if I stood a newly built boat next to a two-hundred-year-old hull and labeled the new one as the original on the grounds that it carried on the name, I would be dismissed, and rightly. Bands keep their names after every founding member has left; restaurants survive changes of owner, chef, building, and cuisine, with the regulars carrying the continuity; yet a painting restored beyond a certain point is quietly reclassified. The same physical facts — total or near-total replacement of matter, continuity of name — are treated differently depending on what kind of thing the object is and on who is doing the treating. And in the clearest case, the law, this administration is fully explicit: a corporation persists through total turnover of its members, and the statute simply says so.

So what I am after is a concept for this layer: the category-relative, community-administered persistence rules that sit above whatever the metaphysical facts turn out to be. I know the neighborhood is the Ship of Theseus, but as I understand it, that debate is about which of two material continuities wins — the gradually repaired body or the reassembly of the discarded planks — and my question is prior to it: what settles which continuity conditions a thing is subject to in the first place, why those conditions vary by category, and why it falls to a community to apply and occasionally revise them. A friend with a philosophy degree muttered the word sortals at me when I described this, but could not say whether the fit is exact, and I have gathered that some philosophers hold the sterner view that any replacement of a part destroys the thing — which is plainly not what museums, fire brigades, or band names are doing. There is also a further feature I would want the concept to capture: the disputes feel substantive to us — labels, grants, and careers turn on them — even though I suspect the underlying rules are conventions we could have set differently. I would like to know whether that appearance of substance is an illusion, or whether a convention can still be applied correctly or incorrectly in a way that makes disagreements about it genuine rather than verbal.

Is there an established concept, term, or body of work for identity conditions of this administered, category-relative sort? If there is, please relate it carefully to the nearest neighbors — sortals, the classical Ship of Theseus problem, and the coarse idea that some things are socially constructed — and say where the fit is loose. If nothing established covers it, coin a term, define it, and trace its boundary cases: does it include nations and languages, or only entities like vessels, bands, and corporations, where the administering community has a recognizable membership? And at what point does the administering community lose its authority — when everyone who cared has died, or before that? A bare label will not help me; what I need is a map of the phenomenon's edges, so I can tell which of our museum's disputes are substantive disagreements about the object and which are merely confusion about which convention we are applying.
