My study partner, a CS PhD student who has lately been reading about what happens when game players can read each other's source code before moving, has an argument that concludes something I think proves far too much, and I can't locate the fault. The game is a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with the usual structure: mutual cooperation beats mutual defection for both players, but defecting is better than cooperating no matter what the other player does. His argument:

(1) A choice is rational only if it maximizes expected payoff given everything the chooser knows.
(2) Suppose you can read my source code before choosing. Then you can determine what I will do in this game — not predict with high confidence, but know in advance, the way you know what a multiplication comes out to before anyone works it out.
(3) A fact you know in advance and cannot affect is, for purposes of your choice, part of the fixed background, like the weather on game day. If my code says I will cooperate, your best move against that background is to defect; if it says I will defect, your best move is still to defect. Either way, defect.
(4) The reasoning is fully general: whenever rational players can read each other's code, transparency changes nothing and they are stuck at mutual defection, exactly as they would be without it.
(5) But consider a pair of agents each of which cooperates exactly when it can prove that the other cooperates with it. Facing each other under transparency, each can find a proof that both cooperate — so both do, and they split the cooperative payoff.
(6) So there are agents that demonstrably extract value from transparency, in the same game where the agents of steps (3)-(4) cannot, and they come out ahead of those agents.
(7) So the argument of steps (1)-(4) proves too much, and one of them is false.
(8) His diagnosis: (1) is false. Rationality is never a property of actions at all, only of programs; asking what it is rational to *do* is a category mistake.

I'm suspicious of the move from (2) to (3) — my move's being knowable in advance doesn't seem to make it fixed the way the weather is, since the code you are reading is itself busy modeling you — but every attempt I make to sharpen that objection seems to break (5) as well, because those proof-checking cooperators also treat each other's moves as knowable facts about the world. And (8) strikes me as proving too much in the opposite direction: if only programs can be rational and no agent can choose its program, then deliberation is impossible and nothing anyone ever does is irrational. So tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs.
