I'm a retired physicist -- about thirty-five years at a national metrology institute, most of them on atomic clock work, caesium fountains and later optical standards, so I've spent literally my whole career making sure clocks agree with each other. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, mostly for something to do in the mornings, and I've just worked through the Meno. The part that stuck with me is the question of why knowledge should be any better than a true belief that happens to be right -- Socrates is oddly casual about it, saying true beliefs are fine so long as they stay put, but worth less because they tend to wander off. That rang a bell for a reason I'll admit is a bit silly: at a family dinner a few weeks ago, my brother-in-law stated some fact about the shipping industry (I forget exactly what, port logistics stuff), and it turned out he was right, everyone later confirmed it -- but my nephew waved it off with the old line that even a broken clock is right twice a day, and everyone nodded like that settled things. Nobody was disputing that he was correct. They were disputing... something else. And when I tried to say what exactly, I found I couldn't. Now I'm wondering if the slogan and the Meno puzzle are secretly the same problem, and it's bugging me enough to ask.

So take the slogan apart for me. What are the distinct precise claims "even a broken clock is right twice a day" could be making? I can see it's doing more than one job -- there's the boring literal one (a stopped analog display does show the correct time twice a day, pure arithmetic), there's something statistical (any procedure that isn't wrong literally all the time will be right sometimes, so rightness on one occasion is weak evidence of a good source), there's something about reliability (the clock isn't a measuring instrument you'd trust going forward, and I have strong opinions about what makes an instrument trustworthy, so I'd love that reading spelled out), and there's maybe something about credit or luck, like the person who trusts a broken clock doesn't *deserve* to be right. Lay out the full menu, from trivially true to substantive, and for each one say whether it's actually true and whether it would even support what my nephew used it for -- which, remember, was dismissing a claim that everyone agreed was true. If the honest answer is that no reading supports the dismissal, say that plainly, but also tell me which reading comes closest.

Then the part I actually care about: does any of this answer Socrates? When the Meno says a true belief is like something that runs off unless tied down, and my nephew says a broken clock is right twice a day, are these the same insight in two costumes -- namely that truth on one occasion is worth less than a stable, reliable connection to truth -- or are they different worries wearing similar clothes? My physicist's instinct is that it's all just reliability in the end, and that the philosophers' distinction between knowledge and lucky true belief will dissolve into measurement theory if you push on it. Tell me whether that instinct is right or whether there's a genuine residue that "this instrument is well-calibrated" simply cannot express. I'd rather be corrected now than at dinner.

(Also, minor thing: if you mention the Meno's statues or Daedalus, go easy on the Greek names -- I'm truely new to all this.)
