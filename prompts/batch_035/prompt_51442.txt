I run a reading group that's half philosophy of mind and half dusty old-school AI, and this semester we're doing concepts — what actually ARE they? The students come in thinking it's settled: bachelor = unmarried adult male, table = furniture with a flat top and legs, done, next topic. I want to knock that loose using letter-string analogies, because everyone has fast intuitions about them and the intuitions genuinely pull in different directions. The setup: if the string "abc" changes to "abd", what should "ijk" change to? Most people say "ijl" — replace the rightmost letter with its successor — but some people say "ijd", just swap in a d, and its actually interesting WHY each answer feels right to the person giving it. Then the nastier follow-ups: what about "iijjkk", where you can't even find the rightmost single letter without grouping? What about "kji", where reading right-to-left flips everything? And does the fact that "z" has no successor make "xyz" a special case, or does the alphabet wrap around, or neither?

Here's what I want you to make: a decision tree (taxonomy also fine, whatever you think works better) that takes a person from some starting commitment about these puzzles to a named position on what concepts are. The branch nodes should be concrete questions or cases the person can answer without having any idea which label they're headed toward — so nothing like "are you a prototype theorist?". Include these cases somewhere (feel free to add your own, and do the ijk one first since it's the entry point):

- the ijk case, and whether the right answer is determined or merely preferred — is there a fact of the matter or just a tug-of-war between pressures?
- concept combination: "pet fish" seems to point at goldfish even though neither "pet" nor "fish" alone points there — is that a problem for whatever view of concepts handles the letter strings fine, or a seperate problem entirely?
- the person who sincerely says elms are trees but can't distinguish elms from beechs at all, and still manages to talk about elms successfully — do they lack the concept ELM, or have it in some thinner sense?
- a membership case: when people decided whales are mammals that merely look like fish, were they discovering a fact about the concept WHALE or changing the concept?

Leaves must include at least: the classical view (necessary and sufficient conditions), prototype theory, theory-theory (concepts as little causal-explanatory theories), Fodor's atomism where concepts are basically mental words that get their content from somewhere else entirely, Hofstadter's picture of concepts as temporary packings-together of pressures inside a microworld (the thing the letter-string stuff was built to demonstrate — concepts fluidly slipping into each other rather than sitting there with definitions), and at least one deflationary "concepts" was never a natural kind leaf. If you think the space has other important residents, put them in.

Build the tree from the logical space first and the names second — figure out which questions actually carve the positions apart, then locate the real views in that space, including the combinations nobody occupies (and say whether you think nobody's there because the combo is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored). Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate with these cases — my hunch is prototype vs exemplar views end up in the same box no matter what you ask, but check that. Audience is upper-level undergrads who've done some logic but no phil of mind, so no unexplained jargon, but you don't need to baby them either. Aim for maybe 6-9 leaves with 5 to 8ish branch questions, whatever that takes.
