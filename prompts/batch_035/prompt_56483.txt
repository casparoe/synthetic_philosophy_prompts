Please describe the most important implications of risk-weighted expected utility theory for how a small charitable foundation should allocate its grants. For context, suppose the foundation makes only three or four large grants each year, drawn from an endowment that will not be replenished. In a given year it can choose between interventions of roughly the following character: Programme A reliably saves 400 lives for each million pounds spent, whereas Programme B saves 900 lives per million pounds with probability one half and saves no lives at all with probability one half. Classical expected utility theory, applied with a utility function that is roughly linear in lives saved, appears to recommend Programme B. Yet many trustees feel that, because the foundation makes so few grants, it cannot rely on favourable outcomes averaging out across many bets, and that this somehow counts in favour of Programme A without any trustee being irrational or having a steeply concave utility function. Please explain how risk-weighted expected utility theory bears on this situation: does it vindicate the trustees' instincts, and if so, on what grounds? In your answer, please also address whose risk attitudes are the relevant ones when an institution, rather than an individual, faces the gamble — the trustees', the beneficiaries', or those of a hypothetical person whose life chances depend on the grant — and whether the theory's implications change once the foundation grows and makes very many grants each year, such that the outcomes of individual funding decisions begin to aggregate.
