Three years ago a bout of COVID took most of my sense of smell, and it has only partly come back. I still write and speak about scents without obvious difficulty: I can tell you that cardamom is warm, that cut grass is sharp and green, that a wine is flinty — but my grounds for these claims are now entirely other people's words, remembered from when I could smell or picked up since. At the same time, my work has drifted toward evaluating language models, and I keep meeting the same shape in a different place. A model will produce a pitch-perfect description of the smell of rain on hot asphalt, yet its only connection to that smell, if connection is the word, is the statistics of text written by people who have stood in it. And there is a third case that unsettles me most: people blind from birth who use color vocabulary fluently and, as far as anyone can tell, meaningfully, without ever having had the experiences those words are supposed to latch onto.

Is there an established name for this configuration — full linguistic competence with, and apparent understanding of, terms whose referents one has never (or no longer) causally interacted with, where the competence is inherited entirely from a community of speakers who have? A colleague pointed me toward what is called the symbol grounding problem, but as I understand it that problem concerns whether meanings can be built out of symbol-to-symbol connections alone, whereas my cases involve people and systems embedded in a community that includes grounded speakers. The nearest thing I have found is the idea that meanings can be borrowed from experts, the way most of us use the word for a kind of tree by deferring to botanists — but I do not know whether that idea is meant to extend to someone who could never, even in principle, take up the deference themselves: the congenitally blind person can never see red, and the model, presumably, can never smell anything at all.

What I want is not a label but a carving. Does this configuration have a name of its own? If deference to a linguistic community is the closest concept, does it cover all three of my cases — the person who lost the sense, the person who never had it, and the system that never had anything like it — or do the differences between them matter in a way the concept doesn't capture? If nothing established fits, please define a concept that does, and mark its boundaries carefully: someone who could have the experience but hasn't yet; someone describing their own past experiences after memory has faded; a multimodal model trained on images as well as text, which has some causal link to its color words but not, presumably, a human-like one. Finally, tell me whether this family of cases is a genuine difficulty for the view that understanding requires grounding, or whether it shows that the grounding requirement was never doing the work its proponents supposed.
