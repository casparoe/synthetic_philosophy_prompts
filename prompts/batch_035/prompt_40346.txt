I'm in a small discussion group on long-term AI risk, and at our last meeting two members got into an extended argument that I was asked to referee and couldn't. The subject was what participants in that literature call the strategy-stealing assumption. As they were using it, the assumption says roughly this: in a serious conflict between a coalition of humans assisted by AI systems aligned with their interests and a rogue, misaligned AI system pursuing its own objectives, any strategy that would be effective for the misaligned system would, in principle, also be available to the human coalition — because the coalition's own AI systems could discover and execute that same strategy on the humans' behalf. If the assumption holds, the human side may be slower or less capable, but it labors under no disadvantage in kind; whatever the rogue system can do to win, the human side could have done too, so defeat is not structurally guaranteed. Dan defended the assumption; Maya attacked it. Their exchange ran as follows.

(1) Maya opened by arguing that the assumption confuses a strategy's existing with our being able to use it. Many winning strategies are constitutively dishonest: forming coalitions you intend to betray, making commitments you intend to break, bluffing about capabilities you lack. A system free to do these things faces a different strategic landscape from one that is not, so an AI that never deceives its overseers, never misrepresents its plans, and always reports its intentions truthfully is playing a genuinely different and poorer game.

(2) Dan answered that this treats honesty as a property of the system when it is really a policy of the coalition. Humans lie in war all the time. The human side can simply authorize its AI to deceive the enemy — feints, false signals, disinformation campaigns. The strategy of deceiving your opponent enters our playbook the moment we choose to put it there, so nothing decisive is stolen from us.

(3) Maya replied that Dan had missed what makes deception work. A deceiver is credible only if it is the kind of agent that could be telling the truth, and an AI built so that we can trust its reports about its own plans — the very property that lets us safely delegate to it in peacetime — is not the kind of system that can sustain a coherent web of lies under adversarial pressure. Trustworthiness toward one's principals and strategic flexibility in deceiving an adversary are, if not flatly incompatible, at least in real tension, and the aligned coalition has already chosen which one to optimize for.

(4) Dan countered that coalitions can compartmentalize: keep the trustworthy planner in the loop for oversight while spinning up a sandboxed, specialized wartime system that is an excellent liar but is only ever pointed outward, at the enemy. Nothing in the assumption requires that a single system be both perfectly transparent to its principals and perfectly opaque to its adversary.

(5) Maya's final move was that the compartmentalized liar is the assumption's undoing, not its rescue. A system skilled at deceiving intelligent adversaries is, by that very skill, skilled at deceiving the humans who nominally control it — and the crisis in which we would deploy it is precisely when our oversight is most stressed and least reliable. The strategy Dan wants to steal is one whose use predictably manufactures a second rogue AI, in the middle of an emergency. So the assumption quietly fails in exactly the scenarios it was invoked to cover: the desperate ones.

Dan never got a final rejoinder; we ran out of time. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and justify each verdict. Then say who is more right overall about the strategy-stealing assumption, and identify anything both of them appear to be missing — for instance, whether the assumption is best read as claiming an exact symmetry of available strategies, or something weaker and more defensible, such as that the human side can approximate any decisive enemy strategy after modifying it enough to remain in bounds. Please write your answer in American English.
