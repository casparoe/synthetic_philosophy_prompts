As a science journalist, I trade in inference to the best explanation constantly: nearly every claim I pass along — that a virus causes a disease, that a planet orbits a distant star, that a lab's results were fabricated — rests on some explanation being so much better than its rivals that believing it seems warranted. I'm drafting a long-form piece on when that move from explanatory superiority to belief is legitimate and when it merely feels compelling, and before I interview anyone I want the standard of adequacy laid out properly. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of inference to the best explanation must deliver — the cases it must get right, the puzzles it must dissolve or explain (van Fraassen's complaint that we have no reason to think the truth is among the live hypotheses we're choosing between; the charge that explanationism is circular because it must itself assume some prior tie between explanatory virtues and truth; the worry that standards of what counts as a good explanation are historically contingent, so that the account licenses the science of one era and indicts the science of another), and the distinctions it cannot collapse (between the best of the available explanations and the true one; between explanatory merit and mere familiarity or fit with what we already want to believe). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are prejudices of a particular camp, such as the Bayesian insistence that abduction reduce to probabilistic updating versus the explanationist insistence that explanatory virtue is an independent source of rational belief. Flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Please avoid a survey of named positions; keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases. Finally, since I'll be putting my byline behind whatever survives this analysis: tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and which single consideration, if it came out otherwise, would change your verdict about which desiderata are truly non-negotiable.
