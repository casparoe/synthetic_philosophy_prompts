Two friends from my rationality meetup got into it last week about the doomsday argument, and I got roped into refereeing, but honestly I couldnt call it. Here's roughly how the exchange went.

Dee started it. Say you number every human by birth order, and assume you're like a random draw from everyone who will ever live. Then being born around rank 110 billion or so is pretty unsurprising if humanity is nearly over, and kind of surprising if trillions of humans are still to come. So just from your birth rank, you should shift your credence toward doom arriving sooner -- maybe within a few centuries. Nothing mystical, she insisted, just ordinary conditioning on a piece of evidence.

Rob said that's absurd: no amount of armchair reasoning can tell you anything about asteroid strikes, pandemics, or fertility rates. And it proves too much -- someone running the same argument in 40,000 BC would have concluded humanity was about to end, and they'd have been catastrophically wrong, so the reasoning must be broken somewhere.

Dee said not necessarily. Whoever ran it back then just got unlucky, the way a poll can miss even when it's conducted properly -- a small chance of misleading evidence doesn't make something stop being evidence. And she offered an analogy: imagine a machine picks a number N between 1 and some huge maximum, prints that many tickets numbered 1 through N, and mails you one without telling you N. If your ticket says 7, you should think N is small. Your birth rank, she said, is like the ticket number.

Rob said the analogy cheats. In the lottery, the setup guarantees your ticket is a random draw from the batch. There's no analogous process for births -- you couldn't have been born in the 500th century, you're the child of your actual parents, with your actual genes and history. So there's no legitimate 'random placement' to condition on, and the whole thing is a typo away from collapse.

Dee answered that Rob is misdescribing what the conditioning requires. It's not that you could have metaphysically been someone else; it's just that your evidence -- I observe myself at an early rank -- is more probable under the hypothesis that few humans ever live than under the hypothesis that many do, and that comparative fact is all the updating needs. She compared it to a scientist sampling fish from a lake: nobody thinks the scientist had to be able to be a different fish for the sample to be informative.

Then Rob pivoted to the reference class. Why number humans at all? Why not all mammals, or all sentient creatures, past and future, across the whole universe? If animal minds or alien civilizations eventually vastly outnumber us, then almost any class that includes them puts your rank at the very beginning, not just early among humans. Pick the wide class and the argument evaporates; pick the narrow one and doom comes back. There's no principled way to choose, so the conclusion is an artifact of the choice.

Dee's last move was to bite that bullet. She conceded the reference class question is genuinely hard, but said every theory of induction has some version of this problem -- it's like the grue predicament, awkward but not fatal. And she said her core point survives in any class you pick: you should end up believing the class contains somewhat fewer members than you would have otherwise expected.

Rob's closer: 'somewhat fewer than otherwise expected' is a massive retreat from 'humanity probably ends in a few centuries,' and if that's all the argument delivers, it's hard to see it as the dramatic result its fans advertise.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each point -- not just who wins overall? I'd also like to know whether there's anything both of them are missing that would change the picture. Please answer in American English.
