Construct a thought experiment in which, applying Aquinas's own principles, a private subject may licitly bring about a tyrant's death while the same death would be illicit for the tyrant's own senior officials — the reverse of the usual direction, in which public standing widens the permission to kill. Constraints: the scenario itself is one paragraph, set in a plausible late-medieval Italian city under a signore, with no miracles, no perfect information, and no foreknowledge of how anyone will react; and the reversal must not be engineered by making the officials secretly wicked or the citizen unusually saintly. It should fall out of the structural commitments of the treatment of killing in the Secunda Secundae (question 64, especially article 7 on self-defence, where the attacker's death is licit only insofar as it is unintended, and article 3, which reserves intentional killing to legitimate public authority executing judgment), together with the counsel of On Kingship that private tyrannicide endangers the commonwealth, that plotters typically seek power for themselves, and that a remedy, where one exists, belongs rather to the multitude that holds the right of appointment. The case is for testing whether Aquinas's permission structure tracks intention and legitimate standing rather than the bare public/private label, so the scenario must generate its verdicts from the doctrines rather than stipulate them. After the scenario, walk through what each of these doctrinal tools delivers, identify which feature of the case is doing the work, say what happens to the reversal if that feature is removed, and give the strongest pushback a Thomist could make to the charge that the case is rigged rather than derived. Paraphrase Aquinas rather than quoting, and refer to the texts only by the standard references given above.
