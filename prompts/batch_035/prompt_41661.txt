I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophy of translation, in which we have been reading Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language (1997), the book he built around Clément Marot's little get-well poem, A une Damoyselle malade — a note to a sick young friend urging her to rouse herself, drink, eat, and soon be well, set in twenty-odd very short lines, most of them only about three syllables, rhymed in couplets. Hofstadter, who refers to the poem as Ma mignonne, filled the book with the many translations of it that he wrote or collected, because he takes it to show that in a poem this small and this tightly patterned, the pattern is not wrapping paper around a message; it is a large part of the message itself.

Through my seminar instructor I hold the student seat on the editorial board of our department's new anthology of French Renaissance poetry in English translation, which will be the required text for a survey course in which none of the students reads French. At our meeting in two weeks the board must choose between two candidate translations of this very poem. The first, by a senior scholar of Marot's century, renders the sense with scrupulous accuracy but writes in free verse of ordinary line lengths, printing each rhyming couplet of the original as a single line, so that his version has roughly half as many lines as the French; read aloud, it comes off as a short speech rather than a chime. The second, by a formalist poet, keeps the tiny rhymed lines and the skipping, breathless pace, but in two places she substitutes an endearment that appears in neither the French nor Marot's century, and she adds a rhyme-driven pun that is hers, not his. A third board member, a medievalist, wants the poem dropped from the anthology altogether; she cites the reviewer who, when Hofstadter's book appeared, complained that Marot's poem is "no more than a charming trifle, with the charm clearly inseparable from its elegant form," and argues that if the charm is inseparable from the form, and the form cannot survive translation, our students would not be reading Marot at all, so we should print something of his that translates more honestly.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the board before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — adopt one translation, adopt one and print the other in a footnote, print both, or drop the poem — and defend that recommendation. It should engage Hofstadter's case that in such a poem form carries much of the meaning; the scholar's case that accuracy of sense is the translator's first duty and that students with no French deserve the literal truth; and the medievalist's case on its own terms rather than by restating Hofstadter. Finally, it should say plainly what the anthology's readers — students who will encounter this poem only in English, and some of it aloud in lecture — would actually be given under each option.
