My spouse and I spent most of a long drive back from a wedding arguing about Moore's paradox, and the argument left me with a half-formed idea I want to turn into a conference paper proposal — but the paper is for you to plan, and I want you to commit to a position rather than hedge.

The argument, briefly. Moore's paradox is the oddity of asserting, in the first person and the present tense, that it is raining but that one does not believe it is raining. My spouse holds that the absurdity lives in the avowal of belief: on her view, the way one settles whether one believes that p is by settling whether p — you answer questions about your own beliefs by looking outward, not inward — so Moore's sentence is a breakdown of that procedure, like asserting what the window shows while refusing to look. I hold instead that the absurdity is a property of assertion and commitment: asserting p while asserting that you don't believe p is a self-undermining performance, closer to promising not to keep one's promises, and it requires a committer to an assertion, not a richly self-transparent believer. Somewhere around the second hour we stopped trading intuitions and started building test cases, and I think the test cases are the paper.

The battery we came up with:

1. Past tense, first person — that it was raining but that I didn't believe it at the time. This sounds like rueful confession rather than absurdity.
2. Present tense, third person — that it is raining but that she doesn't believe it. A perfectly ordinary report.
3. Iterated belief — that it is raining, but that I don't believe that I believe it. We disagree about whether this is absurd in the same way as the original.
4. A disjunction — either it's raining or I don't believe it.
5. The knowledge variant — that it's raining, but that I don't know it — which seems merely modest rather than incoherent, and the asymmetry with the belief version needs explaining.
6. The machine case: a weather service prints on its public display, with no intent and arguably no beliefs, that it is currently raining in Utrecht and that it does not believe that it is raining. Does any absurdity survive, and which of our two views does the answer embarrass?
7. The diary case: I write Moore's sentence in a private diary no one will ever read.
