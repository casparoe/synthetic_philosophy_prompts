I've long held the Popperian line from The Poverty of Historicism: there are no law-like regularities of historical development, so large-scale prediction of society's future course is impossible in principle, not merely in practice. Tell me what would count against this. Which developments would genuinely threaten it — say, a sustained track record of quantitative, long-horizon social forecasts (population trajectories, political instability, state breakdown) beating naive extrapolation out of sample, of the kind structural-demographic modelers claim to be assembling — and which apparent counterexamples would be no threat at all, and why? Attend to the exact location of Popper's own line between unconditional prophecy, which he forbids, and conditional prediction of trends, which he explicitly permits: has that distinction quietly insulated the thesis, so that any successful long-range forecast can be reclassified after the fact as merely conditional? And consider whether his Oedipus-effect argument — that a published prediction of social development changes the very development it predicts — proves too much, given that some engineered self-fulfilling prophecies (bank runs, inflation expectations) are handled without embarrassment by other sciences. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, a clear account of what a committed Popperian would concede as counting against the thesis versus dismiss, and an honest verdict on whether anti-historicism is a substantive, risky claim or an unfalsifiable methodological slogan — and, if it is the latter, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind.
