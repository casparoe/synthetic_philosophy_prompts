I'm taking an upper-level seminar in moral psychology, and we just finished three weeks on moral foundations theory — the idea that humans come equipped with a small set of evolved, modular moral intuitions (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and so on), and that left and right differ mainly in how many of these "foundations" they actually build their moral lives on. My lecturer is fond of an analogy that I've since realized does a lot of quiet work in the room: she says the foundations are like taste receptors. Everyone has the same basic hardware for detecting sweet and bitter, but different cultures cook with different ingredients, different individuals develop different acquired tastes, and nobody thinks a person who hates cilantro is objectively mistaken about anything. Political disagreement, she says, is like two people arguing over whether coriander is delicious — a factual matter of their differing palates, not a dispute in which one of them could be right.

She uses the analogy to push a fairly strong conclusion: that when liberals dismiss conservative positions as merely prejudiced, they are making a category error — you cannot refute someone's palate — and that the mature response to moral-political disagreement is something like cosmopolitanism about cuisines: be curious, be tolerant, don't pretend there's a fact of the matter. In the seminar, a classmate pushed it one step further and said conservatives are like supertasters, since they respond to all five foundations while liberals respond to only two — so if the analogy is right, doesn't it suggest conservatives are actually more perceptive, more finely attuned to the full range of moral facts? The lecturer didn't like that at all, but her objection felt ad hoc to me, and I want to know whether the analogy itself can block it.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Take the taste analogy seriously as an argument rather than as a flourish, and audit it the way you'd audit any argument by analogy. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the taste bud, the flavor, the cuisine, the acquired taste, the supertaster, the food critic, if anything. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks — and here I especially want you to consider the features of taste that the analogy quietly imports: taste buds were selected for a function (detecting nutrition and poison), so there is such a thing as a malfunctioning palate; we don't hold symposia to debate whether bitterness is justified, whereas moral disagreement is conducted through giving and asking for reasons; and some things called "tastes" (a fondness for cruelty, say) seem criticizable in ways cilantro-hatred is not. Third, sort the disanalogies into those that matter for my lecturer's tolerant conclusion and those that don't. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion — no fact of the matter, no one is wrong — survives the audit, whether the supertaster inference my classmate drew is licensed by anything in the analogy or is a kind of equivocation, and what a better analogy for the foundations would look like if taste won't do the work: is it closer to perceptual faculties, like color vision, where disagreement is possible because there's a world to be right about?
