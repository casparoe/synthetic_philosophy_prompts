In my philosophy of science elective, my teacher introduced Popper's idea of verisimilitude with the following analogy, which I gather Popper himself invited: doing science is like a marksman shooting at a target in the dark. No theory ever hits the bull's-eye — the whole truth about the world — but a false theory can still land nearer or farther from it. Newtonian mechanics is a near miss, Ptolemaic astronomy a wider miss, and astrology a shot that goes over the neighbor's fence. The lesson my teacher drew is that science makes genuine progress even though it never reaches final truth: successive theories cluster closer and closer to the target, which is why we can say we know more than people in the seventeenth century even though, by Popper's lights, all our theories are strictly speaking false and always will be.

Since then I have read that Popper actually tried to make the bull's-eye picture precise. He defined the truth content of a theory as the set of true consequences it entails, its falsity content as the set of false consequences, and said one theory has more verisimilitude than another when it has more truth content without more falsity content. But in 1974, Pavel Tichý and David Miller independently proved that this definition has a devastating consequence: on Popper's own account, no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other false theory. The arrow picture collapses — every miss turns out to be, formally, equally distant from the bull's-eye. Miller also argued, if I understand him, that the comparative closeness relation between theories can flip depending on which vocabulary you express them in, which is hard to square with any literal analogy to distances on a board.

I would like an audit of the analogy itself, since so much of my teacher's lesson seems to rest on it. Could you lay out, as explicitly as possible, what maps onto what: the bull's-eye, the arrow, the distance from the target, the marksman, the successive shots. Then identify where the mapping breaks — in particular, whether truth content and falsity content behave anything like regions on a board, and what the Tichý and Miller results show about which disanalogies are fatal to the picture and which are just surface differences. I am especially interested in whether the fatal disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy was used to support: does the failure of Popper's formal definition show that science does not converge on the truth, or only that this particular way of modeling closeness failed? Finally, if the archery picture cannot be repaired, is there a better analogy for the relation between false theories and the truth — or is the whole idea of false theories being ranked by their distance from the truth a mistake that we should give up, even though it is how everyone talks?
