I host a small philosophy of mind podcast with a mostly grad-student audience, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent her whole career defending and refining the multiple drafts model of consciousness. She did her dissertation on the "editorial" picture of information flow in the brain, and her new book argues that the last few decades of cognitive science — especially predictive processing — vindicate the model. She's a great talker and extremely good at disposing of the obvious objections with a quick joke about homunculi, so generic "isn't this just behaviorism in disguise?" questions will just get steamrolled. What I need are questions that press where the actual literature presses.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the model? Specifically I want at least one question each on the following: (a) the color phi phenomenon and the Orwellian-vs-Stalinesque dilemma — pressing on whether saying that "when did the subject become conscious of the second flash" is an ill-posed question amounts to an answer or just a refusal to answer; (b) the worry that the multiple drafts model is really a bundle of empirical claims about perceptual timing and revision dressed up as a metaphysics of consciousness, so that psychophysics could confirm all the timing claims while leaving the hard problem exactly where it was; (c) the unity-of-consciousness objection — my experience right now doesn't feel like a loose editorial process with drafts settling at different times, it feels like one unified thing — and how she responds to that without simply redescribing the feeling in editorial vocabulary; and (d) whether predictive processing has absorbed her view's most interesting content — if everyone now agrees perception is a kind of ongoing draft or controlled hallucination, is there anything distinctive left for the model to say about consciousness as such? For each of these, please also include a follow-up question keyed to the reply she's most likely to give.

One more thing: whenever falsifiability comes up she tends to deflect with something like "no experiment could refute dualism either, so why hold my view to a higher standard?", and I'd like the sequence of questions built so that this deflection gets progressively harder to use — ideally without me having to flat-out interrupt her. Please end the sequence with one question asking what discovery about the brain would actually make her give up on the model, and phrase it so that "nothing, because the model is a framework not a hypothesis" can't be a comfortable landing spot. Feel free to suggest a rough ordering with a sentence on why each question goes where it does.
