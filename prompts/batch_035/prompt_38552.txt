I'm putting together a seminar for officers at a staff college — half of them trained in international humanitarian law, half in moral philosophy — and before we argue about any particular position I want the bar written down: what a theory of the moral equality of combatants has to deliver in order to count as adequate. The purpose matters, so fix it this way: the theory must be good enough both for advising an individual soldier deciding whether to fight in a war he suspects is unjust, and for evaluating the law of armed conflict's near-equal treatment of combatants on both sides as a body. A theory that serves one purpose while staying silent, or contradicting itself, on the other is, for my purposes, a failure — though you may argue that the two purposes should be split between two theories, if you're prepared to say what is lost by splitting them.

Write the desiderata. At minimum, tell me what the theory must say about: the unjust conscript who fights under threat of imprisonment and sincerely believes his side's cause is just; the volunteer who joins a war of aggression for pay or adventure, contrasted with the mercenary who fights defensively for exactly the same motives; the bloodless invasion, in which one state annexes another's territory without a shot fired or a civilian harmed, and the question of whether defensive force may be used at all against soldiers who threaten no one's life; a war in which both sides lack a just cause, so that moral equality, if it holds there, holds between two sets of wrongdoers and threatens to become vacuous; remote warfare, where drone operators commute home between shifts, and whether battlefield presence can matter to liability once it has been uncoupled from risk; and the just-side combatant who commits war crimes, which forces the theory to locate the boundary between what the justness of the cause does and does not excuse. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it either non-negotiable or a camp prejudice — a demand that only a committed traditionalist or only a committed revisionist would press.

Then flag which subsets of the desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect the central three-way pull is: liability to defensive attack tracks individual responsibility for an unjust threat; the blameless conscript lacks the kind of responsibility that grounds liability to be killed; and yet in war it is, and ought to remain, permissible to attack the enemy's blameless conscripts. If those cannot be held together — and I don't see how — say which one you would drop, what theory results from dropping it, and what it costs in cases beyond the conscript.

Also tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding candidate theories. Close with a brief scorecard of three positions against your list: the traditionalist view descending from Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars; McMahan-style revisionism, on which individual liability replaces the permissive symmetry; and a conventionalist or contractarian attempt to vindicate moral equality as a mutually beneficial convention among soldiers rather than a fact about each soldier's individual liability. The scorecard should be a paragraph at most — keep the weight on the criteria, since the criteria are where the thinking is.
