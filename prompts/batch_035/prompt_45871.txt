Take the 2020 English A-levels grading mess as a case study for the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and tell me where the theory illuminates it and where it goes quiet. Quick recap of the facts so we're on the same page: covid killed the exams, teachers submitted predicted grades, Ofqual's algorithm then moderated those predictions so each school's grade distribution matched roughly what that school had historically produced -- which meant small cohorts (private schools, small subjects) basically kept their teacher grades while big state school cohorts got pulled down toward the school's past distribution, about 40% of teacher grades were lowered, individual students could see they'd been graded on their school's postcode, and after a few days of protests the whole thing was scrapped and teacher grades were restored.

The theory I want applied: the impossibility results showing that when base rates differ between groups, you can't have calibration and equalized error rates (false positive and false negative parity) at the same time -- pick one and you lose the other. So map the episode carefully. What was the "ground truth" label here, given nobody took the exam? What was each side of the public fight implicitly demanding -- was the government's defense of the algorithm a calibration defense, and were the critics demanding something in the neighborhood of equalized odds? Was the outrage actually the impossibility result playing out in public, or is that a tidy story that doesn't survive contact with the details? Because some things don't fit neatly: the sharpest anger wasn't about the two groups in the theorem (like two demographic groups with different base rates), it was about individuals versus their own school's history, and about a one-off year with no ground truth at all, which the theorem doesn't really talk about.

Then run it the other way: what does this episode do to the impossibility results as a piece of applied ethics and policy? One reading is that the public demanded the impossible and got it anyway when the government caved -- which would suggest the results are an ivory tower curiosity that politics simply overrides. Another reading is that the impossibility framing itself smuggles in a contestable choice, namely treating "the grade this student would have gotten in the counterfactual exam" as the target variable, when arguably the socially meaningful target is something else entirely. Tell me which reading you find more defensible, what the theory is silent about here (the legitimacy question, the fact that students could SEE the mechanism, the fact that it ran for exactly one year with no track record), and whether any other fairness framework -- individual fairness, counterfactual fairness, the "no patterns you can exploit" idea -- does better on this case than the calibration-versus-parity framing does. I don't want a survey of algorithmic fairness, I want this one case worked hard in both directions.
