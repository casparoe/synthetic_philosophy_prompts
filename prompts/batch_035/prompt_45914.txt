I'm in a graduate seminar on consciousness and physicalism, and Frank Jackson's Mary's room thought experiment has turned into a running dispute between me and a classmate. We agree on the setup: Mary is a brilliant scientist confined from birth to a black-and-white environment, where she has learned everything there is to know about the physical facts of color vision — the optics, the neurophysiology, the information processing, everything expressible in the physical vocabulary. When she steps out and sees a red tomato for the first time, does she learn something new? My credence that she gains genuinely new propositional knowledge — a new fact, not merely a new ability, a new concept, or a new relation of acquaintance — is about 0.65. My classmate is at roughly 0.15. Her view is that the phenomenal concept strategy defuses the argument: on first seeing red, Mary acquires a new recognitional concept that picks out the very same physical state she already knew everything about, under a new mode of presentation, and the intuition that this answer is cheating rests on conflating the epistemic reading of the argument with the metaphysical one.

I'd like your own number, and more importantly the decomposition that produces it. Some terms I imagine belonging: the probability that physicalism is true in the first place; the probability that "complete physical knowledge of color vision" is even a coherent ideal rather than something that somehow fails to be well-defined for an inquirer embedded in the physical world; the probability that the phenomenal concept strategy succeeds, conditional on physicalism being true; the probability that whatever Mary gains is propositional rather than acquaintance-based or ability-based, conditional on it being new in any sense; and any further terms you think the question secretly factors into. Assign each a rough probability with one line of justification, then combine them into your final credence, and show how the combination works. I'd also like you to fold in two wrinkles rather than set them aside. First, suppose Mary's textbooks additionally gave her complete knowledge of everything her own brain does during the first experience of red — does the self-knowledge wrinkle move any of your terms? Second, there's the "proves too much" worry: a person fitted with a repaired fourth cone type, or a pigeon with its tetrachromatic visual system, seems able to gain knowledge of a color neither Mary nor we could have acquired from any book, and it's far from obvious that this threatens physicalism. Is the parallel between that case and Mary's own case sound, or disanalogous in a way that matters, and which of my classmate's sub-credences should the parallel move if it is sound?

Finally, tell me which term in your decomposition you are least confident in, what argument or consideration would move that term the most, and — the part I actually care about — where you think my classmate and I most likely disagree. Is it about physicalism itself, about whether the phenomenal concept strategy works, about the taxonomy of knowledge (propositional versus acquaintance versus ability), or about some defect in the framing of the thought experiment that neither of us has managed to articulate? We've been at this for three weeks and I suspect our argument is really about something other than what we each say it's about.
