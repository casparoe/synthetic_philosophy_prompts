Here is a position I have ended up holding, modeled on Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness (written in the mid-1740s, published posthumously in 1779), though I will state it in my own terms:

(1) The only proper business of a human life is to procure agreeable sensations and sentiments; happiness is nothing over and above satisfied tastes and passions. And since great pleasures are available only to those capable of strong passions, being susceptible to passion is desirable even granting that passions make more people unhappy than happy.
(2) Our impression of that risk is distorted anyway, because unhappiness is noisy while happiness is silent: the unhappy need other people, recount their miseries, and seek remedies and relief, whereas the happy seek nothing and announce nothing. The miserable are interesting and therefore visible; the contented are unknown.
(3) Happiness therefore depends on the management of belief. One should cultivate the benign illusions of ordinary life — a lover's constancy, one's own talents, the prospect of fortune at the gaming table — roughly the way one tends a garden, while reserving the appetite for truth for the one domain where truth pays: study, and the hope of glory attached to it, the only passion that deepens with use rather than exhausting itself.

Corollary: where truth would spoil pleasure, a selective and cultivated ignorance is a technique of happiness, not an intellectual vice.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position. Not a defense of it, and not a lecture on du Châtelet; I hold the view and know the text. I want the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted by kind: empirical findings (about affect, about belief, and about how each behaves over time), the deliverances of other philosophical commitments (say, about the ethics of self-deception or the value of truth), and structural problems internal to the view itself.

Please address at least the following, though do not limit yourself to them:

- The two-regime problem. The position splits a person into one epistemic regime for love affairs and gaming tables and a different one for study. What would show that no such partition is stable — that the habit of managed belief in one domain cannot be kept from contaminating the other? What observable difference would that contamination make, so that I could tell whether it had happened?
- The visibility asymmetry. Claim (2) explains away the apparent evidence against the view. Under what conditions is that a legitimate correction of a sampling bias, and under what conditions a self-sealing immunization? Given (2), is there any finding about the relative incidence or depth of passion-borne misery that should move me at all? If nothing could, say so plainly.
- The regret test. The view counsels illusions one may later discover to have been illusions. Which features of the later situation should count against the view, rather than being absorbed as the price of the earlier pleasure — and which of them would a committed defender, writing in her spirit, have to concede as a genuine cost?
- Unfalsifiability. If every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as an improperly cultivated illusion or an insufficiently strong passion, does the position still risk anything? And is demanding that it do so a category mistake — an unreasonable standard for a practical doctrine of life — or a genuine defect in a view of this kind?

Finally, tell me which of the defeaters you list a thoughtful defender of the position would concede as counting against it, which she would dismiss, and where you yourself would place the view on the spectrum from exposed to well insulated — with your reasons.
