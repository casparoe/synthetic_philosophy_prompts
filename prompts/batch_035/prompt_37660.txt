My roomate and I have been slowly making our way through Brothers Karamazov (we're on book 5), and last week at a dinner party he dropped the Inquisitor's line that "Nothing has ever been more insupportable for a man and a human society than freedom" to argue that any political movement promising security and comfort will always, in the long run, beat a movement promising liberty — so basically people who care about freedom should just accept trading it away for welfare and stop pretending the masses want anything else. Someone pushed back that the quote is Dostoevsky putting words in the mouth of a villain, so it doesn't mean anything on its own, and the whole table descended into chaos before anyone actually defined terms.

I want to walk into the next argument armed. It seems to me that single sentence could be making a bunch of totally different claims and nobody at that dinner was distinguishing them: a psychological/empirical one (people in fact find freedom more burdensome than satisfying), a normative one (people shouldn't be made to bear it, and taking it off their shoulders is an act of love — which is what the Inquisitor himself seems to think), a conceptual one about what freedom even is (maybe the Inquisitor means specifically freedom of conscience, the kind Christ allegedly insisted on in refusing the three temptations, not political liberty at all), and maybe a sociological one about "a human society" — note the sentence has TWO subjects, the individual man and society, and what's insupportable for one might not be for the other. There's also the meta-question of whether the line is meant to be true, or true only as an expression of the Inquisitor's despairing view of humanity, which Ivan may or may not endorse.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, from the fairly trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each one say whether it's plausible, whether it's actually what the Inquisitor's argument in the chapter needs, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it would actually support my roomate's conclusion that security-promising movements will always win? I have a suspicion that even if the psychological claim is true, it doesn't get him the political conclusion he wants, but I can't quite articulate why. A menu of readings with verdicts I can bring to the rematch, please.
