I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I want your help building something I've been mulling over for my team's internal blog. Here's the backstory: we make a lot of what you'd call knowledge claims on the job. "The payment service is healthy," "that deploy didn't touch the auth path," "we know the incident was caused by the cache." And we've all been burned by a dashboard that said the service was healthy because the health check was hitting the wrong port — the belief was true, we had decent reasons for it, and it was still pure luck that it was true. A colleague mentioned the Gettier problem to me, and when I looked it up, it seemed like philosophers have been fighting about exactly my stuck-health-check situation for sixty years. What I want now is a way to sort out where my teammates and I actually stand, because the debate has apparently spawned a thicket of "-isms" and I can't tell which one I hold.

The deliverable: a decision tree (as text, with clear branch labels — I'll turn it into a diagram myself) that starts from the shared starting point "you accept that the original Gettier-style cases show that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge" and ends at named positions. Requirements:

1. Each branch node should be a concrete case with a forced choice, phrased so that someone can answer it from their gut without knowing which position the answer feeds into. Use cases like these: fake-barn country (you form a true belief about a barn in a county full of barn facades); a lottery you've almost certainly lost but can't rule out winning; a monitoring dashboard that is genuinely broken but happens to display the true value for an unrelated reason; and the original-style case where your evidence rests on a false assumption that just happens not to spoil the conclusion. You may add one or two cases of your own, but they should be in the same concrete register, and at least one should come from software or systems engineering.

2. Build the tree from the logical space before you attach names. That is: first choose the questions and enumerate which combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space as leaves. The positions that must appear as leaves include no-false-lemmas, the causal and tracking-style externalist views, safety-based views, reliabilism, virtue epistemology, knowledge-first views, and contextualism — plus a leaf for people who respond to the cases by biting the bullet and denying that the Gettier victims lack knowledge. If some combination of answers corresponds to no standard position, say so at that leaf and briefly diagnose why nobody lives there (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). If two positions can't be separated by your questions — I'd expect tracking and safety to be hard to pull apart, and I suspect my engineering-flavored cases won't distinguish reliabilism from virtue views — flag that explicitly rather than inventing a fake branch point.

3. Keep the audience in mind: smart engineers who don't know the jargon. Every named leaf gets a one-line gloss in plain language, and any node that hinges on a distinction between, say, "the method could easily have given a wrong answer" and "the method is generally reliable" needs that distinction spelled out with an example, because to us those sound like the same complaint worded twice.

4. At the end, include a short honest-assessment section: state how confident you are in each part of the tree, and single out the single point you are least sure about — for instance, whether a case you invented actually does the diagnostic work you assigned it, or whether you've placed a named position at the wrong leaf. Where you're simplifying a live controversy for the sake of the tree, say so rather than smoothing it over.
