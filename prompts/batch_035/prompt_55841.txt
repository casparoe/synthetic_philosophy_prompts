For a while now I've been mulling over the sunk cost fallacy, mostly because of my own life rather than anything I read. I kept a small business going a couple of years past the point where the numbers said to fold it, and even now I can't make myself believe that was simply a mistake. So I've drifted into a position that my friends, who have more economics training than I do, treat as obviously confused: that honoring sunk costs is not always irrational. My reasoning, as best I can articulate it, runs along three lines. First, the amount I've already put in is often the best available evidence of how much the project matters to me — my past choices reveal my values more reliably than my current mood does, especially when the current mood is tired and frightened. Second, there seems to be a self-trust cost the textbook argument ignores: I'm not only deciding about this one project, I'm setting a precedent for how I treat my own commitments, and someone who abandons things the moment the ledger turns red may slowly become someone incapable of credibly committing to anything hard. Third, the standard advice presents quitting as a clean reset, but in my experience it plays out more like a slow leak of regret, which suggests the clean-reset picture is a forecasting error baked into the theory rather than something the theory discovered.

I don't want a defense of my view from you — I already hold it, so I know its attractions. What I want is a map of its exposure. What would actually count against it? Concretely: what kinds of cases would have to exist for it to be false — cases, say, where a person's past behavior is systematically misleading evidence about their current values, or where the self-trust consideration turns out to be doing no real work once you look closely; which of my three supporting considerations are load-bearing, such that if one of them collapsed the whole view would wobble; and what the view predicts that the standard "sunk costs never count" position doesn't, so that in principle the two could be told apart by how things go rather than just by which one sounds wiser. I'd also like a candid sorting of objections: which ones a reasonable person holding this view should concede genuinely count against it, and which ones she is entitled to resist. And finally, an honest verdict on whether the view, once its defenders finish qualifying it — "rational only sometimes," "only for projects tied to who you are," and so on — ends up so hedged that no possible case could refute it, and if so, whether that hedging is a defect in a view of this kind or just the normal condition for views about practical rationality. I'd rather know where my position is genuinely at risk than be told it's safe.
