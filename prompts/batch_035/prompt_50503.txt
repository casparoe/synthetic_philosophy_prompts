I am a graduate student in political philosophy, currently taking a seminar on distributive justice, and I also work twelve hours a week as a caseworker for a municipal emergency hardship fund — crisis grants to individuals, with repayment waived, reduced, or required at my discretion depending on circumstances. The official guidance tells me to weigh how far an applicant is responsible for their own crisis, and to consider attaching conditions (job-search requirements, attendance at budgeting appointments) in cases of what it calls self-inflicted need. I find I have no principled way to make these calls, and my seminar reading suggests the theories not only disagree about where to draw the lines but about whether I should be drawing them at all.

Please write a decision procedure I can actually run in the ten minutes I have per case. Ground it in the desert and luck-egalitarian literature — the distinction between brute luck and option luck, and the familiar objection that people's choices are themselves products of circumstances they did not choose — but keep it usable by colleagues who have read none of it. The procedure should list the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the points where the theory runs out and I would just be exercising values rather than applying rules, and tell me what to do when I genuinely cannot determine whether someone's situation was their own doing. It should also be honest about the ways it will systematically misfire — for instance, wherever it asks me to estimate choice-versus-circumstance for people whose lives look nothing like mine.

Then run it on three cases from my recent caseload: (a) a man who quit his job without another lined up after his supervisor mocked him in front of coworkers for weeks, and now cannot pay rent; (b) a woman whose crisis traces to gambling she resumed after eighteen months of being clean, a relapse that followed her mother's death; (c) an applicant who has twice declined "suitable" work we referred her to — my office is unanimous about the third case, and I want to know whether the procedure agrees with us for principled reasons or merely ratifies our prior convictions.

Please state how confident you are in the procedure overall and single out the step you are least sure of — the point where you suspect the theory is doing the least work and office convention the most.
