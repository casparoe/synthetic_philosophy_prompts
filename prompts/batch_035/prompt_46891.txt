My uncle, who follows the intelligent design movement, keeps returning to one line of argument: DNA is a code — indeed a digital one, with a four-letter alphabet, codons as words, start and stop signals, error correction — and every code whose origin we have actually observed traces back to a mind. So DNA, he concludes, is evidence of a designer. My friend, who works in molecular biology, rolls her eyes at the word "code" but can't quite say what's wrong with the argument, since "the genetic code" is a perfectly respectable term in her field and even popular science books describe DNA as a program for building an organism. Can you audit the analogy he's leaning on? Set out what maps onto what — alphabet, words, message, medium, encoder, decoder — and note how much of the molecular machinery is doing real work in the correspondence. Then identify where the mapping breaks, especially whether the coder/code-reader distinction survives, given that the cell's decoding machinery (the enzymes that actually pair codons with amino acids) is itself produced from the DNA being decoded. Distinguish the disanalogies that bear on his conclusion from the ones that are idle for this purpose: he doesn't need DNA to be a blueprint of the organism, only code-like enough for codes to inherit the property he cares about, namely having an author. Address my friend's implicit worry too — if the coding talk is mere metaphor, why does it work as well as it does in molecular biology, and how literal is "arbitrary mapping" here anyway? Finally, say whether his inference survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other comparison would fit the molecular facts better.
