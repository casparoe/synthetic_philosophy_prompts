I teach a seminar on the ethics of killing, and this term we are doing a unit on the doing/allowing distinction. For our case study I want to use the English conjoined twins case of 2000, and I would like your help thinking through how to frame it for my students. Let me supply the salient facts so we are working from the same page. In August 2000, twin girls — known to the courts by the pseudonyms Jodie and Mary — were born in Manchester, joined at the abdomen. Mary had severe brain damage, no functioning lungs, and only minimal heart function; she was kept alive entirely by a shared artery that drew on Jodie's circulatory system. Doctors judged that without surgery both would die within roughly six months, that separation would kill Mary immediately, and that Jodie had an excellent chance of surviving separation. The parents, devout Roman Catholics from Malta, refused to consent: they believed it was not for them to choose which child should die. The hospitals sought a declaration that surgery would be lawful, and the Court of Appeal in September 2000 authorized the separation against the parents' wishes. The surgery took place in November 2000; Mary died as expected, and Jodie survived. What makes the case philosophically interesting is that the judges themselves could not agree on what kind of act they were permitting. The first-instance judge had reasoned by analogy to the Bland case, treating separation as akin to withdrawing life support — a letting die. The Court of Appeal rejected that framing, and its three judges diverged: one leaned on something like self-defense on Jodie's behalf, another on necessity, another on the surgeons' intentions. So the case is a genuine dispute about the doing/allowing distinction, conducted by judges who knew exactly what was at stake.

Here is what I want from you. First, map the episode carefully onto the distinction: is severing the artery that feeds Mary a killing or a letting die, and on what grounds? Consider at least the following framings — Mary as a parasite or free-rider on Jodie's organs, so that separation is the withdrawal of aid that Jodie has no duty to continue; the Rachels-style argument that the killing/letting-die distinction is doing no work here, since whatever we call it the outcome for Mary is identical; Quinn's contrast between direct and indirect agency; and the doctrine of double effect as a way to hold that the surgeons intend Jodie's survival while merely foreseeing Mary's death. Be disciplined about the mapping — say what each framing takes the act, the agent, and the relevant alternatives to be, since different framings answer these questions differently (separation versus non-separation; the surgeons versus Jodie's body versus the parents). Second, tell me where each framing strains. The withdrawal-of-aid framing has the awkward feature that the aid is being withdrawn from Mary without the provider's (or her surrogate's) consent, and that the withdrawal requires a scalpel applied to Mary herself rather than the shutting off of some machine. Double effect has the familiar problem that the foreseeability of Mary's death approaches certainty, which strains the intended/foreseen contrast. The necessity and self-defense framings must explain why Mary, who is not attacking anyone, is a legitimate target at all. I want you to say which framing, if any, you actually endorse and why, rather than surveying and hiding.

Third, and most important for my purposes: tell me what the episode shows about the doing/allowing distinction itself, as a test of the theory rather than an application of it. Almost everyone's intuition is that separation was permissible here, and almost everyone also accepts that active killing requires extraordinary justification while letting die can be justified much more easily. Does the case vindicate the idea that the distinction carries independent moral weight, or does it suggest that our permissibility judgment is driven entirely by facts about prognosis, consent, and bodily autonomy, with the killing/letting-die label being post hoc — exactly what the appellate judges' inability to agree on a characterization might indicate? Would it change your analysis if Mary had normal brain function, so that the choice was between two persons with equal claims rather than one grievously injured infant, and if so, does that show the distinction's importance is being silently swamped by other considerations? I want a two-directional case analysis: theory applied to the case, and the case turned back on the theory. Where the theory is silent or must be stretched, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak.
