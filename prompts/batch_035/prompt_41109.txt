I've been in a reading group on On Certainty for about a year now, and we've gotten stuck on something I can't tell whether it's interesting or just a slog. The issue: whether hinge certainties are propositions at all. One faction in the group insists that something like "there are physical objects" or "the earth existed long before I was born" is a proposition — it can be true or false, it surfaces in ordinary claims, and the interesting thing is just that it can't be *known* or evidenced, because it's the kind of certainty that functions as a backdrop to justification rather than a result of it. The other faction says this reads Wittgenstein exactly backwards: hinges aren't asserted, doubted, or justified — they're more like rules of the game, or something animal and arational, and once you call them propositions you've smuggled them back into the very game of giving and asking for reasons they were supposed to stand outside. We've read the standard positions in the hinge epistemology literature and honestly nobody has moved an inch in months.

Here's what I actually want, though — not a survey of who thinks what, but a diagnosis of why this is stuck. My working hypothesis is that the fight is downstream of a different, quieter one: whether epistemology by its nature deals only in what's assessable as rational or irrational, warranted or not. If hinges are outside epistemic assessment entirely, then maybe "hinge epistemology" is a contradiction in terms — there's nothing for it to be an epistemology *of* — and some people seem to accept that conclusion cheerfully while others quietly redefine the epistemic so hinges fit inside it. Is that right? Or is the propositionalism question genuinely substantive on its own — would something bad actually happen, philosophically, if you got it wrong? I also suspect part of the problem is that we're running two projects under one name: exegesis of Wittgenstein (what the remarks actually commit him to) versus building a standalone epistemology that uses hinge ideas with or without him, and people keep arguing across that divide without noticing. Is that a fair diagnosis, or an excuse to not engage?

And then tell me what would count as progress. Are there cases — say, two communities whose hinges visibly conflict, or a person whose hinge certainty seems to break down under some strange circumstance, or a hinge that seems to fade out over historical time — that would force one side to give ground? Or is this a dispute where the parties disagree about what epistemology is *for*, in which case maybe nothing should be expected to end it, and I should just pick the one I like and get on with my life? I'd rather not do that if there's a real question here, so be honest about whether there is.
