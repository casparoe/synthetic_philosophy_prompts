My friend Dana has appointed herself the arbiter of secondhand opinions: whenever someone takes over a view about a work they have not experienced firsthand, she rules on whether they may legitimately claim it as their own. Her rulings to date, which I believe to be consistent:

1. A sommelier who tasted the wine described it as tightly wound and fiercely tannic; you repeat this at dinner as your view of it. Ruling: legitimate.
2. A painter friend who studied a Rothko in person told you the composition sits oddly, weighted to one side; you say the same at the gallery. Ruling: legitimate.
3. A colleague who has never seen the film relays the general buzz that it is a masterpiece; you tell others it is a masterpiece. Ruling: not your view.
4. An acquaintance who has never tasted Sauternes, or much sweet wine of any kind, declares it sublime on the strength of the region's reputation; you adopt the declaration. Ruling: not your view.
5. A friend who has tasted the wine and knows your palate says you will love it; you announce that you expect to love it. Ruling: legitimate — a prediction about you, she insists, not an opinion about the wine.

Next month's cases: (a) a conductor who has performed a symphony dozens of times calls it shattering, and you would like to claim that view of the work; (b) a musicologist who has never heard the piece but has mastered everything verifiable about the composer's orchestration calls the scoring garish, and you would like to claim that; and (c) a recommendation engine with a strong record of anticipating your reactions, but which has never tasted anything, tells you both that the wine is delicious and that you in particular will love it.

Please predict Dana's rulings on (a), (b), and (c); articulate the principle she seems to be running and the rival principles that fit her record just as well; say what each of those principles predicts for the three cases; give your best guess at her rulings, with reasons; and propose the one further case that would most cleanly separate the rival principles.
