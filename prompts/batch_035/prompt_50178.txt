I own a small firm, about 40 people, and a couple of times a year one of us has to promote somebody into a role with a lot of unsupervised discretion — managing client relationships on their own, handling money, being the person who decides things when neither I nor my co-founder is around. Interviews have burned me twice now: people who came across as honest and steady in the room and then did dumb, and in one case pretty expensive, stuff once they were actually on their own. I took an ethics course in college and remember liking virtue ethics — judge the character, not just the act — but I also remember the situationist stuff that's supposed to undercut it: the Milgram experiments, the seminary students who literally stepped over a guy slumped in a doorway because they were told they were late, and those old studies where kids who cheated in class didn't cheat on the exam and vice versa. The moral as I took it: how people act is driven alot more by the situation they're in than by any stable trait, so a character-judgment procedure built on asking people about their values is doomed. But I still have to promote *somebody*, and just saying "design the situation well and promote whoever" feels like it gives up on the fact that some people really do seem more trustworthy than others across the board.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure my co-founder and I can actually run in about an hour per candidate, written for people with no psychology training. Spell out the steps in order: what to ask the coworkers who've seen the candidate in real situations (not hypotheticals), what to look for in their answers, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates with a no, and where it has to hand the call back to us because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. Be honest about which steps are still interview-vibes dressed up as procedure — I'd rather know. Also tell me plainly what the procedure *cannot* do, e.g. if it can't tell "person of genuinely good character" apart from "person who has only ever been in easy, well-resourced situations," say that, because that limitation is exactly the thing that's been biting me. Then run it on two candidates: one who's solid and well liked but has only ever worked under close supervision in a smoothly run enviroment, and one who's prickly, not well liked, but has been through genuinely messy situations (a failing project, a coworker everyone knew was cutting corners) and behaved decently throughout. If the procedure's verdict on the second person is one I won't like, so be it — give it anyway. Last thing: tell me whether any part of the output should really be a recommendation about changing the situation they'd be promoted into (checks, buffers, a transition period) rather than a verdict on the person, and where you'd draw that line. Please write the whole thing in American English.
