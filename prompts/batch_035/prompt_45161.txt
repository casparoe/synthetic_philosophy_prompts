My study group thinks it has found a reductio of the view that we should weigh wild animal welfare, and I can't locate the false step. (1) The overwhelming majority of wild animals are r-selected: a typical fish or insect produces hundreds or thousands of offspring, of which all but one or two die before maturity, mostly by predation, starvation, or desiccation. (2) A life of a few days ending in death by starvation is not worth living for the creature that lives it. (3) So the average wild animal's life is net negative. (4) Wild animals are so numerous that, on these assumptions, total wild suffering dwarfs total wild enjoyment. (5) If total wild suffering dwarfs total wild enjoyment, then, other things equal, reducing the number of wild animals that come into existence makes the world better. (6) Habitat destruction reduces the number of wild animals that come into existence. (7) Therefore, other things equal, habitat destruction makes the world better, and conservation makes it worse. I could live with a narrow version of this — perhaps a biosphere with fewer, longer-lived, better-off animals would be better than the one we have — but the argument seems to license bulldozing any ecosystem whatsoever, including a thriving one, and to count the extinction of most r-selected species as a gain. That I cannot accept. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. In particular, I want to know whether the escape runs through the empirical premises, the aggregation, or the slide from welfare totals to the world being better — and whether the escape I choose also commits me to saying something about whether it would be good to create a vast number of barely-worth-living happy creatures, since my intuitions pull apart there too. If it helps: I would most like to keep (1), since it looks like plain biology.
