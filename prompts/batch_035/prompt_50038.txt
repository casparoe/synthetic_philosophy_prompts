In 1980, the biologist Paul Ehrlich — whose The Population Bomb (1968) had forecast mass famines within decades — accepted a wager from the economist Julian Simon, who held that human ingenuity keeps resources effectively unlimited. Ehrlich chose five metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin, and tungsten — and staked $1,000 on their being more expensive, in inflation-adjusted terms, in October 1990; if they were cheaper, he would pay the difference. All five fell in real terms, tin dramatically so, and Ehrlich mailed Simon a check for the difference. A proposed rematch on a broader set of environmental indicators was floated in the 1990s but never agreed.

Analyze this episode through the theory of falsification and rescue in long-run prediction. What proposition, exactly, did the 1990 settlement falsify — Ehrlich's scarcity claim itself, or an auxiliary assumption (the decade chosen, market institutions, price as a proxy for physical scarcity)? Enumerate the rescues available to Ehrlich — wrong decade, prices the wrong test, the bomb still ticking — and judge each by a Lakatosian standard: which are ad hoc, and which are progressive, in the sense of committing him to some new risky prediction he would otherwise not make? Then turn the case against the theory: Malthus's own predictions failed in his lifetime, and his defenders have said for two centuries that he was right but early. Under what conditions, if any, is that form of rescue more than an unfalsifiable escape hatch, and what criterion separates the legitimate right-but-early from the degenerating one?
