Quick background before the question: my ethics seminar is on Scanlon right now, and two guys in my study group, Marco and Dev, got into it on wednesday about whether his contractualism can handle aggregation. I followed along at the time but now I'm not sure who actually won, and I'm supposed to write a paper on this.

For context the way we covered it in class: Scanlon's test for wrongness is whether a principle permitting the action could be reasonably rejected by anyone affected, and he's famous for the anti-aggregation result — no matter how many people you'd spare a headache, that doesn't make it ok to impose a death on one person, because the one person's complaint beats the headaches no matter how many headaches there are. Numbers don't just add up across persons.

Marco said this whole edifice collapses once you climb a ladder. Start with a choice between one person suffering a 100-day headache and two people each suffering a 99-day headache. On the standard reading of Scanlon, numbers count when the burdens are the same kind and roughly similar in seriousness, so the two-person option is worse — the one person's complaint is only slightly bigger, not enough to beat two. Fine so far. But now run the same comparison one rung down: two 99-day people versus four 98-day people, same verdict, the bigger group wins. And again and again down the ladder. Chain the pairwise verdicts together and you get that a million people each with a 1-day headache is worse than one person with a 100-day headache. Keep going past headaches to pinpricks and eventually enough pinpricks is worse than a death — which is exactly the aggregative conclusion Scanlon built the view to block. So either better-than isn't transitive, or contractualism hands the aggregators everything they wanted.

Dev said the ladder cheats at every rung by pretending the comparisons all happen under the same standard. The verdicts at the top (100-day vs 99-day headaches) and at the bottom (pinprick vs death) are made against totally different levels of urgency, and what counts as a reasonable rejection shifts with the level, so you're not entitled to string them into one long argument. It's like a sorites: each step looks fine on its own, and something has to give, and Dev says what gives is the chaining, not the view. He added that vague predicates are everywhere — roughly comparable is vague the same way tall is vague, and nobody concludes from the baldness paradox that height comparisons are broken.

Marco's comeback was that tall is vague but there's a crisp fact underneath it, actual height in centimeters, so the vagueness is harmless; in Scanlon's case there's nothing crisp underneath, the vague notion of comparable seriousness is doing all the normative work, so you can't wave it off the same way. And he pointed out that Dev never identified which rung of the ladder is the first illegitimate one — if Dev can't point to a specific rung, the chain goes through.

Then they kind of ran out of steam and I definately couldn't referee it. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I don't just want a winner — I want to know whether Marco's ladder really forces Scanlon into giving up transitivity, whether Dev's different-levels reply is a genuine answer or a dodge, and whether the analogy to vague words like tall actually holds up.
