I run a little film-and-philosophy night at my apartment, maybe eight people, most of whom have read some Marx but only the greatest hits. Next month is Mike Judge's Office Space, and I want to pair it with Marx's account of alienation from the 1844 Manuscripts — the four-fold thing: alienated from the product, from the activity, from species-being, from other workers.

Don't just explain alienation to me. Give me a reading of the film. Which scenes carry which of the four, and is there a scene that dramatizes one of them better than the argument does? The parts I keep circling: Peter's interview with the two consultants, where he describes how each day of work has been worse than the one before it and admits his real dream is basically to do nothing — that looks like alienation from species-being but the joke inverts it, the film kind of endorses the fantasy instead of diagnosing it. The storyline with the waitress and her manager wanting her to wear more buttons on her vest as self-expression — she's alienated from the product except she never makes anything, the product is her own personality, which might be an interesting complication of Marx's category or might just be a joke. And the ending, where Peter takes a construction job digging next to a crew, which the film plays as wholesome and un-alienated. Is that coherent though? Wage labor on a construction site is exactly the kind of work the Manuscripts have in mind, but the film frames it as the exit from alienation. I want you to take that tension seriously — maybe the film is quietly running its own theory, that alienation is about meaningless paperwork and layers of management rather than about selling your labor per se — and tell me whether that's a genuine alternative position or just the film not thinking it through.

Also: the printer-smashing scene. Is that the cathartic opposite of alienated activity or only the fantasy of it? And what do I do with Milton — the cubicle shuffle reads as alienation from other workers, but his arc goes somewhere darker that the comedy keeps trying to shrug off. Basically: where does the film resist a Marxist reading, and where should I be honest with my group that it does?

No verbatim quotes from the movie please, we'll all have just watched it. And I don't need a reading list — assume everyone's done the standard primary reading. What I'm after is what the film sees that the argument alone doesn't.
