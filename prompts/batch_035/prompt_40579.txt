My friend and I spent a long, circular argument about the carbon-offset checkbox on a flight-booking site, and I've come away less interested in the usual "do offsets work?" empirical question than in something underneath it, which I'd like your help reconstructing.

Here is the practice, concretely. When I book a flight, the site computes the emissions attributable to my seat — some fraction of the fuel burn, divided among passengers — and offers, for a few dollars, to fund an equivalent amount of avoided or removed emissions elsewhere, so that my trip can be declared, sold, and labeled "carbon neutral." The label carries a moral claim: my contribution to the problem has been cancelled. The unit of moral accounting is the tonne, the tonne is treated as fungible across projects, continents, and decades, and the remediation is priced per-person-per-journey, as though each traveler's share of the harm were a discrete, severable item that can be individually neutralized.

My friend's position, roughly, was the inefficacy one: no individual flight changes whether climate harms occur — the atmosphere is a threshold-and-chaos system, my tonne tips nothing — so the offset is either an ordinary charitable donation dressed up, or a purchased indulgence that lets me feel clean while doing what I was going to do anyway. I found myself unable to answer him directly, but I noticed that the practice itself seems to take a stand: it prices and cancels harm exactly as if individual contributions were real, additive, and individually remediable. So the practice presupposes some theory of responsibility for collective harms — and I want to know which one, and whether it can survive my friend's objection.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Specifically: (1) What exactly does point-of-sale offsetting presuppose about the structure of collective harm — e.g., that each contribution is a genuine, fungible share of the total harm, or merely that contributions raise the probability of harm by some amount, or something more symbolic about clean hands? Which of these does the practice need, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? (2) Is the practice internally consistent? The same airlines that sell offsets lobby against per-tonne fuel taxes, and the same environmental groups that certify the labels also publish the argument that a small number of large producers bears most of the responsibility — do these arms presuppose incompatible views, or can one theory license them all? (3) The part I keep chewing on: there is no such thing as a vote offset or a litter offset. In principle the structure is identical — pay a small fee to have someone else abstain or clean up, cancelling your share of a collectively caused bad outcome — yet the carbon version feels natural and the voting version feels absurd. Does that asymmetry track a real difference (carbon molecules really are fungible in a way ballots and litter aren't), or is it historical accident, and what does the answer imply about which theory the practice actually presupposes? (4) Finally, what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict inefficacy view on the one hand, or a straightforward expected-value consequentialism on the other? Would the checkbox survive as a donation, or does it only make sense under the ledger view it currently seems to endorse?

You don't need to cite anyone — refer to positions and arguments however is most useful, and I'm happy to fill in references myself. What I want is the reconstructed theory, an honest statement of where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (i.e., several rival views of collective responsibility would license it equally well), and a verdict on whether my friend's objection defeats the practice's own self-understanding or merely embarrasses the label on it.
