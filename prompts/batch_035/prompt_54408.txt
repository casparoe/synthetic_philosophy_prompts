I'm a retired physicist — most of my career was spent on turbulence and nonlinear dynamics, and I've only gotten into philosophy in the last few years, mostly through my daughter who's doing a masters in ethics and keeps leaving readings on my desk. The thing that's currently breaking my brain is the problem of consequentialist cluelessness: the claim, roughly, that we're not just ignorant of the far-future effects of our actions, but that we have no idea even of the sign — whether donating to a malaria charity makes the world better or worse a thousand years out, through chains of effects nobody can trace. I've gathered there's a distinction floating around between cases where we're confident about expected value despite massive uncertainty about outcomes, and cases where even the expectation is up for grabs, and that some people take the second kind to be a serious objection to consequentialist moral thinking.

Now, my instinct is that I've seen this movie before, just without the ethics. Translate the debate into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: sensitive dependence on initial conditions, Lyapunov exponents and predictability horizons, ensemble forecasting, attractors and invariant measures. Where does cluelessness sit in that picture — is it just the statement that for a chaotic system the forecast horizon is finite and shrinks exponentially with how far out you look? And here's the part I especially want your take on: in my world we have the shadowing theorem, which says roughly that even though your computed trajectory diverges from the true one, there exists some true trajectory of the system that stays close to your computed one forever. Someone could try running an analogous move on the moral problem — "sure, my action's actual consequences diverge from anything I predict, but there's a nearby world where things go roughly as I hoped, so I'm not flying blind." Does that rescue anything, or is it a cheat, and if it's a cheat, exactly where does the analogy break?

Then the residue report, which is what I really care about. What parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto dynamical-systems talk, what parts only fit with distortion, and what parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all? I can already see one candidate for the residue: in weather modeling nobody asks what I *ought* to do about the trajectory, but the philosophers seem to care whether unpredictability of consequences undermines a whole theory of right action — whether I should judge my choices by actual effects, by effects relative to what I could foresee, or by some expectation that maybe I'm not even in a position to form. So tell me straight: is that residue a sign that my vocabulary is missing something (evaluative concepts that dynamical systems talk simply can't carry), or is it a sign that the philosophers' worry is less well-posed than it looks — just the finiteness of a predictability horizon dressed up in moral language? My daughter insists it's the first, but she's biased, definately.
