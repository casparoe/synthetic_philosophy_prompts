I got into a long argument with a friend after a seminar on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and I want a second opinion, in number form.

Here's the setup as we understand it: Harsanyi shows that if each individual's preferences satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms over lotteries, and the social preference ordering satisfies the vNM axioms too, plus a Pareto condition, then the social utility function has to be a weighted sum of individual vNM utilities. So far so good, and so far not utilitarianism — the weights could be anything. My friend says this is the whole result: it's just a representation theorem, and the famous "therefore utilitarianism" gloss is like concluding from a theorem about rational belief that you must believe any particular thing. I pushed back that Harsanyi also had the impartial observer / veil of ignorance argument: if you imagine one person's utility numbers as assigned behind a veil where they could turn out to be anyone with equal probability, equal weights fall out. So the package — aggregation theorem plus the impartiality supplement — really does nail down sum-ranking with equal weights as the uniquely rational social rule. My friend says the two arguments don't combine cleanly, because the impartial observer argument smuggles in its own controversial assumptions about risk and interpersonal comparability, and also someone in the seminar brought up the old coin flip objection: if social preferences themselves have to satisfy vNM independence, then society can't care whether a fixed pie goes to Alice for sure or via a fair coin between Alice and Bob, which seems to mean the setup can't represent fairness at all — so maybe the axioms aren't binding at the social level in the first place, and the theorem's premises are the weak link rather than the weights.
