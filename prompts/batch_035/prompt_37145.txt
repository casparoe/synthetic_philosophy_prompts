For a semester project at my high school, I have been following the restitution debates surrounding our city's museum of art and archaeology, and I have run into a puzzle I cannot solve on my own. The museum's collection was largely assembled by a single industrialist around 1900, and over the past decade its board of trustees has decided four claims that were covered in depth by the local paper. First, brass plaques and carved ivories taken in the 1897 British punitive expedition against Benin City: the board voted to return them, and it did. Second, carved ancestor figures that missionaries seized from a shrine during a conversion campaign in 1911, with the missionaries' own journals describing how they cleared the shrine and shipped the figures home; the claim came from the community's own council of elders, and the board returned those as well. Third, woven textiles that the museum's founder bought directly from the weavers who made them in the 1920s, at prices his ledgers record as fair; a diaspora cultural association claimed them, and the board declined, though it offered a long-term loan and shared curation. Fourth, pottery excavated in the 1930s under an official finds-sharing arrangement with the colonial antiquities service, which split the objects between the excavation and the museums of the colonial power; the modern national government claimed the pieces, and the board declined, while funding a joint research partnership with the national museum.

Now a fifth claim is pending, and the director described it at a training session for us student docents, so I have been trying to work out what the board will do. The object is a carved ancestral drum. Our records show a bill of sale from 1902 signed by the district commissioner of the colonial administration, who sold it to the museum's founder; his diary notes only that he had acquired it in trade during the pacification of the region. The community's elders say that under their own law the drum belonged to the shrine rather than to any individual, that no one, not even a chief, had the authority to sell it, and that whatever the commissioner traded for, it was not his to trade. The claim is being brought jointly by the national government and the community council, the same pairing that succeeded in the Benin case.

The trustees keep saying that their rulings turn on the circumstances of acquisition and on who has standing to claim, but I cannot tell which of those considerations is really doing the work, or whether they even pull in the same direction here. What do you think the board will decide, and what rule or rules best explain its record so far? It seems to me that more than one rule fits the first four cases: perhaps something about documented legal title, perhaps something about whether the originating community consented under its own norms, and perhaps something treating communal and sacred objects as a special, inalienable class. Do those rules genuinely agree on the first four claims and come apart on the drum, or does one of them already strain to explain, say, the excavation case? If they do come apart, which rule should the board follow to stay consistent with itself, and what further claim, hypothetical or otherwise, would best let an outside observer tell which rule the board is actually running?
