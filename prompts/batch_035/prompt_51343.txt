Two senior colleagues at a national risk-regulation agency have been arguing for a decade. One is a committed defender of the precautionary principle in its strong reading: a plausible threat of serious or irreversible harm shifts the burden of proof onto the proponent of the activity, and residual scientific uncertainty is never itself a reason to wait. The other is a committed Bayesian risk assessor, who holds that without probabilities attached to outcomes, precaution either covertly reuses expected-utility reasoning or paralyzes, since every option — including inaction — carries risks of its own. They have finally agreed to a structured adversarial collaboration, and you are to draft the joint statement it produces.

Use as the running case a proposal their agency must actually evaluate: a phased field release of gene-drive mosquitoes engineered to drive a female-sterility gene through local Anopheles populations in a malaria-endemic region. The precautionist's concern is that a gene drive is self-propagating and, once released, effectively irreversible, so that any unanticipated ecological effect cannot be undone; the Bayesian's concern is that every additional year of delay is another year in which hundreds of thousands of people, mostly young children, die of malaria, so that waiting for more evidence is itself a decision with a body count.

Draft the document both could sign. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about the distinction between quantifiable risk and deeper uncertainty, about irreversibility and option value, about the reality of risk–risk tradeoffs, about what could count as evidence for or against a release like this — each phrased so that neither side concedes anything it does not actually accept, and each substantive rather than boilerplate.
2. A disagreements table: for each genuine disagreement, a formulation of the precautionist's position that he would accept as written, a formulation of the Bayesian's position that she would accept as written, and the exact point at which the two formulations part ways.
3. The cruxes: the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of the parties, each stated in neutral wording neither could dismiss as loaded.
4. For each crux, at least one argument, test, staged-trial design, or long-term monitoring scheme that both agree would bear on it, with each party's prediction about the outcome written down in advance.
5. A finding on which parts of the decade-long dispute were merely verbal — disputes about words like safe, proven, or natural — and which parts were substantive.

Do not have either side win. The work is in finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and in separating what genuinely divides them from what was noise. End the document with a single machine-readable line, \crux{...}, giving in one sentence the deepest disagreement that survives the collaboration.
