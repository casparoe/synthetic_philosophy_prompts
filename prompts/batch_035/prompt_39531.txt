you're a decision theorist who also writes formal verse, and I need a sestina about the Pasadena paradox where the form does real philosophical work, not just decoration.

quick refresher since it's an obscure one: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, say on toss n. The St. Petersburg game would pay you 2^n; the Pasadena game pays 2^n/n with the sign alternating — heads on toss 1 you win 2, toss 2 you lose 2, toss 3 you win 8/3, toss 4 you lose 4, and so on. Each toss's expected contribution is ±1/n, so the "expectation" is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 ..., which converges — but only conditionally. By the Riemann rearrangement theorem, summing the outcomes in a different order gives a different total; you can make it anything you like. The game has no fact of the matter about its value until someone fixes an ordering, and no ordering is privileged.

that's exactly why a sestina: six end-words, the same six every stanza, but permuted by the fixed pattern each time — same materials, rearranged, different result. I want the six end-words to be the load-bearing concepts (something like heads, tails, gain, loss, order, sum — or pick better ones) and the poem should track the running total drifting as the stanza order changes. The envoi, using all six words, should land the real point: it's not that the game has a hidden true value we haven't found yet — it's that "what is this game worth" was ill-posed until someone said how to count, and ordinary expected-value reasoning has no room for that thought. Someone who knows Riemann rearrangement should recognize it from the structure alone, and someone who doesn't should still get a good poem out of it about a coin that can't settle on a price.

please brainstorm the structure first before writing — sketch which end-word lands where in each stanza and what the running total is doing — and don't resolve the paradox. the poem's whole point is that the reordering never stops.
