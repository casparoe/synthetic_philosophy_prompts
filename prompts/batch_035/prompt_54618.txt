so heres the thing. me and my brother in law have been having the same argument for like three years now and I want to settle it properly. he's a military history guy and his whole worldview is that history hangs on tiny stuff — a messenger getting delayed, a general falling off his horse, and then the entire next century goes differently. I keep saying no, the big outcomes (empires rising, industrialization happening, whatever) would've arrived anyway just with different names on them. and we never get anywhere, and I've finally figured out why: neither of us has any idea what would actually count as one of us being right. we're just trading vibes.

so what I want is the spec. before anyone — us, a historian, whoever — claims something like "the revolution was doomed to happen" or "if that one battle goes the other way we're all speaking a different language," what does the claim have to deliver to even be a claim? my first pass at what a decent account needs, tell me if I'm right and add what I'm missing:

- it has to keep apart a cause and a background condition (the assassination started the war vs. everyone was already armed to the teeth and looking for an excuse) — because if you can't, then everything explains everything and nothing explains anything
- it has to owe us something about the counterfactual world, not just wave at it. like if you say the outcome would've happened "anyway," you're saying something about a world where the nail was there, and I want to know if we can actually say anything about that world or whether it's just making up fiction with extra confidence
- it has to be specific about what counts as "the same thing happening anyway." this is one I think I came up with myself: there's a difference between an outcome being fragile (one thing goes wrong and it just doesn't happen) and being replaceable (it doesn't happen, but something else with roughly the same downstream effects does). if the printing press never gets invented but literacy spreads anyway through some other channel, is that "it would've happened anyway" or not? any account that mushes those two together is useless to me
- it has to be consistent with what the actual people could've known and done at the time, not just what we know looking back. explanations always look obvious in hindsight and I feel like a good account has to say why it's not cheating to use the hindsight

for each requirement tell me what ordinary explaining-it-to-someone breaks if the requirement isn't met — I want the reason each one is load bearing, not just that it sounds nice. then two more things. first: which of these are real requirements and which are just stuff people repeat, and can any two of them not be satisfied at the same time? my hunch is the "say something real about the counterfactual" one and the "stay consistent with what people actually knew" one are in tension, because the more specific you get about the other world the more you're inventing people who never existed doing things no one could predict, but I honestly can't tell if that's a real problem or just me being confused.

second, real quick, run the three pictures regular people like us actually use against your list: the great man version (individuals swing everything), the deep forces version (economics and geography and it was coming regardless), and the chaos version (it's all sensitive to everything so nobody can say anything, both of us are wrong). which one survives the list best, and does the list tell us whether me and my brother in law are arguing about a fact — how contingent the past really was — or just about which kind of story we like better? because I'd genuinely accept "it's a fact but you two will never measure it" as an answer, I just want to know which fight I'm in.
