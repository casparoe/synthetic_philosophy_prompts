As a novelist, I find that Sartre's notion of bad faith is easier to apply to characters than to people, and I have begun to suspect I apply it inconsistently even to my own. Here are my verdicts, and I would like to know whether some principle captures them or whether some must be overturned. A waiter who performs his waiterness, treating the role as if it exhausted what he is: bad faith, textbook. A woman on a date who lets her companion take her hand and lives the moment as though it is happening to someone else: bad faith. But my small-town doctor, who says that being a doctor is not what he does but what he is, and who means it serenely, I cannot bring myself to call bad faith — on the page it reads as integrity. My character who says she cannot leave her disabled husband, though no law and no gun compels her: not bad faith, at least not as I have written her. My blocked writer who says he cannot work because he is depressed: also not bad faith in my ears, though he is, I take it, no less free in Sartre's sense than the one who cannot leave. And my man who explains his cruelty by his childhood: bad faith, definitely. Is there a principle that distinguishes the doctor from the waiter, and the two who say they cannot from the one who explains by his past, or must I give up some of these verdicts? If you can state such a principle precisely, I would like to know what it says about a character I am stuck on: a man who describes himself as a novelist not by choice — it is simply what he is — and who never once questions it.
