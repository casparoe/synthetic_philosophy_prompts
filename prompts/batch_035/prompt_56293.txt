I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on virtue ethics, and I've written myself into a corner with the term-paper argument below. My commentator's only written comment was "this proves far too much — surely you can't believe your own grandmother was never kind," and I can't see whether she means the argument is invalid or just that I should bite a bullet. I'd like to know exactly which step to reject, why that step looked fine to me, and what rejecting it costs.

The argument:

(1) To genuinely possess a character trait such as honesty or compassion is, at minimum, to have a disposition that manifests in trait-relevant situations, so that knowing how a person behaved in one such situation should let you predict their behavior in others substantially better than chance.

(2) In the classic situationist experiments — the Good Samaritan study, the bystander and ambient-noise studies, the dime-in-the-phone-booth mood studies — small situational differences (being in a hurry, a loud lawnmower, finding a coin) changed trait-relevant behavior dramatically, while measured individual differences predicted very little of the variance.

(3) So trait-relevant behavior is not cross-situationally consistent: situations dominate, and the person contributes almost nothing predictable.

(4) Therefore people do not possess character traits.

(5) Virtue ethics presupposes that some people possess stable character traits expressed across situations; hence virtue ethics rests on empirically disconfirmed psychology.

(6) But the evidence in (2) has the same structure as evidence we have about other states that only imperfectly predict behavior: stated political attitudes predict individual voting decisions rather weakly, and single actions are often predicted better by context than by professed conviction. By parity of reasoning, no one has political convictions, religious commitments, or tastes either.

(7) If no one has traits, then ordinary trait-attributions — that someone is generous, cowardly, patient — are systematically false, and appraising persons rather than acts is never warranted.

(8) So we should abandon both virtue ethics and the everyday practice of praising and blaming people for who they are.

Here is my predicament. I'm fairly confident of (2) — the experimental readings are what the whole situationist literature turns on, and I don't want to deny the data. I can't accept the second half of (8), and frankly I'm not comfortable with (6) either, though I can't say why it doesn't follow. If I had to guess, I suspect something happens between (3) and (4), or between (2) and (3), but every time I try to pin it down I end up relying on exactly the intuition (4) was supposed to be testing. Please tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it looked acceptable, what the standard defenses of virtue ethics would say about it, and what each of them has to pay.
