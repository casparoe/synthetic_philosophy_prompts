I'm an undergraduate in a third-year metaphysics course on dispositions and powers, and for our last seminar of the term each of us has to bring a short piece that captures one of the problems we've studied. I've drawn the counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis — the finks and the masks — and since our professor has a weakness for formal verse, I'd like to submit a poem rather than another prose handout.

Write me a villanelle about a fragile glass that never breaks. The two refrains should carry the philosophical content between them: one refrain should say, in effect, that the glass is fragile; the other should report that it never broke. You'll have to choose the wording so that they rhyme, but their juxtaposition at the end of the poem should do real work — the reader should feel that in a fink case the stimulus and the non-manifestation are not merely compatible but internally connected, since the very blow that should shatter the glass is what summons the sorcerer who renders it no longer fragile at all.

In the middle stanzas I'd like the poem to distinguish the fink case from the mask case, accurately — perhaps by introducing a second object, a poison rendered harmless by an antidote, where the disposition survives intact and only its manifestation is blocked. Someone who knows the literature on finkish dispositions and antidotes should be able to tell which stanza is which and articulate why the difference matters (one case removes or alters the categorical basis, the other merely interposes between basis and display); someone who doesn't know it should simply find a good villanelle about a glass that refuses its fate. Please don't let the ending collapse into a verdict on whether the glass was "really" fragile — leave that tension standing roughly where the literature leaves it. If one line can glance sideways at the opposite direction of failure, the mimicker that would break without being fragile, so much the better, but don't distort the form to fit it in.

Keep to strict villanelle form: nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, the two refrains recurring at stanza ends and concluding the poem together, ABA rhyme throughout.
