I am a novelist drafting the climactic chapter of a book in which a volunteer lifeboat coxswain, in heavy seas, can bring only one more person aboard and must choose between his estranged son and a stranger. My editor has suggested that the coxswain should consciously weigh the two lives before acting, so that readers can see his partiality is morally defensible rather than mere bias. I suspect this runs afoul of Bernard Williams's integrity objection — his complaint, at the end of his essay Persons, Character and Morality, that if a man who saves his wife must reach for an impartial justification for doing so, the theory demanding that justification has burdened him with one thought too many.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology and post-war Anglophone ethics. Think carefully, beginning with a brief brainstorm of the possible readings of Williams's point, and then describe the most important implications of the integrity objection for how I should write this scene. In particular, I would like you to address: whether the coxswain's interior monologue should contain any deliberation at all, or whether deliberation itself is the failure Williams is pointing at; what the objection exposes about the difference between justifying a choice and being motivated by that justification, including how the utilitarian's standard reply — that people with partial loyalties generally produce the best outcomes — manages to accommodate the coxswain while still missing the point; and how I can dramatize a man who has a reason he could give without being moved by the giving of it, without rendering him either a moral saint or a brute.
