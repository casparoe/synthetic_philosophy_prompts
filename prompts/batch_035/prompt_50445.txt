Draft:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems are probably the most overused results in the history of mathematics. They are invoked to prove the existence of God, the futility of physics, the immortality of the soul, and the impossibility of artificial intelligence. Almost all of these invocations are wrong, but explaining why requires saying precisely what Gödel actually proved.

The first incompleteness theorem states that any formal system is incomplete: there will always be true statements in its language that it cannot prove. The proof works by constructing a sentence that, in effect, says of itself that it is not provable in the system. If the system could prove this sentence, it would have proved a falsehood, so the sentence must be unprovable — and since it truly is unprovable, it is true. Notice that we, standing outside the system, can see that the sentence is true. This already suggests that mathematical truth cannot be captured by any mechanical procedure, since whatever rules a machine follows, we can construct a statement it cannot decide but we can.

The second theorem sharpens this: a consistent system cannot prove its own consistency. This is the source of the famous argument, developed by Lucas and later by Penrose, that the human mind is not a machine. Any machine, being a formal system, cannot prove its own consistency; but mathematicians can see, by direct inspection, that arithmetic is consistent. Therefore the mathematician is not a machine. Penrose applied the same reasoning to computers running sophisticated software: however clever the program, there will be arithmetic truths forever beyond its reach, truths we can grasp.

The second theorem also has consequences for physics. Physicists dream of a theory of everything, but a theory of everything would be a formal system, and therefore incomplete. Hawking himself conceded this: since we are ourselves part of the physical system we are trying to describe, our physics can never be complete. The dream of a final theory dies at Gödel's hands.

What do the theorems actually show? Only this: that any consistent, effectively axiomatized theory strong enough to express arithmetic will leave some arithmetic statements undecidable. This is a result about formal axiomatic systems, not about minds, machines in general, or the universe. A chess-playing machine is not rendered impossible by Gödel, and neither is any practical AI, since nothing in engineering requires a system that proves every arithmetic truth."
