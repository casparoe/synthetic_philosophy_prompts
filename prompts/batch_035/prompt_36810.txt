I co-run a small podcast that lives at the intersection of typography and cognitive science — think long rants about why Comic Sans bothers people plus detours into perception research. I've been lurking in rationalist spaces since like 2012 so our audience skews LessWrong-adjacent and is *extremely* intolerant of unfalsifiable framing. Next week's guest is a design theorist who holds a position he calls the skeleton view: a letter is not a shape but a structure. Every 'a' you've ever read instantiates the same abstract stroke skeleton — roughly, a bowl closed on itself plus a stem, joined in a specifiable way — and the wildly different a's in a Didone, a blackletter, and my terrible gel pen handwriting are all renderings of that one structure, like different performances of a single score. He thinks this explains both how we read novel fonts at all (we're not matching templates, we're parsing structure) and why there's such a thing as a *wrong* a even in an unfamiliar style. He's not a crank — he's spent years on this — but he is very committed.

Draft me eight to ten questions that go beyond having him restate the view. I want at least one question each on: (1) double-storey vs single-storey a's and g's — if the skeleton is what makes a letter that letter, are those actually two different letters that convention tells us to treat as one, or is he going to claim there's a shared skeleton underneath, and if so what on earth is it; (2) the confusability cases — in some geometric sans-serifs the single-storey a is nearly identical to a u with a tail, and readers lean on word context to disambiguate, which starts to smell like the skeleton is doing less work than he wants it to; (3) where skeletons come from — a kid learns letters from basically one or two fonts, so what exactly are they learning, and how does the skeleton get abstracted away from those particular shapes, is this even a well-defined process; and (4) how his view relates to Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit research direction, the idea that a letter category is less a fixed template and more something like a style that can re-express itself in new contexts — ally, rival, or the same thing wearing different clothes.

For each of those, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Fair warning: he's partial to the reply that the skeleton is like the grammar of a language — generative, not a lookup table — so at least one follow-up should test whether that analogy can actually carry the weight or whether it's just vibes with a linguistics costume on. One question should also force him to say what observation about real readers or real type would count *against* the skeleton view, because our comment section WILL notice if he can't answer that. Keep the register conversational, we banter on the show, but don't let him get away with anything — last time we had a guest, the top comment was someone pointing out he'd quietly redefined a key term halfway through the episode and I still havent lived it down.
