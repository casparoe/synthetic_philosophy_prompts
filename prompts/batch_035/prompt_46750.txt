You are an expert facilitator in philosophy of biology who has spent years designing simulation games for university seminars — you know both the evolutionary theory and the practical craft of getting a room of undergraduates to run into a conceptual problem before anyone names it.

Design an in-class game for the following setting: an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the units of selection debate, 16 students, one 80-minute session, a flat classroom with movable desks. The game should make participants feel, in their own play, the two central pressures of the debate. First: a trait that is costly to its bearer but beneficial to the group can spread, but only when the competitive advantage of cooperative groups over selfish groups outweighs the advantage selfish players get inside their groups — the participants should experience both directions of pressure simultaneously, and discover that which one wins is a matter of relative magnitudes, not a matter of logic. Second, and this is the part I care most about: at the end, the identical history of play should be re-scored on the board under a different bookkeeping — the same events presented once as group-level selection (some groups out-reproduced others) and once as a gene's-eye accounting (certain strategy cards increased their frequency across the whole population) — so that participants feel that the dispute is partly about where we draw the ledger lines, not just about what happened.

Before writing the final specification, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate mechanics and say in a sentence or two why you chose the one you did. Then give the full runnable design: rules, materials (nothing exotic — cards, tokens, a whiteboard), how many rounds and what happens in each, how groups reproduce or dissolve, a payoff scheme that uses points rather than money, and how you keep individual players motivated to play their cards rather than altruistically throwing the game for the sake of the "right" outcome. Include the debrief in detail: map specific moments of play onto within-group selection, between-group variance, and the re-scoring exercise, and actually walk through how you would do the two-ledger blackboard demonstration using the round data the class just generated. Note that students often commit what one might call the averaging fallacy — concluding that group-level effects must be weak because selfish players obviously do well within their groups — and design the debrief so someone in the room reliably voices exactly that thought and the data answers them.

Also tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how should I handle it? And which feature of the debate can the game not reproduce at all, such that I must supply it verbally? In particular, think about the fact that in the game students *choose* their cards while genes do not choose anything, and about whether your re-scoring demonstration genuinely shows an equivalence of descriptions or only an analogy — say plainly where the analogy is load-bearing.

Do not simply import an existing classroom exercise you may have seen; the mechanics should be built for this session. But keep every theoretical notion you rely on either standard in the units-of-selection literature or defined within this prompt, so that the design is intelligible to someone working only from what they know. Write the whole thing as a polished, self-contained facilitator's plan.
