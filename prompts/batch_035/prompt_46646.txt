You are a professional philosopher of history with expertise in perpetrator studies, the historiography of the Holocaust, and the ethics of interpreting perpetrators in fiction. Please think carefully and methodically about what follows, begin with some brief brainstorming before you commit to a reading, and write your answer in British English throughout.

I teach an undergraduate module on the representation of perpetrators, and next term I pair the Browning–Goldhagen controversy with Bernhard Schlink's novel The Reader (Der Vorleser, 1995). I want to work out whether the novel takes a side in that controversy, or whether it quietly restructures it.

To frame the controversy for students: Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) reads the participation of Reserve Police Battalion 101 in mass shootings as the product of situation — conformity to peers, deference to authority, the difficulty of stepping out in front of one's comrades, gradual brutalisation — against a background of men who mostly found the killing unpleasant. Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) replies that this understates the cultural cause: an eliminationist antisemitism pervasive enough in German society that ordinary Germans required no special pressure to kill. My question is where Schlink's novel falls, and I suspect the answer is not straightforward.

Here is my worry. The novel's central device is Hanna Schmitz's illiteracy. She leaves her job at Siemens rather than accept a promotion that would expose that she cannot read, and joins the SS instead — a decision taken alone, before any situational pressure and without any evident antisemitism: a motive belonging to neither Browning's categories nor Goldhagen's. Yet once inside the machinery, her conduct looks textbook Browning. The episode at the burning church — where guards kept the doors locked on dying prisoners, partly out of fear of the chaos of opening them, partly because a decision to open them would have marked the one who made it as soft — seems designed for the situationalist reading. And her over-confession at trial, admitting authorship of a report she could not have written rather than submit to a handwriting test, is conformity of another, stranger kind. Meanwhile the other defendants collude smoothly to shift blame onto her, which is exactly the ordinary-men dynamics Browning describes, now operating against her.

So I would like a reading of the novel through both books. Which scenes would you point students to as carrying the philosophical weight, and what should each show? Does the illiteracy device displace the whole debate by giving Hanna a motive that is neither situational nor cultural — and if so, is that a deepening of the controversy or a flinch away from it? (I know the novel has been accused of handing its perpetrator an alibi, and of aestheticising guilt; I want to know whether that accusation sticks given that the alibi explains her joining the SS but not her conduct afterwards.) Where does the novel resist the Browning reading, and where does it resist the Goldhagen reading? And what does it add to the controversy that neither history book can — for instance, the perspective of the second generation, embodied in Michael, whose love for Hanna and judgement of her are entangled in ways no third-person historiography has to face? Please be honest about the points where the novel is doing something the philosophical categories simply do not reach, and about any scene that fits both readings equally well, or neither.
