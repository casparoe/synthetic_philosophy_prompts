I host a small podcast on machine learning research, and next week's guest is an interpretability researcher known for defending a fairly strong realist position: the features that interpretability tools find — the directions, circuits, and activation patterns that we label 'sentiment' or 'refusal' or 'deception' — are genuine properties of the network, discovered rather than invented, in roughly the way chemistry discovers real molecular structure. Her critics in the field dismiss feature talk as a convenient lens: a network is, at bottom, just stacked matrix multiplications, and calling a particular linear direction a 'deception feature' is projection of human categories onto arithmetic, not discovery of anything. Draft ten to twelve interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view. I specifically want at least one question each on the following, though you may distribute them as you see fit: (i) polysemanticity and superposition — whether the facts that individual neurons often encode several unrelated things, and that features become visible only once we search in a basis far larger than the model's own dimensions, suggest that features are artifacts of our chosen decompositions rather than properties the model itself has; (ii) whether causal interventions settle anything — if ablating a direction reliably removes a behavior, have we located something in the model, or merely confirmed that this description is predictively useful, which an instrumentalist can happily accept; and (iii) whether her view is just scientific realism applied to artifacts, such that 'the model has a deception feature' should be on all fours with 'the metal is conductive' — and if so, whether that comparison helps her, given that models are engineered objects whose internal organization was never selected for human legibility. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and at least two of those follow-ups should make it progressively harder for her to retreat to the formulation that features are 'whatever our best tools carve' — a phrasing that sounds realist while arguably conceding everything to her critics. Please keep the questions technically precise enough that she cannot answer with platitudes about models being 'black boxes.'
