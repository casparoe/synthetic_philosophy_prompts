I'm teaching a graduate seminar next spring on interpersonal aggregation — the question of how, and whether, we may sum tiny harms or benefits across many lives — and I want to open the semester with the standards of adequacy rather than with the theories. Write the desiderata: the full specification of what any adequate theory of the aggregation of small harms has to deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement.

Here are the distinctions and cases I want you to make sure the list engages with, though you should add others as you see fit:

1. The scattered-harm problem: distinguish harms that are individually sub-experiential — below the threshold of notice or later recall, a pebble in the shoe you forget by evening — from harms that are merely small but registered. A theory must say whether these differ morally, and if so, on what basis. The standard dust-specks case blurs this distinction, and I want it unblurred.

2. The intra- vs. interpersonal asymmetry: a thousand pinpricks in one life plausibly amount to something worse than the sum of a thousand pinpricks spread over a thousand lives (or, on some views, they don't — fatigue, memory, adaptation all matter within a life and not across lives). Whether a theory may treat the person as the unit of aggregation, or must treat moments or episodes as the unit, is itself up for grabs; the desiderata should be stated so that both answers are possible to check against.

3. Continuity and thresholds: say what, if anything, a theory must deliver about whether there is a threshold below which harms cannot sum to a serious harm at any population size — and note that a theory that posits such a threshold owes us an account of why the threshold is where it is rather than slightly above or below.

4. Aggregation as a practice: separate the requirements on the aggregation facts themselves from the requirements on how an agent should act given those facts. A theory under which the facts aggregate but no finite agent can permissibly act on the aggregation (because of uncertainty, because the harms are untraceable, because of downstream effects) should still count as a theory of aggregation; whether that is so should be settled by the desiderata, not smuggled into the theories after the fact.

For each requirement, give the case that motivates it, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (a philosophy must mark what's settled and what isn't: it has become clear to me in this literature that what one theorist regards as a datum another regards as a theoretical posit, so I want you to explicitly flag which of your desiderata are genuinely uncontested among serious theorists of aggregation and which would be disputed by a reasonable minority — do not present the contested ones as consensus, and do not hedge on the uncontested ones either). Where you suspect a subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, any combination that forces both a continuity requirement and a threshold requirement — say so directly and explain the tension; that is the part I most want to see, and I'll structure a seminar meeting around it.

Then, briefly, assess lexical-threshold views (harms below a line cannot sum upward), rough unit-comparability views, and plain additive totalism against your list — no more than a short paragraph each, since the scoring is for my students to do, not for you. Keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them.

I'd also like a short closing note on which single desideratum does the most work excluding theories, and whether that desideratum is itself one of the contested ones — if our most powerful filter is also our most disputed assumption, that fact deserves more attention than it usually gets.
