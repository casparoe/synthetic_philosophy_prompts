I have been following the experimental philosophy literature on the side-effect effect for a while now, and I have come to suspect that the interpretive debate about it is permanently stalled, so I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than another survey of the positions. To be concrete about the target: in the original chairman-style vignettes, people are far more willing to say that an agent who foresaw a harm and went ahead anyway brought it about intentionally than to say the same about an agent who foresaw a benefit and went ahead anyway, even though the causal and epistemic structure of the two cases is identical. Everyone agrees on the data; the fight is over what the asymmetry shows. One camp, associated with Knobe himself, treats it as evidence that the ordinary concept of intentional action is constitutively normative, so that judgments about blame partly constitute rather than merely accompany judgments about intentionality. A second camp reads it as a performance effect: folk theory of mind does the real work, and the asymmetry is a downstream bias in how blame colors our reports, in the spirit of the early Adams and Steadman catch-trial proposal. A third camp, exemplified by Sripada's deep self concordance account, holds that intentionality judgments track whether the outcome expresses the agent's true attitudes, and that morality enters only because moral valence is evidence of such expression. The stalemate is what interests me: every new manipulation — versions where the agent is indifferent rather than antagonistic, versions with corporate or nonhuman agents, versions where the side effect is intended by the agent but not by the corporation, cross-cultural replications, reversed versions where the harmful side effect is desired — gets absorbed by each camp without any of them conceding anything. It is not that the experiments are bad; it is that each experiment seems underdetermined with respect to the interpretive question.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. I suspect the three camps are not actually disagreeing about a single question. The normative-concept camp is answering a question about the semantics or functional role of the word "intentional" in our practice of holding responsible; the performance camp is answering a question about the cognitive processes that generate particular judgments; and the deep self camp is answering a question about what property of agents our concept tracks. If that is right, then the same experimental result can be taken as a fact about the concept, a fact about the psycholinguistics of reporting, and a fact about the tracking relation, respectively, and the debate is partly verbal or at least question-disambiguating rather than a straightforward empirical dispute. But I can also see a reading on which there is a genuine substantive disagreement hidden inside: whether the asymmetry would survive in agents about whom we suspend all blame-relevant attitudes, which looks like a real empirical question about whether moral evaluation is load-bearing in the judgment itself or merely a cue. Tell me which diagnosis you think is right, supported by what the parties actually claim rather than by labels like "revisionist" and "traditionalist," and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a dissociation experiment, a conceptual distinction, a cross-cultural or developmental result, a change of question — or whether the impasse should simply be accepted as the price of asking a question that conflates semantics with psychology.
