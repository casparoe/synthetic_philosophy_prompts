Some background, since the case is for me and its purpose constrains what counts as cheating. I run a small catering company. After a fire in the commissary kitchen last spring and a slow winter, we have perhaps four months of operating cash against monthly costs that do not stop. A friend who trades for a living keeps telling me that the disciplined move, the Kelly move, is to size every exposure modestly and never, under any circumstances, risk ruin. The advice is meant kindly, but it has begun to sound as though it assumes ruin is something I might avoid, when ruin is in fact my default trajectory: cautious play does not preserve my capital, it burns it. My suspicion is that the geometric-growth logic my friend appeals to — the one usually sold as the reason not to buy lottery tickets — actually endorses something close to desperation gambling once a fixed survival cost and a hard deadline enter the picture. I want that demonstrated properly, not asserted, because my next three months may depend on it.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

1. One paragraph, and realistic: a sole proprietor (caterer, contractor, shopkeeper — your choice) with fixed per-period costs, one repeatedly available business opportunity that is genuinely favorable — positive expected return on whatever is staked, every round, and not absurdly favorable — and a hard deadline at which a fixed sum is due or the business is lost. No casinos, no posted odds, no demons, stakes an ordinary person could actually face.

2. The key verdicts must fall out of the reasoning, not be stipulated. Show that an agent maximizing geometric growth of wealth — expected log wealth, or however you choose to formalize the geometric-rationality framework, but with no curved or kinked utility function beyond the logarithm — stakes close to the plain Kelly fraction when comfortably capitalized, yet gambles essentially the entire surplus, round after round, accepting a substantial probability of going to zero, when undercapitalized. The desperation must come from the structure of the case — the burn and the deadline — not from a taste for risk assumed into the agent.

3. Somewhere in the analysis the expected-wealth maximizer should appear. I suspect he goes all-in immediately and is ruined with probability near one while maximizing expectation; if so, say so, and tell me whether there is a region where the geometric agent's policy converges toward his, and whether that convergence is a reductio of geometric rationality or the whole point of it.

4. Then the walkthrough. State each verdict and why. Be explicit about how you formalize ruin inside the geometric framework — this seems to me the delicate part — and say which single feature of the case is doing the work. Show what happens when that feature is removed: does ordinary, scale-invariant, myopic Kelly betting reappear, along with the usual property by which the log-utility bettor can ignore the future?

5. Finally, steelman my friend. He will say my model is rigged: that I am not truly ruined at zero — I have a trade, a family, future earnings — that human capital makes the floor non-absorbing and the desperation an artifact of treating liquid cash as the whole of wealth. Take the objection seriously and tell me whether it dissolves the case or merely relocates the floor; and if the honest answer is that a person with a sellable skill should never behave as desperately as my scenario says, tell me that plainly.

One more thread, briefly. I understand Friedman and Savage explained the poor buying lottery tickets by a cleverly curved utility function. What I want from your construction is the same conclusion with no curve at all — just arithmetic, burn, and time. If it works, then the standard lecture against lotteries, delivered in the name of geometric growth, needs a footnote it rarely gets, and I would like to be able to state that footnote precisely.
