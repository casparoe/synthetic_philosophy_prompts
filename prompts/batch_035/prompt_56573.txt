At a family bbq last month my cousin and my sister's boyfriend Dave (an EMT) spent a solid hour arguing about a hypothetical from Dave's training days, and none of us could referee it. I'm hoping you can go through their argument move by move and say who was right about what — not just a final tally.

The setup they were arguing about: you're first on the scene after a pileup on a highway. One victim is on the northbound side, five are trapped on the southbound side. You only have time to get to one side before it's too late for the other. Everything else is equal — nobody's a friend of yours, all six are equally injured, equally innocent, etc.

(1) My cousin said this is a no-brainer: you go to the five. Five deaths is plainly worse than one death, and choosing the smaller good when the costs to you are identical is basically malicous.

(2) Dave said: worse for *whom*? The five don't die some collective death — each of them loses his own life, one at a time. If he heads north, then nobody in the world is worse off than they would have been had he headed south: the five die, but they die anyway in that scenario too, and the one guy gets to live. So name the person who's harmed by his choice. My cousin's "five deaths is worse" is treating a headcount as if it were itself a victim.

(3) My cousin said that's sophistry and pushed back two ways. First, bad things can be bad without being bad *for* somebody — if a virus will kill either 5 people or 1,000, and it kills the 1,000, the outbreak is worse, full stop. Second, Dave's own view smuggles numbers back in: if the southbound side had fifty people instead of five, Dave would go there without hesitating, so somewhere between five and fifty the numbers start counting for Dave too, and he owes us an account of where that happens and why *there*.

(4) Dave admitted he wasn't sure where, and floated an alternative: give everyone an equal chance. Flip a coin — heads he goes north, tails he goes south. My cousin said that's the opposite of equal: the lone guy gets a 50% shot at life while each southbound victim only gets 10%. Dave said no, the five are being saved or lost as a group, so it's one toss between two groups and each individual had as fair a chance as the world allows. My cousin pounced: this is the exact move Dave rejected in step (2) — the five suddenly get to be a corporate person when it helps him, but not when it counts against him.

(5) My cousin then went for the throat: suppose it's one on the north side and a million on the south. Still a coin flip? Dave said yes — the million have no claim to better-than-even odds just by existing, and his job isn't to make the universe's ledger come out nicely. My cousin said that means Dave is willing to let a million people die to give one guy a 50% chance, and if you can bite that bullet without flinching, something has gone wrong upstream in the reasoning. Dave said the alternative is that the lone guy's life counts for almost nothing the moment enough people stand near him, and that seemed at least as ugly to him.

At that point they were both tired and it dissolved into whether Dave could permissibly head north if the one guy was his friend, which went nowhere. If that thread matters for the overall verdict, feel free to include it, but the main thing I want is a verdict on each of the five steps above: who's right at each one, where is someone talking past the other, and who's more right overall?

One more thing — I think this argument is basically reenacting a 1977 paper by a philosopher named Taurek, whose example involved a guy named David? If so, fine, but I don't want a book report on him; I want you to judge the argument as Dave and my cousin actually made it.
