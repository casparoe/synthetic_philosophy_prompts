I teach an undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I want to pair the unit on peer disagreement with Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957), but I want to do more than show the film and let students cheer. Read the film through the conciliationism-versus-steadfastness debate and tell me what it actually dramatizes, including the ways it resists being conscripted by either side.

The obvious reading is that the film vindicates steadfastness: Juror 8 holds out against eleven identical initial votes and is proved right. But that reading looks shaky once you look closely, and I want you to press on it. Juror 8 never claims to know the defendant is innocent; he opens by saying only that he doesn't find the case beyond reasonable doubt. So is the film about steadfastness at all, or about something the formal debate arguably misses -- disagreement functioning not as a data point to be plugged into one's credences but as a trigger for reopening a joint inquiry, with the knife demonstration and the reconstructions of the two witnesses' testimony (the old man's walk, the woman across the street and her glasses)? The standard peer-disagreement literature tends to model fixed agents with private evidence adjusting their degrees of belief; the film models something much more interactive. Say whether that difference is philosophically significant or just cinematic.

There is also the question the peer condition raises, which the film seems to answer with a resounding no: these twelve are supposed to be epistemic peers by institutional fiat, but the film is largely a taxonomy of ways they aren't -- Juror 3's personal grievance, Juror 7's indifference, Juror 10's prejudice, Juror 4's intelligence wedded to an unexamined premise. Does the film thereby argue that the peer condition is never satisfied in practice and the whole debate is a philosopher's fantasy? Or does it show something subtler: that who counts as a peer is itself something discovered through the disagreement?

Finally, I want you to confront the film's own most uncomfortable feature: the vote shifts from 11-1 toward acquittal come partly through evidence, but partly through something like a conformity cascade -- men switching under the pressure of the room. If the ending is part epistemology and part group psychology, can the film really be used to teach the epistemology without misleading students? Tell me which scenes to assign, what the film adds to the debate that the argument alone doesn't, and where an honest critic has to admit the film is doing something other than philosophy.
