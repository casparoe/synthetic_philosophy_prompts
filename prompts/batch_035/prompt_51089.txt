I teach a political philosophy seminar, and last week we hit the unit on official apologies for historical wrongs -- parliament apologizing for slavery, the Japanese government and the comfort women, the pope apologizing for this or that, etc. In discussion, two of my students ended up talking past each other with dueling analogies and I realized I'd never actually sat down and worked out whether either analogy earns its keep, so now I'm assigning it back to them and want to see how a careful answer would go.

The skeptic in my class kept saying that a state apologizing for something done in 1850 is just like an individual apologizing for something their great-great-grandfather did: you weren't there, you didn't do it, you didn't benefit by choice, and so the apology is either empty theater or, worse, an immoral transfer of guilt to innocent people. The defender fired back that a state isn't like a person descended from a wrongdoer -- it's like a corporation that survives the complete turnover of its shareholders and employees: the entity that owes the pension obligations after the restructuring is numerically the same entity that incurred them, and similarly the state that apologizes today is the same continuing body politic that enacted the injustice, so the apology isn't inherited, it's first-personal.

So that's the assignment, and I want to grade it properly: lay out both analogies side by side with an explicit correspondence table for each -- what plays the role of the wrongdoer, the victim, the debt, the estate, the continuing entity, the assets that persist across turnover -- and then audit where each mapping breaks down. Specifically I want the students to separate the disanalogies that matter for what the analogies are being used to conclude (whether apology is meaningful, whether it implicates current citizens, whether it carries liability in some sense) from the ones that are just noise. And then the real question: does either side's conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If there's a better analogy for what official apologies actually are -- I suspect something like an institution speaking for its own record rather than any person-like agent, but I'd want that argued and not just asserted -- I want to see it developed and stress-tested the same way. My students, being students, will want to declare a winner in one paragraph, so I've told them the table and the separation of load-bearing from ornamental disanalogies is where the grade lives.
