I would like your credence, decomposed, on a question about cross-cultural moral disagreement — not a hedge, an actual number with its derivation shown.

The proposition I care about is this: that at least some moral claims are true independently of the attitudes of any actual or possible agents — that some mind-independent moral facts obtain. Treat it as having a determinate truth value, even if you suspect it is the sort of question on which credences are hard to pin down; if you think a point-valued credence misrepresents your epistemic state, say so, but give the point value anyway and explain the distortion involved.

The background I want taken seriously: moral-psychology research on WEIRD populations (Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic) suggests that folk morality varies considerably across cultures — in the relative weight placed on harm, purity, loyalty and authority, in whether morality is conceived as a system of universalisable rules binding all agents alike or as a web of role-specific duties to kin, neighbours and superiors, and in how far impartial strangers must be treated on a par with intimates. Defenders of objectivity have a stock reply: much of this variation is not genuine moral disagreement but divergence in non-moral factual beliefs or in background circumstances, so that shared thin principles are simply being applied to different inputs. The critics' rejoinder is that this reply proves too much: if it remains available no matter what the data show, then no amount of observed variation could ever count against objectivity, which makes the position suspiciously insulated.

To sharpen matters, consider a thought experiment I shall call the alignment test. Imagine two populations with identical non-moral factual beliefs, identical material circumstances, and full mutual knowledge of one another's moral codes, whose moral judgements nonetheless diverge on some proposition both understand completely. I take divergence that survives the alignment test to be the strongest case of genuine disagreement the empirical world could ever present.

Please provide the following:

1. Your overall credence in the proposition, as a number between 0 and 1.

2. A decomposition into the sub-credences doing the work. At a minimum I want: (a) your credence that a substantial fraction of actually observed cross-cultural moral variation would survive the alignment test; (b) your credence that alignment-surviving divergence would be evidence against mind-independent moral facts rather than merely evidence that at least one population is mistaken — note these come apart, since widespread error is perfectly compatible with objectivity; and (c) any further terms you think the overall number requires, with a one- or two-line justification for each. You need not treat the sub-credences as independent; tell me how you combine them into the overall figure.

3. The sub-credence to which your overall number is most sensitive, and the specific empirical finding or philosophical argument that would move it furthest.

4. My own credence in the proposition is roughly 0.65; a colleague who has read all the same literature sits around 0.25. In a sentence or two, tell me on which sub-credence the two of us most likely diverge, and what question we should put to each other first if we want to locate the disagreement rather than merely restate it.

Please write your answer in American English.
