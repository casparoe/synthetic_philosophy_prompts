I teach an intro ethics course that ends with the situationist critique of virtue ethics — the tradition running from Asch and Milgram up to the claim that what predicts people's behaviour is mostly features of the situation, and that global character traits like courage or honesty do very little explanatory work, maybe don't even exist. For the last session I wanted to do a film night instead of another lecture, and I've settled on the 1957 Sidney Lumet film 12 Angry Men, but I want to run it properly rather than just pointing at the screen and saying "see, justice!" (I first watched it years ago with my dad and only realised later that it's basically a social psychology experiment wearing a courtroom drama's clothes.)

Here's why it feels like the right fit, and where I'd like your help. The whole film is one situation — a locked room, unbearable heat, one juror desperate to get to a baseball game, an initial show of hands with eleven guilty votes — and most of the jurors change their verdicts in ways that track social pressure, shifting alliances, and small situational changes (a pair of glasses, a re-enactment of a limp, who happens to be standing where) far more than any sustained reasoning about the evidence. On a situationist reading the film looks like a dramatisation of the critique: these men don't have stable characters, they have dispositions that the room elicits and re-elicits. But then there's Juror 8, and the film's whole architecture depends on him possessing exactly the kind of trait — moral courage sustained against eleven hostile peers, which is more or less the Milgram setup — that situationists doubt exists at all. And Juror 3's final breakdown over his son reads like the film insisting on a deep characterological explanation after all, right when the situationist reading was winning.

So what I'd like is a proper reading of the film through the critique. Which jurors' reversals are best explained situationally, and do any genuinely resist that treatment? Is Juror 8 a counterexample to situationism, or does the film quietly rig his situation — control of the evidence props, the timing of each reveal, the informal leadership role he drifts into — such that even a good situationist could accept what happens in the room? Does the bigot juror's isolation near the end show character being defeated by situation, and what does the film seem to think that implies about whether people are educable or merely movable? And crucially, run it in the other direction too: does the film dramatise something the situationist literature itself underplays, namely that situations can be engineered by particular individuals — which seems to smuggle traits back in through the side door? Where the film resists the situationist reading I want you to say so plainly rather than forcing it — I don't want to teach my students a neat analogy that collapses the moment anyone pushes on it. Please write in British English, and bear in mind my students will have seen the film exactly once, so stick to scenes and moments that anyone who has watched it will remmeber.
