I teach an introductory ethics seminar for non-philosophy majors — 18 students, one 75-minute session. Design a role-play that puts the moral status of video-game characters in front of them as something that happens to them, not as a debate topic they weigh in on. The design should exploit a specific psychological fact: our sympathy for a character engages through the same channels it uses for animals and people — responsiveness, apparent goals, expressed distress — and then switches off the moment we're told the character is scripted, without our ever articulating what exactly changed. I want the students to catch themselves making that switch, and then to have to say whether it tracks a real moral difference (the absence of experience) or a socially licensed excuse for treating the character however they wanted to anyway.

Constraints: roughly a third of the class should play the characters, each operating from a short card I can hand them — a fixed set of goals and reactions they must stay inside no matter what the other students do. The rest are players whose objectives make exploiting the characters the path of least resistance, and who hold one extra power: the ability to reset a scene so that a character forgets what was done to it. No real stakes or money. Nothing should be labeled as being about video games before or during the activity — frame it however you like (a customer-service simulation, a study with research participants, something else entirely) and let the reveal in the debrief do the work. That debrief should connect particular moments in the role-play to particular parts of the philosophical question: which features of the characters the players actually responded to, what the reset mechanic did to their sense of what they owed the characters, and where the analogy to animals, infants, or people with severe cognitive impairments strengthens the case and where it breaks. Close by forcing the comparison the students will want to avoid: we have no direct access to experience in anyone but ourselves, so what is the difference between a perfectly scripted character and a behaviorally identical but sentient being — and is "it's just code" an answer or merely an assertion?

Finally, tell me what is most likely to go sideways on a first run and how I should handle it, and be explicit about which part of the question the activity cannot reproduce no matter how well it runs — what I will have to assert in the debrief rather than show.
