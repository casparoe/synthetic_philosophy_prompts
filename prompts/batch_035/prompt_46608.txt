I'm a retired physical chemist who's gotten deep into philosophy of mind in the last couple years (better late than never I guess), and my reading group keeps coming back to an old analogy I want to see properly stress-tested. It's the one T.H. Huxley used back in the 1870s when he argued animals are basically conscious automata: consciousness is like the steam whistle on a locomotive -- the engine's workings produce the whistle, you hear it hissing away, but it does nothing, it has zero influence on the machinery driving the train. The folks in my group lean on this constantly, and not just for animals: the conclusion they draw is that whatever consciousness is, science can safely ignore it, because all the real causal work is done by the physical machinery, and consciousness is just noise on top. One guy says it's like the foam on a wave or the shadow a train casts, same idea.

Can you audit this thing properly? Like an actual engineer would: give me the correspondence table first -- what plays the role of the engine, the boiler pressure, the whistle, the sound waves, the listener hearing the whistle -- then show me where the mapping breaks. I keep half-noticing disanalogies but can't get them straight. For instance, the whistle is itself a physical event, made of steam molecules and sound waves, it's publicly observable, and presumably you could measure the tiny bit of energy it carries away -- none of that seems true of consciousness. But some of those breaks might not matter for the conclusion they want, and I'd like to know which ones do.

Here's the part I really want you to dig into, because I suspect it's where the whistle picture falls apart worst: when an engineer hears the whistle, that hearing causes him to say something like "the whistle is blowing." If consciousness is really like the whistle, then shouldn't our reports about our own conscious experiences be caused by those experiences? But the whole point of the analogy is supposed to be that consciousness does no causal work. So either my saying "I'm in pain right now" is caused by the pain, and then the whistle analogy is wrong about the one thing it was built to say, or it isn't caused by the pain, and then it's some kind of miracle or coincidence that my reports track my experiences at all. Is this the thing philosophers call the problem about judgments about phenomenal experience? Does it actually sink the analogy, or does the epiphenomenalist have a decent reply (I've heard people claim the reports are caused by the brain states that cause the consciousness, sort of parallel tracks, and I don't know if that rescues anything or just concedes the point)?

And then the bottom line: once the disanalogies are all on the table, does the "science can ignore consciousness" conclusion survive, maybe in a weaker form? Or is the analogy doing more work as a rhetorical device than as an actual argument? If the whistle is a bad analogy, is there a better one for what the epiphenomenalist wants to say, or does that suggest the position itself is what's broken?

Sorry for the wall of text, my philosopher friends keep telling me I argue like I'm still writing a lab report. Maybe that's not so bad.
