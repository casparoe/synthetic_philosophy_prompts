Draft the joint statement that a geometric rationalist and an arithmetic rationalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about how to manage a bankroll across a sequence of favorable wagers. The geometric rationalist holds that one should stake so as to maximize the growth rate of one's bankroll (the Kelly prescription); the arithmetic rationalist holds that one should maximize the expected bankroll at each opportunity, which means wagering the entire bankroll whenever the expected value of a bet is positive.

Stipulate the following game within the document, so that it is self-contained: a coin lands heads with probability 0.55; wagers are at even money; the bettor begins with $1,000; the opportunity recurs for 100 rounds; and in each round the bettor may stake any fraction of the current bankroll, with no borrowing, winnings and losses compounding round to round. The statement should also address two variants: the same wager offered exactly once to a bettor who faces no similar opportunities again; and a single-shot bet in which the bettor stands to gain $2,000 or lose $1,000, each with probability one-half, her total wealth being only slightly more than the $1,000 at stake.

Structure the document as follows.

(1) A numbered section of agreed claims. These should include the pure mathematics both sides accept: the optimal fraction of bankroll under each objective for the 100-round game; the near-certainty of ruin under repeated all-in betting, alongside the all-in strategy's dominance in expected bankroll; the divergence between the mean and the median of final bankrolls across a large population of independent bettors; and what a simulation of many thousands of such bettors would show — median final bankroll, mean final bankroll, and fraction ruined — since both parties should agree on what the statistics would be even while disputing which statistic is the one that matters.

(2) A disagreements table, each row phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its position; avoid formulations either side would reject as slanted.

(3) The cruxes. I suspect at least one is whether a wager should be evaluated as a one-shot decision or as part of a longer stream of compounding opportunities — that is, whether the horizon is given by the agent's situation or may be adopted by the agent — and another is whether the right statistic for judging a life of betting is the mean or the median of final bankrolls. Add any further cruxes they identify. For each crux, describe a concrete case, computation, or simulation both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, before the outcome is known.

(4) A final section stating which parts of the dispute the two now regard as merely verbal — for instance, whether a Bayesian with logarithmic utility in wealth simply is a Kelly bettor under a different description — and which parts remain substantive even after that separation, with the residual disagreement made exact.

Do not appeal to any outside literature by name or provide references; the statement should stand on its own stipulations and computations. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words, formatted as numbered sections plus a table.
