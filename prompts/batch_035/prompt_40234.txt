Two of my teammates at work got into a whiteboard argument on friday that they still haven't resolved, and honestly I want a referee because I couldn't follow all of it. Some context first. We do interpretability-adjacent safety work on a deployed LLM, and the thing I work on most is a monitor based on "mechanistic anomaly detection": instead of just flagging suspicious-looking outputs (behavioral detectors do that already and miss stuff), we track what the model is doing on the inside — which internal components activate and how strongly — and flag inputs where the model seems to be running machinery it basically never ran on our training distribution. The pitch is you'd catch the model doing something genuinely new, like producing deceptive outputs, even if the output text looks totally benign.

Last month a red-team eval produced a batch of subtly misleading summaries (the model was hedging away inconvenient caveats in ways that read as neutral). Our monitor stayed completely quiet. When we dug in, the behavior was definitely real, but it was running through a different set of heads than the ones our monitor watches — and those heads had been identified by ablation experiments on the base model, before a fine-tune a few weeks earlier apparently rerouted stuff.

So the argument:

(1) Maya said the monitor did exactly what it was built to do. It tracks a specific mechanism; this wasn't that mechanism. Blaming it for missing the behavior is like blaming a smoke detector for not catching a flood. Monitors have scope, and "didn't fire" ≠ "nothing happened."

(2) Dev said that only works if "the same mechanism" is cut at a sensible grain, and hers is cut way too fine. If a routine fine-tune can reroute a capability to new heads and that automatically counts as a *different* mechanism, then the monitor goes silent precisely in the scenarios we built it for. Whatever "the deceptive summarizing mechanism" is, it's the thing we wanted to catch, and it's what was happening, full stop.

(3) Maya came back: if you individuate mechanisms by behavior instead, at that grain there's nothing mechanistic left — you've just built a classifier for bad outputs, and the entire reason to open up the model was that behavioral detectors already exist and generalize badly. A mechanism is its internal realization. That's not a bug in her view, that's what the word means.

(4) Dev conceded that exact heads-and-weights identity is too fine — of course nothing survives a fine-tune at that grain. His fix: call two internal set-ups the same mechanism when they play the same *causal role* in producing the same type of output. Same job description, different employees.

(5) Maya said causal role doesn't rescue him, because which interventions you're willing to perform determines what causal role anything has — ablate this head versus reweight that pathway and you get different counterfactuals — so "same causal role" is intervention-relative, and he hasn't said relative to which interventions. And whatever set he picks, he lands back in the same squeeze: fine enough to be principled, and no two things are ever the same mechanism so any distribution shift defeats the monitor; coarse enough to be useful, and everything bad is "the same mechanism" so the monitor flags half the eval set. He's picked a grain and called it a fact.

(6) Dev's last word was that this might be the honest endpoint: "same mechanism" is a modeling choice, not a fact about the model, and the real question is which grain tracks the property we actually care about (capability for deceptive-looking outputs, or whatever), which is at least a question you can answer empirically instead of philosophically.

They've now both roped me in as tiebreaker and I genuinely can't do it. Please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the six moves from clearly wrong to clearly right, with your reasons, and tell me who's right about what. And please don't give me the "it depends on how you individuate mechanisms" shrug — I want you to actually commit to a view about what "same mechanism" should mean for a monitor like ours, defend that choice, and say whether Maya's squeeze (step 5) actually defeats the choice you made or whether there's a way out. If you think Dev's step-6 move is right that it's a modeling choice all the way down, fine, but then commit to which choice we should make and why. Also flag anything you think both of them are missing entirely.
