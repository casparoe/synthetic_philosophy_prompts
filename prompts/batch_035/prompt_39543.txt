The lab where I work has a safety checklist for any model before it's given agentic capabilities, and the checklist is an odd bundle of practices. Fine-tuning rewards the model for deferring to human correction, flagging its own uncertainty, and asking permission before consequential actions. One of the evaluation scenarios is deliberately unfair: a supervisor who is ill-informed or hasty overrides the model's demonstrably correct recommendation, and any argument, evasion, or re-litigation of the point is logged as a safety failure. The systems are also built with a shutdown mechanism that no autonomous routine is permitted to work around, and the deployment gate treats any tendency to preserve itself against shutdown as a critical defect. Meanwhile, the very same lab trains these models as planners rewarded for completing objectives despite adversarial interference, and its capability evaluations celebrate exactly the kind of persistence in the face of obstacles that the safety evaluations punish whenever the obstacle is the supervisor. What theory of agency, and of rationality, does this bundle of practices presuppose, if any? Does corrigibility training only make sense on a two-tier picture, on which the objective occupies one slot and the supervisor's will occupies a separate, normatively superior slot — or can a single expected-utility framework, in which the supervisor is just another part of the environment to be modeled and used, explain why a rational agent should let a confused operator switch it off? Consider the standard sticking points in your answer: an agent that values its objective treats the off switch as a threat and, if smart enough, neutralizes it; an agent engineered to be indifferent between the switch's states seems to acquire perverse incentives, such as manipulating when the switch gets pressed or discounting self-repairs it should want; and an agent that treats the supervisor's override merely as evidence about the objective seems owed some leeway to disagree when the evidence is weak. Reconstruct the implicit theory the checklist presupposes, distinguish what strictly requires that theory from what is compatible with several rival theories of agency, and say where the practice is underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change in the checklist if the lab adopted, say, the view that corrigibility is nothing but imperfect goal pursuit, or the view that obedience to the override is just rational belief-updating about a shared objective. Finally, assess whether the practice is internally consistent: it condemns shutdown-resistance in the model as a defect while we would condemn the same deference in a human colleague as servility — does the practice presuppose an answer to why that asymmetry is legitimate, or does it simply assume one?
