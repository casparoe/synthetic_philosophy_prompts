I'm on the board of a mid-sized professional society (about 900 members), and we elect our officers — president, secretary, three at-large board seats — by simple plurality, one office at a time. Every election cycle we get complaints that people are voting tactically: lesser-evil votes, members coordinating on social media around a "anyone but X" candidate, that kind of thing. Now there's a motion on the table to switch to instant-runoff voting. One of my fellow board members keeps bringing up something called the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and saying no voting method can be strategy-proof, so the whole debate is pointless and we should just keep what we have. My understanding of the theorem is roughly: if you're electing one winner from three or more candidates, and the outcome depends on preferences in a way where any candidate could win given the right voters, and there's no dictator, then any voting method can be gamed by somebody misreporting their preferences.

What I need is not a treatise on social choice theory. I need a decision procedure the board can actually run whenever someone proposes a voting method — this year it's instant runoff, next year it'll be something else, so it has to be reusable. Concretely:

1. An ordered checklist of questions to ask about any proposed method: what to establish at each step (how many candidates can realistically run, what the ballot type actually is, whether every candidate is a possible winner under some profile of honest preferences, whether some voter or chair is a de facto dictator), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and at which step the theorem's conclusion kicks in and ends the strategy-proofness analysis for good. If a step needs something technical checked, tell me what to ask the proposer and what answer should reassure us.

2. Clear handoff points to human judgment. Once we know a method is manipulable in principle, the real question is about degree — how hard the manipulation is, how much information about others' votes the manipulator needs, whether 900 amateurs with a google group will realistically pull it off. Be honest about which of these has an objective answer and which is a values call the board has to own rather than hide inside the checklist.

3. Worked applications: run the procedure on (a) our current plurality system, (b) the proposed instant runoff, and (c) approval voting, which someone floated as a workaround. I expect the procedure to give us at least one answer we won't like — I've heard instant runoff is still manipulable, and I want to know whether approval voting genuinely escapes the theorem or is just exploiting a loophole in its assumptions (it's not really a ranked ballot, right?). Also handle the awkward edge case that some of our elections only have two nominees — does that change anything?

4. An explicit list of what the procedure can't settle: whether tactical voting is even bad if everyone does it, how the method affects turnout, and anything about the three board seats being elected together (is that still one election per seat in the theorem's sense?). Flag these as out of scope rather than trying to sneak in answers.

Constraints: the board is not made of mathematicians. We have one retired statistician who can sanity-check things but is not going to work through proofs, and we have a single meeting to decide whether to put a constitutional amendment in front of the membership. So the procedure needs to be runnable in an afternoon per proposal, from the proposal text plus maybe one follow-up email.

Please write your answer in American English. And at the end, be blunt: is my board member right that the debate is pointless, or is that a misreading of what the theorem actually lets us conclude?
