Please take on the role of a philosopher of decision theory — someone who works at the boundary of economics and philosophy and has spent years thinking about what formal arguments can and cannot establish about rationality. I'd like you to reason carefully and at length rather than answer quickly; work through the candidate objections in whatever order helps you think, but arrive at a ranked verdict in the end.

Here is the view I hold, which I want stress-tested: money-pump arguments show that preferences forming a cycle are irrational, full stop. To be concrete, suppose I prefer hiking boots to sneakers for getting around this month, sneakers to dress shoes, and dress shoes back to hiking boots. A trader who knows this can start me off with the hiking boots, offer me the sneakers plus a dollar to swap, then the dress shoes plus a dollar, then the hiking boots plus a dollar, and repeat the lap until I have surrendered everything I own while ending each lap exactly where I began. My view is that this vulnerability — being drained by a sequence of trades, each of which I accept — is a decisive argument that cyclic preferences violate a requirement of practical rationality, and that transitivity (or at least acyclicity) is therefore a genuine norm of rational choice rather than a structural nicety of decision theory.

Tell me what would count against this view. I'm aware the argument can be complicated in several ways: a foreseeing agent might refuse the very first trade and never be pumped at all; an agent might adopt a blanket policy of never trading and stick to it even when particular trades look attractive; the pump seems to assume that my preference between two options does not depend on what else was on the table, on what I have already declined, or on how the sequence is sliced up; and there is a question about whether being exploitable in principle is enough, or whether the irrationality only sets in when someone actually runs the pump on me. Do not treat these as a checklist to rebut one at a time. I want to know which of them, if any, would genuinely count against the view, and how the view would have to be qualified to survive each one. Rank the defeaters from fatal, through serious, to merely awkward. Say which of them committed defenders of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, and which they would dismiss, and on what grounds. Then give me an honest assessment of whether, once all the needed background conditions have been spelled out — myopic choice, no foresight, context-invariant pairwise preferences — the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what a mature theorem-with-application looks like. I would also like to know whether anything that actually happens in the world bears on the argument: drip pricing, subscription design, and interface patterns that steer people through sequences of individually accepted losses — are these pumps without cycles, and therefore irrelevant, or do they show something the idealized argument misses? Finally, and this is what I would personally find most persuasive, describe one worked case: a specific agent, with specific cyclic preferences, in a specific sequence of choice situations, where the pump verdict and the intuitive verdict about the agent's rationality come apart — and tell me which way you would resolve it and why.

Please write in continuous prose only. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
