Two philosophers of mind — one an Orwellian (experiences unfold in real time and later contents overwrite earlier ones in memory), the other a Stalinesque theorist (the brain edits before consciousness, so the edited content is the only one ever experienced) — have spent years talking past each other about the color phi phenomenon and other postdictive effects. Draft the joint statement they should produce from an adversarial collaboration. It should contain: (1) the claims about color phi, backward masking, and postdictive perception that both sides can sign; (2) their residual disagreement, stated so that each side accepts the wording of its own view; (3) the cruxes — I suspect one is whether there is any fact of the matter about when a content is experienced, as opposed to merely reported, and another is what a memory of an experience must be a memory of; (4) for each crux, a concrete experiment or argument both agree would bear on it, with each side's predictions recorded in advance — for instance, say what each would predict if a genuinely concurrent measure (a subject signaling the moment they see any color at all, taken mid-sequence without disrupting the illusion) could be obtained, and what each would predict if the second stimulus in the phi pair were sometimes withheld; and (5) a verdict on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so which part and why. Format: a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not let the statement collapse into the familiar conclusion that the question of "when the experience really happened" is just confused — the point of the collaboration is to find formulations precise enough that the two sides make different, advance-recorded predictions.
