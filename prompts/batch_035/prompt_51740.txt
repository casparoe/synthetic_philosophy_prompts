I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of probability — 16 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity in which students run into the Sleeping Beauty problem before I name it. The setup: a fair coin is tossed out of sight; every student is Beauty at once — on heads each is interviewed once, on tails twice, and you must design a mechanism that stands in for the amnesia, so that a student cannot tell which of their own interviews this one is. At each interview they first post a credence that the coin landed heads, then wager tokens at those odds, with payoffs in a class point ledger rather than cash. Include a control run of the same interviews without the double-awakening structure, so that the debrief can show the dispute is not about the coin but about what "this interview, now" is evidence of. The debrief should then map what happened onto the halfer and thirder reasoning and onto the self-locating question — how many times am I having this conversation today? — with the name of the problem withheld until the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the problem no classroom staging can reproduce: the genuine first-person uncertainty about the date cannot be manufactured by rules alone, so say plainly how much I must simply ask them to imagine. Keep the full design, debrief included, under 800 words.
