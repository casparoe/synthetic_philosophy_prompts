me and two friends from my biostats program write a satirical little newsletter for the department (basically a campus Onion, we have maybe 60 subscribers, half of them professors who print it out). Next issue's theme is "technically true," and I want to contribute a fake news article, headline plus ~500 words, about a fictional mid-size regional hospital that issues a triumphant press release: mortality is down in every single patient subgroup — every age band, every admission-severity tier, every department, including the new trauma center — and raw hospital-wide mortality is up 14% year over year. The trauma center is the confounder, obviously: they opened it, the case mix shifted toward much sicker patients, and that's exactly how a Simpson's reversal works, so I want the numbers in the piece to actually be able to coexist — a specialist reader should be able to check that the subgroup improvements and the aggregate worsening are arithmetically compatible given plausible weights, not just vibe-compatible.

The target of the satire isn't Simpson's paradox itself but aggregation-shopping: the hospital's comms officer celebrating the "average of averages," the board voting to report only whichever aggregation level is "most patient-centered" this quarter, maybe a quote from a grateful family ("they saved my dad, statistics can't save everyone"). But I also don't want the piece to lazily conclude that the aggregate number is the "real" one and the subgroup numbers are spin — that's the mistake lay coverage always makes. Both statistics are true; the honest punchline is that the fight is about which question you're pretending you asked, descriptive vs causal. Maybe give the hospital's head of analytics a line where they almost say this correctly and then ruin it. Please include at least one quote from a deadpan state health department statistician who explains the reversal perfectly and is immediately misquoted in the press release's second paragraph. Keep it funny for people with no stats background too — my roommate should laugh without knowing what a marginal distribution is, and my advisor should laugh because the mechanism is right.
