I'm taking a seminar on counterfactual history, and I've noticed that two water analogies do nearly all the argumentative work in our discussions without anyone ever examining them. When someone wants to argue that history is contingent — that QWERTY-style accidents compound and lock in, that Rome's adoption of Christianity or Britain's position on the gold standard could easily have gone the other way and everything downstream with it — they reach for the riverbed: early events carve a channel, the flow deepens it, sediment reinforces the banks, and eventually the river couldn't be anywhere else, even though the water itself is indifferent to where it runs. When someone wants the opposite conclusion — that the outcomes would have re-arrived anyway, by different routes — they reach for water finding the ocean: the terrain of geography, scarcity, and human nature fixes the destination, and the particular path is mere detail. Because both sides use the same substance, it feels like a shared vocabulary, but each analogy quietly imports features the other lacks: the riverbed imports irreversibility and increasing returns (a channel gets easier to follow the more it is used, and switching costs mount with time), while the ocean imports a fixed attractor that the flow was always going to reach regardless of meanders.

Could you audit the pair properly? For each analogy: a correspondence table (what plays the role of early events, institutions, technologies, individuals; what plays friction, gravity, terrain), then where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part our seminar never does — a separation of the disanalogies that matter for the debates we're actually having from the ones that don't. Then tell me whether either analogy can really carry the conclusion it's being used for in cases like whether industrialization needed coal specifically or merely any dense fuel, or whether the timing of abolition was path-dependent or would have converged on roughly the same decade under small changes to the argument landscape. It strikes me as possible that both conclusions are being driven entirely by the features each analogy smuggles in rather than demonstrates, in which case the debate is a coin-flip dressed as a derivation — and if that's what's going on, I'd want to know which premises would have to be established independently to make each conclusion respectable.

Finally, if neither analogy earns its keep, propose a better one: something that captures path dependence while distinguishing cases where lock-in is genuinely irreversible from cases where the endpoint is robust to rerouting — ideally a comparison that would let us look at a historical outcome and say something about which kind of case it is. And if you think the question itself is confused — say, because "the same outcome would have arrived anyway" isn't well-defined enough to compare against a counterfactual path, since outcomes are individuated by the paths that produce them — please say so plainly rather than splitting the difference politely; I'd much rather learn that the debate is ill-posed than receive an even-handed verdict on it.
