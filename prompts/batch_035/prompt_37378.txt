I write a feature column on emerging science and its conceptual foundations, and I am preparing a piece on the intelligence-explosion argument — the claim, descended from I. J. Good and elaborated by Bostrom, that once AI matches human research capability, further progress becomes self-sustaining and explosive. Rather than rehearse the familiar fast-versus-slow takeoff debate, I want to focus on the concrete feedback channels through which improvement is supposed to compound. Please assign each of the following a score from 0 to 1, representing how much that channel alone could compress the interval between AI matching a competent human researcher and AI greatly exceeding the total human research workforce: (a) AI systems improving the training algorithms of their successors; (b) AI-designed hardware loosening compute constraints; (c) massive parallelization of research labor across many independent runs; (d) AI removing human bottlenecks in peer review, licensing, and experimental turnaround. Give one sentence per channel explaining its score, and then say whether any single channel scoring above 0.5 would by itself suffice for a hard takeoff, or whether the explosion argument instead depends on several channels interacting — and if so, which interaction you take to be load-bearing.
