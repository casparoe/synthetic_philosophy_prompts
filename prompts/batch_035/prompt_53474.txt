Next term I'm running a 90-minute session on semantic externalism for a third-year philosophy of language course: 16 students, one seminar room, no lab equipment. I want a staged activity — not a lecture, not a discussion of the literature, and the names Putnam and Burge should not come up until the debrief at the earliest — through which the students run into the externalist intuition before they can label it. The mechanism I'd like you to build on: teams receive sets of unlabeled liquid samples that are indistinguishable by every test available to them (appearance, odor, boiling, freezing, simple flame tests), but roughly half the samples differ from the others in some hidden structural property the students cannot themselves detect. The teams' job is to coin a word for the substance, fix application criteria, and use the word in graded coordination tasks (sorting, describing, betting on samples). Midway through, you should introduce a "chemist" role — one person who alone can test for the hidden property — so the activity can also stage deference to experts rather than just the Twin Earth point.

Please deliver: complete rules and a timeline fitting the 90 minutes; a cheap materials list (water, saline, and glycerol mixtures or whatever works for visible indistinguishability with a detectable hidden difference, plus a trivial test the chemist can perform, like a hydrometer or a conductivity pen); a scoring scheme for the coordination tasks that creates real pressure to agree on word usage; and the reveal stage, in which the hidden difference is disclosed and the students are asked, with points riding on the answer, whether their coined word applied to all the samples all along, only to some, or changed its extension the moment the chemist reported. Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the philosophical claims — that sameness of internal psychological state is compatible with difference in reference, and that the extension of a term can be fixed partly by facts about other people and the environment rather than by speakers' own knowledge — plus a candid section on what predictably goes wrong the first time (students smuggling in the word "water" and its chemistry-class connotations; disputes about whether their criteria were intended as necessary and sufficient conditions; a team that simply refuses to use the coinage and points instead). Finally, be honest about which feature of externalism the activity cannot reproduce: in particular, whether the counterfactual twin-community setup can be genuinely enacted with one group of students or must be supplied by the facilitator as an imagined comparison class, and say how you'd bridge that gap in the debrief without simply asserting the conclusion.
