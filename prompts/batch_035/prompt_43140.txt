I spent thirty-five years as a population ecologist, most of them running small-mammal surveys in tropical dry forest, and in retirement I have been reading philosophy of language. Quine's discussion of the field linguist and the native's word "gavagai" in Word and Object struck me with unexpected force, because it describes a problem I personally fumbled for decades without knowing it had a name. Whenever my guides used a vernacular term, I silently translated it as a Linnaean species, and my species lists were only as good as that translation. Quine's point, as I understand it, is that all the observational evidence available to the linguist — elicited assent and dissent in the presence of scenes, pointing at animals, which utterances co-occur with which — still fails to determine whether the word means rabbit, undetached rabbit parts, or a stage in the career of a rabbit. The evidence for each hypothesis is identical. I have since learned that this is not a puzzle about exotica: it is the ordinary condition of ethnobiological fieldwork.

Here is what I would like. I now advise a small program that trains field teams to do rapid ethnobiological inventories — a few weeks per community, one consultant speaker at a time, output feeding into environmental impact assessments and, occasionally, land-rights documentation where a species list carries legal weight. These teams have no philosophical training and no interest in it. Please write them a decision procedure, grounded in what Quine actually established, for handling vernacular terms for organisms: the questions to ask, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and — this is the part I most want done honestly — where the procedure must stop and record ambiguity rather than resolve it. I take the indeterminacy thesis to imply that no amount of clever field protocol fixes a unique "correct" crosswalk from a folk term to a scientific species, so a procedure that pretends otherwise is lying to its users; the conceptual work is in deciding which of Quine's distinctions must survive the simplification into something a field assistant can run in an afternoon, and which can be dropped without harm.

Then apply the procedure to three cases I have actually met in some form: (1) a single term covering both the juvenile and adult of what I would call two species, with the community treating the juvenile-to-adult transition as a change of kind rather than of individual; (2) neighboring communities whose apparently equivalent terms pick out overlapping but non-identical sets of species, so that a shared checklist quietly forces a choice between their classifications; and (3) a term that seems to track ecological role — roughly, things that raid the maize — rather than any clade, and so cuts across every level of my taxonomy at once. At least one application should end where I do not want it to: with the admission that the clean species-by-species inventory the consulting agency demands cannot be honestly produced from the evidence the procedure licenses. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do, and where the residual judgment calls are — including the uncomfortable one about who bears responsibility when a legal document needs a single answer and the philosophy says the facts do not supply it.
