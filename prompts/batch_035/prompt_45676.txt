I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, 90 minutes — and next week's topic is higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia: cases where your evidence about your own evidence points away from your first-order judgment. My worry is that on paper the phenomenon looks like a technicality, and I want an in-class activity where students run into the akratic combination in themselves before we name it.

Here is the mechanism I have in mind, which you should refine into something runnable rather than replace wholesale. Students take a short forced-choice quiz: about a dozen questions, deliberately mixed between ones they can re-verify on the spot if pressed (simple arithmetic, a syllogism they can check) and ones where they'll have nothing but their first-order memory (geography trivia, say), each with a confidence rating. Then each student opens a sealed envelope. One third of the envelopes say the student was randomly selected to receive the misleading version of the quiz — where the wording of several questions subtly points toward the wrong answer — one third say the student's quiz was clean, and one third are blank, and students know these proportions in advance. In fact every quiz is identical; the envelopes give genuine higher-order evidence of unreliability that is completely uncorrelated with any actual first-order error. Students then decide, question by question, whether to stick or switch, and re-rate their confidence, for payoff points. The interesting data are the students who, on a re-checkable arithmetic question, still switch because of the envelope, and the ones who stick while conceding in the debrief that their total evidence didn't support sticking.

Design the full session: rules, materials, the payoff scheme, how to run the envelope draw so students believe the probabilities, timing, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the distinction between first-order and higher-order evidence, and onto the question of whether higher-order evidence merely lowers credence or outright defeats. The debrief should also connect the checkable versus uncheckable questions to the debate over whether one can rationally re-verify one's way out of higher-order defeat. No lecture beforehand; they shouldn't meet the terms "higher-order evidence" or "epistemic akrasia" until the debrief.

Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the envelope mechanism has failure modes I can already half see, and I want yours. And say plainly which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by discussion afterwards. Finally, be self-critical about the design itself: identify where your reasoning about how students will respond is most likely to fail, and what observation during the session would change your mind about whether the activity is doing its job. End with a line of the form "Verdict:" followed by the single design element most likely to teach the wrong lesson instead of the intended one.
