My roommate has been on a Quine kick and laid an argument on me that I can't get out of my head. It's supposed to be about the indeterminacy of translation but it ends somewhere way worse, and he claims Quine himself is fine with the ending, which honestly scares me more than the argument does.

Here's the argument as he gave it to me:

(1) Everything relevant to translating a native's word is behavioral: their dispositions to assent and dissent to sentences, given the sensory stimulation they're under. There's nothing else to go on, even in principle, even for a translator with unlimited time and perfect data.

(2) A native word "gavagai" said while pointing at a rabbit could mean rabbit, but it could also mean undetached rabbit part, or a rabbit-shaped region of the visual field, or rabbithood manifesting itself — each option can be made to fit every single observation you could ever make, as long as you also translate the rest of their language to compensate (glossing their word for "same" differently, etc.).

(3) So there's no fact of the matter which of those the native means. It's not that we can't find out — there's nothing there to find out.

(4) But notice nothing in the argument turns on the natives being exotic. My evidence for what my neighbor means by "rabbit" is exactly the same kind of evidence: watching what he points at, when he assents to "there's a rabbit," and so on.

(5) So by the same reasoning, there's no fact of the matter whether my neighbor means rabbit by "rabbit" rather than undetached rabbit part. I've never been inside his head, and the "inside his head" option was never on the table anyway once we granted (1).

(6) But that's ridiculous — my neighbor and I have coordinated on rabbits for twenty years. Traps, fence repairs, the time we argued about whether the thing under the shed was a rabbit or a hare. If there's no fact of the matter what he means, what exactly have we been doing?

(7) Therefore (1) is false: meaning isn't exhausted by behavioral dispositions, and something non-behavioral fixes what "rabbit" refers to.

The problem is my roommate says (7) is exactly the conclusion Quine was trying to block, and that Quine just accepts (5). As he puts it, Quine says English is in the same boat as the native language — the indeterminacy is at home too, it's just that the homophonic manual (translate his "rabbit" as "rabbit") is the one we find natural to use, not the one that's objectively right. My roommate thinks this is a feature, not a bug: it's supposed to show something about what meanings are (or aren't).

So here's what I want to know. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'll admit my priors: I'd love to keep (1), since giving it up seems to hand a win to the "meaning is an inner mental something" crowd, and I'd also love to keep (6), because I do feel like my neighbor means rabbits. My roommate says I can't have both and I suspect he's right, but I also have this nagging feeling that something slips between (2) and (3) — like there's a difference between "no evidence could distinguish the options" and "there's no fact which option is true" that's doing more work than the argument pays for. Or maybe the slippage is at (4), where "same kind of evidence" quietly becomes "same evidential situation," since with my neighbor there's a whole shared language sitting around that wasn't there with the natives. But I genuinely can't tell whether those are escape routes or just ways of refusing to see the point. Help me out — where does this go wrong, or does it, and if it doesn't, how bad is it really to give up (6)? (I've heard Chomsky made roughly this complaint about Quine and got told he was missing the point, which makes me nervous that the escape routes are already mapped and closed.)
