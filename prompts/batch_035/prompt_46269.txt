My grandfather passed away three weeks ago, at 94. He wasn't a professional philosopher -- he was a machinist his whole life -- but he read the Zhuangzi constantly, this beat-up Burton Watson translation that lived on his nightstand for literally fifty years, and it wasn't decorative for him. When my grandmother died he sat by the coffin and then, later that night, we found him out on the porch knocking his knuckles on an old metal wash basin, kind of half-singing. He told us the story about Zhuangzi drumming on a tub after his wife died and how it was the only thing at the funeral that had made any sense to him. He talked about death the way the book does -- as part of the transformation of things, energy gathering and dispersing, seasons changing, nothing to get worked up about. He used to say that grieving hard for the dead is like sobbing at autumn for the leaves. I have no idea if that's actually in the text or if it was just his own line, but that was him.

So now I'm giving the eulogy, and I'm stuck. The easy move is the standard comfort stuff -- he's at peace now, he's watching over us, we'll meet again -- and I know he would've hated every word of it. He thought the fear of death was basically a category error, and he was genuinely unafraid in a way I've never managed to be. But here's my problem: I'm writing for a room with his churchgoing sisters who do believe in heaven, my cousin who's furious at the universe right now and needs her grief treated as real, and a handful of his old friends who knew exactly what the wash basin thing meant. If I just deliver Zhuangzi's equanimity straight, it'll land as cold -- like we're shrugging him off. If I drop it entirely, I'm betraying the one thing he actually believed. I don't want to compromise into mush either -- some vague "he lives on in our memories" soup that contradicts the text and comforts no one.

You're a philosopher who knows the Zhuangzi well, so take this seriously. Before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit: sketch out what the eulogy can and cannot say given his views, what the genuinely Zhuangzian resources are for addressing the grief of the living (there's more there than just equanimity, I think -- he loved the passage about the cook whose knife never dulls, all that stuff about working with things rather than against them), and how to use the wash basin story as the centerpiece without making it a lecture. Then draft the eulogy itself -- maybe five minutes spoken, plain language, no Chinese terms unless you gloss them instantly -- and after the draft, tell me honestly which parts are load-bearing Zhuangzi and which parts are me smuggling in comfort he wouldn't have endorsed. I'd rather know where I'm cheating than have you quietly do it for me.
