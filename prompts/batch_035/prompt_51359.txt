I'm putting together background for a reading group on whether the special sciences — economics, cognitive science, evolutionary biology — earn their ontologies or merely enjoy useful fictions. People keep appealing to Dennett's notion of real patterns, together with his distinction between the physical, design, and intentional stances, as though that appeal settled something. Before anyone argues from it, I'd like the specification. Please write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of real patterns must meet, with the motivating case behind each, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

The following sorts of cases should at least be forced onto the list, though you should decide what the full list is:

- Grain-relativity. A pattern can be detectable only by beings with particular computational limits: gliders in an enormous run of Life are obvious at a coarse grain and invisible at the pixel grain. Must the theory count such patterns as real, and if so, real relative to what?
- Usefulness versus gerrymandering. A partition of the night sky into constellations, or a projection that groups randomly scattered dots into shapes that predict nothing a simpler projection wouldn't. What separates a real pattern from a projection that merely suits our interests?
- The thermostat and the chess program. Things we routinely talk about as though they want or believe things. Where should the theory place the threshold for legitimate intentional-stance attribution, and can that threshold be principled rather than merely a matter of habit?
- Non-coincidental success. Cases where the physical stance is predictively hopeless — turbulence, a chess position many plies deep — and the intentional stance does better. What should count as a pattern's predictive leverage being non-coincidental when we have no independent way to check that the pattern tracks anything?
- Levels. If beliefs are real patterns and neurons are real patterns, the theory owes us an account of how patterns at different levels coexist, and of the difference between a coarse pattern being reduced by a finer one and its being eliminated outright.

For each requirement, please say whether it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp (for instance, a demand for observer-independent existence that only a thoroughgoing metaphysical realist would insist on), and identify the single requirement that excludes the most candidate theories. Finally, and briefly: how does Dennett's own account fare against your list, and where is it weakest?
