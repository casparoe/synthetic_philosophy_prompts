I came across a long-form essay in an online philosophy magazine that lays out David Benatar's case for antinatalism, and I'd like a second opinion on whether it represents him fairly. The essay's summary goes roughly like this: "Benatar's famous asymmetry says that pain is bad and pleasure is good, but that the absence of pain is good while the absence of pleasure is not bad — so for a potential person who is never born, the downsides of life are avoided, whereas the upsides are simply not missed. In effect, pain counts against existence twice while pleasure counts for it once: once when present, and not at all when absent. From this it follows that coming into existence is always a net harm, and since a harm inflicted without consent is a wrong, procreation is always wrong. And once we see that life is a net harm, the logic pushes in the same direction for those already alive: if existence is bad for the person, then the antinatalist has trouble explaining why existing lives are worth continuing, which is why Benatar's position is often accused of collapsing into a case for ending lives already begun."

Something about this strikes me as subtly off — I've heard that Benatar is careful to distinguish different arguments and different questions, and that he responds to the pro-mortalism objection directly. But the essay reads like it was written by someone competent, and I can't tell where it's cutting corners. Please go through the passage step by step: say which claims Benatar would accept as stated, which are conflatations or overstatements that a careful defender of the asymmetry would want to rephrase, and whether the final inference — from "coming into existence is always a harm" to the trouble about lives worth continuing — is presented by Benatar himself, or attributed to him by critics. In particular, I'd like to know whether the "counts twice / counts once" gloss is a fair way to render the asymmetry, or whether it smuggles in something the argument doesn't say.
