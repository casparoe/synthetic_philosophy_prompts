For years I have held, without ever having studied philosophy, a simple view about what makes a life go well: pleasure is the only thing that is good for you in itself, and pain the only thing bad in itself, with everything else mattering only instrumentally. Recently I ran into an objection of my own that I cannot get past, and I would like a serious assessment of where it leaves me.

The objection is about how little my pleasures seem to have in common. The taste of ripe fruit, a long run at the moment it stops hurting, finishing a difficult crossword, an evening of conversation with old friends, a particular passage of music — each of these just feels like itself. They do not share any single felt quality the way, say, red things share a color. And if there is nothing common to them, I no longer understand what my own view claims to be summing. "A life with more pleasure than pain" assumes pleasure is one measurable thing; my experiences suggest it is a heap of unrelated things to which I have attached one word.

I have tried two ways out and want your judgment on both. The first is that what pleasures share is structural rather than a felt tone: perhaps every pleasure is an experience you want to continue while it is happening. That fits the music and the conversation, but it fails for pleasures that want to end at exactly the right moment — a piece of music stretched past its proper ending is spoiled, a joke run into the ground stops being funny — and it fits pain better than I would like, since at least some pains are ones I cannot stop attending to even while wanting them gone. It also sits oddly with relief, where the pleasure seems to consist precisely in something stopping. The second way out is to stop looking for a common feature and define pleasure by its role: pleasure is whatever I would choose for its own sake, on reflection. But this feels like it explains nothing — if pleasure is defined as whatever is good for me, then saying "the good is pleasure" becomes an empty bookkeeping exercise rather than a substantive claim.

What I want from you is not a defense of my view but a map of its exposure. What would genuinely count against the thesis that pleasure is a single, summable thing — which observations about my own experiences, which thought experiments, which facts about how pleasures differ from one another? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed hedonist would concede it as real damage or dismiss it, and whether the dismissal would be legitimate or merely protective. Is the wanting-it-to-continue test repairable, or is it hopeless given pleasures that want to end and pains that grip? And answer the question that worries me most: if every candidate for the common feature fails, does hedonism quietly collapse into the definitional trick I described — at which point it is no longer false but unfalsifiable — and would that be a fatal flaw for a view of this kind, or an acceptable one? I would rather learn that my view is wrong than keep holding a view that no longer says anything.
