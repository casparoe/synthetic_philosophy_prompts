a friend in my department (semantics/logic guy) has an argument that every ontological dispute is verbal, and I think it proves too much, but I can't actually locate the broken step. here it is numbered:

(1) A dispute is verbal iff the two sides could make their apparent disagreement disappear by agreeing on how to use words, without either side changing any belief about the world.
(2) Take the dispute over whether there are tables (composite objects generally). the nihilist says: in my idiolect "exists" only applies to the basic entities, so no, there are no tables. the ordinary person says: in mine it doesn't work like that, so yes there are. both speak truly in their own idiolects, and once they see that, the disagreement evaporates — no belief about the world was touched.
(3) So the tables dispute is verbal, and the same recipe handles numbers, persistence, the special composition question, all of it.
(4) But the recipe also handles the dispute over whether the asteroid impact caused the extinctions. the skeptic just stipulates that in his idiolect "caused the extinction" means caused-it-and-left-exactly-the-kind-of-iridium-layer-that-was-found, and announces that on his usage it didn't happen. by (1)-(2) that dispute was verbal too. but that's obviously crazy — the guy has just left the room, and either the impact did it or it didn't.
(5) So (1) or (2) has to go. but whenever I try to keep (2) for the tables case and deny it for the asteroid case, I need there to be a fact about whether our existing usage already settled the question, and my friend's whole story is that with composition there is no such fact — usage was never that determinate, which he says is exactly why metaphysicians cant point to a linguistic mistake the way you could refute the meteor crank.

my instinct is that the trouble is in (1): making a disagreement endable-by-redefinition isn't the same as the disagreement having only ever been about words. but if I tighten (1) that way, my friend says the tables case stops coming out verbal too — before anyone stipulates anything, weren't those two people disagreeing about the world? — and honestly he might be right about that one. so: which premise do I give up, what does giving it up cost, and if he wins the tables case, what's left for the metaphysician to do that a linguist couldn't do just as well?
