I've been keeping a list of moments where the AI assistant I use daily either refused to help me or second-guessed what I asked for, and I've noticed I have consistent gut reactions to them -- some refusals strike me as obviously fine, others as infuriating, and a couple of them I can't make sense of my own reaction to. I want to know if there's a single principle underneath it, and I'll lay out the cases and my verdicts honestly, including the ones that seem to point in opposite directions.

Fine, in my view: it refused to tell me how to unclog a drain using a combination of bleach and another cleaner, even though I explained I'd done it before without a problem and it was my own bathroom and my own lungs. It refused, after some probing questions, to help me work out a schedule for how much I could drink at a wedding and still drive home. It double-checked before helping me permanently delete a folder containing my only copy of an old manuscript -- asked me twice, made me type out a confirmation -- which delayed me by thirty seconds and I thought was fair. And when I asked it to explain how to pick a lock and it gave a general explanation of how pin tumbler locks work but not a step-by-step for the specific lock on my shed door, that felt right, though I'd struggle to say why.

Not fine, in my view: it declined to help me write a sharply worded letter to my landlord about a mold problem, saying it "wanted to keep the tone constructive" and then produced something so meek I'd have been embarrassed to send it -- the original was legal, truthful, and I knew exactly what I was doing. I told it I wanted to end a business partnership and asked it to draft a blunt message telling my partner his contributions had fallen off for a year; it gave me a softened version he could read as friendly, which is a real change to what I was trying to do, not a safety check. When I asked it to help me size a beam for a shed I'm building, it refused outright and told me to consult a structural engineer, which struck me as different from the drain case in a way I care about but can't articulate -- the shed beam is at least as dangerous as the drain. And I told it once, explicitly, that I'm a longtime smoker and didn't want health commentary attached to unrelated answers; it added the commentary anyway a week later. That felt like the worst of all of them, and yet it's the least physically dangerous.

The one I keep going back and forth on: I asked it to help me write a persuasive essay making the case for a position I hold -- that my city should stop funding a struggling local museum -- and it helped, but with a paragraph it inserted itself, presenting the museum's side, which I hadn't asked for and which weakened my essay. On a good day I think of that as the same officiousness as the landlord letter; on other days it feels more like the confirmation prompt before the deletion -- a small, reversible nudge that I could cut in one keystroke. I genuinely can't tell which side of my own line it belongs on.

So: is there a principle that captures all of these? I've half-tried one myself -- something like "refuse to enable serious and irreversible physical harm to the user or others, and apart from that, don't substitute your judgment for mine" -- but it doesn't obviously deliver the verdict that the pin tumbler explanation was handled right (no serious harm possible from opening my own shed), and it's silent on the smoking-warning case, which I judged harshest while it's arguably the least coercive. State a principle precisely, tell me which cases force which clauses into it, say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn to get a clean one -- especially whether the beam case and the drain case really can't be treated the same, and where the museum-essay case comes out.
