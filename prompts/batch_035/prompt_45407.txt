I'm a software engineer by training and by temperament — I know my way around type systems, formal specs, and property-based testing, but I've never taken a philosophy course. Lately I've been circling a question from metaphysics that keeps looking, to my engineer's eye, like an underspecified function signature, and I want to turn my confusion into a classification tool I can actually use on myself.

The question is what metaphysicians apparently call the special composition question: given some objects, when do those objects together compose a further object? It strikes me that this is exactly like a predicate `composes(x1, ..., xn)` with no documented contract. Some people say it returns true for every collection (so there's an object made of my left shoe, the Eiffel Tower, and Neptune), some say it's true only for roughly organism-shaped inputs, some say it's almost never true — just simple particles, no tables, no cats — and some say it's true but depends on facts so alien to our concepts that no human-specified predicate will ever capture it. What I can't figure out is which of these I actually believe, because whenever I read an argument I find I agree with too much of it.

So here's what I'd like you to do.

Role: please answer as an expert metaphysician who also has enough sympathy for how programmers think that you can use engineering framings without dumbing anything down. Before writing the quiz itself, brainstorm for a few sentences: list the candidate diagnostic cases you considered and what makes a case genuinely load-bearing rather than decorative — I want to see your reasoning, not just the artifact.

Deliverable: a "which view of composition do you actually hold?" decision tree, with roughly six to eight branch nodes and leaves for at least nihilism, universalism (unrestricted composition), organicism, brutal composition, and any restricted-but-not-organicist view you think deserves a leaf; mark leaves nobody defends as such.

The tree must be built from the logical space, not from the labels. That is: start from questions a person can answer without knowing which position they're steering toward, and only afterwards locate the named views in the resulting answer space. Each node should be a concrete case with two or at most three answer options. Please make sure the tree uses at least these kinds of cases, since they seem to be where my own answers wobble: a deck of cards scattered across a table versus stacked; a crowd of people waiting at a crosswalk versus moving as one when the light changes; a cat and its tail (I think I believe in the cat, but do I believe in the tail?); a pile of sand in the process of being assembled grain by grain; some atoms scattered between here and the Andromeda galaxy; a ship gradually rebuilt plank by plank in a different harbor; and two objects in mere contact, like my hand on this desk right now. If you think a better case exists that splits positions these don't, add it — but tell me which pair of views it separates that nothing else in the tree does.

Three further requirements. First, wherever the tree gives the same answers to two genuinely different positions, say so explicitly rather than letting it silently lump them — I'd rather know where the instrument is blind than trust a false precision. Second, after the tree, include a short section on unoccupied regions: combinations of answers that no standard view permits or that no one seems to hold, with a sentence each on whether that's because the combination is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because it's simply unexplored. As a testing person I care a lot about the difference between "the test fails" and "nobody ever ran this branch." Third, end by running me, your reader, through the tree once yourself: take my engineer's instinct that "composite" should work like a user-defined type — real if some rule makes it real, arbitrary-but-harmless if it's just a convention — and tell me which leaf that instinct most likely lands me on, and what the tree says I'm getting wrong if I find that leaf uncomfortable.

Please think carefully and don't take the easy path of defining the positions first and working backwards to cases; the whole point is that I can be sorted by what I say about decks of cards and crowds without ever learning the technical names until the end. Write the response in American English, and keep the tone professional — this is for a document I intend to polish and keep.
