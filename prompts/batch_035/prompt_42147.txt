I'm putting together background notes for a study group on behavioral genetics, and I keep running into the same wall: I can't tell how much of the popular writing on heritability I'm allowed to trust. A science column my cousin sent me summarized the concept roughly as follows: twin studies show that IQ is about 50% heritable in the populations researchers have studied, which means that for any given individual, roughly half of their intelligence is inherited from their parents and half comes from upbringing; that heritability is a fixed property of the trait itself, so it sets an upper limit on how much the average could be raised by environmental interventions like better schooling or nutrition; and that a 50%-heritable trait is fixed at birth in the way eye color is, just less completely. The column concluded that programs attempting to raise IQ are in effect fighting genetics, and that we should expect them to be largely ineffective, so effort would be better spent on things environments can actually touch.

Something about the intervention conclusion felt off to me, but I honestly couldn't say whether the problem is there or earlier in the chain, because the middle steps sounded plausible. I dimly remember hearing that height is highly heritable in wealthy countries today yet people got several centimeters taller over the twentieth century, which seems like it should embarrass the "upper limit" claim — but maybe I'm misremembering what heritability measures, or maybe the cases come apart in some way I can't see. The "half of each individual's intelligence" phrasing also nags at me, since I've seen it stated that heritability is a claim about variation in a population rather than about any one person, though I don't understand that distinction well enough to say why it would matter.

Could you go through the column's summary part by part and tell me which claims a careful behavioral geneticist would accept, which are subtle distortions (for instance, does heritability really attach to a trait itself rather than to a trait in a particular population and range of environments?), and which are straightforwardly wrong? I'd especially like the "half of each individual's intelligence" line sorted out — what exactly is being apportioned when a trait is called 50% heritable, and to what does the apportionment apply? Then please assess whether the conclusion about educational interventions follows from any of the claims that survive scrutiny, and, if it doesn't, explain where the inference breaks — ideally saying something about what the height example does and doesn't show about how a trait can be highly heritable and still respond dramatically to environmental change.
