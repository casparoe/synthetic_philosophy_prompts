I've been writing down which bets I would actually take, in Ellsberg-style cases and out of them, and I'd like to know whether one principle captures my verdicts or whether I have to give something up.

Urn A holds exactly 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B holds 100 red-or-black balls in unknown proportions.

1. A $100 bet on red drawn from A rather than red drawn from B: I take A, comfortably.
2. Red from B versus black from B: indifferent — the setup gives me no reason to distinguish them, and preferring either would let a bookie clean me out.
3. Black from A rather than black from B: also A. I'm told that (1) plus (2) plus (3) together let a bookie charge me twice to swap a sure $100 for a sure $100, and I can see the arithmetic, but each preference still feels right on its own.
4. When the B bet pays $150 to A's $100, I take B; at $110 to $100 I still take A. So my distaste for B has a price somewhere between 10% and 50% of the stake — I am plainly not treating B's red as impossible.
5. Outside the lab: between two airlines with identical fares and schedules, one with a long, well-documented safety record and one newly launched with no record at all, I pay a real premium for the documented one; and I would take a job with a published salary band over one with a vague, "negotiable, competitive" package at the same headline number.
6. Yet I left a salaried job for an early-stage startup whose payoff was more ambiguous than anything an urn could represent — no expected value I could even pretend to estimate — and I don't regret it or judge it irrational.

My tentative gloss is that when probabilities are unknown, I mentally average over the possible compositions of the urn and then subtract a penalty proportional to my ignorance, with the penalty shrinking as I learn more. Please make that principle precise, or replace it with something better; test it against each verdict above; and tell me plainly which verdict it cannot save. I would especially like to know: what it predicts for the three-color version of Ellsberg's problem, where people who share my two-color answers tend to prefer betting on the color whose proportion is known (30 of 90) yet prefer the union of two unknown-proportion colors to the union including the known one; how much I should pay to have B's composition announced before I choose, given the penalty model seems to say information is worth exactly the penalty it removes; and whether verdict (6) can be made rational without gutting the principle — or whether the honest conclusion is that my ambiguity aversion is a local, gambling-specific bias I should simply drop.
