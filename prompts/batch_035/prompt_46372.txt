I'm taking a seminar on existential risk and population ethics, and we've just covered the simulation argument alongside the case for what I'd call fanaticism about the far future — the thought, familiar from total utilitarian reasoning, that a sufficiently small probability of a sufficiently enormous future value stream can come to dominate all of one's moral deliberation. What struck me is that the simulation argument might function as an internal dampener on this fanaticism, but through several distinct mechanisms that I want to keep separate: (1) the middle horn of the trilemma, on which almost all civilizations go extinct before reaching technological maturity, which raises rather than lowers the probability that ours will; (2) the possibility that our future, if simulated, is a fixed finite run rather than an open trajectory, so that "shaping the far future" is more like performing well inside an already-scripted ancestor simulation than genuinely steering history; (3) the possibility that the interests of simulated minds count for less than base-level ones, or that a simulation's shutdown is not an extinction-level harm; and (4) plain indexical uncertainty about whether there is any far future of ours at all beyond the simulation's horizon, which multiplies the scenarios over which expected-value calculations have to be aggregated. Please rank these four mechanisms — alongside the null hypothesis that the simulation argument leaves fanaticism untouched — by how much they actually dampen far-future fanaticism, defend the ordering, and then answer the question I actually care about: for someone who accepts both total utilitarianism and the simulation argument's premises, is any of these mechanisms strong enough to change what they should do, or is the dampening ultimately rhetorical?
