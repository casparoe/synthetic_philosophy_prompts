I'm part of a small amateur forecasting team — eight of us, mostly ex-engineers and one statistician, who do quarterly geopolitical and economics forecasts together, partly for fun and partly to see whether we can beat our own baselines. Our book club read a recent popular book on decision-making whose central chapter is about why smart people disagree, and it has stirred up more argument than anything we've forecast. The chapter's summary of the economics runs roughly as follows: the common prior assumption says that two rational agents who have the same information must have the same beliefs; John Harsanyi showed that this assumption underlies all of game theory, and Robert Aumann later proved it correct with his "agreeing to disagree" theorem, which establishes that two people who are rational, and who know each other to be rational, cannot disagree once they have shared their evidence. The chapter then explains why this must be so: a prior is just a belief you formed before you had any evidence, and beliefs formed without evidence are arbitrary, so rationality requires that everyone start from the same one. It concludes that all genuine disagreement traces back to some difference in information, so when two people who have talked everything through still see things differently, one of them has failed to update properly, and the disagreement itself is evidence of bias — which is also, the chapter says, why traders with access to the same data converge on the same prices.

Our statistician winced at "Aumann proved it correct" and said the chapter conflates several things that decision theorists keep separate, but she couldn't articulate exactly which, and the rest of us couldn't follow either way. I confess I'm drawn to the upshot, since it would be convenient to have a principled reason to write off my most stubborn teammate. In fact, one of us has now proposed that after we pool our research notes before finalizing a forecast, anyone who refuses to move their probability toward the group's should have an "irrationality flag" logged next to their track record, citing this chapter as support. Before we vote on that rule, I'd like to know whether it rests on anything solid. Please audit the chapter's summary claim by claim: which parts a careful Bayesian or decision theorist would accept as stated, which parts are subtly distorted (for instance, whatever distinction is being lost between what the common prior assumption actually says and what follows from it, or between what Aumann's theorem assumes and what it concludes), and whether the chapter's practical moral — treat persistent post-discussion disagreement as proof of someone's failure to update — survives once the summary is corrected. I'd also like your view on whether the proposed flag rule is licensed by the defensible core of the ideas, or whether it demands a kind of convergence the theorem doesn't actually guarantee.
