I run a small math-philosophy reading group (mostly undergrads, we just did Hilbert's hotel and a bit of Cantor) and I want to make a "what do you actually believe about infinity?" flowchart for our next session. The idea is to start from the claim that the primes go on forever and end at a named position on actual infinities.

Please build it as a decision tree where every branch node is a question someone can answer without knowing any of the labels, ideally a concrete case rather than an abstract slogan. Some cases I'd like worked in (feel free to add your own): the hotel where every room is full and yet you can always free up room 1; whether "there are more naturals than perfect squares" is true, false, or a badly-formed question; whether the power set of the naturals "exists" in the same sense the naturals do; whether there could be a largest natural number (or whether that's incoherent, or just physically false); and whether Banach–Tarski is a discovery about spheres, a fact about our formalism, or evidence something went wrong.

The leaves should include at least: full-on Cantorian platonism, strict finitism, potentialism (infinity as an unfinished process, never a completed object), formalism of the Hilbertian "ideal elements are tools, not objects" sort, intuitionism/constructivism, and predicativism. If you want to add ultrafinitism as its own leaf, great, but please make the question that separates it from ordinary strict finitism actually do work (e.g. a case about whether huge-but-finite numbers we'll never write down "exist").

A few constraints:

- Build the tree from the logical space first — figure out which questions generate genuinely different answer combinations, then say which positions live where. Don't just hang seven names off one question.
- Flag any two leaves the tree can't cleanly separate (I suspect Cantorian platonism and Hilbert-style formalism agree on every concrete case, and I'd like to know if that's right or if there's a question that splits them).
- Note the empty branches too: combinations of answers nobody has seriously defended. Say whether that's because the view is incoherent, just ugly, or seemingly unexplored.
- 6-8 diagnostic questions total. Enough to sort, not so many the group tunes out.
- Keep it answerable by reasoning, not by citation hunting — assume my students have no internet access during the session. And it should be answerable by a fairly small model, so please avoid dragging in anything super specific beyond the cases above.

If it helps, one member of the group keeps saying infinity is "just a shorthand for unboundedness" and another says that's exactly the mistake Aristotle made — your tree should let both of them find out where they actually stand without either having to concede the other's framing. Also plz keep the tone light, these are 19 year olds.
