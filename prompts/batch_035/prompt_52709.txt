For the past few weeks my partner and I have been arguing about when something outside your head stops being a mere tool you use and starts being part of your mind, and I've been trying to sort out my own verdicts on cases to figure out where I actually stand. Some of them, with my gut responses: our neighbor, who had a stroke a few years back, keeps everything in a pocket daybook she consults as casually and as often as the rest of us consult our memories, and I'm confident that notebook is, for her, doing what memory does for me — it's part of her mind. My husband's GPS, which he consults on every single drive, trusts completely, and could not navigate without, strikes me differently: a tool his mind uses, but not part of his mind. A colleague of mine has kept an elaborate annotated index of everything he's read for thirty years, consulted before and during all his writing; there I waver. The friend who stores every password in a manager and can no longer recall a single one — I'm pulled toward saying the manager is now part of her memory system. And the Scrabble player who looks up anagrams on his phone between turns: clearly not, though I can't say what separates him from the neighbor.

My partner thinks all of this fits a single criterion she formulated during our argument: something becomes part of your mind when it is reliably available, you consult it more or less automatically rather than deliberatively, and you generally endorse what it tells you without re-checking it. That criterion gets the daybook in and the anagram app out, which is a point in its favor. But I can see trouble. My aunt's horoscope app is on her phone at all times, she consults it automatically several times a day, and she endorses its guidance utterly without re-checking — my partner's criterion makes it part of my aunt's mind, which seems badly wrong, and if we patch it with something about reliability, the GPS (reliable, trusted, constantly available) comes back in, dragging my husband's mind out onto his dashboard. And there's the couple we know who have divided their memory labor so thoroughly that neither could say alone whether their nephew's birthday is this weekend — she just knows that he keeps the family dates, and he keeps them partly by knowing that she'll remind him to check.

What I'd like from you is a straight answer about whether there is any principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. If no single principle gets them all, I'd like to know which of my verdicts I ought to give up — I'm genuinely torn between keeping the daybook/GPS distinction at the cost of some ugly ad hoc clause and just conceding that the GPS is part of my husband's mind after all. I'd also want your opinion on whether my partner's criterion can be repaired rather than replaced, and, crucially, what the resulting principle says about cases I haven't listed: the couple with the divided memory, and one more that occurred to me last night — a language learner who has stopped carrying a dictionary because her phone's lookup is so fast and trusted that she now experiences gaps in her vocabulary the way she experiences gaps in memory, namely as things she can fill in a moment later rather than as things she lacks.
