Before I go reading more answers to the special composition question, I want the spec an answer has to meet. Like, when do some xs compose a y -- and I want the list of requirements first, not the theories. Give me the desiderata a theory of composition has to satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each one, and be honest about which are genuinely binding and which are just prejudices that one camp (universalists, organicists, nihilists) smuggles in as "obvious." Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- that part I want most.

Some things I'd want on the list, or tell me why they shouldn't be:

- Extensional adequacy on the clear cases: some things compose a chair, my nose and the Eiffel Tower compose nothing (or do they? you tell me if that's actually a clear case or just an intuition we've been trained to have).
- It has to say something non-circular about the relation itself. If the answer is "xs compose a y iff the xs are arranged chairwise" or "iff life is present," that looks like it just relabels the question unless the arranging/life bit is doing independent metaphysical work.
- Semantic innocence: a good theory shouldn't force me to paraphrase away ordinary talk. If I say "the table has four legs as parts," the theory should let that come out literally true, not "there exist some atoms arranged table-wise." Though maybe this is negotiable -- quantifier restriction is a thing. You decide where it ranks.
- It should have a position on the dual question, not just the question. Everyone asks when composition happens; the junk question is whether everything is a proper part of something bigger, i.e. whether composition bottoms out somewhere or goes up forever. And relatedly gunk -- matter with no atomic parts. A theory that's silent on junk, or that treats gunk and junk as weird edge cases rather than stress tests, is incomplete in my book. But maybe I'm wrong that these are requirements rather than curiosities.
- Vagueness: composition plausibly comes in borderline cases (a ship gradually assembled plank by plank). Does the theory owe us an account of that vagueness, or a proof there is none? Lewis-style thinking says vague composition would infect existence itself with vagueness, which some people find intolerable and others find fine -- say which side of that the theory has to come down on, or whether it can stay neutral.
- Non-arbitrariness: if there's a cutoff ("composition stops at organisms" or "at the border of life"), the theory needs a principled reason the cutoff is THERE and not one case to the left or right. Is "the cutoff is where it is and that's brute" an acceptable answer? Seems like it can't be, but I've seen people defend it.

Then, briefly, run nihilism, universalism, and one restricted-composition view (organicist or whatever) against your finished list, and say which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work. Don't bother scoring all the restricted views, there's a million of them.

One more thing: keep the purpose fixed. I want a theory that's good enough to answer ordinary questions about what exists -- how many things are in this room, whether the wheel is part of the bicycle -- not one that's only good for a formal mereology seminar. If some requirement only makes sense relative to that purpose, mark it as such. Also if you could avoid making every requirement "the theory must be true" -- I know, that's not a desideratum, that's just the goal. Also sorry if this rambles, I wrote it fast.
