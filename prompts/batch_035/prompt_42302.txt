So this came up at dinner after our reading group (we're a mix of people from a philosophy dept and a couple CS grad students who do alignment stuff) and I ended up just listening for two hours. The topic was Yudkowsky's old idea of coherent extrapolated volition — roughly, the thought that an AI should be built to satisfy not what we currently say we want, but what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up together, etc. The idea is that if you just ask humanity what it wants right now, you're polling a confused, mutually inconsistent bundle of cravings and principles, and the extrapolation is supposed to converge on something cleaner. My friend Marco and my friend Devi got into it and I genuinely could not call a winner, so I'm bringing it to you.

Marco's opening move: he said the whole thing is a paternalism machine wearing an autonomy costume. Take the person you actually are, with the actual values you have, and then define the thing to be satisfied as the person you would become under unspecified ideal conditions — that's not respecting your will, that's replacing it. Nobody elected "me if I had thought faster." He put it like this: if a human ruler said "I will give the people not what they want but what they would want if they were wiser," we'd call that exactly the ideology every paternalist government has ever used.

Devi's reply was that we do this to ourselves and each other all the time, and nobody thinks it's tyranny. When someone is mid-craving and says they want a cigarette, we point at the version of them that signed up for the quit program. When someone is drunk, we discount what they say they want that night. The whole practice of advice, education, even promising — all of it involves holding that a better-informed, more reflective version of you gets some authority over the present-tense you, and we consider that respecting the person, not erasing them.

Marco said the cigarette case smuggles in an anchor the CEV case lacks. In the smoking case the person themselves, in a calm moment, endorses the reflective version as the one that counts — that endorsement is the actual thing doing the work, and it comes from the un-extrapolated person. CEV has no such endorsement from the ground level; it just stipulates the idealizing conditions from above. And the conditions are vague enough that whoever fills in "if we knew more" decides the outcome, since almost any change in someone's values can be redescribed as "what they'd want if they knew more." So it's bootstrapping: the extrapolator's theory of what counts as improvement quietly becomes the target.

Devi pushed back that ordinary learning has the same structure and we don't treat it as manipulation. Going to university changed your values and you didn't consent in advance to each specific change; we still call that growth, not replacement, because the changes came through your own faculties responding to reasons. She said extrapolation is just that process compressed — and if continuity through change is enough for education to count as growth rather than erasure, it should be enough here too.

Marco's last word was that there's a difference in kind, not degree: education is incremental with feedback — you remain roughly yourself at each step and can push back when a change feels wrong, so the "you" doing the checking is never lost. CEV as proposed is a jump: you go to sleep as the bundle of cravings and wake up with the AI already satisfying the extrapolated endpoint, with no point along the way where the un-extrapolated you gets to inspect and veto. A change you never had a chance to notice happening to you is a lot closer to replacement than to growth.
