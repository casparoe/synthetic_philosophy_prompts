I teach an upper-level seminar on decision theory — fourteen students, one 75-minute session, no lecture up front. The topic is the absent-minded driver problem, which I will state in full so that nothing depends on outside reading: a driver passes two identical intersections. Exiting at the first gives 0; exiting at the second gives 4; driving through both gives 1. The catch is that at either intersection the driver cannot tell whether it is the first or the second — absent-mindedness is stipulated as part of the setup, not as ordinary forgetting of the payoffs or the route. The plan "continue at the first, exit at the second" is therefore inexecutable: an executable policy is one choice, possibly randomized, applied at both intersections.

Design the session so the students hit the problem in this order: they confidently produce the infeasible plan; discover that it cannot be carried out; grope toward a fixed mixture; and then find it counterintuitive that the optimal mixture instructs them to exit at the first intersection a third of the time — that voluntarily throwing away trips is part of the optimum, not a cost of it. Concretely, I need from you: a grouping scheme; a physical procedure that enforces not knowing which intersection you are at (I am considering a partner who administers each intersection without saying which, plus a distracting counting task in between — improve on this if you can); a points-based scoring system that needs no money; a tournament in which teams submit policies as die-rolls or spinner settings, so the class locates the optimum empirically before it is derived; and a debrief that maps what happened, round by round, onto the formal content — ex ante planning versus choice at a node, the expected payoff 4p − 3p² for a continue-probability p, and the optimum at p = 2/3, including why that beats every deterministic policy available to the driver. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run (cheating, covert counting, and the objection that no real driver is like this — I want your answer to that one), and be honest about which feature of absent-mindedness the activity cannot reproduce and must simply be stipulated by me. Close by proposing one question worth leaving the students with, and justify it.
