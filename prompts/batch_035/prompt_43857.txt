here's the exam question they were answering:

"Consider the following case: you carefully judge, on the basis of how heavy a book feels in your hand, that it weighs about one kilogram. You then place it on a scale you have excellent independent reason to think is well calibrated, and it reads two kilograms. Explain whether it can ever be rational to stick with your original judgment. Use this case to say what epistemic akrasia is, and discuss whether akratic combinations of attitudes (if any exist) are always irrational. In your answer, consider the objection that higher-order evidence is just more first-order evidence about the world, and so there is no special problem here."

and here's the student's answer:

"In the case described, it seems clear that the agent should give up their original judgment and go with the scale. The felt impression is a piece of first-order evidence, but the scale reading plus the background evidence about the scale's calibration gives us higher-order evidence about the reliability of the first-order process, and higher-order evidence always defeats first-order evidence when they conflict. So it can never be rational to stick with the original judgment in this kind of case.

Epistemic akrasia is believing that p while also believing that your evidence does not support p (or supports not-p). But I think akrasia is actually impossible, strictly speaking. If you really believe that your evidence supports not-p, then you ipso facto believe not-p, or at least you don't believe p anymore, because belief aims at truth and is evidence-responsive by definition. So nobody can genuinely be in the akratic combination. The people who seem akratic are really just confused about what their evidence is.

On the objection: I think it's right that higher-order evidence is just more first-order evidence. The evidence about the scale being calibrated is evidence about the world, namely about the scale, in exactly the same way the felt weight is evidence about the book. So there's no special problem of higher-order evidence; the scale case is just a case where the total evidence favors two kilograms, and the rational thing is to follow the total evidence. Disagreement cases work the same way: the equal weight view says you should always split the difference 50/50 with anyone who disagrees with you, and this just falls out of treating the disagreement as ordinary evidence. If my peer disagrees, that's a reason to lower my confidence to exactly halfway, and if I do that I avoid akrasia, which is good because akrasia would be irrational if it were possible."
