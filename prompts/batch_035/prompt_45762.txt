I'm a third-year student taking a seminar on Tolstoy's aesthetics, and I also sit on the acquisitions subcommittee of our university's student gallery, which allocates a small annual budget for purchasing student work. This year's shortlist includes three contested pieces: a procedurally generated video installation whose creator wrote the generative rules but deliberately avoided watching the outputs so the piece would reach its audience unmediated; a large, technically flawless devotional painting whose artist privately told one of our members he painted it as donor bait; and a sincere but clumsy landscape by a first-year student. One subcommittee member, who took our seminar last year, is pushing us to adopt Tolstoy's standard from What Is Art? — genuine art is the transmission of feeling from maker to audience, with the maker's sincerity as the decisive mark — and to apply it by rejecting the installation (no human feeling to transmit) and the painting (insincere), while honoring the landscape. Another member replies that sincerity is unobservable and none of our business, and that we should judge craft and audience response alone. I've been tasked with drafting the written opinion the subcommittee will vote on at our next meeting. Please draft it: recommend a concrete policy for this shortlist and for future algorithmically generated or otherwise origin-doubtful submissions, and engage the Tolstoyan position seriously rather than dismissing it — in particular, address the awkward fact that on Tolstoy's own infection criterion the installation may pass while the painting fails, so a consistent Tolstoyan must decide whether the feeling's origin or its transmission is what does the work.
