I've been cataloguing my gut reactions to hard choices, because I suspect they add up to something and I can't quite state what. Case one: two job offers, similar pay, different cities, both interesting. I weigh them, pick, and never look back, and honestly a coin flip would've been fine too -- call that one a tie. Case two: going on tour with my band for a year versus staying near my dad, whose health is going. Here I feel like neither option is better and neither is worse, but a coin flip would feel obscene, and whichever I picked, if things went badly I could rightly think I chose wrong -- which makes no sense if it's a tie the way the jobs were. Case three: if the tour paid noticeably better, it still wouldn't tip the scales, so it's not a plain tie either, since with the jobs any small difference would decide it. Case four: my yearly giving -- no amount of marble staircases at the opera house adds up to one batch of childhood vaccines, that comparison feels easy and one-sided. Case five: giving up bedtime reading with my kid every night in exchange for a job that pays for a safer neighborhood and better schools -- I think the trade is clearly worth it, but it doesn't feel like one thing outweighing another, more like exchanging between two currencies that don't convert.

So here's my tentative principle, half-remembered from a friend who took a course on value theory: there are at least two kinds of "no better, no worse." One is a plain tie, where nothing much is at stake between the options and any tiebreaker is fine. The other -- she used a phrase like "on a par" -- is where the options are valuable in different currencies, so to speak, and improvements in either currency don't settle it, yet you can't just flip a coin, and whichever you pick you're on the hook for it. But I can see cracks already. The bedtime-stories case seems like different currencies, yet I think it's decided -- so "different currencies" can't just mean undecidable. And the dad-versus-tour case troubles me in the other direction: I genuinely think I'd be entitled to say "it was a hard choice and I chose well" no matter which I picked, and also that I'd be right to feel some pang about the road not taken. Can both be fitting at once, or is one of those feelings a mistake I should talk myself out of?

Can you build me a principle (or a couple of candidates) that fits all five verdicts, tells me where my currency idea is doing real work and where it's just a metaphor, and says which of my verdicts, if any, it has to overturn? I'd especially like it to answer two things I keep circling: whether regret can be fitting after a choice where nothing was better, and whether you can still be choosing "for reasons" at all in that kind of case. And then please test it on a new one I haven't made up my mind about: spending my savings on a last-chance experimental treatment for my old dog versus a year of traveling I've dreamed about since I was twenty. Whatever principle you hand me has to say something definite there, not just that it's hard.
