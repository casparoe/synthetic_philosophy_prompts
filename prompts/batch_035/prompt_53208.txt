I'm in an amateur theatre group and we're rehearsing Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, and reading the play closely this time has unsettled the way I usually hear people talk about it. Whenever the fall of the Roman Republic comes up in the news, the analogy is always to one dramatic figure or moment — a man who won't give up power, a line that gets crossed. But the play itself seems to hint that the Republic was already rotten before anyone drew a sword: the crowd is fickle and easily flattered, the political class is cynical, and in the fourth act the new triumvirs sit around casually drawing up lists of who is to die. I've gathered, from general reading, that many historians think of the Republic's collapse as something slow and structural — institutions and unwritten norms eroding over generations, elites competing for power and status, economic inequality growing — so that Caesar was more a symptom than a cause.

Please read the play through that structural lens rather than the great-man lens. Which scenes would you point our group to as evidence that Shakespeare saw the Republic as already failing regardless of anyone's character or intentions, and where does the play resist such a reading — where does it insist that everything turns on individual choice, honour, and rhetoric? I'd also like your view on whether the play dramatises something the structural account leaves out (what it felt like to live through the collapse, from the inside) or whether, politically, Shakespeare simply has the analysis wrong. And if you think the modern analogies people draw miss what the play actually warns about, please say what that is. Please answer in British English.
