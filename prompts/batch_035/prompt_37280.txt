I have recently moved back to the United States after eight years working in Sweden, and the contrast between the two countries' tax-filing regimes has been on my mind ever since I sat down with my first American return. In Sweden, the tax authority sends each taxpayer a largely pre-completed return, assembled from employer, bank, and property records; most people verify a few figures and confirm the whole thing online or by text message in a matter of minutes. Here, I spent days reconstructing my own income and deductions, under penalty of perjury, knowing that any discrepancy could trigger an audit. What complicates the picture is something I keep coming back to: Sweden, the supposedly high-trust society, also makes individual tax information essentially a matter of public record, so that any neighbor or colleague could look up exactly what I earned there, while the United States, the supposedly suspicious society, guards the confidentiality of returns almost absolutely.

I would like to understand what philosophical commitments these practices presuppose. What view of the citizen, and of the citizen's relation to the state, does a pre-filled, verify-and-confirm system rest on, and what view does a self-reported, audit-enforced system rest on? Do the two regimes genuinely presuppose opposite things about trust — one assuming the state to be an honest bookkeeper acting on the citizen's behalf, the other assuming the citizen to be a potential evader who must certify everything personally — or is that framing too quick? In your answer, please distinguish what each system strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of the citizen-state relationship would license them equally.

I am especially interested in the Swedish wrinkle. Is the public availability of individual tax data a further expression of the same underlying view that motivates pre-filled returns — transparency, egalitarianism, a presumption that there is nothing shameful to hide — or does it stand in real tension with the trust that pre-filling seems to embody, such that Sweden is actually running on two different and only contingently compatible commitments? And correspondingly, does American return confidentiality express a genuine respect for the privacy of the individual against the state, or merely an implicit admission that the state does not trust itself or its fellow citizens with the information?

Finally, since I now have to decide how I will personally relate to both systems for years to come, tell me which bundle of presuppositions strikes you as more reasonable: whether the trust the Swedish system builds on, or the suspicion the American system builds on, is the sounder default for a modern state — and, concretely, what would have to change in each country's practice if it adopted the opposite presupposition wholeheartedly rather than piecemeal. Please write your answer in American English.
