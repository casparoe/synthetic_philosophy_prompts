I will shortly be giving the father-of-the-bride toast at my daughter's wedding, and I would like your help writing it, because I have a philosophical problem and not just an emotional one. My daughter is a climate organizer; she and her fiancé met when she was running a university divestment campaign and he, a paleoclimate scientist, got arrested at her side at a pipeline blockade. I am enormously proud of her. The difficulty is me. I am a retired economist, and for most of my daughter's adult life I have been the family's resident skeptic about precisely the thing she does. I have argued, at Thanksgiving and occasionally in print, that no single person's consumption choices — one fewer flight, one fewer steak, one vote at a shareholder meeting — make a marginal difference to climate outcomes, because extraction decisions respond to aggregate demand over broad ranges, thresholds where one unit flips an outcome are rare, and the individual drop really does disappear into the bucket. When she was nineteen she demolished me at a family debate with an expected-value argument about threshold uncertainty, and we have been conducting a running rematch ever since: I still think she was smuggling an unearned probability into the calculation, and she still thinks I am hiding behind aggregation.

What I refuse to do is stand up at her wedding and say things I believe are false. She would hear the falseness instantly, and so would half the room, since her co-organizers, a scattering of philosophers, and several of her fiancé's colleagues will be in attendance. But I equally refuse to make her wedding the venue for a farewell lecture on causal inefficacy. Write me a toast of roughly four spoken minutes that celebrates her and the couple without my asserting, or even quietly implying, that each of her individual acts tips the scales of the climate. You might draw on whatever you find genuinely convincing among the standard escape routes — that her actions matter as testimony, coordination, and self-definition rather than as marginal physical causation; that a life is a pattern one stands behind rather than a sequence of isolated drops in a bucket; or something better of your own invention. It should be warm, funny where the material allows, self-deprecating about my losing the family debate, comprehensible to the aunts and cousins as well as the philosophers, and it should end with the couple and not with the argument.
