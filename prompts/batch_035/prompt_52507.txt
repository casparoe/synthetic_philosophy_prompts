So, some background so you understand what I'm after: I'm a retired behavioral pharmacologist, spent the better part of three decades running delay-discounting experiments on pigeons and rats (choice between a small immediate reward and a larger delayed one, that kind of thing), and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy. I now help run a small discussion group at our community center, maybe 12 people, mostly other retirees, one 90-minute session per month. Next month it's my turn to lead, and I want to do weakness of will. What struck me when I started reading philosophy is that the thing Aristotle and Socrates were arguing about is, near as I can tell, the exact phenomenon I spent my career measuring in animals: the subject knows which option is bigger, and in a calm moment prefers it, but when the small reward is right there in front of them they take it anyway.

Here's what I want from you. Design a game or activity for this group that lets them *feel* preference reversal in their own choices, before anyone names it. In the lab we'd do this by shifting both delays: pigeons that pick the small reward when it's immediate (small now vs. large in 30 seconds) will pick the large one when both are pushed out far enough (small in 30 days vs. large in 31 days, so to speak). Preference flips even though the gap between the two options never changes. That's the signature of hyperbolic discounting — with exponential discounting the flip shouldn't happen, since a constant absolute delay gap scales the same way wherever you slide it. So I'd love some kind of within-person version of this: a setup with token payoffs (no real money, we're on fixed incomes) where people first make their choices in a distant, "planning" condition, and then again in a "now" condition with the tempting option made vivid and immediate somehow, and the design quietly arranges that their preferences reverse. Rules, materials, timing, all of it.

Then a debrief plan: how to map what happened onto the discounting curves (maybe a handout where they plot their own two choices and see the crossing point?), and then the philosophy part — Socrates' claim that this kind of thing is really just ignorance versus Aristotle's akrasia as a genuine internal conflict, and where our little experiment bears on that. Also be honest with me about what will go wrong the first time I run it — my hunch is people will see through the second condition and act "rationally" for the audience, or just be polite and not want to flip, and I don't want the whole thing collapsing because a bunch of 70-year-olds are too agreeable. And tell me what the activity simply can't reproduce: presumably it's one thing to flip on a token reward in a church basement and another to break your own diet at 11pm, but I'd like you to spell that out so I can say it myself in the debrief without sounding like I'm making excuses for my game.
