I teach political philosophy, and this term I am building a unit on the ethics of collective memory around a puzzle my students keep running into; I want a worked-out analysis I can measure their essays against.

The puzzle is a pattern that modern states run in two compartments at once. In one, the state compels remembrance: some countries make Holocaust denial a crime; legislatures institute days of mourning by statute; curricula are required to teach particular atrocities; archivists are instructed that certain records must be preserved in perpetuity, never to be destroyed even once they are no longer consulted. In the other, the same states compel forgetting: juvenile convictions are expunged after a set term; old case files and personnel records are sealed for decades before anyone may read them; data protection law requires personal data to be erased once its purpose has passed and has been applied to de-list stale search results about a person; a spent conviction becomes something an employer may not ask about — a true fact that the individual is nonetheless entitled to have treated as though it did not exist.

What theory of memory, and of its relation to justice, does this pattern presuppose? Please take up the question along these specific angles:

1. The two compartments appear to sort by subject and by beneficiary. The duties to remember attach to what was done to victims, very often the dead, and seem to be owed by a community; the duties to forget attach to what a living individual did, and are owed to that individual. Is the implicit theory something like: remembering is a debt to the dead that cannot be discharged, while forgetting is a benefit to the living that can be earned? Test that formulation against the awkward cases — denial laws also protect survivors, who are living, and expungement is most often claimed by people who have done something wrong.

2. A skeptic in my seminar offers a debunking explanation in place of a theory: states remember the crimes of others and forget their own. The archives of a state's own atrocities get sealed; the memorials go to the enemy's. On this view the pattern presupposes nothing about justice — it is simply the memory of the victor. Say whether the practice can be given a principled implicit theory that survives this debunking, or whether the debunking explanation is itself the implicit theory.

3. Mark where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined: which rival theories of memory and justice would equally license it. And say what would have to change in the practice if the implicit theory were replaced with the view that beyond the generation that was bereaved, memory has no moral claim on anyone at all.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
