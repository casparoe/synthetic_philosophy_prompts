I'd like your considered credence that the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — Dutch book arguments, money pumps, and the various representation theorems — actually establish their conclusion: that an agent whose preferences over acts fail to have expected-utility form is thereby making a rational error, and not merely departing from a convenient idealization. By "establish" I mean something stronger than "the arguments are persuasive": an agent who violates the theory, knows that it does, and understands the arguments, is still doing something rationally criticizable. I don't want a vague verdict that the arguments are strong; I want a number, with the derivation that produces it.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following terms, adding any others you think belong:

1. The probability that Dutch book and money pump arguments reveal a defect in the agent itself, rather than only in a situation the agent can decline to enter. The standard versions seem to require that the agent accepts bets at posted odds or faces each choice in the pumping sequence as presented; if an agent that violates the axioms can simply refuse the bookmaker's offers, or bind itself in advance so that it never confronts the sequence in which the pump operates — something in the vicinity of resolute choice — it is unclear what has been shown to be incoherent. How much does the strength of these arguments turn on whether such precommitment is available and whether it counts as rational?

2. The probability that the additional premises of the representation theorems — above all the independence or substitutability axiom, the sure-thing principle — are normatively compelling in their own right, rather than being read back off the conclusion they are needed to reach. Here I would particularly like your assessment of how much the Ellsberg-type phenomena and Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility, which abandon exactly that axiom while claiming to preserve coherence, weaken the claim that coherence by itself forces expected-utility form rather than merely something in its neighborhood.

3. The probability that the coherence arguments, even where they succeed, establish expected utility maximization only relative to probabilities and a state-space the agent already possesses — so that they constrain how given credences are used without saying anything about which credences to have — and whether that gap should reduce your credence in the original proposition or leave it untouched.

4. Whatever else you judge belongs: for instance, whether "rational error" is well-defined for preferences at all, whether the synchronic and diachronic arguments deserve different weights, and whether the two ever genuinely diverge in what they license.

Then combine the terms, state the final number, and identify which term the final number is most sensitive to, together with the argument or result that would move that term the most. If you think the proposition as I've framed it is ill-posed — for example, because the coherence results constrain preferences only given a prior representation, making the conclusion close to tautological — say so explicitly, and give your credence under a sharpened reformulation instead, with the same decomposition.
