I moderate a discord server for about 20k members (retro computing stuff, if that matters) and I'm the one who has to decide which of our unwritten rules we write down in the welcome doc and which we leave alone. This fight comes up like clockwork: one mod says "if newcomers keep getting in trouble for breaking a rule they couldn't have known about, write it down," another says "half of these aren't rules, they're just habits, and writing them down turns habits into laws and changes them." I've stopped being able to tell which of them is right about which cases.

I recently read a bit about David Lewis's book Convention (1969) — conventions as solutions to coordination problems, held up by everyone expecting everyone else to conform rather than by enforcement, arbitrary in the sense that some alternative arrangement would have coordinated everyone just as well, but sticky once in place. I'm honestly not confident I have this exactly right, so correct me where I'm off. It struck me that this framework gives me roughly the distinctions I need: mere habit vs. convention proper vs. norm with teeth, plus maybe a story about what codification does to a convention — my worry is that putting it in writing shifts it from "this is how we do things here" to "this is a rule with a rule-giver," which changes its character even if the words are identical.

So: write me a procedure I can actually run on a candidate regularity when the "should we write it down" question comes up. Steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure should stop with a "don't codify," where it should say "codify, and here's the framing that does least damage," and where it has to hand the decision back to me because it's a values question rather than a classification question. Also tell me where the procedure is likely to misfire.

Then run it on three real cases from the last few months:

1. Newcomers keep getting quietly frozen out because they don't know that questions about a certain deprecated tool go in one channel and not another. Everyone who's been around a while knows it; nobody ever states it; no one gets punished, they just get silence.

2. An emoji-reaction habit: one specific reaction has come to mean "I saw this, thanks." It spread from one popular member and now functions almost like acknowledgment etiquette — some people genuinly get offended when it isn't used on their posts. Nobody enforces anything.

3. Self-promotion. This one has real enforcement (mods delete posts, occasionally ban) but it's contested — maybe 40% of active members think we're way too strict — and writing down the current practice would basically declare a winner in an ongoing argument.

Case 3 is the one I expect your procedure to be least comfortable with, and possibly the one where I won't like its answer, but that's what I want it for. Also tell me straight what the procedure can't do — if the whole Lewis framework just goes silent on contested norms with enforcement, I want to know that instead of watching you pretend everything is a coordination problem.

This comes up every few weeks and each round eats a two hour mod call, so keep it usable by the other mods, none of whom have read any philosophy. And keep it honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
