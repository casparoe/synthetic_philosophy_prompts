I'm in a political philosophy course and we just wrapped the unit on desert vs entitlement (Rawls on moral desert vs legitimate expectations, that whole debate about whether anyone actually *deserves* their talents). For my essay I want to read Ratatouille through that material, and I'd like your help thinking it through. Here's what seems loaded to me. Gusteau's motto "anyone can cook" looks like pure meritocratic ideology, and yet the film spends most of its runtime showing that the kitchen isn't a meritocracy at all: Skinner holds power through inheritance paperwork, not talent; Linguini gets the fame for cooking he didn't do; and Remy, who has the only real desert-claim in the whole film, is entitled to literally nothing — he's a rat, and the health code exists precisely to exclude him no matter how good he is. So on paper it's the desert/entitlement gap: the one who deserves can't hold, the ones who hold don't deserve. But then Ego's final review seems to fold that back into meritocracy, just a smarter version of it — "not everyone can become a great artist, but a great artist can come from anywhere." Is that a rejection of the motto or a rescue of it? And I genuinely can't tell whether the ending (restaurant shut down, but the new bistro has Remy cooking in the open with a human staff) is saying institutions can be reformed to track desert, or whether it's just a fantasy patch over the fact that desert and entitlement came apart and stayed apart. I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously: where the film dramatizes the distinction better than the articles do, where it cheats, and whether the movie ends up smuggling in a claim about desert that Rawls would deny. Don't treat it as just an illustration — if some scenes resist the desert reading, say so.
