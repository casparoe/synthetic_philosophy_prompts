I help run a little philosophy-and-film night and next up is Arrival, the 2016 Villeneuve film based on Ted Chiang's Story of Your Life. I want to read it thru Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, and I keep going back and fourth on whether the reading actually works, so I'm hoping you can do the careful version.

Here's the setup as I see it. With Nietzsche you've got (at least) two things going on with recurrence: the cosmological claim that everything recurs identically forever, and the practical test — the demon in The Gay Science who offers you your same life again, every pain and joy, innumerable times and unchanged, where your answer is supposed to reveal something about how you've lived. In the film, learning the heptapod language rewires how Louise experiences time — she effectively remembers the future, including her daughter Hannah's whole life and her early death — and she says yes to it anyway: marries Ian, has the kid, knowing how it ends.

So what's the mapping? Is heptapod time (the circular logograms, the nonlinear writing) playing the role of the cosmological recurrence, or is it really more like a block universe — and does Nietzsche's circle actually require anything like that, or does the film quietly swap in a different metaphysics? Is Louise's foreknowledge the demon's offer? Because the crucial disanalogy, I think, is that Nietzsche's test gives you no new information, it only changes your relation to the life you already have, whereas the heptapods hand Louise actual knowledge — her yes is informed in a way the demon's victim's yes isn't. Does that make her affirmation something stronger than amor fati, or something weaker, closer to a gamble with the odds posted? And there's a genuine choice point: she can apparently not have the kid, or not tell Ian what she knows. Nietzsche's test is about attitude toward a life you cannot change; the film is about deciding to bring about a life you already know everything about. Are those even the same operation?

A related thing that bugs me: all the affirmation comes from the mother's side. Hannah never gets to weigh in on her short life — she's the one who has to live it, and the film never asks her. Does the eternal recurrence only work as a first-person test, and if so, what does the film's structure (everything is Louise's perspective, the audience is locked into it) do with that? Is it a cop out, or is it more honest than the aphorism, which also only ever asks one person?

And last one: the film flirts with fatalism (the free will question is basically waved at and then set aside in a line or two), while Nietzsche, on the practical reading anyway, wants affirmation without causal determinism doing any of the work. Does the film trade on a confusion between "it's fixed" and "I would do it again," or does dramatizing the thing show something the aphorism can't show? Point me to the scenes that would carry the most weight for discussion, and be honest if the whole reading just strains the film into being an illustration rather than something genuinely about this.
