I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts about how one should change one's degrees of belief when the evidence arrives dirty — not as a clean fact learned for certain, but as a shift in confidence — and I want to know whether a single updating principle lies behind them, or whether some of the verdicts must go. My tentative rule, which I suspect is not quite right, is this: when experience moves your confidence in a proposition E from p to q without settling it, redistribute everything else you believe as though you had split into two versions of yourself, one that learned E for certain and one that learned not-E for certain, weighted by q and 1 minus q. I understand something like this is called Jeffrey conditioning. Now the cases.

First, the candlelight one. I glance at a piece of cloth by candlelight and find myself seventy percent confident it is green rather than red, where beforehand I was fifty-fifty. I take it I should also shift my credence in anything hitched to the cloth's color — that it will match the rug, that it will photograph a certain way — roughly in step, and my rule handles that.

Second, a diagnostic test known to be right ninety percent of the time comes back positive for a condition I antecedently thought I had one chance in ten of having. Here I think ordinary conditioning on the positive result is correct, which by Bayes puts me around one half, and that feels right.

Third, the order case. I glimpse the cloth in bad light and become seventy percent confident it is green, then carry it to the window and see plainly that it is green; I should end up effectively certain. If the order were reversed — window first, then the vague glimpse re-considered — I should end up certain too. But applying my rule twice in the two orders gives different answers; the updates do not commute. And I hold, firmly, that two people with the same total history of evidence should not disagree merely because of the order it arrived in. Something has to give, and I cannot tell whether it is the rule or the commutativity verdict.

Fourth, the extra-content case. The glimpse that makes me seventy percent confident the cloth is green also tells me something else: that the light in that corner is strange, that my color vision in this room is less trustworthy than I believed. My rule sees only the shift about green and propagates nothing about the lighting, yet my confidence that the rug near that corner is red should drop as well. The evidence seems to carry content beyond a softened version of learning green.

Fifth, repetition. Two independent glances in the same bad light, each of which alone would take me from fifty to seventy percent, should take me further than one; but going over the memory of a single glance a second time should not move me at all. Applied naively, my rule cannot tell these apart.

Sixth, conditional evidence. A colleague phones and, before the line drops, I catch only that if they used the assay we trust, the sample is positive. What I have is evidence about a connection rather than about an outcome, and it feels different in kind from the candlelight case, though I cannot say whether any rule of this shape should treat it differently.

Seventh, certainty. After looking at the cloth at the window in daylight I think I am entitled to full confidence that it is green, though I know hallucination and strange lighting are never impossible; and I also think no rational person should assign probability exactly one or zero to any empirical claim. I hold both, unhelpfully.

So here is what I want. Is there one principle of belief revision — or a small family — that captures all or most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say for each clause which case forces it, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save and why those are the ones to sacrifice, and then tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed: two witnesses of independent reliability sixty percent and seventy percent both report an event I antecedently thought one in a hundred; should I end up more or less confident than a single witness of some intermediate reliability would make me, and does your principle yield a clean answer there? Write it as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the sort of thing that could be sent as a letter.
