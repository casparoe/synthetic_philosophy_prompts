My two friends Maya and Jonah got into it after our wilderness first aid refresher last weekend. The instructor ended with a classic: you're the only responder, you can only get to one site in time. At site A, five hikers will die without immediate treatment. At site B, one hiker will die. The instructor definately wanted us to just say "the five" and head home, but Jonah wouldn't let it go.

Maya said obviously the five — five deaths is worse than one death, and anyone who hesitates is overthinking it. Jonah said wait, look at it from the single hiker's point of view: that person's claim on your rescue isn't any weaker just because four other people happen to also be dying somewhere else. Each of the five, taken one at a time, has exactly the same claim he does. No one on either side loses more than their own life, so no individual is facing a bigger loss than any other, and there's no person for whom "five deaths" is the loss. If you save the one, none of the five can point to anything worse happening to them than what was already at stake for him.

Maya said she doesn't need to add lives up like that. She could grant that every claim is equal, and still: five equal claims versus one equal claim. The one's claim cancels out one of theirs, and then there are four claims left standing with nothing to answer them. That's not utilitarian bookkeeping, she said — it's just counting claims, the same way we'd count votes, and the side with more claims wins.

Jonah pounced on that leftover-four move. He said it only works if a person's claim gets stronger by outnumbering, which is exactly what he's denying. Or put the other way: if each of the five has a claim equal to the single hiker's, why does his claim count for less just because he's alone? The fact that others would also die seems like a bad reason for his claim on the rescue to get weaker. Being the only one in danger shouldn't make you matter less, and being one of many shouldn't make each of you matter less either.

Maya's last word was: fine, then flip a coin — she said she could of lived with a lottery being fair if you genuinely thought the claims balanced. But Jonah's position, as stated, ends up choosing the one, and she wanted to know what makes "this guy happens to be alone" a better tie-breaker than "there are more of them." Jonah didn't really answer, he said the burden is on her to show the numbers do the work, not on him to show they don't. And then their ride showed up.

I mostly watched, and honestly I can't tell how much of my own leaning toward Maya is just me being annoyed at Jonah for turning a first aid class into a seminar. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And since I might be fooling myself here: also tell me what would change your mind about the final verdict, or where you think your own reasoning on this is most likely to go wrong.
