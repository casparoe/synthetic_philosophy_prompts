my family's from sri lanka and I now go to a mostly-convert zen center in the states, and the two places have basically opposite food norms. back home, my aunts would put curried chicken in the monks' bowls on pindapata morning and everyone understood this as a merit-making occasion -- and the monks eat it, no problem, because of the old rule from the discourse to the doctor Jivaka: meat is fine for a monastic as long as he didn't see the animal killed, didn't hear it, and has no reason to suspect it was killed specifically for him. at the zen center, putting meat in front of a teacher would be somewhere between gauche and a scandal, and a big chunk of the sangha is vegan on explicitly buddhist grounds.

so heres what I want untangled. treat the traditional arrangement as a package: monks accepting alms meat under the seen/heard/suspected rule, laypeople earning merit by donating that very meat, and -- the part that really gets me -- the same monastic code that permits the chicken forbidding monks from digging soil or picking plants, on the ground that this destroys living things and generates bad karma for the monk. so a worm counts but a chicken doesn't? what view of moral responsibility and karma does that whole package even make sense under? is it that karma tracks only your intention at the moment of acquisition, and the animal's death is karmically inert as far as the eater is concerned? but then why the epistemic condition at all -- how does having *seen* the slaughter change anything about what I did or intended? is it secretly a complicity rule, like fencing stolen goods? a market-participation rule before its time? or maybe the merit of giving is just not the kind of thing that can be cancelled by the harm it channels, in which case what exactly is the theory of how good and bad karma stack?

and then the convert version: if you rebuild buddhist dietary norms on straightforwardly consequentialist grounds -- my demand caused that killing, so I should withdraw it -- do you actually land on veganism, or does the logic point somewhere stranger, and what happens to the almsgiving economy along the way?

what I'm hoping you'll do is reconstruct the implicit theory behind the traditional package, tell me which parts of it strictly require something like an intention-only view of karma and which parts are underdetermined (i.e., a consequentialist or a virtue theorist could also sign off on them), and say where, if anywhere, the package is just flat-out internally inconsistent. and if replacing the presupposed view with a consequentialist one forces changes beyond the dinner table -- e.g. in what counts as a meritorious gift -- I want to hear about that too.
