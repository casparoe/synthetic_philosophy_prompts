My roommate is an econ PhD student and he has this argument he trots out whenever redistribution or welfare policy comes up, and I can't find the weak step. He's basically a Robbins-style ordinalist: each person's utility only gets pinned down up to a positive affine transformation, so there's no common unit, and he thinks that makes interpersonal comparisons non-factual. As best I can reconstruct it:

(1) The only evidence that could bear on whether Ann is better off than Bob is evidence about Ann and Bob: what they choose, what they report, how their bodies behave.
(2) Take any such evidence you like. There are utility assignments fully consistent with it on which Ann is much better off than Bob, and equally consistent assignments on which Bob is much better off — you just give each person their own origin and unit.
(3) So no possible evidence could ever favor "Ann is better off" over "Bob is better off."
(4) A claim that no possible evidence could favor is not a factual claim.
(5) So "Ann is better off than Bob" is never factual, and neither is any policy claim that needs it, like "this tax helps the people it helps more than it hurts the people it hurts."

Each step sounds fine to me, but the conclusion seems to prove way too much. Run it with past-me and present-me as the two people: all my evidence about how bad yesterday's headache was consists of memories and reports — exactly the sort of thing in (1) — and it's equally compatible with any rescaling of yesterday-me's units. So it looks like I can never factually say today's pain is worse than yesterday's, and by extension nobody can factually compare their own wellbeing across time. My roommate shrugs and says fine, so what — but he also insists "my headache this morning was worse than my thirst an hour later" is a factual claim, and he definately won't give up the preference ascriptions his entire discipline runs on. I feel like he's paying too little somewhere but I can't see where.

So: which step would you reject, why did that step look fine to me in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost? Please answer in American English.
