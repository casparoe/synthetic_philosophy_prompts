I've read LessWrong and the surrounding blogs for about a decade, and after all the timelines-of-doom arguing I still don't have a settled view on when a long-run prediction counts as falsified versus when the theory behind it is "right but early." So here are my gut verdicts, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

Malthus said population growth would keep pressing against the food supply with famine and misery as the check; England industrialized instead, and I don't call him "right but early" — I think he was genuinely wrong about the mechanism, because the mechanism he described (positive checks releasing pressure) was real in the world he observed but got switched off by the demographic transition, and a mechanism that gets switched off is not a mechanism that merely hasn't fired yet. Paul Ehrlich's Population Bomb scenarios for the 1970s-80s famines: falsified, and the common rescue that he was "right about the trend, wrong about the timing" feels like cheating to me — a prediction with a date that fails by fifty years has to count against something. Hubbert's original curve for US lower-48 oil production: it tracked reality for decades and then hydrofracking broke it, and here I'm inclined to say the theory wasn't falsified, it just had an implicitly fixed technology parameter that someone un-fixed. The Limits to Growth team: their base scenario pointed to trouble around now-ish rather than 2000, they've repeatedly said the model was about the shape of the trajectory rather than the date, and my verdict is that this sits in an uncomfortable middle — I believe them that they never intended a hard date, and I also believe that a theory immune to any non-arrival of its crisis is barely a forecast at all. Finally, my friend who is deep into AI timelines insists that a prediction of transformative AI "sometime this century" is a real prediction even though no observation before the event could disconfirm it, and I half-agree with him and half think this is exactly the Malthusian rescue done prospectively.

A friend from a forecasting meetup proposed a tentative standard that I'd like you to test and repair: a theory is rescued rather than refuted when the intervening years changed the inputs (new technology, new institutions, new resources) and merely failed to fire when the inputs would have stayed the same — so the question is always what the theory held fixed. Test that against my verdicts. It seems to handle Hubbert, but I can't make it treat Ehrlich and Malthus differently, and I do treat them differently, even though both plausibly failed because an input (agricultural technology, the demographic transition) changed in ways the author didn't foresee. Either tell me which verdict to drop, or find me the version of the principle that saves them, and say precisely what it implies for two cases I haven't listed: whether someone in 1910 predicting "eventually a single power will be able to destroy civilization in a day" counts as a successful long-run forecast, and whether Moore's-law-style exponential-resource predictions that fail after a century count as early or as wrong.
