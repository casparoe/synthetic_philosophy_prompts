Please explain how Lewis Carroll's regress of the tortoise relates to Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations, and whether the two are ultimately the same problem in different dress.

Both involve a regress that seems to undermine something we thought was secure. In Carroll's dialogue, the Tortoise accepts the premises of a simple inference but refuses to accept the conclusion, and agrees to write down a further premise stating that the earlier premises together license the conclusion — yet still refuses. Every added premise licenses a new demand for a further premise, so the license to infer never gets exercised; the regress is meant to show that the authority of a deductive step cannot consist in an additional proposition. In the rule-following case, the worry is that any interpretation I attach to a rule (say, the series of numbers generated by adding two) is compatible with a deviant future application, and any further rule added to pin down the interpretation is itself open to interpretation, restarting the problem; the regress is meant to show that a rule's grip on my future behavior cannot consist in a further mental or textual item that interprets it.

I can see that these are kin, but I cannot tell how close. Both look like the schema "the item that was supposed to make X follow from Y turns out to need another item of the same kind to make *it* work." Yet the targets seem different: one threatens the justification of deduction, the other the possibility of meaning or of a rule determining anything at all. Please explain precisely what each regress is supposed to establish, where the structural analogy holds and where it breaks, and whether a Carroll-style tortoise could be recast as a rule-following skeptic — or whether something would be lost in the translation. If they come apart, I would like to understand exactly at which step.
