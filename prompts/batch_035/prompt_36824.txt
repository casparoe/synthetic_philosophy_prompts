I'm putting together a short position statement for our reading group on moral emotions (we've been doing guilt vs shame for a few weeks now, mostly via Bernard Williams and the standard Anglophone anti-shame literature) and before I circulate it I want someone to stress-test it. Here's roughly where I stand:

(1) Guilt is the better moral emotion, full stop — it targets the act rather than the whole self, and it motivates repair (apologising, undoing, changing) instead of hiding. Shame's behavioral profile is basically withdrawal, and that's fatal for a moral emotion.

(2) That said, I think Williams was basically right that the standard critique attacks a strawman: properly construed, shame isn't global self-condemnation, it's being seen (real or imagined) by an internalized other whose view actually matters to you. On that construal shame looks like it can carry real moral weight.

(3) The only deep difference between guilt and shame is intentional object: guilt is about what you did, shame is about what you are / how you appear. Everything else — the repair-vs-hiding thing in (1) — is downstream contingent stuff, not part of the emotion itself.

(4) A culture can, over a few generations, deliberately choose its moral-emotion profile. We basically did it with dueling and honor culture, and the cross-cultural guilt/shame literature treats the East/West differences as malleable rather than fixed. So if shame really is the defective one, a culture would be within its rights to engineer shame down and guilt up.

(5) But any moral emotion that a culture deliberately engineers loses its moral authority. The emotions that bind us have to grow wild — the moment guilt is a policy instrument rather than something people just come to feel, it stops doing the binding.

(6) I also suspect guilt is impossible without norms that were themselves socially engineered at some point — nobody is born with the internalized norm against, say, insider trading.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity rather than just flagging everything. For each real one, tell me what would have to give and what it costs. And then say which single revision would clean up the most. I honestly don't know where the weak joint is — (2) and (1) feel like they're fighting but I'm not sure they really are, whereas (4) and (5) might be reconcilable in a way I haven't seen. Please answer in British English, and don't be polite about it, I'd rather find out now than in front of the group.
