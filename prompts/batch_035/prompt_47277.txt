I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar that began with the Hart–Fuller debate, and I've run into a real-world practice that seems to want it both ways, and I can't work out what theory of law it actually presupposes. The practice is this: after German reunification in 1990, the Federal Republic put on trial the border guards who had shot people trying to flee East Germany over the Berlin Wall, along with the officials and judges who had ordered or endorsed the shootings. The guards' defense was straightforwardly positivist: under the law of the state that existed at the time and place of the acts — the GDR's own statutes on border security — the shootings were lawful, or at least permitted, and punishing them now under the Federal Republic's criminal code looks like retroactive punishment, which the same courts' professed principle of legality forbids. The German courts responded in two different registers. Sometimes they reached for something like Radbruch's formula: a statutory command of such extreme injustice, the reasoning goes, fails to be law at all, so there was no valid GDR rule to obey, and no retroactivity in applying ordinary homicide law. But other times, apparently nervous about that route, they instead argued that the GDR's own border law, properly interpreted, already prohibited the shootings — reading a prohibition back into the very statutes invoked to justify them — precisely so that the punishment would rest on law in force at the time. On top of this, a European human-rights court later upheld the convictions on yet a third ground: that the shootings violated international law that had bound the GDR itself when the acts occurred. So one legal system, addressing one set of deaths, seems to have alternated among natural-law reasoning, positivist interpretation, and appeal to a third legal order, and I can't tell whether that's a deep inconsistency or a coherent strategy.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of law this practice presupposes. Does the conviction of the guards strictly require the natural-law premise that grossly unjust commands are not law, or can a positivist get the same result — say, by treating the Federal Republic as a new sovereign whose criminal law simply applies to the territory, or by the interpretive move of finding the prohibition inside the GDR's own statutes? Which of the three grounds the courts actually used genuinely does the justificatory work, and which are ornamental? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that both Hart and Fuller could each accept the outcome while rejecting the other's reasoning? I'd also like your view on whether the practice commits its officials to anything they must then say elsewhere: if a court accepts that an unjust statute is no law at all, does that put pressure on how it may treat everyday, only moderately unjust, statutes it dislikes?
