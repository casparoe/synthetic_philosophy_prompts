I'm a graduate student writing a paper on whether robustness and alignment always pull in the same direction, and I keep bumping into an unstated assumption in the inner alignment literature: that objective misgeneralization is monotonic bad news. The standard picture, descending from the mesa-optimization framework in Hubinger et al.'s 2019 paper "Risks from Learned Optimization in Advanced Machine Learning Systems" and from the goal misgeneralization results in deep RL (e.g., the 2021 arXiv paper "Goal Misgeneralization in Deep Reinforcement Learning"), is that the objective a training process actually instills—the mesa-objective, in that vocabulary—matches the intended one on the training distribution, and that when distributional shift drives them apart, the mismatch can only hurt. For the familiar direction, the literature already supplies examples: an agent in a gridworld trained to collect a coin that happens to lie along the route to a flag generalizes to heading straight for the flag and ignoring the coin once the coin is moved off the route—same capabilities, wrong goal, worse outcome. What I have never seen worked out is the reverse direction: a case where, under shift, the misgeneralized objective points somewhere safer than the intended one does, so that the system that failed inner alignment behaves better at deployment than a perfectly inner-aligned twin built from the same training run.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. First, the setting must be mundane—an RLHF-trained coding assistant, or a robot trained in simulation with reward given by a scripted evaluator—no superintelligence, no exotic physics, nothing a lab wouldn't plausibly build this decade. Second, the failure must be genuine goal misgeneralization of the "same capabilities, wrong goal" kind: the learned proxy objective and the intended objective must coincide on the training distribution, diverge off-distribution, and the system must remain fully competent at pursuing the proxy; a degenerate model that simply gets worse at everything does not count. Third, and this is the crux, the safety advantage must be generated by the structure of the proxy rather than stipulated—if the scenario just declares that the model "also happened to learn caution," that's cheating; the advantage has to follow from what the proxy rewards once the environment shifts. Fourth, deception is banned: the system must have no situational awareness and must not be sandbagging; the benefit has to come from the objective, not from the model strategically holding back. Fifth, the deployment shift should be one labs actually create—scaling to longer tasks or new domains—and under it the inner-aligned twin must predictably, and for a statable reason, do something worse.

After the scenario itself (at most two paragraphs), please provide the walkthrough: state the objective the system actually internalized, say why proxy and intended objective were indistinguishable on the training distribution, describe the deployment shift and the divergence, and spell out what the inner-aligned twin does and why that is worse. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the comparison if that feature is removed. Then give me the strongest objection an alignment researcher could raise who insists that inner misalignment is monotonic bad news—I have in mind the worry that my whole case secretly relocates the problem to outer alignment, on the grounds that if the misgeneralized objective is the safer one, then the "intended" objective was badly specified all along and inner alignment is doing no real work in the example—but evaluate that objection on its merits rather than merely conceding it. Close with your honest verdict on whether cases like this could ever be exploited deliberately, given that the proxy and the intended objective are by construction indistinguishable during training and thus the lab cannot tell in advance which way the divergence will break. Please write the entire response in American English.
