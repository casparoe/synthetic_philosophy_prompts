I'm contributing to a volume in which political theorists reconstruct how canonical thinkers would have responded to climate change, and I've been assigned Machiavelli. The assignment requires more than the usual cartoon of Machiavelli as proto-realist cheerleading for hard power; the editors want the reconstruction anchored in his actual treatment of fortune and virtù, then stretched honestly to a problem he never faced. Here is the scenario to work with.

Suppose Machiavelli could be fully briefed on anthropogenic climate change: the mechanism, the multidecadal lag between emissions and warming, the possibility of thresholds and irreversible shifts, and — crucially — the fact that the threatening process is not a blind external accident but the aggregate byproduct of centuries of ordinary human industry, and that it can be forecast probabilistically decades in advance even though it cannot be precisely predicted. In his famous treatment of fortune in The Prince, he compares fortune to a destructive flooding river that men can restrain only with dikes and embankments raised in calm weather; he claims fortune is the arbiter of about half of our actions while leaving the rest to us; he remarks that fortune favors the bold and impetuous over the cautious; and throughout the book he measures princes by their capacity to discipline circumstance rather than submit to it.

Climate change strains each element of that framework in a different way, and I want you to reconstruct his likely response piece by piece. First, the conceptual question: does slow-onset, anthropogenic, probabilistically forecastable risk even count as fortune in his sense, or does fortune require genuine unforeseeability? If foresight converts fortune into something more like an approaching enemy or a degenerating internal condition, which of his categories fits best — external fortune, an external enemy, or the corruption (corruzione) he diagnoses in declining republics? Second, the agency question: virtù in Machiavelli is a property of individual princes and, occasionally, of well-ordered republics acting as a body. What does his framework have to say about a harm jointly produced by nearly everyone and remediable only through coordination across generations, where no single ruler's virtù can be decisive? Does the framework simply fail to reach the case, or does he have resources — for instance his account of how institutions, not just leaders, can embody collective discipline — that carry over? Third, the practical question: given his claims that preparations must be made in calm weather and that audacity beats caution, reconstruct his likely ranking of the main policy families — early mitigation, adaptation, and solar geoengineering. Is geoengineering the impetuousness he prizes, or an overreach his framework would flag? And would he regard the political difficulty of acting before the flood visibly arrives as the deepest obstacle, given his skepticism about whether peoples and princes can be moved to prepare for troubles they do not yet feel?

Please commit to definite answers wherever his framework genuinely yields one, and be explicit about the points where you are interpolating rather than extrapolating — the editors want the seams visible. You need not provide citations; refer to the arguments and works by name and I will supply references myself.
