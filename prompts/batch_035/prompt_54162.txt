ok here's my deal: I'm doing one of those workshop things where everyone writes down their decision-theory commitments on a card and then we all attack each other's. Here's my card, and I'd like a consistency audit before I hand it over:

(1) I think the vNM axioms apart from independence — completeness, transitivity, continuity — are genuine requirements of rationality, and what justifies them is the exploitability / dynamic consistency style of argument: you shouldn't choose now in a way you'd pay to undo later, and that's what nails down transitivity etc.

(2) In the classic Allias pair I take the sure million over the gamble with the small chance of nothing, and then in the paired choice I take the bigger gamble — and I don't just concede these are biases. I'd choose the same way with unlimited time to think, and my choices don't flip when the lotteries get redescribed, so I don't think I'm being confused by framing.

(3) When I introspect on why, the reason is comparative: getting $0 after passing up a sure $1M is worse for me than plain $0, because of what I could have had instead. The regret and the disappointment are part of the badness of that outcome, not some separate psychological tax that a rational agent should be trained out of counting.

(4) I'm broadly a consequentialist about choice: the value of a gamble is exhausted by how good its outcomes are and how probable they are. Nothing extra attaches to the "texture" of the gamble itself or to the story of how you got there.

(5) I think the ambiguity-averse choices in Ellsberg's urns are irrational, and I'd want to defend that verdict using the same battery of arguements I appealed to in (1).

So: does this hang together? Rank whatever tensions you find by how load-bearing they are — tell me which pairs are genuinely in trouble, which only look that way, and what the single cheapest revision would be if I want to keep as much of the card as possible.
