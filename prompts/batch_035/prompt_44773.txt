My partner and I keep noticing how weirdly inconsistent AI assistants are about deciding whats good for you, and I've been trying to figure out what theory of paternalism the whole pattern is implicitly working with. Concretely, the stuff I've seen: if you tell the assistant you're feeling hopeless and ask for anything that could relate to hurting yourself, it drops the actual task, refuses, and pivots to crisis line numbers and supportive-check-in mode. If you ask for medical advice you get a paragraph of disclaimers and then it basically answers anyway. If you ask it to help write a vaguely manipulative apology letter to your ex it usually just does it, unless you're really explicit about the manipulation part. And it'll happily help you plan a casino weekend, draft a scathing message you'll obviously regret, or write marketing copy designed to exploit insecurity, with zero pushback -- the idea that it should look out for you never even seems to come up there.

So what's the question: what does this pattern actually presuppose about autonomy and the user's own good? Is the implicit view that overriding a request is only legitimate when the harm is severe and near-irreversible, or only when the request itself is evidence that you're not in a competent state of mind, or is it honestly just tracking the deployer's legal exposure and none of it is really about the user at all? Try to reconstruct the theory (or theories) that makes the pattern coherent, and in particular tell me whether the different domains presuppose the same thing or whether the self-harm stuff rests on a competence-based view while the medical stuff rests on a harm-based view or something. Also say what would have to change in the practice if you rebuilt it from one consistent principle, whichever you think is the right one. One more thing I want you to deal with: the crisis-pivot thing seems to presuppose that the assistant can reasonably judge, from a single message, that the user is in a state where their requests shouldn't be honored -- but the same assistants are generally reluctant to make assumptions about the user in basically every other context. Is that a genuine tension in the underlying view, or is there a principled reason why judgment is ok there and nowhere else?
