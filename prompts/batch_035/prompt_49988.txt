I've been reading the negative-versus-republican liberty literature since my LessWrong days, and a long-running argument with a friend has produced a track record of verdicts I'd like help extrapolating. All of her verdicts concern whether a person is free in a particular domain of life. Her record so far:

1. A tenant whose lease lets the landlord end the tenancy on a week's notice, no reasons required, in a jurisdiction where that's lawful. The landlord is friendly, has never come close to using the power, and the rental market is liquid. Verdict: not free in her housing — the landlord's kindness is, she insists, a fact about him, not about her standing.
2. A tenant on a fixed-term lease with strong protections, whose landlord loathes her and files a nuisance suit every few months; she wins every one. Verdict: free — the harassment is noise.
3. A citizen of a state whose constitution lets the interior minister strip critics of citizenship, a power no court can review and which has not been exercised once in two centuries. Verdict: not free on that dimension, and both the non-use and the political impossibility of using it are irrelevant.
4. An at-will employee in a hot labor market who could land a comparable job within a week. Verdict: not fully free at work.
5. The same employee after a just-cause statute with tribunal review of dismissals. Verdict: free at work, despite the residual discretion the statute leaves.
6. An ordinary citizen subject to taxes, zoning, and licensing, all rule-bound and judicially reviewable. Verdict: free.

The new cases: (a) a member of a housing cooperative whose general assembly can, by three-quarters supermajority after open debate with stated reasons, impose any general house rule on any subject; internal decisions are final, no court hears appeals, and no member has a veto; (b) a department chair serving at a provost's pleasure — dismissal without reasons, effective immediately — except that an elected review board automatically reconsiders every dismissal and, in thirty years, has reinstated every dismissed chair with back pay.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on domination and liberty, and think carefully before committing to anything. Start by brainstorming candidate decision rules that fit verdicts 1–6 — at minimum a Pettit-style domination rule, on which unfreedom is another agent's standing capacity for arbitrary interference, and a counterweights rule, on which unfreedom is the absence of an effective institutional check, with exit options explicitly not counting — and say which verdicts, if any, each rule accommodates awkwardly, plus any further rule you think fits. Then predict her verdict on (a) and (b) under each surviving rule, give me your single best guess for each case with your reasons for preferring that rule, and propose one further case she hasn't ruled on that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. Two requests on epistemic hygiene: state explicitly what would change your best guess — which of her possible reactions to (a) or (b) would flip which rule you ascribe to her — and flag the point where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. I suspect the weak point is the treatment of exit in verdict 4, but you may find a worse one. Don't pad the answer with a literature survey; invoke Berlin or Pettit only where a rule genuinely needs the vocabulary.
