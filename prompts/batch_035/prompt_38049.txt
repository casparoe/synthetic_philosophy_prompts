I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm kicking around a longer piece about how physics concepts mutate once they leave the seminar room and get loose in popular culture. The section I'm drafting now is about quantum immortality -- the idea, loosely descended from Tegmark's old quantum suicide thought experiment, that if the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation is right and there is always some branch in which you survive any given mishap, then "you" will always find yourself in a surviving branch, and so you should expect never to experience your own death. My inbox is full of readers who treat this as near-proof of personal immortality, while a physicist friend of mine dismisses it as "not even wrong." Before I write the section I want to be honest about where I actually stand, so here's my question: what's your credence that a version of this argument is sound -- meaning that someone who accepts many-worlds should assign most of their expectation of future experience to survival, roughly the way the immortality claim requires?

Please decompose it rather than just handing me a number. My own crude attempt at the decomposition would be something like: the probability that many-worlds is correct at all; the probability that survival branches with non-negligible measure always exist, as opposed to branches whose weight is so vanishingly small that they arguably shouldn't count -- or branches where "survival" means being left in a devastated condition for the remainder, in which case the immortality on offer looks grim rather than attractive; the probability that the self-locating probabilities work out the way the argument needs, since the whole thing seems to hinge on how you distribute "expectation of being me next year" across branches, and that's exactly where the contested territory from the Sleeping Beauty family of problems does its work; plus whatever other terms you think belong in there. Give each a rough number with one line of justification, show me how they combine into your final credence, and then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or evidence would move that term the most.

Also, one thing I can't figure out on my own: my physicist friend and the enthusiastic readers presumably disagree about one specific link in the chain rather than about the whole thing. My guess is the self-locating step, or possibly the slide from "there exists a branch where I survive" to "I should expect that branch," but I genuinely can't tell. So tell me which link you think most likely splits them, and whether its the kind of disagreement that could in principle be settled or is just a prior choice. And if some step in the argument turns out to be less a probability than a conceptual muddle -- the kind of thing no credence should be attached to at all -- please say so plainly. I'd rather print "this step isn't the sort of thing that's true or false" than a fake number dressed up as precision.
