Hi — I teach metaphysics and I'm running a seminar unit on personal identity this term. I want to stop my students (and, honestly, myself) from hand-waving on mind uploading, so I've cooked up a variant of the classic gradual replacement case and I'd like you to commit to an actual credence on it, properly decomposed. Not "it depends on your theory of personal identity" — a number, with its derivation showing.

The case: you know the standard one — your neurons are swapped out one at a time for functionally identical silicon chips, and you wake up each morning reporting that you feel entirely normal. My variant adds a wrinkle. Each excised biological neuron is kept alive in a dish at the other end of the lab, and for the first few hundred swaps the surgeons cross-check that the dish-neuron and its replacement chip agree on their output spikes given the same inputs — they always do. By the end, the biological neurons are firing away in the dish, loosely coupled to one another but with no body, and the chip-brain wakes up and walks out of the hospital.

The proposition: the person who walks out is numerically the same person as the one who walked in — not a very good copy. What's your credence?

Please decompose. My suggested terms, which you should feel free to revise, add to, or throw out:

- the probability that some form of psychological or functional continuity theory of personal identity is correct, versus a biological-continuity view or a closest-continuer view;
- conditional on that, the probability that the chips preserve the relevant organisation — they're matched on single-neuron input-output profiles, but who knows whether gap junctions, neuromodulators, glial involvement, timing jitter at the population level matter;
- the probability that the extra wrinkle — your old neurons still being alive in a dish — changes anything, e.g. by creating a second candidate continuer, and whether that makes identity indeterminate rather than merely complicating the story;
- anything else you think genuinely belongs in the decomposition.

Give each term a rough number with a one-line justification, then combine them, and be explicit about the combination rule you're using and whether the terms are actually independent or you're double-counting somewhere. Then tell me which term you're least confident in and what argument or empirical finding would move it the most.

Two things my students always try, and which I'd like you to handle explicitly rather than in passing if you lean on them: "it's just like waking up from anaesthesia" and "your neurons are replaced naturally over a lifetime anyway, so this is nothing new." Say for each whether it survives the dish wrinkle, because in my case the old neurons aren't dead and dispersed — they're sitting there in a lab, still firing.

Please take on the role of a professional metaphysician who is also comfortable reasoning probabilistically — treat the numbers as a real commitment, not decoration. Do some brainstorming before you settle on the numbers, show the live options first, and don't hedge. Answer in British English please. Also, if you think the whole question is somehow confused — that "numerically the same person" isn't well-defined here — you can say so, but then you still owe me a credence on whether the confused-looking question has a correct answer under the best precisification, and what that precisification is.

One more ask: after you've given your final number, tell me what my credence would have to be, and on which term, for us to have a productive disagreement rather than just talking past each other. which term your least confident in is the one I most want to see defended.
