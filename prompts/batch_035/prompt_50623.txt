I'm in a small reading group that started out as a climate-anxiety thing and has drifted into archaeology — so far we've done the late Bronze Age (the picture where it wasn't one invasion but a whole network of trade and diplomacy unravelling at once), the Maya (where the "collapse" turns out to be mostly the kings and the monuments stopping, while millions of Maya people carried on), and Easter Island, where the classic ecocide story has taken a beating lately. The problem is we keep arguing past eachother using two analogies, and I've started to suspect neither of them can carry the weight we put on them.

The pessimist wing says modern civilization is like an airliner at cruising altitude: it doesn't have a "lower power" setting. Degrowth isn't a gentle descent, it's cutting the engines midflight, because the whole thing is built to only work at speed — global supply chains, just-in-time logistics, specialization so deep that almost nobody could feed themselves if the system slowed down. So on their view there's no managed way down: stay at speed or crash. The optimist wing says societies don't die, they shed — like a forest after fire, or a snake shedding skin. What archaeologists call a collapse is usually the political shell coming off while the people reorganize, which they claim is what actually happened with the Maya, and they'd say the same about anywhere else. So for them "collapse" is just a word historians use for changes they personally dislike, and the fear is mostly aesthetic.

Audit both analogies for me. I want a correspondence table for each — what plays the role of the engines, the wings, the cruise speed on one side; the forest, the fire, the regrowth on the other — then where each mapping breaks, and what each one quietly smuggles in. The plane picture smuggles in a designer, a single optimum, no redundancy; fine, but what does the forest one smuggle in about how much of the population is around to enjoy the "regrowth", or about how long the transition lasts in human lifetimes? Then tell me straight: can the pessimists' analogy actually support the conclusion that a managed descent is impossible, and can the optimists' support the conclusion that collapse is nothing much to fear? And if both are being overworked, sketch what a better analogy would look like — one that keeps the archaeologists' real lesson (the elite package failing is not the same as the people vanishing) without sliding into "so it's fine, actually".

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
