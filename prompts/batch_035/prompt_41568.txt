Something that nags at me every autumn when I teach first-order logic, and I'd love a proper answer to it. We spend week two telling students that a sentence is not a list -- that "Socrates is wise" isn't just the heap {Socrates, wisdom}, because a heap of a thing and a property is exactly as true or false as any other heap, i.e. not at all, and Bradley's regress is supposed to be the deeper version of that point. Then we turn straight to the board and write "W(s)". Which is... a list. Two glyphs, side by side, plus the conventions. The semantic clause doesn't obviously help: we say the atomic formula is true iff s belongs to the extension of W, but a pair of an object and a set containing it is no more a truth-bearer than the original heap was -- we seem to have relabelled the unity rather than produced it.

So here's my question. What theory of the unity of the proposition does our notational and pedagogical practice actually presuppose? Some colleagues of mine say the notation is strictly neutral, that "W(s)" is just shorthand and the real work is done by the semantics, and the semantics is just maths, so we've presupposed nothing. Others say that the moment you write predication as functional application -- one expression saturated by another -- you've conceded the Fregean story where the predicate comes with a gap and the individual fills it, and that story quietly answers Bradley by fiat: the function/argument structure is supposed to be articulated without needing a relation to bolt it together, but is that an explanation or a refusal to ask? I genuinely don't know who's right, and I'd like the reconstruction done carefully: does the practice strictly require some answer to the unity question, or is it underdetermined between several -- Fregean saturation, the Wittgensteinian line that the proposition shows what it can't say, maybe a view on which the concatenation itself is the fact-like constituent, maybe even a quiet nominalism on which there's no unity problem once you go syntactic all the way down?

Two more things I'd want covered. First, my lot also teach intro syntax alongside logic, where the students draw trees and the mother node gets labelled "TP" or whatever -- is that label doing any unifying work, or is it just another list with a badge on it? I'd like to know whether the two notations (the flat string and the labelled tree) presuppose the same thing about unity or different things. Second, the forward-looking bit: if we took Bradley's regress seriously as a live threat rather than a museum piece, what would have to change in the practice? Would we need a notation that can't be built up compositionally sign by sign -- and could such a thing even be written on a board, given that anything written on a board is, you know, a list of marks? Or would the change be in what we tell students the notation is doing, while the marks stay as they are?

For context on where I'm coming from: I'm not trying to refound logic, I just want to know whether I've been teaching a notation that smuggles in a metaphysics I never argued for, and whether the honest lecture two addition is a disclaimer. And don't spare me the bits where the practice turns out to be compatible with views I'd rather it weren't.
