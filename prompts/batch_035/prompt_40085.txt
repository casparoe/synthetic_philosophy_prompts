I chair the protocol-review committee at a mid-sized biomedical research university. We are not debating whether animals feel pain at all — institutional policy and the law have settled that, and every species we see gets some baseline protections. But the next tier of decisions — enrichment requirements, default analgesia protocols, humane endpoints, the frequency of welfare observations — turns on comparative judgments about the richness of different species' mental lives, and right now those arrive as nothing better than committee intuition, which means they arrive inconsistently and are impossible to defend to investigators who ask why their species was tiered that way. I would like to try grounding the tiering in an actual historical test rather than vibes: Descartes' two marks of a thinking being from Part Five of the Discourse — the use of speech or signs to communicate one's thoughts, and the ability to act appropriately across varied circumstances rather than by fixed bodily disposition — together with his explanatory standard from the correspondence, that an animal's behavior only counts as evidence of a thinking soul if it cannot be accounted for by the disposition of its organs alone.

Please write a procedure the committee can actually run, in under twenty minutes per species, as part of protocol review: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (and what counts as merely citing a behavior without establishing which side of the line it falls on), where the procedure stops and assigns the species to a tier, and where it must hand the decision back to the committee because the question is one of values or of institutional risk tolerance rather than of fact. Two departures from Descartes as written will have to be built in, and I want them flagged rather than hidden: first, taken literally, his criteria assign every nonhuman animal to the automaton tier, which our legislation and our own consciences forbid — so the procedure needs an explicit rule for how a species that fails both Descartes' tests still gets a nonzero tier; second, his tests were designed for the human/animal boundary, not for ranking animals against each other, so say which distinctions must survive the adaptation (genuinely referential signaling versus fixed signals tied to eliciting conditions, novel-circumstance flexibility versus stereotyped response, the explanatory test of whether a mechanical account could in principle cover the behavior) and which can be dropped as dead weight. Where you drop something, say what the procedure loses by dropping it.

Then run the procedure on three species we actually see: octopuses, mice, and honeybees. Include at least one case where the procedure returns a tier assignment the committee will not like — either because it tiers a sympathetic species lower than we currently treat it or because it tiers an unpopular species higher than our reviewers will accept — and show how the hand-off to human judgment is supposed to work in that case. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do, especially this: our analgesia defaults ultimately turn on the difference between suffering from pain and merely detecting and responding to noxious stimuli, and I want to know whether Descartes' tests, adapted or not, can mark that distinction at all, or whether we are dressing up one more round of intuition in seventeenth-century clothes.
