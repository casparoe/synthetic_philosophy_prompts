My roommate is on a compatibilism kick ever since she read about Frankfurt cases, and last night she built an argument that I'm pretty sure proves too much, but I honestly cannot find the broken step. Help me out before our next pub argument, because she's insufferable when she thinks she's won.

Her setup, roughly: Jones is about to go vote, and Black — a neuroscientist who's implanted a device in Jones's head — wants him to vote for the Democrat. The device monitors Jones and would force him to vote Democrat if it detected any sign he was going to do otherwise. In the actual run of events, Jones decides entirely on his own to vote Democrat, the device never fires, and Black plays no causal role whatsoever. Her argument:

(1) Jones votes Democrat of his own accord — the actual history of his vote contains nothing but his own reasons.
(2) Jones could not have done otherwise: any incipient deviation would have been detected and corrected before it could become a vote.
(3) Since Black's device never actually did anything, its mere presence can't affect what Jones is responsible for.
(4) So Jones is morally responsible for his vote even though he could not have done otherwise — the principle of alternative possibilities is false.
(5) And if what keeps Jones responsible is just that the action flowed from his own reasons, with alternatives playing no role, then alternatives never matter to responsibilty.
(6) Now take Stacy, who spends the whole evening in a room whose door is locked from the outside, because she wants to finish her book — she'd have stayed anyway. And take Dan, who hands his wallet to a mugger with a gun pressed to his ribs — the handover goes through his own deliberation (fear, weighing his options), not through the mugger moving his arm for him.
(7) By (5), Stacy is responsible for staying in the room, and Dan is exactly as responsible for handing over the wallet as he would be for donating to a cause he believes in.
(8) But coercion excuses, or at least mitigates — that's about as close to a fixed point as ethics has. And whatever we say about Stacy, (7) with respect to Dan seems flat wrong.

She says she's happy to accept (7): her line is that Dan is responsible for the handover but not blameworthy for it, and once you split responsibility from blameworthiness the pressure goes away. I'm not convinced that split does the work she wants, but I can't articulate why. What nags at me is that (2) somehow feels different for Jones than for Dan — Jones "couldn't" do otherwise only because Black was standing by, whereas the gun is right there in Dan's face the whole time — but I can't turn that feeling into an actual objection, and she just says "the gun is standing by too, it just fires for real."

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think her responsibility-not-blameworthiness move can do real work, show me how, and if you think it can't, show me where it breaks. Also please be upfront about where your diagnosis is shakiest — mabye it's the part where you're leaning on an intuition about a variant case — and tell me what would actually change your mind. Like, is there some version of the story where, if my gut went the other way about it, you'd tell me to reject a different premise entirely?
