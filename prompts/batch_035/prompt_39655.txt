A guy in my lab keeps calling LLMs stochastic parrots and thinks that settles the whole debate — a parrot says "Polly want a cracker" with no idea what a cracker is, an LLM predicts the next token with no idea what anything is, same thing scaled up. He uses it to argue that interpretability work is a waste of time, since if it's a parrot there's nothing in there to interpret, and that behavioral tests of "understanding" are tests of mimicry by definition. Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the parrot's vocal tract, the trainer rewarding approximations of human speech, the years of exposure to humans talking, whatever plays the role of Polly's (non-)grasp of "cracker" — and then where the mapping actually breaks. Annoyingly for him, parrots sometimes turn out to be decent at reference, and LLMs at least do in-context learning, which no parrot does anything like. Then the part I actually care about: which disanalogies matter for his dismissive conclusion and which don't — and does the conclusion survive once those are on the table, or was the parrot carrying it the whole time? If a better analogy is available (autocomplete on steroids, a kid who learned language from radio transcripts, whatever), tell me what work it does differently.
