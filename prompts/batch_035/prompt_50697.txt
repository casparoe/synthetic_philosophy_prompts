Last night a friend and I spent the better part of three hours — over dinner, then standing in his driveway — arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker, which we had covered that week in a rational choice seminar. In the case, a hitchhiker is picked up in the desert after promising to pay the driver on arrival; the hitchhiker has no money with him, so the driver's only assurance is the promise, and the driver reasons that a fully rational hitchhiker, once delivered, would simply not pay — no enforcement, no repetition, no reputation at stake — and so refuses to stop for anyone he takes to be perfectly rational. My friend opened by dismissing the whole thing as a dressed-up point about enforcement: promises without sanctions are cheap talk, and the case just rediscovers that institutions matter; nothing in it shows anything about rationality being "self-defeating," since a rational agent in a world with police, contracts, and memory does fine. I answered that this misses the direction of the result: the hitchhiker who does best is the one disposed to do, at the destination, what is worse for him by every self-interested measure at that moment — so it seems it can be rational, in advance, to make yourself into someone who will act irrationally, which is a genuine embarrassment for any theory that evaluates acts one choice at a time. He replied that I was just redescribing commitment devices: binding yourself to the mast is rational, the disposition is rational, and the act that flows from an intact disposition is rational too — calling any part of the package "irrational" was, he said, Parfit being provocative rather than precise.

I pushed back that the disposition has to be cashed out in acts: a disposition that never issues in the payment is worthless, so when the hitchhiker stands at the bank counter, knowingly poorer, having kept a promise that no longer benefits him, either the theory calls that act irrational — in which case rationality has told him to install irrationality in himself, which sounds like a contradiction — or it refuses to call it irrational, in which case it has stopped evaluating anything at the very point where evaluation was wanted. He answered that this is a confusion about time-slices: the theory's job is to advise ex ante, before you're at the destination, and grading the agent from inside the payoff point is like blaming the map for what the territory did later; besides, he said, real agents are never in the stripped-down world the case posits — they have reputations, repeated dealings, and emotions that keep promises for them — and once you strip all that away you haven't isolated pure rationality, you've just deleted everything that makes choice possible. I said that was exactly backwards: the thought experiment earns its keep precisely by subtracting the enforcement and reputation he kept smuggling back in, and what's left — an agent who benefits from a trait that will require a self-sacrificing act — is not an artifact. By the end he conceded it would be good to be the sort of person who pays; he just refused to let that be called a case of rational irrationality, and I couldn't tell whether we had reached a real disagreement or only one about labels.

Neither of us walked away feeling we'd won, and I'd like a proper adjudication rather than a pep talk for my side. Please go through the exchange round by round — his enforcement opening, my self-defeating-rationality reply, his commitment-device response, my act-vs-disposition point, his time-slice and "stripped-down world" rejoinders — and say who is right at each step, and to what degree, rather than only who wins overall. Then address the question we circled but never settled: is the claim that it can be rational to cause oneself to be irrational a substantive thesis about rational choice, or does it collapse into a verbal dispute about whether rationality is predicated of acts, of dispositions, or of whole lives? And finally, tell us what, if anything, both of us were missing.
