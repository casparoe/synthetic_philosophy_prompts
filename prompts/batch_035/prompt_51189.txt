My corner of metaphysics has me watching the special composition question go absolutely nowhere, and I'd like a diagnosis of why. Quick setup so we're on the same page: the question is under what conditions do some things compose a further thing. The three camps are familiar by now — van Inwagen's view that composition happens only when the xs are all part of a single life (so tables come out not real, organisms stay), Merricks's nearby nihilism where the artifacts go but living things remain, and the unrestricted camp, Sider being the loudest, with the argument from vagueness: if composition were restricted, it'd be vague where the restriction sits, or else there's a razor-sharp line between qualitatively identical cases, and neither is supposed to be tolerable, so composition never fails. And the whole thing has been frozen since roughly the turn of the millennium. Every paper I read restates a position rather than moving one. The unrestricted people treat the vagueness argument as basically decisive and think the restrictionists are just refusing to count; the restrictionists treat it as a trick that smuggles in a meta-level claim about how many things exist in a room, and they answer with intuitions about the fender and the car, which the unrestricted side then waves off as artifact-talk. Nobody's budged in ages.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the fight isn't really about composition at all, it's about whether "the xs compose something" is a substantive question in the first place — whether there's a joint-carving relation of parthood out there waiting to be discovered, or whether "one thing" is a projection of our sorting habits. On that reading the two camps are answering different questions under one heading (what exists versus what our words latches onto), and I suspect the argument from vagueness only has force if you've already bought the substantive reading, which is exactly what's in dispute — a quiet begging of the question that nobody wants to say out loud.

But I want the diagnosis, not confirmation of my story. Here's a nudge toward what I find telling: look at the cases that do the intuitive work for each side and notice how little they overlap. Take the card house — stack playing cards into a little house; is the house one thing? My seminar splits on it and neither half can say why. Or the murmuration: a starling flock that wheels around like a single animal. Half the room says obviously not one thing, the other half says obviously yes, and everyone's confidence drops when I ask whether the answer changes if it's thousands of birds rather than dozens. If this dispute were substantive, shouldn't cases like these make the intuitions sort themselves out? Or is the inability to sort a sign that our "one thing" concept just isn't precise enough to have an extension — in which case the debate is verbal in a particular, fixable way, or the question is ill-posed and the honest move is to give up on it?

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Substantive but hard, verbal in the sense that each side is right on its own disambiguation, or ill-posed because the concept of a "thing" is too mushy to settle it? Diagnose it using what the camps actually say — not a survey of positions. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a new family of cases, a formal result about vagueness, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. And since this is diagnosis rather than adjudication, please say how confident you are in each of the major claims you make, and single out the one point you're least sure of — for my part it's the begging-the-question suspicion about the vagueness argument, which I've honestly never seen worked out properly in either direction.
