I'm a retired chemist (spectroscopy, mostly, 35 years) who got serious about philosophy about two years into retirement, and I've been working through the history of economic thought because it's the part of the ancient world I knew the least about. A pop-level book on the history of money -- I'll leave it unnamed, I lent it to my nephew -- summarizes Aristotle's position like this:

Aristotle drew a hard line between household management, whose goal is use, and wealth-getting, whose goal is accumulation, and he condemned the second kind outright as contrary to nature. Money for him was purely a convention, a token invented by agreement to make unlike goods commensurable, and it has exactly one function: to serve as the medium of exchange. Since fair exchange requires the goods traded to be equal in value, and value is fixed by the amount of labor each craftsman puts in, the shoemaker and the carpenter can only trade justly once money has measured out their labor and the amounts come out equal. This is the first statement of the just price doctrine, which the medieval schoolmen then adopted wholesale, and which survives today in the labor theory of value.

Now, the commensurability bit is what hooked me -- it sounded like dimensional analysis, the thing I drilled into every grad student: you can't add a length to a time without a conversion factor, and here's Aristotle saying money is the conversion factor between qualitatively different goods. That's the kind of bridge between my old world and this one I eat up. But the more I poke at it, the more I suspect the book flattened things to make Aristotle sound like a proto-economist. For one, "condemned outright" seems too strong given what I've gathered about some distinction between natural and unnatural kinds of acquisition. And the labor bit smells anachronistic, though I can't say why.

Go claim by claim and tell me which parts of that paragraph a scholar of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics would accept, which are simplifications, and which are genuine distortions -- I especially want to know whether Aristotle actually thought justice in exchange requires equality of some measured quantity, or whether the structure of his view is different (something about reciprocity, proportional rather than arithmetic?). Also: does the book's punchline -- that Aristotle originated the just price doctrine that the scholastics later formalized -- follow from whichever parts of the summary are actually true? Treat me as a philosopher with two years of training wheels and a scientist's instinct for when a number is being measured versus when it's just being asserted.

End your reply with a final line of the form Verdict: [one of: fair / fair in outline but wrong in the details / seriously misleading] -- the single worst distortion, in one clause.
