I'm an American exchange student taking a seminar on comparative political culture at a German university, and in our last session the instructor used the proverb "Ordnung muss sein" — roughly, "there must be order" — to do an enormous amount of work. She deployed it to explain why Germans wait at red pedestrian lights on empty streets at three in the morning, to characterize what she called a distinctively German moralization of rule-following, and finally to wave off a classmate's suggestion that such behavior might be straightforwardly prudential — fear of fines or of social sanction — rather than an expression of moral character or national ethos. I have a seminar paper due on the German/American contrast in attitudes toward rules versus individual conscience, and I've realized I don't actually know what precise thesis that proverb is supposed to express — and, crucially, that different theses would support very different uses of it.

Could you lay out the distinct claims "Ordnung muss sein" might stand for? Candidates I've been mulling: a banal empirical observation that German society is comparatively rule-abiding; a normative claim that order has intrinsic value rather than merely instrumental value; a claim about the social function of unconditional compliance, i.e., that everyone following a rule regardless of circumstances keeps the norm self-sustaining in a way selective, case-by-case compliance would not; a claim that scrupulous rule-following is itself constitutive of moral character rather than a mechanical substitute for it; and a claim about national character as such, which would sit uneasily with how we'd go about explaining functionally similar behavior in, say, Japan or Scandinavia without invoking anything specifically German.

For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or dubious — and, the part I most need, whether that reading would actually license my instructor's move of dismissing the prudential explanation. My suspicion is that several of the readings are compatible with the prudential story rather than competing with it, and that only one or two of the stronger readings would genuinely rule it out, but I can't sort out which is which. A menu I could organize the paper around would be ideal.
