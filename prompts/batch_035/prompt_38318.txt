I'm working through the chapter of Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov (1880) in which Ivan recites his "poem" about the Grand Inquisitor, and I'm stuck on one stretch of the Inquisitor's speech to the silent Christ. I don't trust my memory for the exact wording, so what follows is my own paraphrase, offered as a paraphrase and not as a quotation. As I understand the passage: the Inquisitor tells the returned Christ that by refusing, in the wilderness, the three offers — bread from stones, a leap from the temple, and the kingdoms of the world — Christ rejected the three powers by which alone, on the Inquisitor's view, the mass of mankind can actually be held together and governed; that Christ thereby overestimated human beings, who are weak and restless and incapable of the freedom of conscience he demanded, a freedom only ever available to a small number; that the Church, by accepting the tempter's gifts, has corrected Christ's work and will in the end deliver to most of mankind the quiet, obedient happiness Christ was too proud to grant them, while the few who remained faithful to the truth are set apart as the only ones, on the Inquisitor's telling, who will be happy; and that the Inquisitor and his fellows knowingly take onto themselves the sin of deciding for everyone else, accepting damnation for it, so that the weak may be spared the burden.

My question concerns the logical structure of the argument, not its theology. Specifically: is the Inquisitor's case a simple trade-off, in which the happiness of the many is purchased at the price of their freedom, or is it subtler — the claim that the freedom Christ insists on could only ever have been enjoyed by a minority anyway, so that Christ's demand effectively condemned the majority to a freedom they could not use and a guilt they could not carry? If the second reading is right, the argument seems to turn on how freedom is distributed across people rather than on what freedom is worth in itself, which would make the Inquisitor's position harder to dismiss than the usual "happiness versus liberty" gloss suggests. Relatedly, where does the final move fit in — the declaration that he and his kind accept the sin and the damnation on behalf of the weak? Is that a genuine cost the argument has to count, so that the arrangement is meant to look like a sacrifice for the many rather than a swindle of them, or is it a rhetorical flourish that does no work once the rest of the case is granted? Help me understand the passage with these structural questions in view.
