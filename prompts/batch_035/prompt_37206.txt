A colleague of mine has a habit of issuing snap verdicts whenever questions about vagueness come up in seminars, and after years of this I have a fairly complete record of her rulings. Here it is.

1. Shown a sorites series of men, each with exactly one hair more than the last, she refuses to point to any adjacent pair where "bald" flips to "not bald" — and she insists this is not merely practical difficulty: no improvement in eyesight, lighting, or counting technique would ever make such a pair identifiable.

2. For Marta, a clear penumbral case of "tall," she holds that "if Marta is tall, then Marta is not short" is straightforwardly true — a constraint that any adequate account of what our words mean must respect, not an empirical finding about Marta.

3. "Marta is tall and Marta is not tall" she calls flatly false, and she scoffs at the suggestion that such a sentence might be partly true, or true to some degree.

4. "Marta is tall or Marta is not tall" she calls flatly true; she bristles whenever anyone suggests that vagueness forces repairs to classical logic, and she insists that excluded middle and non-contradiction come through the phenomenon unscathed.

5. She cheerfully concedes higher-order vagueness: some people are borderline cases of "determinately tall," some of those are borderline cases of being borderline, and so on without a fixed bottom — any account that cannot tolerate this nesting, she says, is a non-starter.

6. She denies that whether someone counts as tall depends on the company: if Marta is tall, she is tall whether the room is full of gymnasts or of basketball centers.

Next week our department is staging a debate on vagueness, and she will be put on the spot about Hugo, a man whose head nobody in the department can classify — our local paradigm of a borderline case. The moderator's question: is the sentence "Hugo is bald" either true or false, even if no possible evidence could ever settle which?

Predict her answer. Identify the view she is implicitly running — epistemicism and supervaluationism are the obvious candidates, but consider whether anything else fits her record equally well — and be careful about how much her fourth verdict actually settles. Then tell me which further question or case, put to her in the same setting, would best discriminate between the candidate views: something she could not answer the same way under both. I am less interested in the label than in the reasoning — what exactly each view predicts about Hugo, and why her record so far leaves that prediction underdetermined.
