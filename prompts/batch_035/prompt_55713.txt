I am a novelist, and a disagreement has broken out between my editor and a friend of mine who teaches psychology, over a character in my current manuscript. The character, Mara, discovers that her brother deliberately let her take the blame for something he did. I have written her response as a kind of calm, administrative detachment: she calculates what he owes her, arranges repayment, limits his access to her life, and feels no resentment whatsoever — she treats him, in effect, the way Strawson said we treat the mentally ill or the very young when we take up the objective attitude, as a case to be managed rather than a person to be blamed. My editor and my friend disagree about whether such a character is coherent, and I would like your help adjudicating their exchange move by move.

(1) My editor argues that Mara as written is either self-deceived or psychologically impossible. On his reading of Strawson, resentment, gratitude, and hurt feelings are not optional additions to regarding someone as a responsible person — they are what regarding someone as a responsible person consists in. The objective attitude is fitting for those who cannot participate in relationships at all, but it cannot carry a sibling relationship; a brother one never resents is a brother one has never really treated as an agent. Either I write her serenity as a mask, he says, or I have written her out of the human interpersonal world altogether.

(2) My friend replies that Strawson conflated what we happen to feel with what we must feel. Whole traditions of moral discipline treat resentment as a defect to be outgrown, and therapy and maturity demonstrably weaken reactive attitudes in individuals without dissolving their relationships. He adds that Strawson's claim that we cannot generally abandon the reactive attitudes reads as a description of current human psychology dressed up as a philosophical constraint, and that Mara can hold her brother fully responsible — she requires repayment, she withdraws trust — while declining to add the feeling, since the practical consequences and the emotion are separable.

(3) My editor answers that strip away the feeling and the wrong stops registering as a wrong: on Mara's detached picture, her brother did not wrong her, something merely happened to her, and the repayment becomes a transaction between objects rather than an accounting between persons. He adds that even forgiveness, which the outgrown-resentment ideal presumably permits, is itself one of Strawson's reactive attitudes, so the ideal secretly depends on the framework it claims to supersede.

(4) My friend replies that forgiving without ever having resented is precisely the ideal; the presupposition the editor appeals to holds only if one accepts Strawson's taxonomy, which is the very point in dispute.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point: assess each of the four moves, say who is right about which specific issue, identify what both parties may be missing, and, since I must eventually choose, indicate what your verdict implies for whether I should write Mara's serenity as genuine or as suppressed resentment. Please answer in American English.
