I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind for advanced undergraduates — about 16 students, one 90-minute session. Next week's topic is the symbol grounding problem: Stevan Harnad's claim that a system whose only grasp of its symbols is through other symbols — a purely formal, dictionary-like pairing of symbols to symbols — never gets those symbols to mean anything, and that meaning has to enter through some non-symbolic route, such as the sensorimotor ability to sort the things the symbols are about. My students will have read Searle's Chinese Room argument but not Harnad, and they will not be told the name of the problem until the end of the session.

Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the difference between an ungrounded symbol system and a grounded one. The core staging I have in mind: one group of participants must accomplish real tasks — sorting physical objects, executing instructions, answering questions about the room — armed only with a mapping table written in a script they don't know, where every entry in the table is defined only by other entries in the same unknown script. A second group gets the identical table plus a handful of grounded tokens: a few symbols whose meanings are fixed by ostension to actual objects, colors, or actions in the room.

The hard part, and the part I most want your design work on, is that the ungrounded condition must be genuinely tempting rather than obviously hopeless. The mapping table should have internal structure — a consistent grammar, compositional rules, entries that seem to build systematically on one another — so that students in the ungrounded condition experience the seductive feeling of being almost there: fluent, productive use of the symbols, with the meaning stubbornly missing. That feeling of fluent nonsense is, to my mind, the philosophical heart of the topic, and a design in which the ungrounded group just stares at gibberish would not reproduce it. So please work out concretely how to build such a table: roughly how many symbols it needs, what internal structure it should have, and how the tasks should be calibrated so the grounded group visibly succeeds where the ungrounded group either fails or confidently produces well-formed but wrong performances.

Deliverables: the full rules and materials, including how to construct the mapping table; a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't use real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; how to split 16 students across conditions; and a debrief to run afterwards that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal distinction — in particular, where the grounded group's success shows the non-symbolic route doing work the symbolic route cannot do by itself. I'll name the problem only at the end of the debrief.
