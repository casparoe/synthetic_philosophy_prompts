so my therapist has me doing narrative therapy — the idea being you figure out who you are by telling the story of your life, spotting the themes, and re-writing the chapters that arent working. i was fine with it until I described it to a friend who's deep into Dennett, and he said: hold on, the self IS a story, a fictional character your brain writes because it's useful — like a center of gravity for the narratives, not a thing that exists in there. so narrative therapy isn't discovering anything, it's just choosing which fiction to live inside. and since there's no fact of the matter, you should pick the version that serves you best. be the hero of your own memoir, basically.

something about that feels off to me. in my own material there's the story where my marriage failed because my ex was checked out, and the story where I was cold and unavailable for two years and she reasonably gave up. both of them "explain" my behavior, both could be told compellingly, and my friend's view gives me zero reason not to run with the flattering one. but it seems like the flattering one is just... false? or is "false" not even available here, since there's no pre-narrative fact to be false about?

and here's the part I can't get past: my therapist keeps saying the revised story has to "ring true," not just feel good. but if the self really is constructed out of narrative, what is ringing true tracking? it's not correspondence with some inner object, on my friend's picture. is it just the story that best predicts what I'll actually do? best coheres with the raw record of events? something else? and what happens when two stories fit the record equally well but pull my life in totally different directions going forward — does the choice between them become arbitrary exactly when it matters most?

so, three questions. (1) is my friend right that if the self is narrative all the way down, revision is just pragmatics — pick what serves you? (2) if not, what actually constrains which self-story is better than another, given that the "object" is itself a story? (3) what does the therapist's "rings true" criterion amount to, and does it survive my friend's picture?

i want an actual position here, not a tour of the options — my friend is smug enough as it is. if you think he's basically right, say so and tell me honestly whether the flattering divorce story is fine. if you think he's making a real mistake, tell me where the mistake is and what work "fit" or "truth" is doing once the self is a constructed character. don't hedge into "it depends on your values."

whatever you conclude, end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one short sentence stating which story I should tell and why — blunt is fine.
