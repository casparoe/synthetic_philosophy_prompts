I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy with an undergraduate degree in statistics, and I have three years of guaranteed funding, with a fourth year likely but not certain. I have decided that my dissertation will be on imprecise probabilities — the view that a rational agent's credence in a proposition may be an interval or a set of probability functions rather than a single sharp number — but the area sprawls, and I must settle on a specific problem by the end of this academic year. What I would like from you is a dependency-aware prioritization of the open questions in this area, together with a defense of the ordering.

So that you can correct my map of the terrain, here is how it looks to me. (1) A representation question: whether "belief" should be modeled as a set of sharp probability functions at all, given that this seems to commit one to odd claims about how the agent relates to the members of the set, and given the ongoing back-and-forth about whether unspecific evidence demands unsharp credences — Roger White's "mushy credence" argument on one side, Adam Elga's argument for sharpness on the other. (2) An updating question: how agents with sets of priors should respond to evidence, and in particular whether the cases where learning strictly widens your interval ("dilatation") are a defect of the theory to be engineered away, a welcome feature, or an artifact of the set-representation itself. (3) A decision question: the proliferation of decision rules for imprecise agents — E-admissibility, maximality, Γ-maximin — and the fact that they can disagree not just about which option is best but about which options are even comparable. (4) An interpretation question: what it is, metaphysically and phenomenologically, to believe something to degree [0.3, 0.7] — what state of a person does an interval describe, and what does it feel like from the inside to have one. (5) A statistical question: whether the long-standing disconnect between the robust-Bayesian sensitivity-analysis tradition in statistics, where sets of priors are treated as a convenient engineering device, and the philosophical tradition, where they express a thesis about the nature of belief, conceals a genuinely productive dissertation-sized problem. (6) An applications question: ensembles of models in machine learning are natural credal sets, yet almost nobody in that literature asks which decision rule the ensemble should use when the members disagree.

Against that background, tell me the following. First, which of these problems (or one I have missed) is most worth three years of someone with my profile, where my comparative advantage is mathematics and formal work rather than metaphysics? Second, which questions are upstream of which — which ones, if settled, would change the terms of the others? Third, which are tractable for a single person in three years, and which are famous but effectively stalled? Fourth, what criteria are you actually using — importance, tractability, neglectedness, downstream dependencies — and what are the trade-offs between them, including where you would deliberately choose a less important question because of something about my situation? I would rather write a dissertation whose result changes what the next five papers in the area must say than one that merely inherits a famous debate; please take that preference seriously, and say plainly if you think it is misguided. Also flag anything in my description of the terrain above that you believe is confused or outdated. Please write your answer in American English.
