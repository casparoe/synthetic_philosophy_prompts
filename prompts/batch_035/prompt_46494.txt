I'm giving an address in three weeks and need help drafting it, having just spent most of last night arguing about it with a friend who teaches philosophy of mind.

The occasion: my aunt is retiring after thirty-five years as director of a coastal sanctuary that rescues and rehabilitates marine animals — seals, seabirds, the occasional dolphin — and which, in its last decade, built a serious cephalopod program after she became convinced that octopuses were being shortchanged by everyone, including the sanctuary's own staff. The audience will be roughly half scientists and animal-care staff and half donors, family, and long-term volunteers. A mixed room, and one where the wrong kind of speech would be noticed by at least one half.

The philosophical problem the address has to navigate is the one my friend and I fought about. My aunt's intellectual signature, if you can call it that, was a refusal to overclaim about which animals are conscious. She held that consciousness is distributed across the animal kingdom in degrees — that it "fades in," as she liked to say, rather than switching on — and she was equally impatient with people who granted nothing beyond mammals and with people who spoke of the "sentience of the sea" as though a scallop's inner life were a settled matter. She once made the communications team remove the word "compassion" from a poster about crabs, on the grounds that it asserted more than anyone knew.

My friend's position last night was that this scrupulousness is where her view becomes a liability rather than a virtue. His argument: a graded picture is precisely what lets people draw the line wherever it's convenient. If consciousness "fades in," then no one ever has to say that a fish feels pain; "it's complicated" quietly does the work that "no" used to do, and industries and institutions are only too happy to let it. He thinks a fitting tribute would do the opposite of what she did — take a clear stand that the animals the sanctuary protects feel, full stop, and let the line fall where it may beyond that. He accused me of planning to launder her fence-sitting as wisdom.

I ended the argument unconvinced but genuinely unsettled, because I think he's right that graded views can be abused that way, and wrong that this is what my aunt was doing.

Please draft the address — around seven minutes read aloud — subject to the following constraints. It should do justice to her epistemic scrupulousness without making her sound cold, evasive, or fence-sitting to the volunteers, many of whom came to the sanctuary precisely because she made them feel that the animals mattered. It should not concede my friend's demand for a bright line, but it must not dodge him either: it should show why taking a graded view seriously actually changes how you treat the hard cases — the fish, the crabs, the octopuses she was proudest of — rather than allowing you to defer them indefinitely. And if it can contain one concrete image or example that a marine biologist and a donor's ten-year-old would both keep and retell, that would be the single most important thing it does.
