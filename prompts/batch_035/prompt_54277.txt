Write a dialogue between two philosophers waiting to test a new transport device. Maya holds a Parfitian view: psychological continuity is what matters in survival, and personal identity is not itself what we should care about. Theo holds that what matters requires the continued existence of one and the same functioning brain, and that a perfect copy is, however similar, someone else. Neither is a strawman — Theo should be allowed to concede where his intuitions waver, and Maya should be allowed to concede where her position strains — and each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than delivering prepared speeches.

The device they are waiting on has three unusual features, which should shape the argument. First, the scan is non-destructive and takes six hours, during which the subject stays awake. Second, the replica on Mars is activated the moment the scan completes, so — by policy — the original and the replica will coexist for exactly 24 hours and can speak to each other by video link. Third, at the end of that day the original is painlessly terminated, and everyone involved has known this from the outset. The dialogue takes place the evening before Maya's scheduled transport, with Theo arguing she should withdraw and Maya arguing he should sign up for the next slot. They should press each other on the hard spots: what the six hours of scanning does to Theo's claim that the brain's continuity is preserved-or-broken in a single stroke; what Maya should say to her replica during their overlapping day, and whether her answer is consistent with her theory; and whether Theo's position can explain why the 24-hour overlap bothers him more than the ordinary destruction-on-scan would.

Begin directly with the dialogue itself, with no preamble or framing of any kind. It may end unresolved. After it, append a short coda — no more than 150 words — distinguishing which of the positions and arguments voiced in the dialogue reflect something close to a settled view among philosophers working on personal identity, and which are genuinely contested, so that neither character's contested stance reads as the consensus.
