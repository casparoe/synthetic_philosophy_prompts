I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my views on identity over time before a metaphysics reading group, and I'd like a consistency audit of my own package rather than a survey of the literature. Here is what I hold: (1) the Ship of Theseus, repaired plank by plank over the years, is the same ship as the one that left port — artifacts must survive gradual part-replacement or nothing man-made persists at all; (2) the ship later reassembled from the discarded planks is also the same ship, since it is composed of all of the original matter in the original arrangement; (3) identity is transitive — if A is B and B is C, then A is C — and I regard that as non-negotiable; (4) the repaired ship and the reassembled ship are nonetheless two distinct ships, since they sit in different places at the same time and nothing can be in two harbors at once; (5) persons persist by the same criterion as artifacts — continuity of organization rather than sameness of matter — which is why I survive the complete turnover of my cells; and (6) which ship deserves the title of the original is partly a conventional, language-relative matter, but the number of ships that exist after the procedure is a perfectly determinate fact. Please tell me which of these commitments are in genuine tension and how badly, which only appear to be, and what the cheapest single revision would be. I honestly don't know where the problem lies, so please order the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious.
