I'm giving a colloquium next week on promissory obligation, and the audience is a department with two card-carrying Kantians, a long-serving Humean conventionalist who thinks obligation just is what the practice of promise-keeping sustains, and a formal decision theorist who distrusts any concept that can't be operationalized. My thesis is that neither convention nor Scanlonian assurance explains why promises bind; rather, in promising, the promisor exercises a normative power that grants the promisee a temporary, revocable directive authority over the promisor's future conduct — the promisee comes to have a say the promisor previously reserved to herself, which she can release, waive, or demand be exercised. I use this to explain why promise-breaking wrongs the promisee specifically, why obligations to the dead and the forgotten persist, and why release from a promise requires the promisee's consent in a way mere forgiveness does not. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come and phrased in their own vocabularies — the Kantian worry that directing another's will is self-alienation, the conventionalist charge that my authority is epiphenomenal on the practice it rides on, the decision theorist's demand for a non-circular criterion of who holds it — each with a workable two-minute reply, a note on what the reply concedes, the weakest joint in my own position that I should shore up rather than hope they miss, and the question I'm praying nobody asks.
