The exam question read as follows: "This question has three parts. (a) Raz holds that some options are incommensurable: neither is better than the other, nor are they equal in value. Explain why, on his account, this is a fact about the values at stake rather than a limitation of our knowledge or a vagueness in our language, and illustrate with his treatment of friendship and money. (b) Chang argues that when the trichotomy of better, worse, and equally good fails, the options may still be comparable in a further way: they can be on a par. Explain what parity is, how it differs both from equal goodness and from any sort of imprecision or roughness in our judgments, and what role Chang assigns to the agent's will in hard choices. (c) Raz insists that choosing in a hard case is neither a matter of discovering further considerations that settle the matter nor a merely expressive act. Explain what he means, and then construct your own example of a hard choice, defend the claim that the trichotomy really fails for your case, and say whether the case is better handled by Raz's or Chang's machinery."

The student's answer, in full: "(a) On Raz's view, a hard choice can be one where the trichotomy fails: neither option is better than the other, and they are not equal in value either. The reason is that the values at stake are of different kinds, for example the value of creative achievement versus the value of pleasure, so there is no common measure in terms of which they can be summed. This is not a limitation on our knowledge, as though there were a fact about which life is better that we simply cannot access, and it is not like the vagueness of a word such as 'heap': even ideally informed agents with perfectly precise value concepts would find the options incommensurable. Raz's example is friendship and money: the value of friendship is so great that no amount of money could outweigh it, which is why someone who asks how much their friend's companionship is worth in monetary terms has misunderstood friendship altogether — such a person simply lacks the concept. (b) Chang agrees with Raz that the trichotomy can fail but argues that Raz leaves hard choices underdescribed. Her proposal is that the two options may be on a par, which is a fourth valuational relation in addition to better than, worse than, and equally good. Two options are on a par when they are close enough in value that no small improvement to one would make it better than the other, yet they are not exactly equal in value; in other words, parity is a kind of approximate or rough equality where the values are nearly, though not perfectly, balanced. Chang also gives the agent's will a role that Raz denies it. For Chang, when neither option is better, the agent can put herself behind one of them, and in doing so she creates a reason to choose it: the choice is arbitrary from the standpoint of comparative value but not from the standpoint of the will. Raz, by contrast, treats hard choices as settled by whatever the agent already cares about most, so that the choice merely reflects her antecedent concerns rather than generating any new reasons. (c) Consider a graduating student choosing between two job offers in the same field, in the same city, at equally prestigious firms: one pays $4,000 more, the other has a shorter commute. This is a hard choice in Chang's sense. Neither offer is better than the other and they are not exactly equal in value, so they are on a par, and whichever the student picks, she can make the choice her own by putting her will behind it, which then gives her a reason that was not there before. Raz's machinery is not needed here, since the two options involve the same kinds of value."
