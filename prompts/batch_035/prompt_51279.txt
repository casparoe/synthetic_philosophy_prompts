Background: I'm a retired radio astronomer, spent most of my career at a big observatory fitting models to noisy data, and now in retirement I've gotten into philosophy of science. Reading about Bayesian confirmation theory and I've hit a snag I can't shake off.

Here's the thing. In my working life, some of the best moments were when a new model retrodicted something we'd already measured. The classic case is Einstein accounting for the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion in 1915 — that anomaly had been known for like fifty years, it was old news, and yet obviously everyone felt general relativity got a huge boost from explaining it. Same thing happened to me on a smaller scale more than once. But if I understand the Bayesians right, once you already know E, then P(E) = 1 and also P(E|H) = 1 for basically any H, so the likelihood ratio is 1 and old evidence can't confirm anything. That just seems wrong about how science actually works, and I say that as someone who spent decades actually doing this.

I've dug around a bit and found a few candidate fixes people have proposed. I'll paraphrase since I don't have the books in front of me:

1. The counterfactual fix: ask how surprised you would have been by the anomaly if you had somehow not known about it, and use that hypothetical prior to compute confirmation.
2. The "learning the theory is new information" fix: what's really new in 1915 isn't the Mercury data, it's the moment you first grasp what GR predicts — before that instant you didn't know E follows from H, so relative to your actual epistemic state P(E|H) wasn't trivially 1 yet, and confirmation can happen then.
3. Bite the bullet: old evidence strictly doesn't confirm, and the warm feeling physicists got in 1915 is just a psychological quirk, not real confirmation.

Can you rate each of these fixes on a scale of 1 to 10 for how well it would survive a sharp critic, with a sentence or two on why each gets its score? For what it's worth the counterfactual one feels closest to what we actually did in the lab, but something about it bugs me — I HAVE seen the data, so what does it even mean to ask what I'd have expected in a world where I hadn't? Feels like pretending to have amnesia. Curious how you'd score them, and also whether there's a fourth option I've missed that's better than all three.
