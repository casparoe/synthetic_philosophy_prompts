For the closing session of my graduate seminar on future contingents, I want to hand the students something livelier than another reconstruction of De Interpretatione 9. Over the semester they have become fluent in the positions — the fatalist who holds that "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" has had a truth value since eternity, the Łukasiewicz-style three-valued theorist who lets it lack one until the fleets actually meet, the Ockhamist who traces a thin red line through the tree of possible futures, the open-futurist for whom the branches are all equally unreal but genuinely open — but they treat these as museum pieces, and I want to force them to see the commitments as live, with real costs and real payouts. My plan is a piece of satire that a specialist would judge fair.

Please write a mock marine insurance policy for a firm I'll call The Sea Battle Mutual Assurance Society, in the dry register of a Lloyd's-style underwriting manual: a short preamble about the Society's founding (its actuaries having concluded, on reflection, that premiums are either due or not due and always have been) followed by numbered clauses with headers, as real policy documents have. The positions on future contingents should be smuggled into the fine print rather than named, and the humor must come from getting the metaphysics right rather than from generic insurance jokes: a bivalence clause under which every claim was payable or not payable from all eternity and the adjusters' work is therefore a formality; a rider available to policyholders of the three-valued persuasion, under which a claim's status is indeterminate until the battle occurs and the Society will neither pay nor refuse payment in the meantime, simply corresponding with the claimant at length; an exclusivity rider to the effect that only one of the futures described in any itinerary of scheduled engagements is on the Society's books, the rest being "scenarios for illustration only"; and a schedule of premiums for open-future customers, who are asked to pay in branching installments, one per candidate future, with rebates for futures that fail to actualize. Somewhere there should be a disputes procedure whose arbiter is required to determine whether the truth about tomorrow supervenes on facts about today, and a clause in which the fatalist underwriters admit, without any sense of irony, that there is no point to any of this, since the payout was settled before the Society was chartered — and renew the charter anyway. The irony should stay deadpan and legible: a reader who knows the literature should be able to identify each view from its commitments alone and feel it has been treated fairly, while a reader who doesn't should still find the document comprehensible as an insurance policy that has gone quietly mad.

Please keep the piece under 600 words in total, numbered clauses with headers, and do not cite or attribute anything — no names of philosophers, no works, no bibliography. The whole point is that the views should be recognizable from how they behave in the fine print.
