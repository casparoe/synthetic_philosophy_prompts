I'm a backend engineer at a company that runs a large distributed platform, and every quarter our team has to rank a backlog of reliability and security projects — things like adding checksumming to a replication path, pre-provisioning spare capacity in a second region, rewriting legacy failover logic — by expected dollar impact over the next two years. The distributions we're implicitly dealing with are brutally heavy-tailed: most scenarios cost nothing, a few cost a few thousand dollars, and one scenario (the region-wide outage, the silent data-corruption incident) produces a number that our spreadsheet renders in scientific notation. When I sum over my scenario list, the total is completely dominated by whatever I happened to put in the tail, and I've noticed that the ranking of projects changes depending on how I enumerate and group the scenarios — which sounds like it shouldn't matter. A colleague who reads decision-theory blogs mentioned something called the Pasadena paradox, apparently a variant of the St. Petersburg game where the expected value isn't just infinite but genuinely undefined, because the series you're supposed to add up gives different answers depending on the order you add it in. I strongly suspect the same thing is happening in my spreadsheet, minus the infinity, and I'd like to know what philosophy and decision theory actually say about choosing under such distributions, translated into something my team can run.

Concretely: please write a decision procedure that a group of engineers could apply in a half-day planning meeting, with no philosophy background and no sign-off available for hand-wavy reasoning. Lay out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a "no" (project doesn't make the cut), and where it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values or risk appetite rather than facts. The procedure should take a definite stance on the core question — whether to keep using straight expected value in the tail, switch to something like a truncated or capped version, or abandon EV ranking for these projects altogether — and defend that stance rather than listing options for us to pick from. It should explicitly address the order-dependence issue: under what conditions does the enumeration of scenarios legitimately change the answer, and when is that just an artifact of how I built the list. It should also say what the procedure does with my most annoying colleague's proposal, which is that since the sum is order-dependent anyway, we should choose the scenario ordering that best matches our fiduciary duty to the company and let the ranking fall out of that.

Then run the procedure on three worked cases: (1) the checksumming project, where a single rare corruption scenario accounts for essentially all the estimated value; (2) a dependency-security patch whose expected value is tiny but whose worst case is catastrophic; and (3) a capacity-reservation project whose headline number is the biggest on the backlog but which the procedure should plausibly reject or demote — I want to see it give us an answer we won't like, since that's the real test of whether it's a procedure or a rubber stamp. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as process. After the prose, please provide a JSON object with the following schema: a top-level object with fields "procedure_summary" (one sentence), "steps" (an array of objects, each with "step_number", "question", "sufficient_answer", "stops_with_no", and "judgment_required" as a boolean), "worked_cases" (an array of objects, each with "case", "verdict", "confidence" taking one of the values "low", "medium", or "high", and "reasoning"), and "failure_modes" (an array of strings describing where the procedure breaks down).
