I'm giving a short seminar presentation on supertasks next week, and I've been keeping a list of my verdicts on what happens at the end of various infinite task sequences, because my coursemate and I have a standing bet on whether there's a single principle behind them. I'd like you to figure out what my verdicts amount to. In the classic Thomson's lamp case — switch toggled once at one minute before the end, then again after half that, then half again, and so on, so that infinitely many toggles all complete before the two-minute mark — my verdict is that there is simply no fact of the matter about whether the lamp is on at the end; asking feels like asking for the largest natural number. But for the ordinary Zeno-style runner who crosses infinitely many bisection points of a track, I have no hesitation at all: he ends up at the finish, in a perfectly determinate place. And in a dimmer variant, where instead of toggling on/off the knob rotates through a fixed angle each step so that the angles converge to ninety degrees, I'm confident the knob ends at ninety degrees. Likewise, in a version where the lamp is switched with the same toggle schedule but the filament has enough thermal inertia that its temperature swings get damped toward a convergent value, I think the temperature at the limit is determinate.

Here is where I get uneasy, though. Consider a long corridor with an infinite sequence of identical particles, each placed half the distance to the next, where collisions successively transfer the motion down the line until, by the limit time, every particle has been flicked off to the right and the corridor is empty. I find myself firmly judging that the corridor is empty at the end — determinate again — even though the presence of particles in a region seems every bit as discrete a matter as the on/off state of a switch. And then there is a case I keep flip-flopping on: the lamp is toggled exactly as in Thomson's setup, but we add that the toggle mechanism is spring-loaded so that "off" is its unforced resting position, and that at the limit instant the mechanism is destroyed. Part of me says the lamp is now determinately off; part of me says I've just smuggled in an extra stipulation rather than derived the state from the task sequence. My tentative principle is something like: the end state is determined exactly when the quantity in question is a continuous physical magnitude whose values through the supertask converge, while purely discrete magnitudes inherit nothing. But that principle seems to fit the runner, the dimmer, the filament, and the lamp itself, while sitting badly with the particle corridor, and I can't tell whether the spring-loaded switch confirms or refutes it — the spring is a discrete mechanism, yet its verdict feels continuous with the state of the filament's heat.

Please state, as precisely as you can, the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures these verdicts, motivating each clause with the case that drives it; say what your preferred principle predicts about the spring-loaded switch and about any further supertask cases I haven't listed; and be explicit and honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, with a recommendation for which I should give up. If you think no single principle survives, tell me that plainly rather than patching it with epicycles. Keep the whole answer under 650 words.
