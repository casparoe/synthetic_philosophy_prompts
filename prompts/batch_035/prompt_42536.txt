I'm a junior and one of the co-leaders of my high school's philosophy club, where each meeting a student leads a twenty-minute discussion on a topic of their choice. Next month it's my turn, and I want to build the session around a question I keep snagging on: if hedonism says pleasure is the only thing good in itself, what single thing do all the different experiences we call "pleasures" actually share? The taste of cold water after a long run, the feeling of finally cracking a stubborn calculus problem, pride when my younger sister wins her debate tournament, the drop on a roller coaster, and the quiet contentment of reading on a rainy afternoon all get called "pleasant," but I can't tell whether they share some one property that makes them all pleasures, or whether "pleasant" is just the word we use for a grab bag of unrelated feelings. Bentham's hedonism seems to need the first answer, since it treats pleasure as a quantity that can be measured and summed, and I can't see how to give it.

I'd like a plan for the session rather than an explanation of the issue for my own sake. Specifically: what order the pieces should come in and what depends on what — for instance, whether I first need to separate the question "what is pleasure?" from "what is happiness?", or whether that distinction is better introduced later; which single case should carry the whole session (I've been considering the calculus example, but I worry it's too easy to dismiss as "just relief," which is arguably not a pleasure at all); the misconceptions my classmates will predictably bring or form — I expect "hedonism is just advice to be selfish and indulge yourself," "pleasure means the same thing as happiness," and "all pleasure is basically physical sensation, so the answer is just brain chemistry" — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off; what I should openly decline to get into at this level, such as the neuroscience or technical vocabulary; and one closing question I could ask that would show whether someone has genuinely grasped the problem rather than just nodded along. If it helps, the candidate answers I've come across in my own reading are that all pleasures share one distinctive felt quality, that they're all really satisfactions of desire, and that they share nothing in common but a family resemblance, the way Wittgenstein said games do — but I don't know how much of that to put in front of them up front and how much to let them generate on their own.
