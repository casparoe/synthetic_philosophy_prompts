I'm writing a term essay for a graduate seminar on epistemic normativity, defending the claim that epistemic akrasia — believing that p while also believing that, by one's own lights, one ought not to believe that p, or that one's belief was formed through a method one knows to be unreliable in that context — is sometimes rationally permitted rather than always a failure of coherence. The literature I'm engaging with mostly constructs akrasia cases in one standard way: the first-order evidence is made overwhelming (the agent can literally taste the sweetness of the coffee, or see the defendant in the dock), and the higher-order evidence arrives from outside — an anonymous note, a phone call, a peer's testimony — announcing that the agent is unreliable. My supervisor thinks both features are doing illicit work, and I want a case that can't be dismissed on either ground.

So: construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The setting must be realistic and contemporary — a profession in which snap judgements are made under time pressure, such as radiography, air traffic control, quality auditing, or tournament chess; no demons, no futuristic technology, no clever philosophy-department contrivances. The higher-order evidence must be self-generated: it must come from the agent's own knowledge of her personal track record in that specific sub-domain — a pattern of overconfidence or of a systematic directional error she has honestly documented in herself — and not from any external informant delivering the news that she is unreliable. The first-order evidence must be genuinely balanced in what the agent can articulate: the case must not do its work by stacking the first-order deck, which is the standard trick. You may, however, let the belief draw on trained perception that outruns the articulable evidence — a gestalt read of a scan, a feel for a position — provided you do not stipulate that this trained deliverance is accurate in the instance at issue. Finally, the agent must have no opportunity to recheck: the judgement is made once, in the moment, and the moment passes.

After presenting the scenario in a paragraph or two, walk through what the verdicts are and why they fall out that way: say what a steadfast approach (resisting the higher-order defeat) and a conciliatory approach (letting knowledge of one's own unreliability undermine the belief) each recommend in this case, and be explicit about which feature of the case is doing the work. Then add three things. First, tell me how a committed conciliationist would attack the case — what she would deny or redescribe — and whether the attack is principled or ad hoc. Second, say what happens if I swap the self-generated higher-order evidence for an external source reporting the very same facts about the agent's past performance; if the intuition shifts, tell me whether that reflects a genuine epistemological difference or merely the drama of being told by someone else. Third, identify where the case is weakest as a defence of permitted akrasia, since my supervisor will find it. The purpose of this case, remember, is to support a thesis in an essay: it must generate its verdicts from the theories under discussion, not stipulate them, so if you find yourself writing a scenario in which the permissibility of the akratic combination is quietly built into the premises, start again.
