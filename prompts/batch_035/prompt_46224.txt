I've been reading the rationalist blogs since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, and these days I spend most of my epistemic energy on forecasting platforms, where I've internalized a fairly strict norm: you only earn credibility on claims whose probabilistic commitments were fixed in advance, under a registered question, before the evidence came in. Recently that norm collided with something from my philosophy-of-science reading, and I'd like to turn the collision into a proposal for a one-semester term paper of about 6000 words.

The philosophy side is what's usually called the problem of old evidence. As I understand it: Bayesian confirmation is standardly a matter of a hypothesis assigning the evidence higher probability than its alternatives do, and this seems to require that the evidence not already be certain for you. When it is certain — the classic case being the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, known for decades before general relativity explained it, or more mundanely any fit between a model and the data its builder was staring at the whole time — the probability of the evidence is already pinned at 1, and the machinery seems to say no confirmation occurs, which looks like the wrong answer. The standard repairs, as I understand them, involve counterfactual credences: asking what you would have believed about the evidence had you not known it. Everyone seems to agree these repairs are uncomfortable, because they require evaluating probabilities conditional on forgetting information, and there are many ways to model the forgetting that give different verdicts.

Here's the forecasting-practice side: my community appears to have solved this, at least institutionally. Preregistration, registered questions, and out-of-sample scoring enforce that a theory earns confirmation only from predictions committed to beforehand. On reflection this looks less like a practical workaround and more like a diagnosis: old evidence was never really evidence, and the cases where we're tempted to credit retrodiction are actually doing something else — trading on the theory having been independently generated, or on its later novel successes.

So the paper I want to propose asks whether the forecasting community's discipline constitutes a genuine dissolution of the problem of old evidence, or merely an institutional dodge of it. My half-formed thought — the thing I'd most like the proposal to sharpen — is this: the preregistration norm can be read as a commitment device that makes determinate the very counterfactuals the standard repairs need but cannot pin down. By fixing in advance what the theory-builder would have said about the evidence, registration turns an ill-defined counterfactual ("what would I have believed without knowing E?") into a well-defined historical fact ("what I did publicly commit to before E was observed"). If that's right, old evidence was a problem about underdetermined counterfactuals all along, and the solution was always going to look like an institution, not a probability assignment.

Please write the proposal so that it includes: a precise statement of the question, framed so it has a determinate answer rather than inviting a survey; why the existing positions — the counterfactual-credence repairs on one side and simple refusal to credit old evidence on the other — leave the question open; the method, meaning what I would actually do, month by month, over a semester; what a result would look like on each side, including what the Mercury case should be classified as if the dissolution succeeds; and an honest assessment of what could make the project fail — in particular whether my commitment-device reading is just the standard counterfactual-credence repairs restated in sociological vocabulary, and if so, whether that restatement is a real contribution or a change of subject.
