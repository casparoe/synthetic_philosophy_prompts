I spent thirty-five years in condensed-matter physics, most of it doing numerical work — band-structure codes, Monte Carlo, molecular dynamics — and retired three years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy of mind in earnest: I have worked through Lucas's original paper, both of Penrose's books, and a fair amount of the subsequent literature. I would like a consistency audit of where I have landed. My commitments, numbered for convenience:

1. I am a physicalist about the mind. Cognition is what brains do; no immaterial substance does any causal work in explaining thought, and I regard substance dualism as untenable.

2. I hold, as an article of empirical faith carried over from my working life, that physics is computable. Every system I ever modeled, and every law in every textbook I taught from, is effectively computable: given initial conditions and sufficient computing power, I can simulate any of them to arbitrary precision. I cannot name an established law that is uncomputable, and I find it hard to believe the final theory will contain one.

3. The Lucas-Penrose argument strikes me as basically right. When I work through the incompleteness theorems, I find that given any consistent formal system, I can see that its Gödel sentence is true, while nothing within that system can establish it; and on that basis I conclude that mathematical insight is non-algorithmic.

4. I am a fallibilist about mathematicians. The historical record — the shaky rigor of nineteenth-century analysis, the Italian school of algebraic geometry, Kempe's proof of the four-color theorem standing for a decade — shows that individuals and whole communities assert falsehoods with great confidence for long stretches. I take the Gödelian argument to survive this, because its premise is not that we are consistent but that we can see, from outside a specified system, what follows from that system's consistency.

5. Mathematical truth is independent of us: arithmetic was true before there were brains to notice, and we sometimes manage to know it.

6. I do not accept Penrose's specific escape route. I do not expect whatever quantum gravity turns out to be to be uncomputable in the relevant sense; I expect equations one can integrate numerically, like everything else I have ever dealt with.

So: does this set hang together? Please go through it and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is, all things considered. I confess I cannot tell whether (3) drags me toward dualism, which (1) forbids — whether non-algorithmic must mean non-physical — or whether that apparent conflict dissolves on inspection. And please be blunt with me if you think I have misunderstood the incompleteness theorems or the dialectic surrounding them: I have been at philosophy for three years and would rather be corrected than accommodated. If the whole audit rests on a misreading on my part, say so at the outset rather than working around it.
