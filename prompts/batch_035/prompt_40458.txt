So I'm a science and ideas writer, and I've been sitting in on this art criticism seminar run by a friend of mine, Lena, who's a critic with very definite opinions and about twenty years of reviewing under her belt. I've started keeping notes on her verdicts because I want to write a piece about her whole approach, and honestly I can't reverse-engineer it. Her track record on the art-vs-morality stuff, as best I can reconstruct it: she thinks Caravaggio's paintings are obviously masterpieces and his having killed a man is simply none of the paintings' business; she refuses to call Triumph of the Will a masterpiece no matter how technically groundbreaking it is, saying its spectacle is inseparable from its worship of its subject; she thinks Lolita is a great novel precisely because it makes you complicit in Humbert's charm and then makes you see what you've been complicit in; when a very good living sculptor was convicted of serial abuse last year she argued his retrospective should absolutely go ahead and wrote it up glowingly; and she just last month savaged a morally impeccable, endlessly earnest marble sculpture about charity as kitsch that no good intentions could rescue. So the artist's biography seems irrelevant to her, the work's own moral stance sometimes matters, and moral virtue doesn't buy you aesthetic points.

Here's the new case thats come up and that I'd love a prediction on: an anonymous collective (literally no identified members) has an immersive installation where you spend an hour inside a lovingly, gorgeously reconstructed authoritarian personality cult — warm, communal, seductive, the whole thing — and it never breaks the spell, never shows you your own complicity, nothing like that. The collective's only public statement says it's meant as an act of mourning for a decade the country lost, not a critique and not a recruitment film, and it's only being shown inside a big-city art museum where the audience presumably isn't exactly the target demographic for conversion anyway. Lena hasn't said a word about it yet. What's she going to say?

I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rules that fit her record — like, is the thing she cares about whether the work internally endorses the evil versus reflects on the endorsement (which would separate Lolita from Triumph of the Will), or is it whether appreciating the work actually sustains or spreads the evil out in the world, or something else entirely that also fits the same cases? Give me the candidate rules, what each one predicts about the cult installation, your best guess at her verdict with reasons, and one further case (it can be hypothetical) that would really split the rules apart — I want to be ready when the review drops so my piece can say she's running rule X rather than rule Y.
