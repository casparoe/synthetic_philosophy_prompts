Quick context: I teach epistemology and philosophy of science, and every year at our faculty seminar a logician colleague trots out this argument of his about assurance and self-audit. I keep nodding along and then hating myself afterwards, and next time I want to actually be able to answer it. He calls it "just Gödel applied honestly." The punchline is that every assurance regime — a bank's internal audit, a university's self-study, a court system reviewing itself, even a person checking their own reasoning — is ultimately ungrounded. Here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency using its own resources.
(2) An audit is exactly this kind of attempted self-certification: when a bank's compliance department checks the bank's books, it uses those very procedures — accounting standards, arithmetic, the bank's own record-keeping — to check themselves.
(3) So no institution, and no reasoner, can ever certify its own reliability; every self-audit must leave its own soundness unestablished.
(4) Bringing in an external auditor doesn't help, since the external auditor faces the same problem with its own procedures, and so on up: the regress never terminates in anything that certifies itself.
(5) Therefore all assurance — institutional or personal — rests at bottom on something that cannot itself be checked: trust, habit, or power. And since this follows from a theorem rather than a value judgement, he claims it's as solid as arithmetic.

My own suspicion is that he's sliding from "can't prove its own consistency" to something like "can't verify anything about itself," and that treating an audit as a formal proof of consistency is doing sneaky work in (2). But I also suspect the regress in (4) might be survivable in a way he isn't admitting — maybe a regress of audits isn't vicious the way he needs it to be — and I genuinely can't tell which of my two suspicions is doing the real work, or whether both are. And there's a further thing that nags me: the deflated version of his conclusion (some principled limits on total self-certification) might actually be true and worth something, and I'd like to know whether it is, or whether even that is just a dressed-up triviality dressed in mathematical clothes.

Diagnose this for me: which steps are fine, which step is load-bearing in the bad sense, and what genuinely survives once you strip the bad move out. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion — just the five points. And British English please, realise and programme and the lot.
