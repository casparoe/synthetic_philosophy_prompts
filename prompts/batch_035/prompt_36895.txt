So here's my situation: I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, 35 years mostly doing experimental work on phase transitions and critical phenomena, and now that I have time on my hands I've fallen down the philosophy of science rabbit hole. The thing I keep circling back to is reduction vs emergence, and I want an essay that actually gets at it through the stuff I spent my career on rather than the usual hand-waving about consciousness or "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts."

Here's the specific thing that bugs me, and I want the essay to take it seriously. In my corner of physics, wildly different micro-systems -- a magnet near its Curie point, a fluid near its liquid-gas critical point, binary mixtures, even lattice models cooked up on a computer -- turn out to share the same critical exponents. Same universal power laws, to several decimal places, despite having completely different microscopic constituents and interactions. The renormalization group explains this: the short-range details get "washed out" under successive coarse-graining, and systems flow to the same fixed point. Now the philosophers I've been reading seem split on what to make of this. One camp says: look, this is textbook emergence -- the higher-level (long-wavelength) behavior is autonomous, its own fixed points and universality classes, and knowing the microphysics tells you almost nothing about which class you land in or what the exponents are. You can't read off the exponent 0.625 from the Hamiltonian the way you'd read off the mass of a molecule. The other camp says: hold on, the renormalization group IS the microtheory -- it's quantum/statistical mechanics all the way down, the universality is deduced, not imposed, and calling it "emergent" just means "the deduction is long and goes through a fixed point." So which is it? Is renormalization-group universality a genuine case of the special sciences' autonomy that philosophers talk about via multiple realizability, or is it actually one of the best arguments AGAINST that autonomy, since unlike psychology or economics, we actually have the derivation?

A couple of things I'd like the essay to wrestle with specifically. First, the fact that the coarse-graining only works because of a separation of length scales, and the renormalization semi-group is irreversible -- you can coarsen but not un-coarsen, information really is discarded, and yet the macro predictions survive that discard. Is throwing away most of the micro-details while keeping predictive power a kind of emergence, or just a compression algorithm? Second, whether the analogy to other sciences holds up: is "water is H2O" a different kind of reduction claim than "magnetism is whatever the Ising fixed point governs," and if so, does that difference matter for the philosophy? Third, I've seen philosophers claim the universality case shows that lower-level facts are explanatorily irrelevant to higher-level ones, and that seems too strong to me -- the fixed point IS a low-level object in some sense, dimension and symmetry do constrain the exponents.

I don't need name-drops of who said what, I've had enough of reading essays that are just bibliographies with verbs. What I want is a real argument that reaches a verdict: does my own field, the one place where the micro-to-macro bridge is actually built and crossed, support the emergentist or the reductionist, or does it show the dichotomy is confused? If your conclusion is some version of "the dichotomy needs restating," fine, but say precisely what the restatement is and show it earns its keep on the phase-transition example. Write it like you're explaining it to a colleague over coffee, not like you're submitting to a journal, but don't cut corners on the physics -- I'll notice, I spent four decades on this stuff and I know exactly how sloppy people get with universality when they're making a philosophical point.
