You are a professional philosopher with joint expertise in bioethics and the formal epistemology of debiasing, equally at home in the analytic literature on procreation and in the applied literature on human enhancement. Before drafting anything, I want you to brainstorm openly: sketch two or three candidate framings of the research question below, state which you would choose and why, and only then write the proposal proper. Think carefully at each stage; a vague, hedged proposal is worse to me than a precise one that turns out to need revision.

Some context on who is asking. I have been a regular reader of the rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, the kind of reader who thinks Bostrom and Ord's reversal test is among the best pieces of applied epistemology that corner of the internet produced, and who has followed the recurring embryo-selection threads with a mix of enthusiasm and unease. I am now applying for a six-month research fellowship in bioethics, and the idea I want funded sits at a junction I have never seen taken seriously, either in the academic literature or in the community's own discussions.

The idea, in rough form. Community members tend to defend Savulescu's procreative beneficence principle — roughly, that parents have a moral reason, and arguably an obligation, to select, among the children they could have, those with the best life prospects — with a debiasing move borrowed from the reversal test: the intuition that there is something wrong with selecting embryos looks suspiciously like status quo bias, and the standard check is to ask whether, in a world where selection were the default, we would argue for returning to unassisted, random conception. So far, so familiar. But the reversal test works by holding the affected person fixed while flipping the direction of intervention, and embryo selection is a paradigm case of a non-identity intervention, in Parfit's sense: the child created through selection would not otherwise have existed at all, and there is no coherent question of how that particular child's life would otherwise have gone. It appears, then, that the community's favorite debiasing tool is undefined at precisely the point where it is most needed. Three possibilities present themselves, and I do not know which the project should commit to: (a) the test can be patched for identity-changing interventions; (b) the failure is superficial, because some other debiasing procedure covers such cases; or (c) the failure is deep — in which case the standard rationalist dismissals of the anti-selection arguments we hear from Habermas's book on the future of human nature, with its claim that selecting children disrupts the symmetry between generations that children need in order to be autonomous, and from Sandel's appeal to giftedness, have never actually been answered, only waved at.

Your task is to turn this into a research proposal containing: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, which is to say an argument, not a literature survey; the method, month by month, of what I would actually do — I expect mostly conceptual reconstruction of the reversal test's formal structure, and testing whatever patched version emerges against stock cases from the non-identity literature; the candidate conclusions and the considerations that would decide among them; realistic deliverables; honest failure conditions, including what would make the project collapse or prove too big for six months; and a closing paragraph answering the question a foundation will certainly ask, namely why philosophy should be funded on an issue that fertility clinics will face commercially within a decade.

Constraints on the output: roughly two pages for the proposal itself; no bibliography, since I will supply citations myself; refer to authors, works, and arguments by name where necessary, but do not put anything in quotation marks, and do not lean on dates, page numbers, or other specifics I would have to verify. In your initial brainstorming step, please also flag any point where the community's reconstruction of the reversal test might diverge from what the original argument actually requires — I want the proposal to be robust against that kind of drift.
