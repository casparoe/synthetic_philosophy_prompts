I spent the better part of four decades as an experimental physical chemist, and in retirement I have taken up philosophy of language and logic in earnest. I have now read enough to encounter the deflationary family of views on truth — the idea that truth is not a substantive property of propositions at all, that the predicate exists chiefly to remove quotation marks or to let us endorse indirectly — and I find myself torn. My instinct, formed at the bench, says something must be missing: we spent entire careers trying to bring measurements into line with how things are, and it strains belief that "true" merely punctuates that effort. But instinct is not an argument, and I would rather be corrected than flattered. So, before I commit in either direction, I want the specification first: write me the desiderata — the list of things any adequate theory of truth must deliver — with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

I would like the list to address, at minimum, the following, though you should add or reorganize as judgment dictates: what the theory owes for the equivalence between asserting a sentence and asserting that the sentence is true, and whether those equivalences could really be all there is to truth, or whether they merely fix the predicate's extension while leaving its deeper role unexplained; how the theory grounds the utility of generalization, where the predicate compresses what would otherwise be an infinite conjunction — as when a lab director certifies that everything in a technician's report is true without being able to enumerate the report's claims; whether truth must be kept distinct from justification, given that a claim can be well warranted in its day and yet false, or dismissed and yet true — and which side of that distinction the theory itself needs for its own statements; what to say about approximate truth and idealization, which is where a scientist actually lives — a model with frictionless planes is strictly true of no real system, yet ranks above one that gets the sign of a force wrong, and I want to know whether a theory of truth must make sense of such degrees or may dismiss them as belonging to a different inquiry; whether truth must be compositional, so that the truth of a conjunction flows from the truth of its parts, and whether that is a substantive demand or an artifact of Tarski's construction; how the theory is expected to survive the liar and its relatives without machinery — gaps, hierarchies, revisions — that strikes a newcomer as a saving epicycle; and finally whether the phrase "true in a model," as mathematicians use it, names the same property as plain truth or is a homonym the theory must account for. For each requirement, state the motivating case, mark which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which you suspect are prejudices of one camp dressed as demands, and say plainly if you believe some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — if so, that is the part I most want to see. Then, and only briefly, score the main contenders against the list: the redundancy and disquotational forms of deflationism, the prosentential variant, Horwich-style minimalism, and a straightforward correspondence view. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring; I can do the scoring myself once I trust the list. And close by telling me candidly whether my bench-trained conviction that truth is a substantial feature of the world is itself evidence of anything, or merely a habit of speech that a deflationist would predict I would have.
