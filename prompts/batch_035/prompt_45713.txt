Please compare two views of how a machine could come to think, where the crux is where the teaching signal comes from and what it presupposes about the learner.

The first view is Alan Turing's own sketch, at the end of his 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence in Mind, in the section he titles Learning Machines. As I understand it, the proposal there is that rather than programming an adult mind directly, one should build something like a child machine and educate it, with the process governed by rewards and punishments administered in something like the way a child is taught; he suggests that the machine might be spared the slow evolutionary search that produced the human brain, provided it undergoes some analogue of cultural transmission; he expects random elements to be useful, since the space of possible minds is so vast; and he remarks that an important feature of such a machine would be that its teacher need not know much about what is going on inside it. I may be garbling details, so feel free to correct me.

The second view is implicit in how contemporary large language models actually learn: a first stage of passive prediction over an enormous fixed corpus, in which nobody rewards or punishes anything and the signal is just the discrepancy between predicted and actual text, followed by a second stage in which human judgments shape the system, but as graded comparative verdicts on whole outputs rather than as environmental consequences of the learner's own actions.

What I want adjudicated is whether the second view vindicates, revises, or quietly refutes the first. Some points to address: whether gradient descent over a fixed corpus is usefully seen as Turing's cultural transmission with the corpus standing in for the culture, or whether that stretches the analogy past its purpose; whether Turing's emphasis on reward contingent on the learner's own actions survives at all in a pipeline whose first stage involves no actions of that kind; whether the second stage, where the reward comes from a learned model of human preference rather than from the world, is a descendant of Turing's teacher or a fundamentally different source of signal; and whether Turing's observation that a teacher ignorant of the learner's internals would be an advantage has any counterpart in the present situation, where developers have extensive access to internals and still struggle to predict behavior. Where the two pictures genuinely diverge, say so plainly rather than reconciling them; where the divergence is superficial, say that too.
