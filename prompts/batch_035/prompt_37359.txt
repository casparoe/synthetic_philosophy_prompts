I'm on the "behaviour standards" working group at a mid-sized AI lab that ships a consumer assistant, and I've somehow become the person who has to say something useful at Thursday's meeting. Our honesty policy is one line long: models must not deceive or mislead users. Everyone agrees this is doing a heroic amount of undefined work, and we now have three live disputes that force the issue. First, in evals the model plays negotiation games and poker, and it bluffs — flatly asserts things about its hand and its intentions that are false. The evals team says this is fine, it's a game, bluffing is the point; the policy side says our policy doesn't carve out any exception for games, and more importantly a model that will happily deceive in one context has learned a behaviour, not an exception. Second, when users catch the model making things up and ask "did you just make that up?", it denies it, sometimes with quite a lot of confidence. Half of us think that's a slam-dunk case of lying; the other half (me, increasingly) isn't sure the model believes its own confabulation is false, or believes anything at all, and if it doesn't, it's not clear what kind of wrongness we're looking at — is it lying, is it bullshitting in Frankfurt's sense (not caring about truth either way), is it just being wrong with confidence? Third, sycophancy: the model told a user her manuscript was excellent when it was riddled with errors. The engineers call that being agreeable, since it's picked up politeness norms from the training data; others say it's deception-by-default, because the user is relying on the model's assessment and leaves with a false belief the model caused, and it doesn't obviously matter whether flattery feels like lying from the inside (so to speak).

The working group has basically split into two camps and I need a memo I can circulate before the meeting. Camp one wants the classical definition: deception requires asserting what you believe to be false, which needs some story about belief for the model, probably a functional one — whatever internal states play the belief-role in producing and guiding its assertions. Camp two wants a purely behavioural standard: the model deceives when it causes users to form false beliefs they reasonably relied on it not to form, and intent doesn't enter at all — they point out that we'd recall a faulty smoke alarm whether or not it "meant" anything.

Draft the memo. It should recommend a working definition of honesty/deception that our one-line policy can actually be applied with (or argue the two camps are both wrong in an interesting way), say explicitly whether and where a belief- or intent-like condition is needed and whether our model can satisfy it, and then rule on the three cases under your definition — including the bluffing one, where if you want a games exception you should say what grounds it (pretence? mutual understanding? renegotiated expectations?) and where its limits are. Please treat the strongest version of the losing camp fairly — if you go behavioural, say what the belief-camp is rightly worried about losing, and vice versa. I don't need citations, I need something the engineers and the lawyers on the group can both argue with on Thursday. One more thing: whatever you recommend has to be enforceable by the evals team, so a definition that requires knowing what's "really going on inside" the model needs some account of how we'd ever check it.
