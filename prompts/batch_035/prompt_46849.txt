At our department's works-in-progress session on moral revolutions this week, two colleagues — an early-career historian and a senior moral philosopher — fell into a disagreement neither could resolve, and I confess I could not referee it cleanly either. (1) The historian opened with the Eric Williams thesis: British abolition of the slave trade and of West Indian slavery is the paradigm case of a supposed moral revolution that was really interest all the way down — plantation slavery was becoming economically marginal to an industrializing Britain, and abolitionist argument was so much window dressing. And, he added, the pattern generalizes: dueling died when the state consolidated its monopoly on legitimate violence and gentlemen no longer needed pistols to certify their status, so what we call moral revolutions are just economic and institutional shifts that moral rhetoric later dignifies. (2) The philosopher replied that this makes the causal story run too fast, and that Appiah's central lesson is precisely that neither pure argument nor pure interest explains abolition, dueling, or foot-binding: sustained moral argument does real work, but only once it attaches to a shift in what a community treats as honorable versus degrading — Clarkson's evidence and the Quakers' campaigning mattered exactly when slave-trading became shameful among the people with power to stop it. (3) The historian pressed the reduction: if what does the causal work is a change in which practices confer standing on elites, that is interest again under another description — honor is simply the currency in which status interests are paid, so Appiah's third alternative collapses back into the Williams thesis with extra steps. (4) The philosopher countered that this misreads both the phenomenology and the causal structure: honor is constitutively non-instrumental for the agent — duelists faced death and abolitionists accepted costs precisely where prudential calculation counseled otherwise — and a motive that regularly overrides interest cannot be explained away as interest in disguise. Neither would budge on whether Appiah's position is a genuine third alternative or a disguised variant of the interest thesis. Please adjudicate each of the four moves in turn, saying who is right about what, where each overreaches, and what, if anything, both are missing about how interests, arguments, and honor actually interact in norm change. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the move-by-move reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the exchange.
