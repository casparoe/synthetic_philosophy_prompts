Been reading Scott Alexander and the old LessWrong threads about prediction since forever, and now I run a small rationality meetup (about 14 people, mix of programmers and humanities types, we meet for 2 hours on a Sunday). Next month it's my turn to run a session, and I want to do chaos and predictability. Here's the thing I keep failing to get across in discussion: everyone nods along at "deterministic doesn't mean predictable", but they still reason as if a deterministic universe is in principle forecastable by a smart enough predictor, and then they import that assumption into arguments about free will, about AI superforecasting, about whether the universe is "just physics so it's all settled". I want them to FEEL the prediction horizon in their bones, not recite it.

So: design me a session. My idea, which you should improve on or replace if you have something better, is some kind of prediction game with betting chips where the underlying system is dead simple and completely deterministic, and everybody knows the exact rule, and yet... Logistics I'm imagining:

- Some dynamical rule simple enough that people can run it by hand or on their phones in a second — the logistic map at a chaotic parameter value, or a toy tent map, whatever you think works best pedagogically. Small measurement noise would be great too (e.g. you only get to see the state rounded to one decimal place), so they can viscerally watch tiny observational error blow up.

- A prediction market structure: half the group are "oracles" who get to observe the system for N steps and then bet chips on where it'll be at step N+5, N+20, N+100, vs a bank. I want the payout odds to be set so they discover empirically that short horizon = free money, long horizon = the house always wins, and there's a fairly sharp transition between the two that moves when you let them measure with more precision.

- Maybe a second phase where the "system" is another human following the rule on paper, so nobody can peek, which I think isolates the interesting bit: even when no hidden information exists at all, only observation error and the stretching of nearby trajectories.

Then a debrief: map what happened onto Lyapunov exponents / the e-folding time of errors, onto the difference between "the future is fixed" and "the future is computable by anyone inside the system", and onto why this does NOT settle anything about free will (I want the debrief to preempt the smug leap from "chaos exists" to "so determinism is false", cos that's exactly the move one of my guys always makes). Also honestly tell me: what part of chaos theory can no game with chips and rounding actually reproduce? (I'm guessing something like shadowing — that a wrong-looking trajectory is still shadowed by a true one — and the whole quantum side.) And what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it? I assume someone will try to arbitrage the odds, someone will insist on just writing a script that simulates it perfectly, and someone will get bored during step 100. How do I handle each without lecturing at them.

If you know of a cleaner deterministic-but-wild system than the logistic map that works better for hand computation, please say so. British spelling please, and keep the maths at the level of a motivated amateur — a few of the group have no calculus at all.
