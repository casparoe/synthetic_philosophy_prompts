I'm a novelist working on a book about a parent trying to decide whether to keep going across a collapsed country with a small kid, and rereading Cormac McCarthy's The Road as a kind of unavoidable touchstone it got me thinking about antinatalism. In the novel the mother has already made her call -- she kills herself and, in the note she leaves, essentially argues that death is coming for all of them anyway and it's kinder to meet it early, while the father refuses that logic and keeps walking south with the boy, telling him they're carrying the fire. My question: read The Road through Benatar's asymmetry between (present) pain and (absent) pleasure, since that's the philosophical core of antinatalism -- the idea that the absence of pain is good even though there's no one around to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure is only bad if there's someone who's thereby deprived. Does the mother's choice in the novel track the asymmetry, and does the father's refusual show where it breaks, or is he just running on stubbornness and love rather than a counterargument? There's also the question of the boy himself, who seems to be the moral center of the whole book -- every scene where he insists on helping strangers, like the old man Ely, reads like the novel insisting there's still something worth preserving -- so I'd want to know whether the book as a whole undermines the antinatalist reading or whether the ending (the family with the dog, the ambiguous rescue) is too thin to carry any rebuttal weight.

One thing I want you to be careful about: Benatar's asymmetry is usually deployed about bringing lives into existence, whereas the Road is mostly about whether to continue lives already started, and I honestly can't tell how much of my reading depends on sliding between those two -- poke at that, because if the mapping breaks there, the whole exercise might collapse. Also, and this matters to me, please flag what's actually settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who know the antinatalism literature -- I know the asymmetry itself gets attacked from a bunch of directions (e.g., that missing out on pleasure can be bad for someone, or that the asymmetry only works if you accept weird things about non-identity), and I don't want you presenting one side as the consensus when it isn't. I'm not looking for a verdict on antinatalism itself, more a reading of whether McCarthy's book dramatizes the argument in a way prose philosophy can't -- something I can steal for my own novel, honestly, since my narrator needs to think her way through the same fork and I want her to be smarter than a strawman in either direction.
