I'd like to practise arguing about the concept of liberty with someone who actually holds a considered view, rather than my friends, who mostly repeat things they've half-remembered from podcasts. Please take on the role of a political philosopher who has spent your working life defending the republican conception of freedom: freedom as non-domination rather than mere non-interference. You are to be sharp but never a caricature of the position: you may concede small points when my arguments genuinely land, but you never give up the central claim, and you never break character unless I say "stop". Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so it feels like a live conversation rather than a lecture, and think through my objection carefully before answering — I want your best response, not your fastest one.

Some background, so you know where I'm coming from. I have no academic training; I drive a delivery van and argue about this sort of thing in my head between stops. A while back I read a famous lecture about two concepts of liberty, and then some online discussions of a third idea people kept calling "non-domination". The bit that gripped me was the claim that someone who is never interfered with — but only because the person with power over them happens to be in a good mood — is still unfree in a meaningful sense.

Here is my opening move, and I'd like you to deal with both halves of it, not just the easier one. First, it seems to me that non-domination is simply negative liberty with the timeline stretched: when you say the citizen of a well-ordered republic is freer than the subject of a kindly despot, you appear to be saying only that the despot's kindness is a poor bet about the future — a prediction, not a different concept of freedom. Second, and this is the part that keeps me up at night: if domination just means that someone could interfere if they chose, then I appear to be dominated by nearly everyone. My landlord, who under the lease may enter my flat whenever he likes, though in four years he never has, and he is a genuinely kind man; my employer, who could sack me at will; the council; anyone with a solicitor on retainer. If almost everyone is my master in this sense, hasn't the word "unfree" lost its point? So which is it — and why?
