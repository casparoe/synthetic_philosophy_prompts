**The answer I have to grade:**

"The gradual replacement argument comes from Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*. Imagine that neurosurgeons replace your neurons one at a time with silicon chips that perform exactly the same input–output function. After each replacement, you report that you feel fine, and your behavior is completely unchanged. Since each individual replacement makes such a tiny difference, and since you notice no difference at any step, it is implausible that your consciousness could be fading a little at a time — we would have to imagine your experiences becoming steadily less vivid while you sincerely insist that nothing has changed, which seems incoherent. So by the time the last neuron has been replaced, your consciousness is fully preserved.

This shows that functional organization determines experience, and therefore that functionalism is true. It also settles the identity question. Because the replacement happens gradually, the Ship of Theseus worry doesn't arise: the person who walks out of the lab is literally you, not a copy. Someone might object that scan-and-copy uploading is different, but the gradual case shows that any functionally identical duplicate would be you, since from the inside there is no 'further fact' to being you beyond psychological continuity. Notice also that the argument could in principle be tested empirically: run the replacement experiment and check whether subjects still report experiences. This is why critics of functionalism have largely abandoned the absent qualia possibility."
