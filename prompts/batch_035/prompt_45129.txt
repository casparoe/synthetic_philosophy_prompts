I've been hanging around the decision-theory corners of the rationalist internet since roughly 2009, and I've noticed that one venerable puzzle gets re-litigated every couple of years without anyone changing their mind: the absent-minded driver. I'd like a diagnosis of why this particular debate has stalled, rather than another run-through of the positions.

The case, in case the details matter: a driver on a highway with two identical intersections, unable to remember whether he has passed one already. Exiting at the first intersection gets him 0; exiting at the second gets him home, worth 4; continuing past both leaves him at a motel, worth 1. Since the intersections are indistinguishable, his behavior must be described by a single policy — continue with probability p at whichever intersection he's at. At the planning stage the math is easy: expected payoff is 4p(1-p) + p², maximized at p = 2/3, yielding 4/3. Piccione and Rubinstein introduced the example in their 1997 paper "On the Interpretation of Decision Problems with Imperfect Recall" and claimed a genuine inconsistency between the planning stage and the action stage, where the driver sits at an intersection and must reason about what to do now. The reply by Aumann, Hart, and Perry in the same journal argued there is no paradox once the driver at the intersection remembers that his current decision is inseparable from his decision at the other node, and that he has learned nothing at the intersection that he didn't already know at the planning stage. Then the rationalist community got hold of it: Wei Dai's 2009 LessWrong post on the driver became one of the motivating exhibits for updateless decision theory, and the case has been folded into the broader FDT-versus-CDT argument — the driver's two decisions are logically linked, an updateless agent treats itself as choosing the output of its decision algorithm, picks p = 2/3, and the whole "paradox" evaporates.

And yet the disagreement persists, and I want to understand its structure. The updateless answer feels obviously right to me, but the objections never die. Some say the two "stages" are simply different decision problems — choosing a policy ex ante and choosing an act ex interim — and the alleged conflict only exists if you demand that a single "should" cover both; on that view the debate is largely verbal. Others press the epistemology: the probability the driver should assign to being at the first intersection, given that he is at an intersection, depends on the very policy he is choosing — commenters on that old LessWrong thread described it as smuggling a time loop into the problem — so it's not clear the interim calculation is even well-posed, and patched versions that maximize the conditional expectation (one lands near p ≈ 0.53) visibly double-count the path where both decisions occur. Meanwhile CDT's defenders respond that an agent with a randomizing device can implement p = 2/3 perfectly well without any updateless machinery, so the case shows nothing about causal decision theory. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the stalled debate is really about the individuation of decisions — whether the driver at the two intersections makes one decision implemented twice or two decisions of a common type — and because that question has never been settled formally, each camp just helps itself to the individuation that makes its answer come out, and the argument re-runs indefinitely. Is that right, or is there a substantive formal disagreement hiding in here?

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or an artifact of an idealization (perfectly indistinguishable experiences at numerically distinct locations) that no agent could instantiate? Please diagnose it using what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal account of strategy-relative self-location, a theorem delimiting when interim and ex ante advice can come apart, an argument that the case matters for real agents with memory limits — or whether no progress should be expected, and why.
