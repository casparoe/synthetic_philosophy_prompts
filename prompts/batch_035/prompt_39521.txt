I belong to an online support community for a poorly understood chronic illness. Most of us have had dismissive experiences with doctors, and over the years the group has developed its own trusted figures — a few sympathetic practitioners, some patient-researchers who read the literature closely, and a set of shared working beliefs about what helps. Lately I've noticed a pattern in how we handle disagreement from outside. When an official guideline or a new study contradicts what our trusted figures say, the response isn't usually an argument about the evidence — it's that of course the establishment says that, since these are the same people who told us it was all in our heads. Two things bother me about this. First, a couple of members who left and later returned said that while they were active in the group they had simply lost track of mainstream developments in the field — not that they'd weighed them and rejected them, but that they never encountered them. Second, and this is what makes it hard, the group's record isn't bad: it was right about a treatment approach that the establishment ignored for years before the evidence came around, and that history is a large part of why people here trust the insiders over the outsiders. I've been asked to draft community guidelines for how we should treat outside sources — whether to require engagement with them, how to discuss them, and so on. What should I be thinking about as I write them, and how can I tell whether our problem is that we're insulated from outside views or something more structural than that?
