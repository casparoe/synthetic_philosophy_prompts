I've spent the last few months, mostly on long drives, trying to sort out what I believe about whether the way things feel to me actually does anything, or whether it's just along for the ride. I once heard someone describe the feeling of pain as being like the whistle on a train: it happens whenever the train moves, but nothing the whistle does makes the train move. I think this position has a name — epiphenomenalism — but I don't want to get the philosophy-book version; I want to figure out my own case-by-case verdicts and see whether they add up to anything. Right now they don't, and I'd like help finding the principle behind them, or being told there isn't one.

My verdicts: When I touch a hot pan and snatch my hand back, I believe the pain is doing something. I can't shake that. When I tell my wife that cilantro tastes like soap to me, I'd swear the soapy-ness itself is what my words are about — the feeling is why I say it that way. When I hear a particular chord in a piece of music and get goosebumps, I think the felt beauty of it is what raises the hairs, not just the nerves. But now the ones that cut the other way. If everything in my brain were exactly as it is, but the feeling attached to cilantro were swapped with the feeling of cinnamon, I have to admit I'd probably still say "soap" — the words come from the brain, and the brain hasn't changed, so the feeling wasn't doing any work in the report after all. I've also read about people who are blind in part of their visual field yet can still guess where objects are if forced, while insisting they saw nothing — that seems to show that seeing is what makes you say you saw something, which cuts back toward the feelings mattering. And apparently there are patients who say they still feel pain but that it no longer bothers them, and they don't flinch or pull away — feeling without the action. Finally, the case that bothers me most: I half-believe that an atom-for-atom copy of me with no inner life at all would do everything I do, including sit here typing this paragraph about feelings, and I find that half-belief hard to give up.

Here is the principle I've been trying out: feelings only make a difference when the topic is the feelings themselves — so they can't cause me to pull my hand away, but they can cause me to say "that hurts." My problem is that the swapped-cilantro case seems to break even this: if the feelings were different but the brain the same, my report about the feeling would apparently still come out the same. So either the swapping case is sneakier than I think, or the principle is wrong, or some of my verdicts have to go.

Which is it? If you can state a principle that saves all my verdicts, please do, even if it needs to be complicated, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't made up my mind about: whether my enjoyment of a song is what causes me to play it again, and whether the flash of feeling when I finally understand a proof is what causes me to say "oh, I get it." And if no principle can save them all, tell me plainly which of my verdicts I should give up and why those rather than the others.
