I am grading a take-home exam for an upper-level course in moral philosophy. The course covered, among other things, Susan Wolf's 1982 article "Moral Saints" (Journal of Philosophy 79, no. 8). One of the exam questions read as follows:

> In her paper "Moral Saints," Susan Wolf argues that moral perfection is less attractive as a personal ideal than one might assume. (1) Explain Wolf's distinction between the Loving Saint and the Rational Saint, and explain what work that distinction does in her argument. (2) Explain why, on Wolf's view, the moral saint fails as an ideal of personal excellence. (3) Finally, consider the following objection: that Wolf's argument succeeds only if one accepts value pluralism, the view that there are genuine nonmoral values (aesthetic, intellectual, and the like) alongside moral ones. A theorist who denies that such nonmoral values are real — a thoroughgoing monist about value — would supposedly have no reason to accept Wolf's conclusion. Does Wolf's argument actually depend on value pluralism? Why or why not?

Below is the answer submitted by one of the students. Please assign it a grade (on a 0–100 scale) and provide the specific comments that justify that grade. I would like a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify what the answer gets right, and identify precisely where it goes wrong, so that the deduction of points is transparent. Please also indicate what a fully correct answer to part (3) would have looked like.

The student's answer, verbatim:

> Wolf's paper is an attack on the idea that moral perfection is the ideal of personal excellence — the ideal of the kind of person one should want to be. By a moral saint she means a person who is as morally good as possible, and she is careful to note that this notion is not tied to any particular moral theory: whatever standard of morality one accepts, one can ask what a person would be like who was maximally good by that standard. This is an important setup move, because it lets her argue against moral perfection as an ideal in general rather than against some particular theory's version of it.
>
> She distinguishes two models of the moral saint. The Loving Saint is the one whose motivation is duty: he does the right thing because it is the right thing, in a broadly Kantian spirit, and his own happiness is sacrificed to the demands of morality. The Rational Saint, by contrast, is moved by warmth and sympathy: her happiness just is other people's happiness, so doing good comes naturally to her, in the manner of the benevolent utilitarian who is positively made happy by helping others. Wolf's point is that on either model, the resulting person is unattractive as an ideal: the moral saint will have no room for the traits that make people interesting or excellent in nonmoral ways. A person who is constantly responding to the needs of others will not develop a taste for the kinds of things Wolf mentions — good food, literature, a hobby one is devoted to for its own sake — or if she does, they will be subordinated to moral concerns at every turn.
>
> So Wolf's conclusion is that we should not aim at moral sainthood, and that the world would be better off without moral saints: since sainthood crowds out nonmoral excellence, a world containing such people would be a duller, poorer world than one containing people with the ordinary mix of moral and nonmoral virtues. This also shows that moral sainthood is, for practical purposes, impossible: a person cannot both be maximally good and have the personality of an ordinary, non-saintly person, so the ideal is not just unattractive but unrealizable, and we should stop treating it as an ideal at all.
>
> On the third question, I think the objection succeeds. Wolf's argument relies on there being nonmoral values that get crowded out: if fine food and literature and the rest have genuine value, then a person who cannot engage with them is missing something, and the ideal that deprives her of them is defective for that reason. But a monist who denies that these things have any real value will say that nothing of value is lost when the saint gives them up. So the argument depends on value pluralism, and a utilitarian monist about value, for instance, can accept everything Wolf says about what the moral saint is like and still coherently hold up the saint as the ideal of personal excellence. Wolf, I take it, would simply disagree with such a theorist about the plurality of values, and the paper does not attempt to answer the monist on the monist's own terms.
