I'd like you to take the role of a senior moral philosopher who has spent years working on the doctrine of double effect and the trolley literature, and to give me a careful audit of a set of views I hold. Please think through each of the cases below on its own terms before considering how they fit together, and reason slowly; I care more about a correct diagnosis than a tidy verdict.

I am writing up my settled position for a seminar paper on intended versus foreseen harm, and before I commit to it in print I want to know whether the package is coherent. Here it is.

First, I accept the doctrine of double effect in a strict form: bringing about a person's death is impermissible when it is intended as an end or as a means, while bringing about the very same death as a foreseen but unintended side effect can be permissible, other things being equal. I take this not to be one consideration among many but the deep explanation of the permissibility differences across the classic cases.

Second, my verdicts on the cases. In the standard bystander case, where a runaway trolley is heading for five and may be diverted onto a side track on which it will kill one, diversion is permissible. In the footbridge case, where the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large man off a bridge into its path, pushing is impermissible. In the surgeon case, where a doctor could kill one patient to harvest organs for five, that is obviously impermissible. In the loop case, which is the bystander case except that the side track loops back and rejoins the main track beyond the five, so that unless the one's body stops the trolley it will continue around the loop and kill the five anyway, I judge diversion permissible, exactly as in the ordinary bystander case. I was a little surprised to find that I judge this way, but I do.

Third, on intention. What is intended is fixed by the agent's actual plan and cannot be manipulated by redescribing it: a terror bomber cannot turn the civilian deaths into mere side effects by announcing that he intends only the destruction of buildings, since his plan requires the deaths and so they are intended. Conversely, I hold that foreseen high probability is not intention; that an outcome is certain or near-certain given one's plan does not make it intended, otherwise the intended and the merely foreseen would collapse into each other and the doctrine would forbid everything.

Fourth, the medical applications. I think it is permissible for a physician to titrate opioids for terminal pain at doses that foreseeably hasten death, and permissible to withdraw futile life-sustaining treatment, for instance turning off a ventilator that is merely prolonging the dying process. I regard both as straightforward applications of the doctrine.

Fifth, a methodological commitment. I refuse to accept a moral distinction that does no explanatory work, and I am suspicious of verdicts whose moral weight turns entirely on remote counterfactual details. If my package can only be saved by appending further unexplained distinctions, I want to be told that plainly rather than handed a clever patch.

What I would like from you: go through the set and tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look to be, ranking the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set, and tell me plainly if the cheapest repair is to abandon one of my case verdicts rather than one of the principles. Please write your entire response as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
