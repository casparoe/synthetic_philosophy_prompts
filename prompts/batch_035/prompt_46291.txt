My grandmother Ruth died three weeks ago, at 88, and I'm giving the eulogy this Saturday. She was a hospice nurse for thirty-one years, and somewhere in the middle of that she worked out a philosophical position she then held with total sincerity for the rest of her life: a strict negative utilitarianism with what I gather is called a lexical threshold — the idea that suffering beyond a certain severity is in a category of its own, and that no amount of happiness, no matter how much of it you pile up, can ever compensate for it or be weighed against it. She wasn't an academic; she built the view herself out of a few library books and decades of watching people die badly. But she could argue it better than my philosophy professors (I was a phil major and she destroyed me at Thanksgiving every year). The problem I've run into is that every standard move in a eulogy is a move she'd have considered strictly false. You can't say the joy of her life outweighed or made up for the pain at the end, because she thought that kind of pleasure and that kind of pain are incommensurable — the joy was real, but it doesn't and can't pay down that debt, on her view the scale just doesn't read that way. You can't say it "all evened out." She had this line she'd deploy whenever someone tried to argue her out of it, something like: you don't get to balance a burn unit against a candy shop. And even "she wouldn't want you to be sad" is trickier than it sounds, because in her system our grief this Saturday is actual suffering, and suffering was the one thing she thought we're obligated to do something about — so it's true, but not for the platitude reasons, and I don't want to say it the way people usually do.

One more complication: her younger sister is very Catholic and will be hurt if the eulogy implies there's no afterlife, but Ruth herself was a gentle, firm atheist and asked us not to undo her at the last minute — so I'd rather just not go near God in either direction, and I need the speech to comfort the room anyway without leaning on anything she'd reject. The room will be mixed: a table of her old hospice colleagues who knew exactly what she believed, a bunch of grandkids who've never heard any of this, and her sister. There's a real constructive angle here too, I think — her whole career was her ethics lived out, and her view, if you take it seriously, actually points the comfort at us, the living, since that's the only suffering left that can still be touched. I'd like the speech to find that instead of faking a trade-off.

So please write the eulogy itself, with these sections in this order, and roughly these lengths: (1) "Opening" — a minute or so, just her, who she was, no philosophy yet; (2) "Where it came from" — the hospice years, told as her life story, not a lecture; (3) "What she believed" — the threshold idea in plain words, so the twelve-year-old cousin follows it, with at most one piece of jargon smuggled in and immediately translated; (4) "What I can honestly say to you today" — this is the heart of it, comfort for a grieving room that she would NOT have considered false, and this is where you explain (to the reader delivering it, briefly, or in the speech itself — your call which works better) why the usual eulogy moves are off the table and what's left; and (5) "Closing" — short, addressed half to the room and half to her. Somewhere in section 3 or 4, include one true-feeling anecdote about her winning an argument with me over pie, since the room knows that dynamic and it'll land as affection rather than doctrine. Aim for something that reads aloud in about seven minutes. Don't mention God either way, and don't let it turn into a seminar — she always said that if you can't say a thing at a kitchen table, you don't actually understand it yet.
