I teach modern European history at a big public high school, and every unit ends with a "why did X happen" essay where I get ninety-odd answers arguing things like "the printing press caused the Reformation" or "the Depression caused the rise of the Nazis." When I grade these I find myself making the same calls over and over by gut: is a trigger a cause? Does it count if the student names something that was necessary but obviously not enough on its own (Luther without printing, printing without Luther)? What about kids who argue some version of "someone else would've done it anyway" -- which I think is the overdetermination thing from philosophy of causation, or maybe it's the preempting thing, honestly I get those confused. I've read a bit about this stuff (INUS conditions, but-for tests, necessary vs sufficient) but never enough to turn it into anything I actually use on a Sunday night with a stack of essays.

What I want is a grading procedure I can run on a single causal claim in about three minutes, on paper, no philosophy training required -- me and the other teacher on the course both have to be able to apply it the same way, since we split the sections and the students compare grades. So it has to say: what questions to ask about a claim in order, what counts as an adequate answer at each step, at what point a claim gets full credit even if it's framed in naive language ("Princip shot the archduke so the war started" -- a 16-year-old isn't going to say 'but-for condition'), and where the procedure has to give up and just flag it as my judgment call, because I suspect a lot of causal-historiography grading is values dressed up as rubric. It should also tell me what to do with the "it would've happened anyway" move, both when the student uses it to knock down someone else's cause and when they use it to excuse one (the south would've seceded eventually, so was Lincoln's election really the cause?).

Then run it on three real claims from last year's stack: "the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused WWI," "the invention of the printing press caused the Reformation," and "the humiliation at Versailles caused WWII." Include at least one case where the procedure gives full or near-full credit to an answer I instinctively want to mark down (I'm pretty sure Princip as cause is one of these), and one where it marks down an answer I like -- and be honest about where the three-minute limit makes the procedure dumber than the philosophy it's based on.
