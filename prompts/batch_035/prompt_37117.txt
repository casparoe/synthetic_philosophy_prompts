so here's the setup for this one: you're a professional epistemologist who's done a lot of work on testimony, institutional trust, and fringe beliefs, and you've been asked to mediate something.

two friends of mine have been arguing for literally a year and a half and i want to commission the document they should have produced instead of all the shouting. one of them is what you'd call a generalist about conspiracy theories — he thinks there's something epistemically defective about the category as a whole, that the typical conspiracy theorist reasons badly in a characteristic way, and that this is true even before you look at any particular theory's evidence. the other is a particularist — she thinks that's just prejudice dressed up as epistemology, that every conspiracy theory is just a claim about the world like any other, and that treating them as a class with a special pathology is exactly the kind of thinking that lets institutions off the hook (her go-to move is pointing out that watergate was real, tobacco companies really did conspire to bury the science, and so on — actual conspiracies exist, so why would "this posits a conspiracy" be evidence of anything?).

i want you to draft the joint statement they could both actually sign after a proper adversarial collaboration. before you write it, do a bit of brainstorming first — think through where each side genuinely has a point and where they're just talking past each other, because i suspect some of their fight is purely verbal (they've never once agreed on what counts as a "conspiracy theory" vs just "a theory about a conspiracy," and i want you to figure out if that distinction does any work or is just noise).

then the document itself, with these sections:

1. agreed claims — what both can sign about how people acquire beliefs about hidden events, about the track record of institutions vs the track record of theorizers, about base rates of actual conspiracies, about what makes any belief epistemically good or bad. be careful here, i don't want platitudes like "evidence matters" — these two actually disagree about surprisingly much, so the agreements need to be real.

2. disagreements — the exact points where they part ways, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. so the generalist's position has to be written such that my generalist friend would say "yes, that's my view, fairly put," and same for her. no strawmen, no hedging into mush.

3. cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one of them. i have guesses about what these are: whether institutional sources get some default entitlement, or have to earn it; whether the *style* of reasoning behind a belief can count against it independent of the evidence; whether there's a meaningful asymmetry between doubting an official story and asserting an alternative. but you might find better ones, trust your judgment.

4. for each crux, a concrete case or scenario both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. so like, if the crux is institutional default trust, the test case might be a newly declassified document revealing a decades-old program, and you'd write what the generalist predicts it should teach us vs the particularist. make the cases specific enough to actually generate divergent predictions — if both sides would predict the same thing, it's a bad case.

one more thing: if you conclude that part of their dispute is just verbal (say, the definitional thing), say which part and why, and put it in a separate little section at the end, because they'll want to know what to stop fighting about.

take your time with this and get the phrasing right — the whole value of the document is that both of them would sign it. a statement only one of them accepts is worthless to me. length: as long as it needs to be, but don't pad the agreed-claims section to make them look closer than they are. honest emptiness is fine.
