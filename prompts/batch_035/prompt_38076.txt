You're a professional philosopher working on collective memory and the ethics of commemoration — the literaure around Avishai Margalit's The Ethics of Memory and David Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting is home turf for you — so come at this as a specialist, not a curious generalist. Take a moment to brainstorm before committing to a verdict; I want to see how you get there, not just where you land.

Context: I teach a course on historical responsibility, and every year the same analogy shows up in seminar, in op-eds, in truth-commission debates, basically everywhere: a nation's relationship to its historical crimes is like a trauma patient's relationship to their trauma. The duty-to-remember side uses the therapy frame one way — a patient who represses doesn't heal, so a nation that buries its history is sick, and testimony, memorials, anniversaries are the therapy; you have to walk through it to get past it. The move-on side uses the very same frame the opposite way — the whole point of therapy is that the patient stops being ruled by the memory, and a healed patient doesn't rehearse their grief on a schedule every year, so a healthy nation eventually sets the memory down, and commemoration in perpetuity is just pathological rumination. One analogy, two opposite conclusions, which allready makes me suspect neither side has looked at the analogy itself very hard.

So audit it, the way you'd audit any analogy that's doing real argumentative work. First the correspondence table: what plays the role of the patient, the psyche, repression, the symptom, the therapist, the cure? Then where it breaks. Some breaks I can half-see myself — a society has no intrapsychic anything, the patient outlives every actual victim and perpetrator, descendants inherit a memory that was never theirs to repress, and public commemmoration is a performance for an audience rather than a private defense mechanism — but tell me which of the breaks actually matter, and whether they cut harder against one side's use of the analogy or the other's. Also flag what the trauma frame smuggles in that a drier framing wouldn't: that there's a single patient with a single interest, that there's a fact of the matter about what healing looks like, that the past is over rather than ongoing. Then the big question: once the disanalogies are on the table, do the duty to remember and the case for forgetting each survive on their own merits, or was the therapy analogy carrying them the whole time? If some weaker version of the analogy is salvageable, say exactly what it licenses and no more. And if there's a better analogy waiting in the wings — I keep half-thinking about debts rather than trauma, something about who owes whom and whether debts die with the creditors — sketch it and tell me whether it does better work or just breaks differently.

Be concrete where you can — truth commissions, national anniversaries, the way some countries teach the bad chapters and others don't — and don't be afraid to conclude that neither side gets what they want from this. I'd rather have an honest audit than support for my priors, and I genuinely don't have a settled view on which side the analogy fails first.
