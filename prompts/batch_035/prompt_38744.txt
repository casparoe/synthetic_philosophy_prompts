I'm teaching a political philosophy seminar on the ethics of office, and for the session on the problem of dirty hands I want to pair Michael Walzer's 1973 essay "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" with Spielberg's film *Lincoln* (2012), which covers the final push to pass the Thirteenth Amendment in January 1865. Please write me a reading of the film through the dirty-hands problem that I can use as the backbone for the discussion.

The relevant structure of Walzer's problem, as I understand it: a political leader confronts circumstances in which doing the right thing overall seems to require performing a wrong act, so that either she does wrong and succeeds, or keeps her hands clean and lets a catastrophe or great evil proceed; Walzer's distinctive move is to argue that even when the wrong act is justified, the agent ought to feel guilt, and that we should distrust the politician whose hands get dirty without visible cost. The film gives us at least three bodies of material to test against this: the vote-buying operation that Seward's men run targeting lame-duck Democrats, mixing patronage jobs, cash, and outright bribery; Lincoln's cultivation of plausible deniability about that operation, not asking for details he would then have to act on; and the decision to keep the Confederate peace commissioners waiting on a boat downriver so that the prospect of an imminent end to the war cannot drain away the votes for the amendment, while Congress is left uninformed. There is also Thaddeus Stevens' smaller but pointed dilemma about strategic concealment — his willingness to soften what he will say on the House floor about equality so as not to scare off votes he needs.

The questions I want the reading to take seriously: Is any of this a genuinely dirty-hands case in Walzer's sense, or is it mostly ordinary legislative bargaining that a healthy democracy runs on and needs no moral anguish about? Walzer and his critics disagree about whether the category is even coherent — whether "justified wrongdoing" makes sense, and whether guilt is a fitting response to an act that was, all things considered, the right thing to do — so please tell me which parts of the dirty-hands framework are settled terrain and which are live disputes, and don't present one side of the critics' debate as the consensus view. Relatedly, the film's Lincoln is notably calm and unconflicted in his resolve on most of this; does that serenity read as a failure to dramatize the guilt Walzer demands, as a refutation of the demand itself, or as the film's implicit claim that this category of transaction simply isn't the dirty-hands case at all — more like necessity than like dirtying? Does the concealment from Congress have a different moral structure from the bribery, and if so, does the film track the difference?

A further complication I'd like handled honestly: the film's claim of necessity — that the amendment had to be rammed through before the war's end or would be lost forever — is itself a contested reading of the historical situation, and the vivid scenes of the lobbying operation are partly the screenwriter's invention. Please flag where the film is dramatizing a contested historical interpretation rather than settled fact, so I don't mislead my students. And finish by telling me what the film adds to the dirty-hands debate that the philosophical literature alone doesn't give us — for instance, what the dramatization of plausible deniability, of a wrong distributed across many agents so that no single actor holds it whole, shows that a clean statement of the problem cannot.
