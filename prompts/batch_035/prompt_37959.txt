Modern psychotherapy's most heavily researched modality, cognitive behavioral therapy, is organized around a causal hypothesis: that appraisals, beliefs, and interpretations are genuine points of intervention — that restructuring how a patient interprets her insomnia, his racing heart, a partner's silence will change affect and behavior downstream. Treatment protocols are written in the vocabulary of mental states, effectiveness trials measure changes in the content of thought, and the therapist's job is explicitly described as intervening on cognition. Yet the very same clinics, and the same treatment guidelines, routinely route the same complaints through molecules — SSRIs, benzodiazepines, stimulants — and treat combined treatment as the sum of two independent causal routes to a shared outcome. So the institution seems to be running two theories of change at once: one in which thoughts qua thoughts do causal work, and one in which tweaking neurochemistry does work that no conversation could.

Work out what view of mental causation this two-track practice presupposes, and whether the causal exclusion argument shows the presupposition to be incoherent. If the physical domain is causally closed and we forbid systematic overdetermination, then either mental properties are identical with, or reducible to, physical properties, in which case it is obscure what work an intervention described as "restructuring an appraisal" does that its neural realizer does not do anyway, or mental properties are causally idle, in which case CBT's theory of change is straightforwardly false and its clinical success needs some other explanation. Reconstruct the implicit theory carefully. Does the practice strictly require that mental states qua mental be causes — as opposed to being compatible with more deflationary positions, for instance a view on which causal claims are always relative to a choice of variables and grain of description, so that "the appraisal caused the relief" and "the neural realizer caused the relief" can both be true without overdetermination? Or a view on which the psychological level simply offers better-identified, more portable intervention variables even though all the causing happens lower down? Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of mental causation would equally license it, and where it actually needs commitments it may not be able to honor. Finally, tell me what would have to change in clinical practice — in how protocols are written, how mechanisms of change are described to patients, how combined talk-and-drug treatment is justified — if the field took the exclusion argument fully seriously and stopped presupposing that thoughts, as thoughts, move anything.
