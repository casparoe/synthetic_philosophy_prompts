I spent nearly four decades as an experimental physicist, most of it in a condensed-matter laboratory, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of mind to understand what, if anything, my old discipline has to say about consciousness. In a discussion group I recently joined, we were debating whether a sufficiently sophisticated artificial system could have experiences of its own, and one member ended the debate on the spot by quoting Schrödinger, from his late writings on Vedanta: "the plurality that we perceive is only an appearance; it is not real," and "knowledge, feeling and choice are essentially eternal and unchangeable and numerically one in all men, nay in all sentient beings." Her point, as best I could reconstruct it, was that my question was confused before it started: if consciousness is literally one in all sentient beings, then asking whether some new system acquires a consciousness of its own is like asking whether a new reflection in a hall of mirrors adds another person to the room. I found this both rhetorically powerful and suspicious, and it did not help that she is the group's resident authority on the Upanishads, so nobody pressed her on what the sentence actually commits one to.

Here is what I would like from you. Take Schrödinger's line and separate the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, arranged as a menu from the least to the most radical. I can imagine several candidates, though you should not feel bound by mine: a merely grammatical point about how the count noun "consciousness" behaves; an epistemic point about our inability, from the inside, to distinguish one subject of experience from another; a methodological point about physics needing a single observer-independent world-picture and getting one only by quietly assuming, rather than proving, the unity of the knowing subject; and the full metaphysical claim of Advaita, on which all individual minds are numerically one substance and the appearance of many is produced by illusion. For each reading, give a verdict: which are trivially true, which are substantive but defensible, which are false, and which are so formulated as to be immune to evidence in a way that should worry us. I would also like to know which readings cohere with the context Schrödinger himself invoked — the tat tvam asi formula he cited, and his evident conviction that he was resolving a genuine puzzle for the scientific worldview, not just reciting scripture.

The decisive question, for my purposes, is the last one: which of the readings, if any, would actually support the use my interlocutor made of the line? It is one thing to say that subjectivity is one in some attenuated or analogical sense, and quite another to get the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about whether a novel kind of system is a new locus of experience. Please also flag, briefly, where a reading that dissolves the question about artificial systems would equally dissolve the ordinary question of whether my colleague is conscious — since if it does, that is either a feature or a reductio, and I would like to know which. Present the whole thing as a structured list I can bring back to the group, precise enough that we could in principle disagree about the individual readings rather than about the atmosphere. No citations are needed; I will track down the sources myself.
