Exam question:

(a) State Berlin's conceptions of negative and positive liberty, one sentence each. (b) Explain how negative liberty and Pettit's freedom as non-domination deliver different verdicts on the case of a slave whose master never interferes in her life, and say which verdict each theory gives and why. (c) Is freedom as non-domination best understood as a version of positive liberty, as a version of negative liberty, or as a third conception? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

(a) Negative liberty is the absence of interference by others: I am free to the extent that no one actually prevents me from doing what I want to do. Positive liberty, by contrast, is self-mastery: I am free when I am governed by my own higher, rational self rather than by appetite, impulse, or other people. Berlin drew this distinction in his 1958 lecture Two Concepts of Liberty.

(b) The slave case shows where the two theories we have studied come apart, and it also shows where the republican theory earns its keep. Under negative liberty, a slave whose master is kindly and never interferes is roughly as free as anyone else: there is no actual interference to point to, and unfreedom on this view is a matter of what is done to you, not of what could be done. Republican liberty, as Pettit develops it in Republicanism, is freedom as non-domination: you are free when no one has a standing capacity to interfere in your affairs on an arbitrary basis, with impunity. The master has exactly that capacity; he leaves her alone only as long as it suits him. So the slave is unfree on the republican view even though no one ever lifts a finger against her — which is the point of the example: one can be unfree without anyone doing anything to you at all.

(c) I think republican liberty is really a version of positive liberty, for two reasons. First, both conceptions locate freedom in who is in control rather than in what is obstructed: positive liberty asks whether the self rules itself, and non-domination asks whether the agent is secured against others. It is the same idea at different scales. Second, Pettit's theory clearly carries a positive-liberty commitment to political participation: on his view a citizen is not fully free unless she is actively engaged in collective self-government, which is the ancient conception of liberty as the sharing of public authority. Pettit effectively concedes this when he describes his conception of freedom as going beyond mere non-interference.

Finally, note that the state itself is an agent with an unchecked capacity to interfere in anyone's affairs. It follows that every citizen living under any state is dominated, so republican liberty cannot be realized under a state at all; at best a state can reduce the degree of domination people suffer. Consistently applied, Pettit's theory is therefore a case for anarchism — a conclusion Pettit, understandably, declines to draw.
