I am a novelist, and my current book is set largely inside a 1960s psychiatric institution; before I write any further chapters I have been rereading Ken Kesey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest as my principal model, with a philosopher friend's copy of Ian Hacking's essays on the side. Hacking's thought, as I understand it, is that classifications of people are unlike classifications of quasars: a human kind is interactive, so that once the human sciences sort some population under a label, the labeled people come to know themselves through the label, act accordingly, and thereby change what the label actually names — the category and its members settling into a shape neither would have taken alone, in a loop that can run for generations. My friend swears the novel is practically a case study in this, and I have a half-formed suspicion that she is right in a way that will damage my book if I don't understand it properly, since I am about to invent a fictional ward full of diagnoses and don't know whether I am describing an institution or participating in the loop myself.

So I would like a reading of the novel through Hacking, one that is honest in both directions. Which scenes genuinely dramatize the loop — I think here of the ward's sorting of the men into Acutes and Chronics, a taxonomy the men gradually treat as a fact about their own bodies rather than an administrative filing decision, and of the Chief, whom everyone takes for a deaf mute and who therefore becomes one for all practical purposes — and which scenes only seem to? I am especially interested in the moment McMurphy learns that he, unlike the men on voluntary status, is committed, so that his release depends on the very authority he has been performing for; but also in how the staff's diagnosis of him as a psychopath, which they use to explain away his conduct, begins to organize the conduct it explains. And where does the novel resist the reading — because a great deal of the book seems to be about naked institutional power rather than about classificatory feedback, and I would want a critic willing to say that the Hacking lens distorts Kesey into a story about labels when it is really a story about force.

Finally, two things I care about as a working novelist. First: does the book know what it is doing, or does the fit come only from my imposing a philosopher who wrote decades after it? Second: what does the novel give me that Hacking's essays don't — some felt sense of what it is like to live inside the loop, narrated by a man the system has classified out of personhood? Please attend to the Chief's narration in particular, since my friend claims he is the loop's most interesting specimen, and tell me plainly if that claim overreaches. The film is well known, but I mean the novel throughout.
