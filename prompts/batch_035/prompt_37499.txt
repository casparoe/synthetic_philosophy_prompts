I've been around rationalist blogs and forums long enough that "if your credences aren't coherent, someone can Dutch book you" is basically muscle memory for me at this point, and honestly I still think the synchronic version is fine. But the diachronic version — the one that's supposed to force conditionalization on you as a rule for updating — has been bugging me more and more, and I want someone to make the case against it properly. Argue that the diachronic Dutch book argument fails to establish conditionalization as a rational requirement.

The specific thing that bugs me: to run the book, the bookie has to trade with you at t0 and then come back at t1 and trade again, with the second round chosen to fit whatever your posterior turned out to be. So the "sure loss" is only sure relative to an adversary who gets to observe your future prices before deciding what to sell you. You, standing at t0, cannot foresee the loss — which is weird if the argument is supposed to generate a norm that binds you *at* t0. And if foreseeability of loss doesn't actually matter, it's not clear why the same style of argument wouldn't also "prove" that you ought to precommit to never revising your credences at all, or to some updateless policy, which feels like proving too much. Maybe commitment to a policy is the real lesson and conditionalization is a red herring — I'm honestly not sure.

I want you to take on the strong replies, not the easy ones: the bookie adapting online rather than needing to predict you in advance, the standard convention that you'd accept each bet individually at your stated fair price, and the attempts to rerun the whole thing in terms of scoring rules / accuracy instead of betting losses. Also please tell me plainly what survives the attack — I'm not trying to torch coherence theorems, I just want to know exactly how much they buy and where the extra premises get smuggled in. Bonus points if you can say whether the same foreseability issue secretly infects the synchronic argument too — I have a suspicion that "willing to take either side of any bet at your fair price" is quietly doing more work than people admit, and that a bounded agent with any sense of stake-sizing and transaction costs was never really in the bookie's target demographic.
