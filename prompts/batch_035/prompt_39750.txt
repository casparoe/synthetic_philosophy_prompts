You are a professional philosopher of religion, an expert on divine knowledge and providence, and you have been retained as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration between two theologians who have been arguing past each other for over a decade. The first is a Molinist: he holds that God possesses middle knowledge — knowledge, logically prior to any decree of creation, of all true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom ("if placed in circumstances C, agent A would freely choose X"). The second is an open theist: she holds that there are no determinate future-tensed truths about free creaturely decisions until those decisions are made, that God's omniscience is knowledge of everything knowable rather than of everything true, and that God relates to an open future dynamically. Both are classical theists, both affirm libertarian freedom in creaturely choices, both reject theological determinism, and both insist that God's providence is real and governance of history genuine. Yet each thinks the other's account sacrifices something essential: the Molinist charges the open theist with making God a risk-taker who cannot guarantee any outcome of free choices; the open theist presses the grounding objection — what could possibly make a counterfactual of freedom true when the choice is neither actual nor necessitated? — and worries that middle knowledge makes God's knowledge depend on something outside God.

Draft the joint statement the two of them could sign after a good-faith collaborative effort. Think carefully before writing: begin with some private brainstorming about where the genuine cruxes lie, which disagreements are substantive and which are merely verbal (for instance, whether the dispute over the definition of "omniscience" is a real disagreement or a word-choice), and which formulations each side would refuse to sign as slanted. Then produce the document, structured as follows:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can accept — concerning God's exhaustive knowledge of the actual course of history, creaturely freedom, what providential governance does and does not require God to control, and their shared rejection of simple-foreknowledge and deterministic alternatives.
2. A disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement, containing: (a) the exact statement of the disagreement, worded so that each side would accept the phrasing of its own view; (b) the crux it turns on; and (c) where possible, a case, analogy, or argument both sides agree would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. At least one row should concern an indeterministic, chancy process — say, a quantum coin — and whether counterfactuals about how it would land, were it flipped, have determinate truth values; another row should concern whether God's actualization decision based on counterfactuals of freedom compromises divine independence.
3. A short concluding section in which each party states, in a single paragraph, what would lead them to abandon their position.

The drafting is the test: agreed claims must be neither vacuous nor slanted, and if either party could not sign a sentence, it does not belong in the agreed section. Where part of the dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. Do not adjudicate between the two views.
