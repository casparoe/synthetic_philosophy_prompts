you're a poet with a serious philosophy background -- like, someone who's taught seminars on the later Wittgenstein and also publishes actual verse. i want a poem about the private diarist from the Philosophical Investigations: the guy who decides to keep a diary of some recurring private sensation, and just writes "S" in it each time the sensation shows up.

the constraint i care about: the token "S" has to appear in every stanza, and the form should do the argumentative work. early stanzas, the diarist is confident there's a fact of the matter about whether tomorrow's "S" is a correct entry -- he's tracking something, even if only he could ever check. middle of the poem, the criterion for correctness should start thinning out, and by the last stanza the reader should genuinely not be able to tell whether the final "S" still refers to anything or is just a mark he keeps producing, like a groove worn into a path by walking it. the ending has to stay open on wether his practice has collapsed or is merely very impoverished -- dont resolve that for me, the unresolvedness is the whole point.

20-28 lines, no fixed form, but the repetitions need to feel deliberate, not accidental. someone who knows the private language argument should feel it coming, the moment where seeming-right and being-right come apart (you know the bit -- at some point there's nothing left to distinguish a correct application of "S" from one that merely seems correct, and then the right/wrong distinction has quietly slipped away), and someone who doesnt know any philosophy should still get a decent poem about a person alone with a notebook. please dont quote wittgenstein or name him inside the poem itself. brainstorm where the turns should fall before you write anything, and if your first idea for the ending is "the S's fade to blankness", throw it out -- too cheap. i want the last stanza to keep some pressure on the reader.
