I'm a high school junior writing my extended essay on what makes pain bad, and my partner for the project has produced an argument that I cannot make go away. The topic is pain asymbolia -- the strange condition, caused by damage in and around the insula and cingulate cortex, in which patients say they feel pain as clearly as ever but are not bothered by it: they can identify a pinprick as painful, rate exactly how intense it is, and then giggle while it is still happening. Here is the argument.

1. Either the badness of a pain is located in the sensation itself -- in the raw feel of it, its intensity, its burning or aching quality -- or it is located in the aversive reaction the sensation provokes: the hating it, the wanting it gone.
2. In pain asymbolia the sensation is preserved while the aversion is gone; the patients can still tell you precisely how strong the pain is.
3. If the patients' pains are not bad for them, then the badness was never in the sensation, because the sensation is all still there.
4. The patients' pains are not bad for them, because nothing about pain bothers them; they are in roughly the state of a person who has an itch and has simply decided not to scratch.
5. So the badness of pain resides in the aversive reaction.
6. But aversive reactions can be selectively stripped away while the sensation stays put -- this is what asymbolia does, what some lobotomy patients seemed to show, what soldiers with terrible wounds have reported, and what morphine sometimes achieves: the pain is still felt, it just no longer matters.
7. So nothing that remains once the aversion is gone is bad in itself; the felt quality of pain -- the burning, the aching -- is not intrinsically bad at all.
8. So a person drugged in advance to strip out all aversion, then tortured for a week, suffers nothing bad, provided the drug works perfectly.

My partner insists that every step is a truism and that the conclusion should simply be swallowed: if a sensation isn't disliked, it isn't bad, and torture with the sting removed is closer to a strange light show than to suffering. My problem is step (4), which seems to assume that not minding a pain and not being harmed by it are the same thing. But perhaps I am only clinging to that because step (8) horrifies me -- and my partner points out that if I go the other way and say the sensation itself is bad, I am stuck saying that the asymbolics' pains are bad even though the patients themselves are perfectly fine with them, which would seem to justify forcing pain relief on people who do not want it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the right move is to attack step (1)'s assumption that the sensation and the aversion are two separable things in the first place, explain how that can be squared with the fact that asymbolics plainly still feel the pain; if the right move is somewhere else, tell me where, and tell me what I have to pay for it.
