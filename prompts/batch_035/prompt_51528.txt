I'm a third-year PhD student in bioarchaeology, and the argument in my department about the agricultural transition has, over the decades it's been running, reached a state I can only call entrenched. On one side sit the declinists: they point to the skeletal record of early farming populations — declining stature, rising frequencies of enamel hypoplasia and cribra orbitalia as markers of childhood stress, the crowding and zoonotic disease that come with settlement — and to Sahlins's case in The Original Affluent Society that foragers satisfied limited wants with limited effort. On the other side sit the progressivists, who respond that the skeletal indicators are confounded by what the osteological paradox taught us — selective mortality and hidden heterogeneity in frailty mean you cannot read population health off lesion frequencies; that Lee's famous work-hour figures for the !Kung were revised substantially upward by subsequent Hadza fieldwork; that recent syntheses of degenerative joint disease find osteoarthritis did not rise uniformly with farming; and that comparative energetics work among living foragers suggests total daily energy expenditure is not so different from that of sedentary Western office workers, undercutting the image of metabolically harsher lives. Every new isotopic or paleopathological dataset that comes out gets claimed by both camps within a year, and no one I know has changed their mind since I arrived.

I've developed a working hypothesis I'd like you to test rather than merely endorse. It is that the disagreement is not primarily about the evidence, because both camps largely accept the same findings — early farming populations do show more skeletal stress markers; foragers did (and do) face different and sometimes severe trade-offs. Instead, I suspect the real work is being done by three deeper, mostly unacknowledged commitments. First, a covert disagreement about the currency of wellbeing being aggregated: work hours versus famine variance versus morbidity versus autonomy, with each side privileging the dimension on which its favored population does better. Second, an aggregation question that nobody states explicitly: whether the claim is about average welfare (which may well have fallen for some millennia) or total welfare (which rose enormously as populations grew), and whether the person-affecting structure of the comparison is even coherent, given that most of the farmers whose welfare we're assessing would not have existed in the counterfactual hunter-gatherer world. Third, a definitional slippage in the notion of affluence itself — Sahlins's affluence was preference-indexed (wanting little), while the declinists' measures are objective and comparative, so the two camps may be answering different questions under a single heading.

So here is what I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, in the sense that once the terms are disambiguated the two sides' claims are compatible? Is it genuinely empirical but epistemically underdetermined, such that the osteological record cannot in principle decide it? Is it a difference about standards of evidence, where each side treats the other's favorite indicators as methodologically discredited while exempting its own from the same critique? Or is it bedrock intuition about naturalness and civilization that neither side can argue for directly? Support the diagnosis with what each camp actually says, not a survey of the field. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a measurement technique, a conceptual distinction between the average-welfare and total-welfare readings, an explicit statement of the counterfactual being evaluated, or a reframing of the question as being about variance and risk rather than central tendency — or else argue that no progress should be expected here and explain why the impasse is structural rather than contingent.
