I'm a novelist three-quarters of the way through a book set during a nurses' strike at a regional hospital, and I've gotten stuck on something that turns out to matter for the ending. The plot hinges on whether the strike works, and I can't decide that until I know what a strike actually is, because my two main characters explain it to each other in ways I can't referee. The hospital CFO's son, a business-school type, keeps telling anyone who'll listen that the union is just a cartel — OPEC for nurses. It restricts the supply of labor to push the wage above what the market would pay, and since we'd never let the hospitals band together to push wages down, the only reason unions get away with it is that the law plays favorites. The organizer fires back that a single hospital in a small city isn't a market at all — it's the only buyer of nursing labor for fourty miles, so the union is just un-rigging a price that was already rigged. Neither of them is stupid and I refuse to make either one a strawmean.

So I need to know how far the cartel thing actually holds, because whichever character it favors is the one my plot is quietly agreeing with. Where does the mapping work — what plays the role of OPEC's quota, of enforcement against defectors (is that what picket lines are?), of the consumer who pays the higher price? Where does it break? And do the breaks actually matter for the conclusions each character is drawing from it — the CFO's kid implying right-to-work laws are just antitrust applied fairly, the organizer claiming the strike is self-defense rather than extortion? I also vaguely remember there's a term in economics for a market with only one buyer, and if it's relevant I want to get it right in the book.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no intro and no wrap-up: (1) the correspondence mapping between a union-as-cartel and an actual cartel; (2) where the analogy breaks in a way that matters for the argument the CFO's son is using it for; (3) where it breaks in a way that doesn't matter for that argument, even though it might matter for other things; (4) what the single-buyer point, if it checks out, does to his argument; and (5) which character, if either, is left standing once all that's on the table.
