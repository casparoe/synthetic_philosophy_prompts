A business-psychology column I clipped explains the Ellsberg paradox like this: "Ellsberg asked people to choose between two urns. One held fifty red and fifty black balls; the other held a hundred balls, red and black in proportions nobody knew. Nearly everyone preferred betting on red from the first urn rather than from the second, and equally preferred black from the first rather than from the second. Ellsberg pointed out that these two preferences contradict each other outright: the first says the unknown urn holds fewer than fifty red balls, the second that it holds fewer than fifty black. Since expected utility theory holds that rational preferences cannot contradict one another, Ellsberg proved that ordinary people are irrational, and the reason is plain risk aversion — the unknown urn is simply the riskier bet. The moral is that a rational agent should treat the two urns as identical, since by symmetry each color is exactly as likely to come up in one urn as in the other."

I have no way of checking this against the literature myself. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a decision theorist would accept as stated, which they would want rephrased or qualified, and which are simply wrong. Three moves worry me in particular, and I would like each addressed directly. First, the column says the two preferences "contradict each other" — is that right as stated, or does the contradiction only emerge once some further assumption is added, and if so, whose assumption is it? Second, the column explains the behavior by risk aversion and calls the unknown urn "the riskier bet" — is that an explanation of the finding, a restatement of it in different words, or a misuse of a technical term in its everyday sense? Third, the closing moral: does the correction of the summary actually oblige a rational agent to be indifferent between the urns, or does that conclusion depend on a further substantive assumption that the column has quietly inserted? Please close by telling me, in a few sentences, what the column should have said instead.
