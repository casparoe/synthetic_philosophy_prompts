I am a novelist, and my current book is set in Vienna and Sarajevo during the five weeks between the assassination at Sarajevo at the end of June 1914 and the outbreak of the First World War at the beginning of August. I have drafted the early chapters twice: once with an atmosphere of gathering doom, in which every waltz and every state banquet is quietly larded with signs of what the reader knows is coming, and once with the events rendered as genuinely open, the participants preoccupied with other matters and the crisis seeming, from within, to be one more Balkan affair that would blow over as the previous ones had. I want to decide between these drafts on principled grounds rather than on instinct, and the decision turns on a question I cannot settle myself: how much of the apparent inevitability of the July Crisis is a fact about 1914, and how much is an artefact of our retrospective standpoint?

Please give me your credence — an actual number, not a review of the scholarly debate — that a counterfactual in which the Archduke's driver does not take the wrong turn on the Appel Quay, so that the assassination never occurs, would still have produced a general European war within, say, five years. But the number matters to me less than its decomposition, so please break your estimate into the sub-questions it rests upon and give each a rough probability with a line or two of justification: the probability that the alliance commitments and mobilisation timetables of the great powers made some such war highly probable regardless of any particular spark; the probability that our confident sense that the crisis escalated because it had to escalate is largely a product of hindsight bias, of the kind that makes outcomes feel overdetermined once known; the probability that narrative historical explanation of the sort I have been reading is structurally incapable of distinguishing the causes of the war from the mere background conditions, since any coherent story told backwards from a known ending will find its path smooth; and any further terms you think belong in the decomposition. Then combine them, tell me which term you are least confident in, and say what evidence or argument would move that term most.

Finally, connect it back to my craft decision, please: given your decomposition, is the doom-laden draft implicitly taking a philosophical stand — one you find defensible or not — on whether the narrative fallacy distorts historical explanation at its root, in the manner in which a novel told from a position of retrospective knowledge might be said to reproduce the very distortion I am worried about falling for?

I would be grateful if you could write your answer in British English.
