I'm a novelist — twenty years of close third person, a fair amount of free indirect style, and a working fluency in the narratologists' toolkit: Genette on focalization, Booth's implied author, the story/discourse distinction, and the looser workshop cousins of these terms that I use daily. I have no training in philosophy of mind, but I've read enough to gather that epiphenomenalism holds conscious experience to be caused by physical brain states while itself causing nothing — and that this is supposed to generate a paradox about phenomenal judgment: if my utterance "I am in pain" is produced entirely by physical causes, and the pain itself contributes nothing to those causes, it becomes obscure how the utterance manages to be *about* the pain. I want this whole position, and the paradox it breeds, restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as the translation allows, followed by a residue report.

Here is the sketch I'd like you to correct, refine, and where necessary reject. In narratological terms, epiphenomenalism seems to say: the stream of experience is narrated content; the physical world is the discourse that records it; and the recorded content never reaches back to shape the record. The paradox of phenomenal judgment then looks like a familiar feature of retrospective first-person narration: when the narrating "I" reports the younger self's anguish, that report is not, within the story world, caused by the anguish — it issues from the narrating instance, which stands outside the told time and answers to the author. Every confessional novel works this way, and nobody finds it paradoxical. So my instinct is that the translation is almost too smooth, and that its smoothness is suspicious.

Now the residue report, in detail. Which parts of the epiphenomenalist position map cleanly onto focalization, narrating instance, and story/discourse? Which parts have to be distorted to fit? And is there anything in the debate with no counterpart in narrative theory at all? In particular, I suspect my sketch smuggles in something epiphenomenalism cannot admit: an author standing outside the physical world, producing the narration from elsewhere. If the translation works only by supplying that external producer, then either the analogy is doing illicit work — making epiphenomenalism feel tractable by handing it a God it doesn't believe in — or the philosophers' paradox is genuinely unlike anything in my craft, and I'd like to know which. Please be blunt about where the residue lies: is it a defect of my framework, or a sign that the original position was less clear than it looks?
