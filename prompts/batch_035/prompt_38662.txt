Two philosophers have spent three years in an adversarial collaboration on the levelling-down objection. One is a committed telic egalitarian: she holds that equality is intrinsically good, so that inequality is a bad-making feature of an outcome even when it makes no one worse off in any other way, and that levelling down the better-off therefore makes an outcome better in one respect even when it benefits nobody. The other is a committed prioritarian: he holds that benefits to the worse-off carry greater moral weight than benefits to the better-off, that this weight is fixed by the beneficiary's absolute position and not by how anyone else is faring, and that the distribution of positions across persons has no intrinsic value at all. Each has privately concluded that the levelling-down objection — that egalitarianism implies a world where everyone is equally miserable is, qua equality, in one respect better than one where the worst-off are better off — is either decisive against his opponent or answerable, and each has failed to move the other.

Draft the joint statement they should now be able to sign. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover what both accept about the formal structure of levelling-down cases; what both concede to the other side (the prioritarian conceding, if he does, that his weighting function looks flatly arbitrary without some story about why the worst-off matter more; the egalitarian conceding what she in fact concedes about the burden the objection places on her); and anything both accept about which variants of the objection are weak.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement twice: once in wording the egalitarian would sign as a fair statement of her own view, and once in wording the prioritarian would sign of his. Neither formulation may be slanted, question-begging, or so hedged as to be vacuous.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the three or four sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, and say which. I expect at least one crux to concern the person-affecting constraint: whether a feature of an outcome can count as making it better if it is good for no one. Another likely crux is whether the difference between saying equality is "good in one respect" and saying it is "pro tanto good" does any work, or is a verbal maneuver. Include whatever other cruxes you think genuinely divide them, not just these.

4. A tests section. For each crux, describe at least one concrete case, argument, or line of evidence both would agree bears on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. You must include these two cases among them: (a) a two-person case in which the only available action lowers the better-off person slightly without raising the worse-off person at all, where no one ever learns what happened or how anyone else is faring — no envy, no status harm, no expectations defeated; and (b) a comparison between a prioritarian improvement (raising the worse-off at some cost to the better-off) and a levelling-down that leaves the worse-off at exactly the same absolute position. You may design further cases yourself. Where a prediction concerns how untrained people would respond to a case, state the prediction as a conditional about patterns of intuition rather than as a citation to any study.

5. A closing verdict, of one paragraph each side would also sign, on which parts of their remaining dispute are substantive and which, if any, are merely verbal — and, if any part is verbal, whose wording should carry forward into future work and why.
