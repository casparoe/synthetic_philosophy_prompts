At a recent reading group on population ethics, one of the members offered the following summary of negative utilitarianism, and I would like to know how much of it to trust:

'Negative utilitarianism holds that only suffering carries moral weight, so happiness counts for nothing. Since no pleasure, however vast, can ever outweigh any pain, however slight — a pinprick outweighs paradise — the view commits its holders to preferring the painless annihilation of all sentient life to any future that contains suffering. Worse, it renders values incommensurable: because pleasure and pain are measured on entirely different scales, no trade-off between them is even intelligible, so the utilitarian calculus simply breaks down. Since no one accepts painless extinction, and since the exchanges we make every day show that pleasure and pain are commensurable, negative utilitarianism is refuted on both counts.'

I suspect this is unfair, though I cannot locate the errors myself. My understanding is that negative utilitarianism is not a single doctrine but a family of views, and I have heard that some versions introduce lexical thresholds precisely to avoid conclusions of this kind — but I do not know enough to say where the summary goes wrong or whether its conclusion survives the corrections. Please audit it. In particular, I would like you to address the following:

(i) The speaker moves from 'happiness counts for nothing' to the view as a whole. Which members of the family accept that claim, which reject it, and is the summary treating the strongest version as if it exhausted the position?

(ii) The incommensurability claim. Does lexical priority actually place pleasure and pain on different scales such that no comparison is intelligible, or does the summary conflate a strict ordering of priorities with a breakdown of comparison altogether? And is 'the calculus breaks down' a fair description of what a lexical ordering does to trade-offs, or a misdescription of a perfectly determinate — if controversial — way of resolving them?

(iii) The pinprick-over-paradise claim. Is this something every version of the view is committed to, or only the strongest ones, and at what point does threshold machinery block it — for instance, by allowing aggregations of small sufferings to outweigh a pleasure, or a pleasure to outweigh a suffering below the threshold?

(iv) The everyday-exchanges argument. Do the trade-offs we actually make show that pleasure and pain are commensurable in the sense at issue, or only that we behave as though some exchanges are permissible? Could a defender of a lexical view accept that we make such trades while denying that they are correct?

Finally, tell me whether the speaker's conclusion — that the view is refuted on both counts — survives once these corrections are made, or whether the accurate residue supports something weaker: perhaps that the strongest version is unstable, and that the family is driven, by pressure from exactly these objections, towards a threshold view that concedes more commensurability than the summary allows. I should say that I am inclined to think the speaker is right that something in the neighbourhood of the pinprick argument bites, but I want that impression tested rather than confirmed.
