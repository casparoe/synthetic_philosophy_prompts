I'm part of a small reading group that works through popular science books on evolution and human nature, and the current one spends a whole chapter on the idea that religion is a virus of the mind — a framing with a lineage going back to Richard Dawkins' memetics. The claim is that beliefs and rituals are cultural items that spread because they are good at getting themselves replicated, not because they benefit the people carrying them. The book leans on this to explain what it calls religion's "scandalous success" despite its obvious costs: it takes time and resources, demands hard-to-verify commitments, enforces celibacy in whole classes of specialists, and occasionally gets its carriers killed. In the viral picture each of these becomes intelligible: the costs are virulence, the fear of death is an entry point, promises of posthumous reward and punishment are tricks for commandeering our motivation, early instruction of children is infection of the naive immune system, and taboos on apostasy are mechanisms for suppressing the host's defenses.

A friend in the group is doing a master's in the cognitive science of religion, and she says the virus picture is exactly what her field spent the last few decades backing away from. On the account she sketches, religious belief is mostly a byproduct of cognitive machinery we would have anyway — an overeager tendency to detect agents and purpose, a capacity to think about minds we can't see — so religion isn't a distinct replicator with its own agenda but something like the noise this machinery makes when it runs. She also says there's a third position in the literature, on which religions are cultural adaptations for group cooperation, with features like monitoring gods and ritualized commitment surviving because the groups that had them outcompeted the ones that didn't — which would make the relationship closer to symbiosis than infection.

I'd like a proper audit of the virus analogy that the book rests on. First, the correspondence table: what plays the role of the virus, the host, transmission, mutation, virulence, immunity, and reservoirs in the religious case. Second, where the mapping breaks — I'm especially interested in whether the breaks that matter are the ones my friend's byproduct story needs (no separate replicator at all) or whether they're compatible with something viral surviving at a lower level, since religious traditions do visibly accumulate, recombine, and lose material over generations. Third, the verdicts: which features of the analogy the book quietly imports that its target lacks, and whether the conclusion it was used to support — that religion persists for its own benefit rather than ours — actually falls out of the analogy or was carried by it. If there's a better comparison available (parasite, byproduct, domesticated crop, symbiont), say what it is and what it gets right that the virus framing gets wrong. Assume I have no background in biology beyond a good undergraduate course.
