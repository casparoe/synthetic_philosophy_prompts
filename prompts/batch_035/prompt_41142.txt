I'm a PhD student in health policy, and I sit on the committee that allocates our university's effective-giving fund. Our selection procedure is a fairly standard cause-prioritization rubric, and I've started to worry about what it tacitly assumes. Concretely: we score every candidate cause from 1 to 10 on importance (scale of the problem), tractability, and neglectedness; we then multiply the three scores into a single priority number and rank causes by the product. For cross-cause comparison we also use "moral weights," which convert outcomes of very different kinds — averted deaths, reduced suffering, better future institutional quality — into one cardinal unit, so that everything bottoms out in something like dollars per unit of value. Yet the same committee, and the movement we borrowed the framework from, insists in other contexts that some of these judgments resist arithmetic: we tell students that cause selection is objective and quantifiable, but that choices among careers within a cause are matters of personal fit that no rubric should touch, and we exempt certain considerations (rights, dignity) from being traded off against welfare gains at all. The neglectedness criterion also quietly assumes a specific picture of how effort and money crowd into a problem — that returns diminish smoothly and that what matters is what others are already spending.

What I'd like is an audit of the practice, not a defense of effective altruism. What theory of value does this rubric actually presuppose — full cardinal commensurability across kinds of value, or something weaker that could survive a compromise? Is multiplying the three scores even coherent, given that they are ordinal-ish judgments stretched onto a 1–10 scale? Which parts of the practice are strictly required by that theory and which are merely convenient, such that a rival theory (say, one on which some values are incommensurable and hard choices are resolved by commitment rather than comparison) could keep parts of it? And if we replaced the presupposed view with such a rival, what concretely would have to change — could the fund still issue a ranked list, or only a partial order, and what happens to the moral-weights step?
