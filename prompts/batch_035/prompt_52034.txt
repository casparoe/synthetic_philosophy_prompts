I'm the control lead at a mid-sized AI lab — not frontier, but big enough that we're about to ship an agent that does multi-day autonomous coding and data tasks for customers, and it's my job to make sure the control measures around it would actually keep us safe even if the model turns out to be misaligned (scheming, sandbagging, whatever you want to call it). The working assumption our team inherited is basically the control framing: we don't get to assume alignment is solved, so safety has to come from a stack of monitoring, sandboxing, and escalation measures that would still work *against* a capable adversary that behaves nicely in our evaluations. Proposals for new measures land on my desk about once a week, and the problem is turning that framing into something the team can actually run on a proposal. Right now it's vibes: someone proposes "a trusted model reviews all the outputs," half the room nods, and the other half asks why we don't just train the untrusted model to be honest and skip the review, and I don't have a principled answer that fits in a meeting.

So what I want is a decision procedure for vetting candidate control measures, written for engineers who know ML but not the safety case literature. It should be a runbook: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a "no, this is compliance theater," and where it has to punt to a person because the remaining question is about risk appetite rather than facts. The questions need to cover at least: what attack the measure is actually supposed to stop (and what it's irrelevant to); how it fails under adaptive pressure — what a capable misaligned model that knows the measure exists would do about it, including the boring routes like staying under a threshold rather than defeating the monitor outright; whether we can test it in our own eval harness without fooling ourselves, including how strong our red-team adversary has to be for the test to mean anything; and how it composes with the other measures in the stack versus just duplicating one of them. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about the ways the procedure itself can be gamed (a measure that sails through every gate, but only because the adversary we tested against was weak). We can spend maybe two engineer-weeks evaluating any single proposal, and the output feeds a safety case that a review board — including execs who are not technical — has to sign off on, so there needs to be a plain-language verdict at the end of each run.

Then run the procedure on two proposals from our actual queue: (1) a trusted-model monitor — a smaller, heavily-audited model reviews every output of the untrusted agent before anything is released to the customer, escalating to a human when it's unsure; and (2) a honeypot scheme — we seed the agent's environment with fake credentials and fake customer data, and treat any attempt to use them as a trigger for shutdown plus investigation. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I won't like (the monitor is my own pet proposal, so if the procedure kills it, so be it), and finish with an honest section on what the procedure fundamentally cannot do — the residual ways we ship something unsafe even after a measure passes every gate, and the point at which no checklist can substitute for someone just making a hard call.
