At a public meeting of our county historical society, a long-running dispute produced something I would like to understand better. The society has been revising its exhibit on a nineteenth-century labor conflict, and our archivist has assembled court records, land deeds, and contemporary newspapers indicating that the account our town commemorates annually — the one taught in local schools and carried forward by descendants' oral traditions — materially misrepresents who did what and to whom. When the archivist presented these findings, one council member waved them aside with the remark that history is written by the victors, and drew two conclusions from it: that the dominant local story simply reflects who really prevailed and should therefore stand, and that the archive is itself just another weapon in that victory. Moments later, another member invoked the same proverb to argue the precise opposite: that the oral tradition is the suppressed voice of the defeated, and that it, not the documents, deserves the final word. One sentence, two members, opposite verdicts.

Before our next meeting I want to be clear on what the proverb could actually be claiming. Please lay out the distinct, precise theses it might be expressing — from the near-trivial to the substantive to the false — and, for each, say whether it is plausible and which of our two members' uses, if either, it would actually support. In laying out the menu, I would ask you to keep several candidate readings distinct rather than blending them: that the winners of conflicts tend to produce more of the surviving sources, as a causal claim about archives; that victors actively suppress or destroy the accounts of the defeated; that every history, documentary or oral, is perspectival and there is no neutral record to which appeal can be made; and that whatever the descriptive facts, institutions ought to treat communal memory with suspicion, or alternatively with deference, when it conflicts with the documentary record. Note also where the proverb's truth would be irrelevant: if it is true only in the weak causal sense, does it follow that the archive should be distrusted in this particular case, where the documentary record is being used to overturn a narrative that is itself locally dominant? I want a disambiguation with verdicts that I can bring to the meeting — a menu of readings, not a defense of one — and for each reading an explicit statement of whether it supports dismissing the archivist, privileging the oral tradition, or neither.
