I keep going back and forth on care ethics versus impartial ethics, so I've started collecting my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just a mess. Here's what I've got. It's fine for me to skip a charity shift to drive my mum to hospital when she's ill. It's not fine for a judge to give a lighter sentence because the defendant reminds her of her son, even though I sort of admire the impulse. A nurse spending an extra half hour comforting a dying patient she knows well, while a new admission waits an extra half hour in pain: fine. The same nurse giving a patient's medication late because a favourite patient wanted a chat: not fine. Favouring your own kids over strangers whenever it doesn't cost much: obviously fine, I can't even see the objection. Giving your own kid a leg up through your professional network over a better-qualified stranger's kid: not fine, that one feels almost corrupt. And I think a good parent should be partial but a good judge shouldn't, and I can't explain that without saying "because they're different jobs", which feels too easy.

So: is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close -- something about caring being legitimate when it responds to a relationship rather than breaches a role, or whatever you actually think is right? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn and why? Please reply in British English, and don't give me a survey of care ethics or a reading list -- I want the principle stated precisely, its verdicts on cases I haven't mentioned, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it can't save.
