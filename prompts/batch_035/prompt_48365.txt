I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of punishment, and this term the seminar has crystallised into two entrenched camps: the retributivists, who insist that the guilty deserve to suffer in virtue of their past wrongdoing, full stop, and the consequentialists, who maintain that any institution of punishment must be justified by its future effects — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation. The exchanges have been philosophically rich but dialectically sterile: each side has stopped engaging with the other's strongest form and begun parading the other's crudest. Before the seminar collapses into an annual ritual, I want to try something I have read about in other fields: an adversarial collaboration, in which articulate proponents of opposing views jointly produce a document setting out exactly where they stand.

Draft the joint statement these two camps should be able to sign. I want the following components, in this order:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered, covering the common ground: what they both take the defining features of punishment as an institution to be (hard treatment intentionally inflicted, for an alleged offence, by an authority); what each side accepts about the other's complaints (the retributivists' charge that pure consequentialism licenses scapegoating and the punishment of the innocent whenever it maximises welfare; the consequentialists' charge that retributivism, taken neat, struggles to explain why adding suffering to the world is ever a good thing rather than a second evil requiring its own justification); and whatever else the two camps genuinely share, which I suspect is more than either admits.

2. A disagreements table, with each disagreement phrased in wording its own side would accept as fair. I am particularly interested in whether they can agree on the precise formulation of the central dispute: whether the connection between culpability and deserved suffering is constitutive (the suffering is deserved because of the past act, and that is a bedrock fact about value) or derivative (the practice of desert-talk earns its keep through what it achieves). Include the harder, less-discussed disagreements too: how each side handles the lapsed offender whose crime is decades old and who has since lived blamelessly; the problem of the many guilty — a jurisdiction with more convicted offenders than it could conceivably punish, where the choice is which ones, not whether; how attempts versus completed offences should be punished, given that consequences loom large for one side and culpability for the other; and whether a hypothetical rehabilitation pill that reformatted an offender overnight would leave anything for the retributivist to demand afterwards.

3. A cruxes list. For each crux, name the case, argument, or body of evidence that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. If the honest answer is that some crux is empirically untestable in principle, say so and say why, and note whether both sides agree that it is untestable or only one of them thinks so.

4. A short final section on what Norval Morris called limiting retributivism — the hybrid on which desert sets a ceiling (and occasionally a floor) and consequentialist considerations operate within that range. Each side should state, in its own acceptable wording, whether this is a genuine synthesis, a truce that merely hides the disagreement inside the width of the range, or a view neither could actually hold once the range narrows.

Two further stipulations. First, if either side comes to believe that part of the disagreement is merely verbal — that the two camps are using 'desert' or 'justification' in equivocal ways — the document should say which part and why, since my own suspicion is that at least the dispute over whether retributivism needs a 'positive' justification for desert rather than a merely limiting one is partly definitional. Second, resist the temptation to adjudicate: this is a document both sign, not a verdict. The test of success is that a reader of either camp would nod along to the formulation of their own view, wince at nothing, and learn precisely where the other side's position is strong.

Please write the whole thing in British English.
