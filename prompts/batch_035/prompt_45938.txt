I lead the component-qualification group at a manufacturer of battery systems and sealed electrical enclosures. Two or three times a week, someone asks us to certify a dispositional claim about a part: that a housing is waterproof, that a battery cell is not disposed to go into thermal runaway under fast charge, that a fuse is disposed to open within ten milliseconds of a specified overcurrent, that a gasket remains flexible at minus forty. Our method is roughly to apply the stimulus and watch for the manifestation: submerge the housing, overcharge the cell, pulse the current, chill the gasket. A colleague with a philosophy background recently looked over our test plans and told me we had rediscovered a known problem in the literature on dispositions. As he explained it, the simple view is that a thing has a disposition just when it would show the manifestation if the stimulus conditions were met, and this view is broken by two kinds of interfering factor. One is a fink: something that, at the moment the stimulus arrives, removes the disposition itself — his example was a live wire attached to a device that makes the wire dead just when someone is about to touch it, so the wire never shows what it was disposed to do. The other is a mask: the disposition remains and the stimulus occurs, but something standing between the stimulus and the manifestation blocks the effect — glass packed in straw is still fragile even though it would not break when struck. I cannot vouch for his command of the literature, but the phenomena are exactly ours, and they are damaging our certification record:

1. Our battery packs pass the overcharge test because a thermal cutout inside the pack disconnects the cell just as its temperature crosses the fault threshold. The bare cell, without the cutout, runs away. Marketing wants the record to state that the cell is not disposed to thermal runaway. I do not believe that is what we tested, but I cannot say what claim our data does support.
2. Our sealed enclosures pass the submersion test because the test fixture clamps shut a seam that is left unclamped in field installation. On my colleague's vocabulary, the rig is masking a disposition the housing still has.
3. We have a recurring fight about scope. When we certify the pack, the pack includes the cutout, the firmware that watches the cell, and the harness. When the same cell ships inside a client's product, none of that travels with it. Whether the certified disposition travels with the cell or stays behind with the assembly is exactly the fight I keep losing to sales, who want it to travel.

Please write a procedure my team can actually run on an incoming claim. It should take us through questions in a fixed order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; indicate, for each question, whether the answer can come from a test, from teardown or post-test inspection, or only from a judgment call I have to sign; and define the stopping conditions under which we return "cannot certify on this evidence" rather than a qualified pass. It should tell us how to decide, per claim, whether the thing whose disposition we certify is the part, the assembly as shipped, or the assembly as installed — I suspect this is the same question the packed glassware was raising, and I want the procedure to take a position on it rather than refer it back to me. Then run the procedure on the three cases above, and expect that at least one verdict will be unwelcome to someone here; if so, say so plainly. Finally, be honest about where the procedure will misfire: the classes of claims it was not built for, and the steps that dress up a judgment call as a mechanical one.

For context on constraints: we are three people, two engineers and a technician, with about half a day per claim; the client-facing record has to survive audits by our insurers and, occasionally, scrutiny in court; and the team has no philosophy, so any distinction that matters has to survive translation into their working vocabulary.
