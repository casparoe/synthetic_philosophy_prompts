I sit on the grants committee of a mid-sized global health foundation (roughly $10M to allocate each funding round, committee meets quarterly), and we're heading for a genuinely ugly deadlock at the March meeting, so I want a written opinion I can circulate beforehand. Some background so you understand what we're fighting about: we have three live options for this round. Option A is one big gamble on a single unproven intervention — call it a coin flip, about a 50% chance of saving ~2,000 lives and a 50% chance of saving nobody, for an expected value around 1,000 lives. Option B is the boring workhorse program that more or less reliably saves ~900 lives. Option C is splitting the pot between two independent coin-flip interventions (different diseases, different teams), each roughly a 50% chance of saving ~1,000 lives with half funding — so the same expected value as A, but only about a 25% chance of saving nobody.

The thing is, our official doctrine has always been straight expected value, and EV makes you indifferent between A and C and mildly prefers both to B — so on paper this should be easy. But it isn't, for three reasons. First, a new committee member (philosophy PhD, thinks we're all dilettantes, honestly she might be right) keeps going on about risk-weighted expected utility — she attributes it to a philosopher named Buchak — the idea being that a rational agent doesn't just weight outcomes by their probabilities but by some risk function over how the gamble could turn out for them, so you can rationally prefer C to A even though the expected values are identical, and the usual excuse for caution (diminishing returns, hedging your money) supposedly doesn't apply, since we're counting lives and lives don't compound. Second, our chair says that even if that's true for a one-shot decisionmaker, we're not one — the foundation will run fifty more funding rounds, so the coin flips average out over the years and anything other than EV maximization just leaves lives on the table. Third, another member argues that if anything we should be risk-SEEKING (something about how, if you're risk-seeking in the relevant sense, the gamble with the highest EV plus most variance is the one you should pile into) — I honestly didn't follow that part and I'd want the memo to say clearly whether it even makes sense given our numbers.

So: please draft the memo itself, in American English — the actual document I'll circulate, with a clear recommendation for what rule we should adopt for this round (and ideally going forward, with a worked comparison using the real numbers above), reasons the chair and the philosophy PhD could each actually respond to, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses rather than a strawman. Keep it under about 900 words because nobody on this committee reads anything longer, and please flag anywhere I'm being too loose with the setup.
