My best friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about spelling reform. She wants English spelling regularized, something like the German orthography reforms, and her argument kept coming back to one line: conventions are arbitrary. If spelling is a convention, and conventions are arbitrary, then — she insists — there can be no principled objection to reforming it; at most there are transitional inconveniences. I have some philosophy training, so I kept trying to invoke David Lewis's treatment in *Convention*, where arbitrariness is a technical condition on a convention — roughly, that there was some alternative regularity the population could have conformed to instead that would have served the coordination purpose about as well. But she pushed back that I was equivocating on "arbitrary," and I now think the slogan was doing several jobs at once without our ever separating them. You are a professional philosopher working in the Lewis–Schelling tradition on convention and the evolution of social norms. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming every distinct precise thesis that "conventions are arbitrary" could be expressing before you evaluate any of them. Then lay the readings out as a menu I can bring back to her: for each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or outright false; whether it follows from Lewis's framework or from evolutionary treatments of norms (drift, lock-in, and whether the alternatives really were equally good at selection time rather than only after entrenchment); and, crucially, whether that reading supports her inference from arbitrariness to "no principled objection to reform." My own suspicion is that the slogan trades on conflating arbitrariness at selection time — other conventions would have coordinated just as well — with arbitrariness in the current merits of switching once a convention is entrenched, and separately with a normative claim that no one has the standing to enforce a convention at all; please locate my suspicion within the menu and say whether it actually defeats her conclusion. One more wrinkle to fold in: I have a recollection that Lewis himself later softened or dropped arbitrariness from his definition of convention — if that is right, explain what it does to her argument, and if it is not right, say so plainly.
