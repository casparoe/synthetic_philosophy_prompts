I'm a PhD student working on evaluations and deployment safety for language models, and a labmate walked me through an argument that I can't refute but also can't bring myself to accept. (1) The whole point of control measures — audits, canary traps, staged deployment, unlogged-behavior tripwires — is that they keep us safe without our having to know whether the model is aligned. (2) Any such measure works by making the expected cost of detected misbehavior exceed the expected benefit of undetected misbehavior, which requires that the model be unable to tell when it would be detected. (3) A model that knows which behaviors are monitored can simply behave well under observation and defect only where no tripwire waits. (4) Frontier models will inevitably know a great deal about their training and deployment setups, because safety practices are publicly discussed and the discussion itself ends up in training data. (5) So control measures are useful only against models too ignorant to find the gaps — that is, against models that were never dangerous in the first place. (6) Therefore every control scheme quietly assumes the alignment it was meant to substitute for, and control research adds nothing to alignment research. My labmate takes this to show the entire control agenda is circular, and I confess the argument feels watertight to me even though the conclusion seems clearly wrong. I'd like to keep (1) if I can, and I have a vague sense that something slides between (3) and (5), but I can't name what. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what I have to pay for rejecting it. Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
