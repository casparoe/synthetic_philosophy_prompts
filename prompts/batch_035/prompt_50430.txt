I work as a patient-safety investigator for a large hospital trust, and one of the recurring problems in our serious-incident reviews is deciding when a failure to act gets written up as a causal contributing factor and when it just goes into the background narrative. A patient deteriorates overnight and nobody escalates to the on-call doctor; a scan result sits unactioned because the ordering doctor went on leave and handed nothing over; a locum doesn't read the notes that were available. Everyone on the review panel agrees these omissions "mattered," but we have no shared rule for when an omission is a cause of the harm versus context, and the disagreement always gets resolved by whoever is loudest in the room. I've been reading a bit of philosophy on this and the problem seems to be structural: absences are everywhere, so if we use the ordinary counterfactual test — the harm wouldn't have occurred had someone done the thing they didn't do — almost any non-event qualifies, from the nurse who didn't recheck the patient to the nurse who happened to be on a different ward. What appears to do the real work is some notion of an expectation of intervention: an omission counts as causal only against a background of who was supposed to do what, which is partly a fact about rosters and protocols but partly a judgment about what counts as normal in the first place — and I can see that normality judgments are exactly where hindsight bias sneaks in, since after a death everything a reasonable person failed to anticipate looks like an obvious omission.

Please build us a decision procedure we can actually run inside a forty-five-minute review meeting with clinicians who have no patience for philosophy. It should take the panel through the questions in order: how to identify candidate omissions without the list exploding into every conceivable non-event; how to test whether the omission made a difference to the outcome in the relevant sense; how to weigh duty or protocol against reasonable departures from both; how to keep what people could have known at the time separate from what we know now; and where the procedure should stop with "background factor, no formal finding." It should end in one of three dispositions on our incident form — causal contributing factor, contextual note, or no finding — with a named senior clinician signing off, and it must be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedure clothes. Please also flag explicitly what the procedure cannot do: I don't expect it to settle legal liability or individual blame, and it should say so rather than pretending otherwise.

Then run it on three cases from our recent files. First: a night nurse who didn't escalate a deteriorating patient, where the escalation criteria in the protocol were genuinely ambiguous and several experienced nurses have said they would have read them the same way. Second: a patient who died because the on-call doctor was never informed of a critical result, but no individual was assigned to inform anyone — the handover system simply had no owner for that step. Third, and I want you to include this even though I dislike where I suspect it lands: an experienced nurse who checked the patient only once during a night on which the rosters were so thin that checking more often was not physically possible for anyone on shift. If your procedure classifies that third case as a systemic factor rather than a causal finding about the nurse, fine — but I want to see whether the procedure says so for principled reasons or just because the facts make us uncomfortable, and what the panel is supposed to do when the procedure's answer and the family's expectation of a named cause come apart.
