I'm grading finals for an intro ethics class and could use a second pair of eyes on one answer. The exam question was:

"State the doctrine of double effect and its traditional conditions. Explain how the doctrine is supposed to distinguish between the bystander-at-the-switch case and the footbridge (fat man) case, and then discuss one objection to the doctrine. Finally, say how the doctrine handles the 'loop' variant of the trolley case, where the diverted trolley would circle back and kill the five unless the one man's body stops it."

The student's answer, roughly verbatim:

"The doctrine of double effect says an act with both a good and a bad effect is permissible when: (1) the act itself is not intrinsically wrong, (2) the agent intends only the good effect, with the bad effect merely foreseen but not intended, and (3) the good effect is proportionate to the bad one. In the switch case, the bystander intends to save the five by redirecting the trolley, and the one man's death is a foreseen side effect of the track change, so the doctrine permits it. In the footbridge case, pushing the large man is impermissible because his body is used as a means to stop the trolley, so his death is intended. Notice that this shows the doctrine is really just the act/omission distinction in disguise — intending harm versus merely foreseeing it is the same line as doing harm versus allowing it to happen. Foot introduced both of these cases in her original 1967 paper, where she argued that pushing the man follows directly from double effect. As for the proportionality condition, the doctrine is basically a consequentialist principle, since it tells us to weigh the good outcome against the bad one and permit the act when the good outweighs the bad. A common objection is that intention is too subjective, so two agents doing the exact same thing could differ in permissibility just based on what's in their heads. On the loop variant: here too the doctrine gives the right answer easily, because the five are saved as a side effect of diverting the trolley, and the man's death is not intended, so it's permissible just like the ordinary switch case."
