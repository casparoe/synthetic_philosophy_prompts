Background: I spent 35 years doing statistics for agricultural field trials, retired two years ago, and have since gotten properly obsessed with philosophy — enough that I now run a little evening discussion group at our community center. About a dozen regulars, mostly curious retirees plus one or two grad students, 75 minutes a session, no equations unless I write them out very slowly on the whiteboard.

Next month's topic is Dutch book arguments, and I want to run it as a game rather than a talk. The idea has always appealed to me as someone who spent decades telling researchers their p-values didn't mean what they thought: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, a sharp bookie can offer you a set of bets where you lose no matter how the world turns out. But when I rehearse explaining this out loud I can tell people will just nod along. I want them to get cleaned out first, THEN see why.

So design the activity. Assume participants have no formal probability background beyond hazy school stuff, but they're smart and enjoy arguing. I'm imagining something like: people commit to odds on some events (dice, cards, weather, whatever works), someone — or me, or the group — gets to play bookie against them, and the sure-loss just emerges. But you work out the details. What I need from you:

- The actual rules, the events to bet on, how to state odds in a way non-mathematicians can handle, and a payoff scheme using chips or points — no real money, though the chips should sting a little to lose.
- The sequencing: which violation of the axioms they should walk into first (I'm guessing betting odds that don't sum to one is the gentlest entry point, but you tell me), and what comes after.
- The debrief: how to map exactly what happened onto the formal machinery, without me having to lecture for 40 minutes to do it.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people refusing to bet at all, someone playing clever rather than sincere, the group catching on too fast, whatever it is.
- And an honest note on which part of the actual argument the game cannot reproduce. I keep half-remembering that the real theorem needs idealized bettors and exact credences and a bookie who can act on every scrap of information, and I suspect the leap from "you lost your chips" to "your beliefs are irrational" is doing more work than the fun version admits. Tell me where I have to just say this out loud at the end.

Keep the whole plan under 600 words — I need to be able to print it on one sheet and still read my own handwriting on the whiteboard after. And please dont suggest readings, the group doesn't do homework.
