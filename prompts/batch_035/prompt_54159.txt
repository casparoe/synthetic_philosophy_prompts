i'm putting together my position statement on logical omniscience for a phil logic workshop thing and want a consistancy check before i send it round to the group. here's roughly what i've landed on:

(1) logical omniscience in our formal models is basically fine — a harmless idealization, same as frictionless planes in physics, and people who lose sleep over it are confusing models with agents.

(2) i'm a pretty orthodox bayesian about credence: the probability axioms are binding on rational agents, and the dutch book argument is more or less the reason why.

(3) i think it can be perfectly rational to have a credence of 0.6 in the riemann hypothesis, or in any other open math question with a determinate truth value.

(4) ought implies can, full stop. no norm of rationality can demand something computationally out of reach, which is why i don't think you can fault an agent for failing to notice a logical entailment.

(5) whatever residual problem there is with logical omniscience, the right fix is just to widen the algebra: treat logically equivalent propositions as distinct atoms and define your probabilities over those. no impossible worlds, no exotic semantics, just ordinary probability theory on a bigger space.

(6) my friend passed on a guaranteed profit in a prediction market because she didn't notice that two contracts were logically equivalent, and i want to say she was fully rational in doing so. this is kind of my litmus test case for the whole thing.
