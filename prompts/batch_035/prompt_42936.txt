For the first time in my life I've been summoned for jury duty, and in preparation I rewatched Sidney Lumet's Twelve Angry Men, expecting a lesson in reasonable doubt but finding instead what looks like a live case study in the epistemology of peer disagreement. Juror 8 begins as one dissenter against eleven peers who have heard exactly the same evidence, and if you take peerhood at face value, conciliationism seems to demand he split the difference with the majority; instead the film appears to vindicate steadfastness, since his lone resistance is progressively revealed as justified. Please read the film through this debate. Is Juror 8 actually a counterexample to conciliationism, or does the film quietly show that the eleven were never his epistemic peers to begin with — their beliefs driven by prejudice, deference, and the wish to get home, which would allow even a conciliationist to discount them? And where does the film resist the reading, as when the later flips to not guilty seem owed to herd dynamics rather than evidence, which no peer-disagreement theory should applaud? I'd like a treatment that takes both the film's specific scenes (the knife demonstration, the reconstruction of the old man's walk, the argument about the woman's eyeglasses) and the conciliationism-versus-steadfastness literature seriously, and that ends by saying whether the film dramatizes something the standard arguments miss — perhaps that peerhood is not a fixed status but something the jurors earn and forfeit over the course of the afternoon.
