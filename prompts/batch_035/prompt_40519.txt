Two friends of mine, an ML engineer and someone who works in biosecurity, had a long argument at a BBQ about whether AI could realistically end the world, and I got lost somewhere in the middle. He opened by saying the whole "p(doom)" discourse is pseudo-quantitative: nobody has a real probability for something that has literally never happened once, and there are no falsifyable predictions, so it's vibes with numbers attached. Maya said we put probabilities on unprecedented events all the time — full-scale nuclear war, an engineered pandemic — and refusing to give a number just means your estimate is hidden rather than absent. He said fine, but the doom case needs a long conjunctive chain (agent-like AI, misalignment that survives deployment, actual takeover capability, no human counterplay), and multiplying uncertainties down a chain like that gives you a number with no meaning at all. She answered that this assumes the risk is conjunctive when it might be disjunctive: many different failure paths that don't share steps, so you can't multiply small numbers across them, and the burden is on him to show every path is negligible, not on her to nail down one. He came back with two points at once: the worry is partly manufactured (safety teams funded by the same labs selling the capability), and even if it's real, a slowdown just means the careful actors pause while the careless ones race ahead. She finished by saying that even a modest probability cut on existential risk dominates any economic cost — the stakes are so lopsided that ordinary cost-benefit reasoning just doesn't apply here. He said that proves too much: it licenses throwing unbounded resources at any tiny-probability catastrophe story anyone can tell, and asked how her last move differs from Pascal's mugging. Then they both looked at me. Can you go through the exchange move by move — who's right about what, and what (if anything) they're both missing?
