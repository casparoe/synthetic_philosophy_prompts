In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol 4–1 at Go. Three facts from that match, in case they've blurred: in game 2 it played the famous move 37, a shoulder hit on the fifth line that human commentators initially read as a blunder and that the machine gave roughly a one-in-ten-thousand probability of a human playing; in game 4 Lee won with move 78, after which AlphaGo's own evaluations showed its position collapsing; and between games AlphaGo's network was fixed — its loss in game 4 produced no learning, and it came back for game 5 exactly as it was.

Analyze the match through Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. I want a disciplined mapping, not a victory lap for the machine: does the offline lookahead of its tree search make it a textbook Popperian creature, letting moves die in its head instead of in the world? Do its training runs — selection among candidate policies, first against a corpus of human games, then against itself — constitute a Darwinian layer, and does it matter that this "evolution" happened in an engineer's lab rather than phylogeny? Was inheriting a corpus of human games a kind of Gregorian move, or does Gregorian mindedness require something AlphaGo lacked? And note the inversion I find most interesting: it was highly Popperian within a game while being less than Skinnerian across games, since a dog that loses a fight learns more than AlphaGo learned from losing game 4.

Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the case do to Dennett's ladder? Is it a claim about architecture, about the origin of an agent's design, or about behavioral competence — and if AlphaGo sits differently on the ladder depending on which we pick, does the taxonomy still carve anything? Say where the taxonomy is silent about an artifact like this rather than forcing it to speak.
