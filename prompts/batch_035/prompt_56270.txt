The question was: "We hold that breaking a promise is wrong even when no harm results from the breach -- if I promised to meet you for coffee and simply don't show up, with no inconvenience to you (you'd brought a book and enjoyed the wait), many still think I've wronged you. Explain why the obligation seems to persist in the absence of harm. Discuss at least two accounts from the literature."

Student's answer:

"The puzzle is that if morality were only about consequences, a broken promise that harms no one shouldn't matter. Yet we feel the promisor has done something wrong. Two main accounts try to explain this.

The first is the practice view, developed by David Hume in the Treatise, who argued that promises are artificial virtues that derive their force from conventions, like money and property. On this view, a promise binds because there is a social practice of promising, and the practice is justified by its consequences: it lets people coordinate and make plans around each other's commitments. Breaking a promise, even a harmless one, is wrong because it is an offense against the practice itself -- abusing the convention everyone depends on. Hume's insight was later taken up by Rawls in his distinction between the summary view and the practice view of rules.

The second is the expectation view, associated with T.M. Scanlon. Here the wrong consists in violating a duty not to frustrate the reliance that others place on your assurances. When I promise, I induce an expectation in you, and I now have an obligation not to cause you the harm of having that expectation let down. This straightforwardly explains why the breach is wrong even without material inconvenience: the injury is to your expectation, not your schedule. Scanlon's account thus cleanly solves the puzzle, since the wrong is fully internal to the expectation and needs no reference to social practices at all.

Both accounts agree the binding force comes from something other than the consequences of the particular breach. The practice view locates it in convention, the expectation view in the reliance of the promisee. Since both converge on this, the phenomenon is well understood."
