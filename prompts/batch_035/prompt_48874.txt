I hold that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible, on grounds of the familiar consistency argument: if I ought to do A and I ought to do B, then by agglomeration I ought to do both together; if "ought" implies "can," and A and B cannot jointly be done, then at most one of the original oughts was real. So-called dilemmas -- Sartre's student torn between joining the Free French and staying with his mother, the doctor who can save one of two patients, the informant who must either lie or betray a friend -- are on my view artifacts of uncertainty (I don't know which act is all-things-considered best), of failing to distinguish pro tanto reasons from outright obligations, or of a threshold phenomenon: when both options are barely permissible, we misread the flatness of the ranking as conflict. I also think the residue argument is weak: the guilt one appropriately feels after, say, abandoning a parent for a cause is best explained as a socially and psychologically useful disposition -- a commitment device that keeps us from drifting into easy trade-offs -- rather than as a truthful register of a violated obligation, which is what dilemma theorists like Williams and Lemmon take it to be. Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters sorted by severity, from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them to include: concrete cases or kinds of cases it cannot describe without strain; formal results, e.g., what happens to deontic logic if agglomeration is simply dropped rather than defended, and whether non-adjunctive "ought" talk remains principled or becomes ad hoc; results or arguments from neighboring areas it stands or falls with -- say, accounts of supererogation, the lexical priority of certain duties, or whether "ought implies can" itself can survive if Frankfurt-style cases show obligation to be independent of alternatives. Distinguish what committed opponents of dilemmas would themselves concede as counting against them from what only dilemma theorists insist on, and tell me honestly whether my fallback moves -- the uncertainty reading, the threshold reading, the debunking explanation of residue -- have quietly qualified the position into something unfalsifiable. If they have, say whether that's a vice for a metaethical claim of this kind or just what responsible defense looks like. What I would personally find most persuasive is either a case where the residue interpretation does real explanatory work the debunking story cannot do, or a demonstration that rejecting agglomeration costs less, formally and philosophically, than my position assumes.
