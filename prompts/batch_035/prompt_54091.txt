Intro draft:
"Goodman's 'new riddle of induction' is usually told as a story about predicates. We infer that emeralds examined before some future time t are green, and this seems justified by their observed color. But define 'grue' as: green if examined before t, blue otherwise. Every observation we've made is equally a confirmation that emeralds are grue. Since 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' conflict in their predictions about what we'll see after t, and both fit the data equally, something must break the tie — and whatever that something is, it can't just be the observations. Standard answers locate it in the predicates themselves: 'green' is 'entrenchable' in a way 'grue' is not, or 'grue' is somehow gerrymandered, positional, or disjunctive in a way that disqualifies it.

I want to argue that this whole framing concedes too much to the grue-ite. The tie is not broken by anything about the predicates. It's broken by our background beliefs about emeralds — specifically, beliefs about what kind of stuff emeralds are and how their observable properties depend on their microstructure. 'Grue' isn't a bad predicate in itself; it's a perfectly good predicate that describes an object's light-reflection behavior *jointly with* facts about observers' histories. Once you notice that 'grue' smuggles a component that we have independent, inductively-supported reasons to treat as irrelevant to an emerald's color — namely, the time of examination — the riddle dissolves. It was never a riddle about greenness. It was a riddle about which variables matter."

Rest of the essay (currently just notes):
- Objection I anticipate: saying we have "inductively supported reasons" to treat examination-time as irrelevant seems circular, since the question was how induction gets off the ground at all. My reply is that Goodman's riddle only bites given a regimented, impoverished picture of evidence where the ONLY thing you know is "observed emeralds were green/grue." Real inquirers never stand there. We know emeralds are crystals of a specific mineral, we know that minerals don't typically care what year it is, etc.
- Second part of the essay: I apply the same treatment to a (possibly dumb?) example of my own — imagine we discover a gemstone, call it "shiftyte," whose observed color has empirically turned out to vary with the lab that examines it. Like, labs in the northern hemisphere see it red, labs in the south see it blue. Now the grue-style positional predicate ("red if examined in a northern lab") is exactly the RIGHT one to project, and the "natural" non-positional predicate is the gerrymandered one. Point being that entrenchment/gruesomeness isn't an intrinsic property of predicates, it's relative to what the world turns out to be like — which supports my background-beliefs story over the predicate story.
- Planned conclusion: projectibility is not a fact about language but a fact about our total epistemic situation, so the new riddle is really just the old riddle wearing a costume.
