In the philosophy of language seminar I'm taking this term, we spent two weeks on Grice, and a classmate of mine — a former linguistics graduate student who thinks the whole Gricean apparatus is decorative — has been pressing an argument that the distinction between what is said and what is conversationally implicated cannot do any work at all. The rest of us find the conclusion incredible, because the distinction seems obviously vindicated by the cancellation test: an utterance of "some of the students passed" can be followed without contradiction by "in fact, all of them did," but not by "and none of them did," and that asymmetry looks like it tracks something real about the difference between an implicature and part of what is said. Yet none of us can locate the flaw in her reasoning. She lays it out in five steps:

(1) On Grice's account, a speaker conversationally implicates that p when, in saying that q, she intends the hearer to come to believe that p, the hearar being meant to work this out on the assumption that the speaker is observing the Cooperative Principle, taking what is said — that q — as the premise of the calculation.
(2) But the hearer's recovery of what is said is itself an exercise of the very same capacity: settling the referent of "she," choosing among the senses of "bank," deciding whether "three children" is being used literally — all of this is done by asking what the speaker could reasonably have intended, and Grice himself later allowed that pragmatic reasoning can reach into what is said rather than stopping at its edge.
(3) So there is no difference in kind between the hearer's route to q and the hearer's route to p: both are inferences to the best intentional explanation of the utterance, constrained by an assumption of cooperativeness.
(4) But the definition of implicature requires the calculation to run from what is said as an independently secured premise; if what is said is itself the output of the same kind of calculation, then the premise is never secured independently, and the calculation is circular wherever it occurs.
(5) Therefore either nothing is ever conversationally implicated, because no case has a clean premise, or else everything the speaker intends the hearer to recover — including q itself — is implicated; and in either case the saying/implicating distinction does no work.

I would rather keep step (1), since it is close to a textbook statement of the theory, and I have a half-formed suspicion about step (4) — that it demands an independence for "what is said" that the definition never promised — but I cannot make the suspicion precise, and I cannot see how to accept (3) while denying (4). Please respond with exactly five numbered items, one corresponding to each step of her argument, each one or two sentences stating whether that step should be accepted and, if not, precisely what rejecting it costs; give no introduction and no conclusion.
