I coordinate the testing center at a mid-sized community college, and I've spent the last few years chewing on a problem I stumbled into without meaning to. Some years back I read a magazine piece about two philosophers who argued that a notebook could literally be part of someone's mind: they described an older man with memory problems who wrote everything down in a binder, and argued that since the binder did everything for him that other people's biological memory does for them — was always with him, was consulted automatically, was trusted without double-checking — it wasn't a tool he used to think with but a part of his thinking itself. I have no philosophy training, so I can't tell you whether that's a respected view or a fringe one, but it has refused to leave me alone, because it lands right on my desk every semester.

Here is the situation. My office receives dozens of accommodation requests a term, and a growing share of them are about memory and organization: students with concussions, ADHD, stroke recovery, anxiety that blanks them out under pressure. Many of these students have built elaborate external systems — paper planners with color codes, a phone app that photographs the whiteboard every class, a smartwatch that buzzes with the next step of every task. Under exam rules we confiscate exactly those things. Whether that's fair is supposed to depend on whether the item is an "accommodation," but that word only pushes the question back, because it never tells me what the item is an accommodation of. If the philosophers I read are right, then for some students the planner isn't helping them remember the way a ladder helps someone climb; it's the remembering. Taking it away during a closed-book exam isn't removing a shortcut, it's removing part of the student's mind and then testing the remainder.

I also have my own question, which I privately call the stolen-backpack question: when the student's system is lost or broken, does the student act like someone whose bicycle was stolen (annoyed, but they can still walk) or someone who lost a limb (not the same functioning person until it's replaced)? I suspect the students I should worry about are the second kind. But I can see that my question and the philosophers' criteria can pull apart, and I don't know which should win.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my front-desk staff — two student workers and one part-time proctor, none of them philosophers — could actually run on a request, in about ten minutes, before the exam. It should turn whatever the extended-mind idea has to offer into an ordered set of questions: what to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it has to hand the file to me because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. It also has to be honest about the hard boundary: a closed-book exam is, in a sense, an institution deliberately testing the biological core rather than the whole extended person, so the procedure can't just say "if it's part of his mind, he gets it" — it needs a principled place to stop, and I want that place marked and defended, not smuggled in.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a student recovering from a concussion whose doctor documented a memory impairment, and who wants her paper day-planner, which contains her checklists for everything in her life, on the desk during a closed-book nursing exam; her instructor is certain this is cheating. Second: a student with ADHD whose entire life — course notes included, pasted in alongside everything else — lives in the notes app on his phone, and who wants the phone in a statistics exam where recalling formulas is the point. Third, the one that keeps me up at night: a student who, after a stroke, had the course's formula sheet tattooed on his forearm and wants to take the exam with bare arms, and whose neurologist says he's relied on it for two years exactly the way the others rely on their planners. If the procedure approves the first two, I can see it having to approve the third, and I don't like that answer — so either show me where the procedure legitimately stops him, or show me exactly where my discomfort is doing the work that the procedure refuses to do. Finally, tell me plainly what this procedure cannot settle, and where my stolen-backpack question fits in if it fits at all. Please write the whole thing in American English, at a level my student workers can follow without dumbing down the ideas underneath.
