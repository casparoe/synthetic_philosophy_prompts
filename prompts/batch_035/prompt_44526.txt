I've been an ICU nurse for twelve years, and I've decided to stop hand-waving about the killing/letting-die distinction and work out what I actually believe, the way you'd reconstruct a colleague's practice from her charting. I'm going to give you my honest verdicts on cases I've either seen, done, or can picture vividly, and I want you to act as a philosopher of action with a specialty in biomedical ethics: think carefully, brainstorm candidate principles before committing to one, and then tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern. If no single principle fits, say so and offer the smallest set that does, with the cost of each added clause made explicit.

The cases and my verdicts. (1) We extubate a patient at the documented, verified request of the family after the patient is found to have a devastating prognosis; he dies within the hour. I think this is letting die, and clearly permissible. (2) A colleague, at a family's tearful request, pushes a large bolus of potassium chloride specifically intending that the patient die now rather than in two days. I think this is killing, and wrong, even granting that the outcome is the same death two days earlier. (3) I titrate a morphine drip upward for air hunger in a patient who is dying regardless, knowing full well the new rate may suppress respiration and hasten the death by hours. Fine, somehow, and I don't want to hear that it's just slow-motion case (2). (4) We decline to start a new course of broad-spectrum antibiotics that would buy a confused, cachectic patient perhaps three bad days in restraints. This feels easier than case (1), even though the philosophers tell me withholding and withdrawing are morally symmetrical. (5) A patient has an unwitnessed arrest with an ambiguous code status; nobody starts compressions. Fine. (6) Off shift, in street clothes, I walk past a stranger hemorrhing from a leg wound I could stem with a shirt and pressure. That's letting die, but it feels nearly as bad as killing to me. (7) Meanwhile I've never joined the bone marrow registry, though matching would save a stranger's life at modest cost to me, and I feel approximately zero guilt about that, let alone anything approaching murderer-level guilt. Something about (6) and (7) is in tension, and I can't name what.

Here's my tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong: the difference is whether death comes through something I initiated versus something already in progress before I arrived. But that can't be right, because the morphine drip in (3) is something I initiated, the extubation in (1) is an intervention, and the hitman's bullet is an intervention while the storm that cuts power to a ventilator isn't -- yet I judge the storm a letting and the bullet a killing without hesitation. So please state the principle or principles precisely, clause by clause, and say which verdict each clause is doing the work for. Then tell me what your favored principle says about two cases I haven't decided: a patient loses ventilator power in a storm and I have to hand-bag him for one hour until a transport ventilator arrives -- am I obligated, and for how long, and does the answer change if it's twelve hours? -- and whether the same verdict covers the relative who, with sincere motives and no medical authority, unplugs the ventilator himself. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle forces me to give up; I'd rather lose a verdict than lose a distinction, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying.
