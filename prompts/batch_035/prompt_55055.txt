I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consequentialism at a small liberal arts college. For the next session, on aggregation and the moral weight of suffering, I have 16 students and 75 minutes. Design an in-class activity in which students act as the steering committee of a philanthropic foundation with a fixed budget of tokens, distributing it across a menu of interventions I can write on the board — for example, curing a severe chronic pain condition, lifting families out of desperate poverty into modest comfort, funding a program that brings a few additional moderately happy lives into existence, and a speculative project meant to raise the average cheerfulness of people who are already doing fine. The interventions should be priced so that a straightforward classical-utilitarian calculation and a gut-level focus on relieving suffering yield visibly different allocations, and the menu should include at least one choice that forces students to reveal whether they treat the creation of happiness and the prevention of suffering as commensurable currencies at all. I want a structure with rounds and at least one twist midway — a price change or a new piece of information — so that students discover, before anyone names it, how easily one slides from the weak claim that suffering counts for more than happiness to the far stronger claim that no quantity of additional happiness can outweigh certain suffering. No lecture up front, and they should not hear any labels like negative utilitarianism or suffering-focused ethics until the activity is over.

For the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto specific positions in the literature: the weighted-aggregation view on which suffering simply gets a higher multiplier, lexical or threshold views on which intense suffering is not commensurable with happiness at all, the person-affecting worry about counting new happy lives in the same ledger as existing suffering people, and the standard reductio-style objection to suffering-focused views — the thought that a sufficiently consistent negative utilitarian would in principle endorse outcomes most students will find monstrous, and how defenders typically respond. Also include the practical details: rules, a way to score or settle the allocations without real money, a realistic minute-by-minute timeline for 16 students and 75 minutes, and a candid note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance students coordinating their allocations in advance, someone refusing to choose on principle, or the discussion collapsing into general debate about whether you can measure wellbeing at all — together with how to handle each failure mode. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the underlying philosophical disagreement the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief, and why.
