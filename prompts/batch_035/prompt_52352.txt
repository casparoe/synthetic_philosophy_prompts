After years in corporate fundraising I've unexpectedly joined the grant committee of a small family foundation that gives away roughly two million pounds a year across a scatter of causes, and the trustees have decided we can no longer just fund whoever has the best photos in their annual report. They've settled on the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework after reading about it somewhere, but nobody on the committee has any analytic training, and our last attempt collapsed into trustees awarding each cause a score out of ten for each factor and multiplying. Please write a decision procedure the committee could actually run in a two-hour meeting on each shortlisted proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision back to the trustees because the question is one of values rather than facts. I'd like the procedure to grapple honestly with the places where the framework bends — the fact that a cause's neglectedness score shifts the moment we fund it, that tractability estimates usually come from the applicants themselves, and that importance refuses to aggregate when you're comparing small certain harms against large unlikely ones. Then run it on three actual dilemmas from our current docket: a research programme on lead exposure in low-income countries, a community literacy scheme in our home city whose importance is modest but whose outcomes we could measure almost perfectly, and a speculative early-career grant programme in pandemic preparedness that may achieve nothing visible for a decade. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer the trustees will hate, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
