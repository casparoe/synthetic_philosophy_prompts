In debates over evolutionary psychology, people in my circle have taken to dismissing this or that proposed adaptation — a taste for music, reading, religious inclination — by announcing that the trait is just a spandrel, and that the proposer has committed the Gould–Lewontin error of assuming every visible feature must have a function. The underlying picture, as I understand it, comes from the architectural example in the 1979 Gould–Lewontin paper: the spandrels of San Marco are the roughly triangular spaces that necessarily appear where arches meet a dome, and although they are lavishly decorated, nobody supposes they were built in order to display mosaics; the decoration exploits a space that exists for structural reasons. The claim then transferred to biology is that traits can be necessary byproducts of other structures or of a body plan, rather than direct products of selection, and that adaptationists systematically mistake byproducts for adaptations.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Please give me a correspondence table first: what plays the role of the architect, of the dome-and-arches structure, of the spandrel itself, and of the mosaicist who decorates it after the fact, in the biological case? Then work through where the mapping breaks. Some candidate trouble spots I'd like you to address, though feel free to add others: an architect designs with foresight while selection has none, so what, if anything, corresponds to the dome being chosen first; spandrels are geometrically necessitated by the design in a way biological byproducts arguably are not — a byproduct in biology need only be developmentally or genetically correlated with something selected, which looks like a much weaker relation than architectural necessity; and there is the further wrinkle of exaptation, where a feature shaped for one use is co-opted for another, which is not the same as a pure byproduct acquiring a function.

Two further questions, please. First, does the disanalogy between necessitated byproducts and mere correlated byproducts matter for the polemical use my circle puts the term to, or is a spandrel in the loose biological sense enough to block any inference from usefulness to selected-for-ness? Second, and most important: does the conclusion people draw from the analogy — that calling something a spandrel defeats the adaptationist explanation, case closed — actually survive? It seems worth noting that a space can become load-bearing after renovation, and that the mosaicist's work still exists and still functions. So tell me whether the dismissal is licensed by the analogy, or whether the analogy was carrying the dismissal the whole time. If the spandrel picture is the wrong tool for either of these jobs, say what a better one would be — whether from Gould and Lewontin's own toolkit or your own construction — for the specific question of when it is a mistake to infer function from usefulness.
