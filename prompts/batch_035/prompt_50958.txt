I've been doing incident response at a big tech company for like 8 years now, so my native vocabulary is blameless postmortems, contributing factors, action items, error budgets, MTTR, "root cause" as a term we've basically banned. Somewhere I picked up that philosophers argue about something they call the "responsibility gap" -- the idea that when an autonomous system (say, a trading algo or a self-driving car) causes a serious harm, the usual conditions for holding anyone responsible come apart: the operator on shift couldn't reasonably foresee what the system would do, the designers are too remote in time and couldn't predict this failure mode out of all possible ones, the manufacturer didn't intend anything, and the system itself isn't the kind of thing that can be blamed. So there's this gap where the harm happens and nobody is properly *answerable* for it, and the philosophers disagree about whether the gap is real, whether it's just an artifact of how we individuate agency, and what to do about it (some say just accept it, some say manufacturers should be strictly liable, some say we should talk about "distributed" or "shared" responsibility, some even say we should make the systems themselves more like responsible agents).

Translate that whole debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. What does the gap look like in terms of contributing factors, counterfactual test failures, model cards, action items, and error budgets? Is "distributed responsibility" just what a postmortem already does when it lists twelve contributing factors, or is the philosophers' version claiming something my version doesn't? Then give me the residue report: which parts of their debate translate cleanly, which parts only translate if I distort them, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. My suspicion is the big one is *desert* -- we can say exactly who causally contributed and who should have built what guardrail, but my culture has no way at all to say who *deserved* it. Related question I actually argue about with collegues: is blameless postmortem culture itself a position in this debate (namely, that blame talk is noise that ruins learning, so the gap is a feature not a bug), or is it just a division of labor where blame quietly gets outsourced to HR, legal, and regulators? And finally: does the residue point to something my vocabulary is missing, or to the philosophers arguing about something less substantive than it looks? Be honest if some of it's the latter -- I don't want to import their confusion into my postmortems, but I also want to know if "blameless" is doing more philosophical work than we admit.
