I'm a distributed-systems engineer with no philosophy background beyond half a book. I work mostly on event-sourced services: instead of one authoritative mutable database, we keep an append-only log of events, and various replicas each build their own local state by replaying that log at their own pace, converging eventually. When I read about Dennett's multiple drafts model and his attack on the "Cartesian theater" — the idea he's against is that consciousness requires some single place where all the streams come together for an audience, and the idea he's for is that there are many parallel processes editing, revising, and out-competing one another, with no final screening room. My immediate reaction was: I already believe this. This is just event sourcing with neurons. No single point of serialization, content-addressed state built by replay, and "now" is just whatever the consumer happens to have processed so far.

I'd like you to take that reaction seriously rather than flatter it, by doing a systematic translation and then telling me where it breaks. Specifically:

1. Translate the multiple drafts model into my vocabulary as precisely as you can: what corresponds to the append-only log, what corresponds to the replicas, what corresponds to eventual consistency and to the "last write wins" heuristic, and where Dennett's "editing" and "narrative fixation" would sit. Where the fit is loose, say so rather than forcing it.

2. Do the same for the Cartesian theater itself — not as a strawman but as the strongest version of what Dennett is denying (some stage where contents become conscious all at once). Is there anything in my world that plays that role, or that my vocabulary would tempt me to invent? I ask because engineers have a bad habit of smuggling a "current state" table back in on top of the event log, and I wonder whether philosophers do the analogous thing.

3. Now the residue report. Dennett spends a lot of time on temporal anomalies — the color phi phenomenon, the cutaneous rabbit, the question of whether an experience was revised before or after it "happened," and his claim that there is no fact of the matter between the Orwellian and Stalinesque descriptions. Some of this seems to translate (out-of-order delivery, backfilling a consumer's view) and some doesn't. Tell me honestly what doesn't survive: is there anything in the debate about consciousness that has no counterpart in event-sourced systems at all? I suspect the first-person side is the problem — my replicas don't have anyone it is like anything to be, and no consumer whose "seeming" can be wrong in a way that matters. If that residue is real, I want to know whether it shows my vocabulary is missing something, or whether the philosophers' claim was less clear than it looked.

For the verdict itself: don't hedge into "both perspectives have value." Commit either to the translation substantially succeeding or substantially failing, and tell me the single consideration that would most change your verdict — a feature of the model I've misread, a disanalogy I haven't named, or a fact about event-sourced systems I'm misdescribing — so I know what to check. And flag the one step of the translation you're least confident in, since that's probably where the whole thing quietly gives way.
