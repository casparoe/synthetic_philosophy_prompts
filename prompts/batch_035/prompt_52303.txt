I spent thirty-five years as a spectroscopist before retiring, and the past two years I have spent reading historiography — Braudel, E.P. Thompson, Ginzburg, and the quarrels between the Annales school and the more traditional historians of courts and chanceries. My son is a historian of early modern diplomacy, and he tells me my latest argument commits an error I should have learned to avoid in the lab. Perhaps. But neither of us can locate the error precisely, and I would rather have a diagnosis than reassurance.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) A historical claim counts as knowledge only if the total evidence available to us — the surviving sources together with background knowledge — rules out the serious alternatives to it.
(2) The sources for the doings of states were produced almost entirely by the elites themselves: chronicles commissioned by rulers, diplomatic correspondence kept by chanceries, memoranda written by the very ministers whose decisions they purport to record.
(3) Such sources cannot distinguish between the story "the minister resolved on X and X then occurred" and the story "the minister recorded resolving on X while deeper material processes — harvests, prices, manpower — were already carrying events toward the same outcome," because both stories are consistent with every sentence the sources contain.
(4) When two stories fit the sources equally, and one of them invokes the unobserved mental lives of the powerful while the other appeals only to recoverable material constraints, parsimony directs us to prefer the material explanation.
(5) Therefore no attribution of causal efficacy to named individuals at the top — no claim that a king's choice or a minister's cunning made a difference — is ever historical knowledge.
(6) Therefore only history from below, resting on records produced independently of the persons it studies — parish registers, wage series, tithe accounts, muster rolls — can constitute knowledge of the past.

The conclusion seems to me to prove too much: it would annihilate not only the books my son writes but nearly every narrative I have enjoyed since retiring. My own suspicion is that step (4) is the corrupt one — in the lab we preferred the simpler model only when it fit the data at least as well, and "fewer kinds of causes" was never among our criteria — but when I proposed rejecting (3) instead, my son replied that if the sources could settle such questions, historians would never argue about them, and they argue about them constantly.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I would like to keep (1) if possible; I take it to state what knowing anything amounts to, in history as in spectroscopy. I am least attached to (6), which smells like a non sequitur to me, though I cannot say exactly why.

Since my instincts have already betrayed me once in this dispute, I want the answer to end by flagging its own fragility: state which consideration or counterargument would most plausibly change your verdict, and identify where in your diagnosis you are most likely to have gone wrong. Then close with a final line of exactly the form "Verdict: reject step N", with nothing after it.
