So here's my situation: I'm a journalist, mostly covering science and ideas, and in three weeks I'm giving a talk at a workshop on social epistemology where I'm basically the token non-academic. The organizers invited me because a longread I wrote last year got a lot of attention and a lot of angry email, and I'm going to defend its thesis, which is roughly this: there is no epistemically principled line between a "conspiracy theory" and an investigative lead. The line we actually draw is institutional -- the same claim, that powerful people coordinated in secret to deceive the public, counts as journalism when it comes from a newsroom with sources and as conspiracism when it comes from a blogger with none. And if the line is institutional rather than evidential, then the label "conspiracy theory" functions as a credibility pre-discount applied before any evidence is looked at, and my industry leans on it constantly, mostly to save ourselves work.

My supporting argument, for the objections to chew on: the probabilistic arguments people give against conspiracy theories (big secrets leak, too many participants, someone defects eventually) don't actually distinguish conspiracy theories from good leads. They distinguish successful conspiracies from failed ones. Watergate leaked. The tobacco industry cover-up leaked. They leaked after decades, through journalism that was initially dismissed as conspiracist thinking. The discount gets applied to the true ones and the false ones alike, which is exactly what you'd expect from a label doing social rather than epistemic work. I also want to say the unflatifiability problem -- that conspiracy theories absorb counterevidence by expanding the conspiracy -- applies just as well to institutional "nothing to see here" framing, but I'm honestly less sure how to press that one.

Now the audience. Mostly mainstream epistemologists who think conspiracy theorizing is irrational on ordinary evidential grounds -- expect the self-sealing reasoning point, the too-many-participants point, the base rate of actual conspiracies being tiny relative to conspiracy theories. Plus one or two social epistemology types who've written sympethetically about conspiracy theorizing being sometimes warranted and will give me friendly amendments that quietly gut my thesis. Plus one virtue epistemology person who'll want to talk about intellectual character and will probably be annoyed that my framing leaves no room for individual epistemic vice. No political scientists, no pollsters, nobody who studies radicalization -- the organizers had a guy like that drop out, which I think changes what I'll get hit with.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet. The eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way an epistemologist would actually phrase it (base rates, priors, unfalsifiability, the claim/theorist distinction). For each one: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede upfront because fighting it would cost me more than giving it up, and where my reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I have my own guess about which one it is but I don't want to bias you. And pay special attention to the sympathetic people: tell me which of their friendly amendments would quietly change my thesis into something weaker, because I suspect those are more dangerous than the hostile stuff. It's a 20-minute talk and a full hour of questions, so I'll have time to get in trouble.
