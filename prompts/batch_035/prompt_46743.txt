I'm taking a final-year course on algorithmic fairness, and in this week's seminar our lecturer defended risk scores of the sort used in sentencing and lending by analogy to weather forecasting. A probability estimate, she said, is doing its job just in case it is calibrated: when the score says 70%, roughly 70% of such cases should see the outcome, within every group, just as a meteorologist who announces a 70% chance of rain should be rained on 70% of the time she says it. From there she concluded that, since calibration is the whole of a forecaster's job, a calibrated risk score is a fair score, and complaints about false-positive-rate gaps between groups are asking the score to do something no honest forecaster could do.

The seminar then covered the well-known impossibility results showing that, when base rates differ between groups, a score that is calibrated within each group cannot also equalise false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, short of perfect prediction or equal base rates. So the analogy is doing real work in her argument: it's being used to claim the trade-off is not a scandal of badly built algorithms but simply the logic of honest forecasting, and that we should want the forecaster's virtue rather than the activist's.

Please audit the analogy for me. First, set out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the weather, the forecast, the forecaster, and the later verification? Then identify where the mapping breaks. Two candidates occurred to me, but treat them sceptically rather than as settled: a forecast does not change the weather, whereas a risk score feeds a decision — bail, a loan — that can shape the very outcome it estimates; and a forecast covers the whole sky for everyone, whereas a score is deployed against particular populations and may even determine which outcomes we ever get to observe. Then tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion and which are incidental, and whether the conclusion — that a calibrated score is thereby a fair score — survives once they are all on the table. If you think a better analogy is available for reasoning about scores of this kind, propose it and say what it gets right that the forecast picture gets wrong.
