I help run a monthly philosophy night at a bookstore in my town — usually about fifteen people, mostly retirees plus a couple of college students, smart and curious but no formal philosophy background, and a few of them come mainly because they like to argue. Next month it's my turn to lead, and I've decided to do Samuel Scheffler's idea from Death and the Afterlife (the one he calls the afterlife conjecture): that human extinction would be bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths it involves. What hooked me were his scenarios — one where an asteroid ends humanity right after the last currently living person dies a natural death, so nobody's life is shortened at all, and the infertility version in Children of Men territory, where everyone alive now (including anyone already on the way) lives out a full life but no new people are ever born. The thought being that alot of what we care about now — scientific work, art, justice movements, even raising kids — quietly presupposes that humanity keeps going after us, and that this suggests our own survival matters to us less, in one specific respect, than the continuation of the species. I get ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and no handouts, so it has to work as pure talking.

What I want from you isn't the lecture itself, it's the plan for the lecture. So: what has to be in place in the audience's head before the infertility scenario will even register as a puzzle rather than just sad — I suspect the "wait, who exactly is harmed here?" question needs handling almost immediately, but I'm not sure how to do that without losing the room in the first ten minutes. Which example should carry the whole thing, and why — is Children of Men too bleak to open with, and should I treat the asteroid case and the infertility case as two stages of one example or seperate examples doing different work? Then a map of the misconceptions I should expect, ideally in the order this particular crowd will actually produce them. My guesses are: the word "afterlife" itself dragging several of them (we have a few religious folks) toward personal immortality; people hearing the claim as "future people have rights against us now," which is not what Scheffler is saying; and the skeptic move that this is all just psychology — we'd *feel* despair, sure, but that doesn't show extinction is *bad*, period. Tell me where in the sequence each of those should be headed off, and whether there are others I'm not anticipating — I bet there's at least one I can't see. Finally, one question I can pose at the end that would let me tell whether the idea actually landed rather than everyone politely nodding, and one thing I should explicitly decline to get into at this level and say so out loud — I'm worried someone will pull us toward whether we owe future people existence at all, and I don't want to burn the last half hour on population ethics when we havent even gotten the main thought straight.
