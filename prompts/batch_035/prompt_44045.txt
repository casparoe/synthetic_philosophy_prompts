A friend and I spent most of an evening on the procreation asymmetry — the view that bringing into existence a child whose life would go badly counts as a reason against creating that child, whereas failing to bring into existence a child whose life would go well does not count as a reason in favor. We managed four substantive exchanges before giving up for the night, and I would like a verdict on each of them.

(1) He opened with the subject-based defense. A child created into misery is someone for whom things go badly; there is a person whom the creation harms. A child never created into happiness is no one — nothing is missing from anyone, because there is no one. Hence the asymmetry's two claims come apart exactly as the view says: the first act has a victim, the second has no beneficiary.

(2) I replied with a choice case. Suppose a couple can conceive now, when the resulting child would flourish, or wait six months, by which point a treatment the mother will have begun means the child conceived then would face a short and painful life. Everyone agrees they ought to conceive now. But on his reasoning, conceiving now is neither good nor bad — creating a happy person does no one any good — and conceiving later is bad. So the correct choice comes out as the mere absence of an evil, and the happy child's life contributes nothing positive to the outcome, even though choosing now looks like aiming at a good rather than merely dodging a harm.

(3) He answered that I had smuggled in a view about the value of outcomes. His claim concerns what prospective parents ought to do, not which possible world is better; philosophers reject the inference from "this act is wrong and that one is not" to "that act's outcome is better" all the time. The asymmetry, he said, is a constraint on procreation — a prohibition on creating suffering — and need not rest on any claim about sums of wellbeing. Refusing that premise is also what spares him, he noted, from the conclusion that a vast population whose lives are barely worth living outvalues a smaller population of excellent lives.

(4) I rejoined that this leaves the constraint ungrounded. If what makes creating the miserable child wrong is the badness of its suffering, then badness has come back in through the back door, and it is open to me to ask why the goodness of happiness cannot enter by the same door. If instead the constraint is basic — a rule against causing suffering with no matching rule for producing happiness — then the asymmetry no longer has anything to do with the future child's welfare, and the victim-and-beneficiary story he led with drops out entirely. He closed by observing that some moral asymmetries simply are basic — no one demands a proof that betraying someone is worse than being loyal is good — and by then it was late, so we stopped.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four numbered steps (and his closing remark), say who is right about what, and where either of us overreached. Then tell me whether his final appeal to basic, ungrounded asymmetries is a legitimate stopping point, and what, if anything, both of us missed.
