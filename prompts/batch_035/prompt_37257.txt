I sit on the clinical ethics committee at a county psychiatric hospital, and next month we have to settle our policy on psychiatric advance directives. The trigger case: a patient, "M.", with recurring psychotic episodes, signed a directive while well instructing that if she ever refuses antipsychotics during an episode, staff should override the refusal and treat her anyway. She even included a clause about what to say in the moment: she wants us to tell her that "the M. who signed this knew you'd be against it." Her lawyer says honoring the directive is exactly what autonomy requires — that its the whole point of a Ulysses contract. Her treatment team is uneasy: the woman in front of them, terrified and refusing, is a person with rights *now*, not a ghost being ruled by her past self. Our state law is no help — it says such directives are "presumptively valid" without saying when the presumption can be rebutted. Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend a concrete policy (honor, refuse, or honor with conditions), give the strongest version of the treatment team's objection a fair run, and take a position on the Ulysses analogy itself — including the wrinkle that Ulysses was giving orders to his crew, not asking a doctor to decide for him, and whether that matters. Also say something about the "tell her that M. of March agreed" clause specifically, since none of us can find any guidance on scripted responses like that anywhere.
