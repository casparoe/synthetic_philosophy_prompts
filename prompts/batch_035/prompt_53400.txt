I'm drafting a position paper for a workshop on causal reasoning in accident investigation, and I've come to suspect that many of our disputes about crash reports are really disguised disputes about what a theory of causation by omission has to deliver. Before I argue for any particular analysis, I want the specification settled. Please write it for me.

Act as a professional philosopher specialising in the metaphysics of causation, who also has practical familiarity with how causal attributions are made in accident investigation, epidemiology, and tort law. Before you compose anything, brainstorm: sketch the full space of candidate requirements, test each against the cases below, discard any that turn out to be mere prejudices of one camp rather than genuine demands, and only then write the final list.

The setting, so you know what the theory is for: our reports routinely attribute outcomes to things that didn't happen. The alarm that never sounded. The second inspector who was never hired. The rumble strip that was never installed on a road that predated the regulation requiring one — is that omission part of what caused the lane-departure fatality, given the road was grandfathered and no one breached any duty? When an outage at a data centre causes losses, we must decide whether to write that the standby generator's not being installed caused the losses, or merely enabled them, or neither. Reviewers dispute these attributions constantly and never say what would make one legitimate. Hence the desiderata.

Write out the requirements list — the cases an adequate account of omissive causation must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the results from neighbouring practice it cannot contradict — with the motivating consideration behind each. At minimum, the list should face up to the following:

- separating omissions that genuinely cause outcomes from mere background enabling conditions, and saying what licenses the distinction (the gardener's not watering the flowers versus some randomly selected stranger's not watering them);
- the notorious relativity to norms and expectations, and the question of whether a purely descriptive, non-normative account can recover which omissions count as causes, or whether normativity is load-bearing;
- preemption and preemption among omissions (two standby guards both asleep, either of whom would have raised the alarm had the other not failed first);
- double prevention and whether omissive double prevention should be treated better or worse than its event-causal cousin;
- transitivity failures and chained omissions;
- the problem of proliferating causes — if absences can cause, it seems everything that never happened is a candidate cause of nearly everything;
- fit with the routine causal claims of epidemiology and law, which are happy to say that a failure to fortify flour caused the neural-tube defects, or that a failure to fence the pool caused the drowning, however little these resemble paradigmatic causings.

Mark each desideratum non-negotiable or camp-relative, with a sentence of justification. Then — the part I most want — tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. Specifically: can the demand for a principled, non-arbitrary line between causing omissions and omissions that merely obtain be satisfied at the same time as the demand for the systematic, context-insensitive causal claims that an accident report needs, where two investigators must be able to agree rather than defer to which omissions they find salient? If you believe the joint satisfiability fails, say plainly what has to give, and what the cost is of each way of giving it up. If forced, I would sooner sacrifice fit with legal usage than systematicity, but tell me what that preference buys me and what it costs. Finally, and briefly only, score the three obvious contenders — the plain counterfactual-dependence treatment, the norm-involving accounts, and causal-model approaches that represent omissions explicitly — against the list; keep the weight on the criteria, not on the scorecard.

Please think carefully and structure the answer with headings. Answer in British English.
