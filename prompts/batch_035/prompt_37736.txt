I'm teaching the philosophy of language section of our intro course this semester, and I'm the one stuck writing the final exam. My students spent two weeks on Grice and mostly came away with two party tricks: reciting the four maxims from memory, and shouting "that's an implicature, not an entailment!" at anything that moves. So the essay question I have in mind needs to be built so that neither trick earns any real credit. It should be answerable in about 90 minutes, and I want it to force them into the actual hard part: whether Grice's account of conversational implicature is even consistent with his own definition of speaker meaning. He defines speaker meaning in terms of a reflexive intention -- I intend to get you to believe p by your recognizing that I intend exactly that -- but a conversational implicature is standardly generated by the hearer working out what must be assumed for the utterance to be cooperative. What happens when the speaker doesn't specifically intend the thing the hearer works out, or genuinely didn't foresee it? Is that still speaker meaning, or has the account quietly come apart? I want the question to make them pick a side on this and defend it, not just gesture at "the Cooperative Principle."

I'd also like the question to include a test sentence they've never seen, so they have to actually apply the machinery rather than pattern-match to examples from lecture. Here's the one I want to use, or something along these lines if you think it can be improved: "Look, even my nine-year-old figured out Dan was lying, and she only met him twice." I'm imagining the question asks them to classify every contribution this sentence makes -- what's entailed, what's presupposed (factive verbs should come up), what carries a conventional implicature (the "even" and arguably the "only"), and what's conversationally implicated (something like: the evidence was overwhelming, so adults have no excuse) -- and then to say for each classification what test they'd use to defend it, with the cancellation test doing real work rather than being name-dropped. If one of those labels is actually contested or messy, that's a feature, I want the good students to notice the mess.

So the deliverables: the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points built around maybe five must-hit moves, with a note for each on how students typically botch it. Please make the scheme actually distinguish an A answer from a B answer -- I want it to name the specific move that separates them (my guess is the B answers will classify the sentence correctly and then never touch the speaker-meaning tension at all, but you tell me if the real dividing line is somewhere else). And if you think the test sentence is overloaded or one of the phenomena in it is ambiguous in a way that would just create grading headaches, flag that and propose a fix. I have to have this to the course coordinator by Friday and I'd rather not find out at the grading meeting that my own question has a hole in it.
