I'm giving a commentary at a small symposium our department runs each spring where two people present their current positions and someone else gets to poke at them, and this year I'm the target. We're supposed to circulate a short statement of where we stand beforehand. Mine is on assertion, and writing it up I've started to suspect I've assembled a position that doesn't exactly cohere, but I genuinely can't tell whether the problems are real or just me being paranoid. Here's what I've committed to: (1) knowledge is the norm of assertion, and it's constitutive -- a rule that makes assertions assertions, not just a standard we happen to apply to them, the way I'd want to distinguish assertion from mere guess or conjecture; (2) invariantism about knowledge itself, so whether you know that p has nothing to do with your practical situation; (3) a firm intuition that in the high-stakes case -- the one where somebody tells you their bank account will be drained unless you act now and you assert "your money is safe" on decent but unconfirmed evidence -- the asserter is doing something improper even though, by my own lights, they flat-out know the proposition; (4) selfless assertions can be fine: the schoolteacher who has lost her faith but keeps teaching the material and asserts it all with perfect propriety in front of her class doesn't know what she's asserting (she doesn't even believe it), yet she's not violating any norm I care about; (5) Moorean utterances of the form "p, but I don't know that p" are always somehow self-undermining, and I think the knowledge norm is the best explanation of that; and (6) whatever norm governs assertion, the same norm should govern acting on a premise in practical reasoning, since asserting to someone and acting for yourself seem like two deployments of one competence.

So that's the profile I'm planning to circulate. What I want before I send it around: go through the pairs and tell me which are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and for the real ones rank them by severity rather than handing me a flat list of "issues." For each genuine one, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair is -- cheapest in the sense of preserving the most of the overall picture, not just the fewest words changed. And at the end I'd like your single recommendation: if I revise exactly one commitment, which one cleans up the most mess? I have a sinking feeling about (3) and (6) interacting in some way I haven't fully thought through, since if stakes affect when you can assert but not whether you know, and acting is supposed to parallel asserting, I might have paid for invariantism with the wrong currency. But please don't just confirm my suspicion -- check the whole set, including the parts I haven't flagged.

A couple of constraints on the audit: don't tell me to just read more and become a pluralist about norms, that's not a fix, that's a surrender. And if you think one of my "commitments" is really two commitments smuggled into one clause (I worry about (1) doing double duty between the norm's content and its constitutive status), split it and tell me which half I actually need. Also worth knowing: the audience will include a couple of grad students who are not going to let me get away with hand-waving the bank case, so whatever you say about it, say it like I'll have to defend it out loud in a Q&A. typos are fine, conviction is required, hedging my way through the symposium is what I'm trying to avoid.
