hey, taking a philosophy of technology seminar right now and we just did a unit on automation, and there's something I keep bumping into that the readings don't really address. In the US, a bunch of welfare programs have work requirments — e.g. able-bodied adults on SNAP generally have to be working or job-searching a certain number of hours a week to keep the benefit, and politicians defend this by saying work is what gives people dignity and skin in the game, that just giving people money for nothing erodes them. But a lot of the same people will turn around and celebrate automation when it eliminates jobs, on the grounds that it frees people from drudgery. So my question is: what view of the value of work does the work-requirement side actually presuppose? Is it that work is intrinsically dignity-conferring, or only instrumentally valuable (self-sufficiency, not being a burden on others), or is it a desert thing where benefits have to be earned? And then: can any of these views stay consistent with cheering on job-destroying automation, or does the practice quietly rule out some of the views it usually gets defended with? Also curious which parts of the policy are actually forced by the presupposed view versus which parts several different views of work's value would all license. And if you think the real presupposition is the instrumental one, say what the policy would have to look like if we took the dignity view seriously instead — would we have to start funding make-work jobs, and is that even coherent?
