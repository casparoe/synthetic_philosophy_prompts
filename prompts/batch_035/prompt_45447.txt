I'm a PhD student working on the alignment of large language models, and I keep circling an odd tension in how the industry actually elicits values from humans. I'd like someone to reconstruct the implicit theory rather than restate the marketing.

The standard pipeline is roughly this: contractors hired by a lab compare pairs of model outputs and mark which is better; their judgments are treated as votes and aggregated, typically by training a reward model on the comparisons; the policy is then optimized against that reward. But layered on top are several curatorial moves that refuse to treat those judgments as raw data about value. Annotators are screened, trained on written guidelines, and occasionally removed for deviating from them too systematically; persistent disagreements among annotators are resolved not by counting noses but by revising the guidelines themselves; and a separate stage critiques and revises outputs against a published list of principles drawn partly from human-rights instruments, in some variants replacing human feedback entirely with model-generated feedback graded against those principles. So the practice simultaneously claims to learn human values empirically and declines to defer to whatever the humans involved, taken in aggregate, actually prefer.

What theory of value aggregation does this presuppose? Preference utilitarianism with an expert correction term? Something like Rawlsian liberal neutrality, assembling an overlapping consensus out of documents no one fully endorses? Trusteeship, in which the lab stands to its users as a guardian to a ward? Please reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, say which rival views the practice quietly rules out, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined (that is, which genuinely different theories would still license the same pipeline), and explain what would have to change in the pipeline if a lab committed wholeheartedly to straight majority aggregation of uncurated user preferences instead.
