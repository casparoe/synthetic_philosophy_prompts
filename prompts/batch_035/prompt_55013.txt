For the past several years I've been working through Madhyamaka entirely on my own — no formal training, just reading, sitting with it, and arguing with myself in notebooks — and next month I've been asked to lead a two-hour session at our local meditation group on emptiness and the tetralemma (the fourfold negation: it is, it is not, it both is and is not, it neither is nor is not). The attendees are mostly long-time practitioners with no philosophy background, and I know from past sessions that several of them quietly take emptiness to mean that the world is an illusion or a kind of cosmic void. I don't want you to explain emptiness to me; I want a plan for the session.

Concretely, I'd like you to address the following. First, the order of ideas: I keep going back and forth on whether to introduce dependent arising before emptiness or the other way around, and on whether the tetralemma should come at the beginning as a puzzle or at the end as a payoff. What has to be in place before what, and why — where are the dependencies that, if I get the sequence wrong, will make everything downstream sound like mysticism? Second, the carrying example: I've always reached for the old chariot-and-its-parts illustration, but I worry it's both worn out from overuse and quietly misleading (a chariot is at least a heap of parts standing in a row; whatever emptiness is supposed to claim, it doesn't seem to be just that). Should I keep it, and if not, what single example should carry the whole session, and what must that example have and lack? Third, the misconceptions: besides the void/illusion reading, I anticipate people treating the four corners of the tetralemma as a menu of positions and picking "neither" as the clever fourth option, as if "neither true nor false" were itself a doctrine to believe; people folding emptiness into the two truths so that conventional truth becomes a lie and ultimate truth becomes the real story about a hidden reality; and people hearing "no intrinsic nature" as "nothing matters." Sketch the objection map in the order they'll actually raise these, and tell me where in the sequence each one should be absorbed rather than rebutted at the end. Fourth, what to leave out: I assume the later scholastic refinements and the sectarian disputes belong to a different session — say openly what I should refuse to cover at this level, and how to say so without making people feel the topic was dumbed down. Finally, give me one question I could pose in the last ten minutes whose answer would tell me whether they've actually grasped the idea rather than just nodded along, and tell me what a good answer would look like and what the telltale sound of the void-misreading would be.
