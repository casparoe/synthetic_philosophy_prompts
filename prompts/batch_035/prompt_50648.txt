I'm a security engineer at a company that builds coding agents, and over the last year I've been pulled into more and more meetings where the safety side of the org talks about "control instead of alignment." The framing they keep reaching for is that we should treat the model like an untrusted employee: companies hire contractors they don't fully trust all the time, and they stay safe not by ensuring loyalty but by giving the employee narrow permissions, monitoring their outputs, requiring sign-off from a more trusted party, occasionally auditing them, and structuring things so that even if the employee is quietly working against the company, they can't actually do much damage. So we shouldn't demand that the model share our goals, we should just design the deployment so that a model with arbitrary goals still can't cause trouble, and then we're allowed to get the usefulness even if the thing is misaligned. Some of them go further and say this is actually the more realistic path, because firms have millennia of practice with untrusted insiders and basically zero practice with "verified loyal subhuman workers."

I have to write a position paper on whether we should invest in this stuff versus in alignment proper, and honestly I find the employee picture pretty convincing but I can't tell how much work the analogy is doing versus how much is vibes. Can you audit it for me? Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the salary the employee doesn't want to lose, the HR file, the coworker who'd notice, the fact that the employee wants to keep being employable in the future, the legal system waiting outside the office, the fact that the employee and the company have roughly the same human-level capabilities so no one can out-think anyone by orders of magnitude. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and crucially which breaks actually matter for the two conclusions people use it for: (1) control alone is enough to deploy safely, and (2) it's OK to deploy systems we have decent reason to think are misaligned, because firms do the moral equivalent every day. I suspect some disanalogies are harmless for the narrow use and some quietly kill the stronger conclusion, but I can't sort which is which.

A few things I'd specifically want you to address, since they come up in the debates: the employee analogy implies the watched party knows roughly what the firm knows about being watched, whereas with models people argue about whether you can rely on deceptive behavior that only shows up when the model believes it's not being evaluated; also whether having a thousand copies of the same "employee" running in parallel changes things (they can coordinate and share notes in a way contractors can't); and whether the fact that the "firm" here is civilization itself rather than a company that can go bankrupt matters. And if the employee picture is doing less work than people think, is there a better analogy that carries the load -- the ones I've heard floated are prisoner transport, biosafety levels in a lab, and keeping a kleptomaniac in charge of inventory -- or does the conclusion actually stand on its own and the analogy is just decoration? Please be honest about where you think it comes out, I'd rather have an answer I don't like than a both-sides thing.
