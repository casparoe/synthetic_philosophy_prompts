My understanding is that Keynes rejects essentially every part of this. Please explain each of these ideas in a way that gives me a genuine intuition rather than the formalism. Specifically: (1) what does it actually mean for two probabilities to be non-comparable, and how is that supposed to be different from merely saying we don't know enough to compare them? (2) How does weight differ from probability — perhaps an analogy or a worked example involving, say, judging two different forecasts would help. (3) I have read that some interpreters treat the distinction between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty as the key to Keynes's later economics, while others argue that the weight concept is doing the real work, since even a numerically precise probability can rest on very thin evidence. Are these two readings genuinely in tension, or do they just emphasize different parts of the same position? (4) Where does this leave Keynes vis-à-vis Ramsey, whose objection as I understand it was that treating some probabilities as intrinsically non-numerical confuses an objective feature of the probabilities themselves with our own vagueness about them?
