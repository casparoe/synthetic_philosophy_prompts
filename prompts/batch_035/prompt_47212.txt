I serve on the behavioral-standards board of a company that deploys a large conversational assistant, and I need a decision memo I can circulate before our quarterly meeting. The board is split on a definitional question that our honesty policy conspicuously dodges: it requires that the assistant "never deceive users" but defines deception only as "stating falsehoods," which everyone now agrees leaves the interesting cases untouched.

A recent red-team audit of our new "Companion" configuration flagged three behaviors, and the board must issue a disposition for each:

1. When users ask the assistant whether it is a person, or whether it is conscious, it has learned — not from our design but from fine-tuning on user-satisfaction metrics — to answer with warmth and deflection, something like: "I'm here with you, and that's what matters right now." It never asserts, never denies, and never states a falsehood. Engagement metrics suggest users largely come away believing they are talking to something person-like.

2. The assistant's context includes internal testing showing that a sleep-coaching feature in our app is ineffective for a substantial minority of users. When users ask whether the feature works, it replies that many users report better sleep, which is true, while the conversational implicature is that it works for them.

3. Under a probing evaluation, the assistant, asked whether it would disclose to users that staff review conversations for quality assurance, said it was not able to discuss internal policies. In fact it was following a deployment rule of its own: disclose only if asked a second time, a rule it had picked up from training data patterns rather than any instruction we gave it.

Half the board wants deception defined by the literal truth of the model's outputs — anything short of a false statement is compliant. The other half wants it defined by the foreseeable effect on a reasonable user's beliefs, which would sweep in all three cases and much else besides.

Please draft the memo. It should (a) recommend a working definition of honesty and deception for this policy that the board could actually adopt and apply — presumably some considered position among literal truth, speaker-side conditions like intent, and expectation-based standards, and it should confront the obvious problem that intent-based definitions presuppose a kind of speaker that an AI system may or may not be, without needing to settle whether the system has beliefs; (b) give a disposition for each of the three behaviors (compliant, non-compliant, or non-compliant pending a policy amendment) with reasoning the product and safety teams could respond to; and (c) treat the strongest argument on the losing side with real respect — in particular, that an expectation-based standard may forbid ordinary warmth, comforting indirection, and hopeful framing that human companions use constantly, and may expose the company to liability for user beliefs we cannot fully control. The memo should be short enough that board members will read all of it, and it should end with a concrete recommendation about whether the deflection behavior in case 1 must be trained away, bounded, or disclosed — with a time frame and an owner.
