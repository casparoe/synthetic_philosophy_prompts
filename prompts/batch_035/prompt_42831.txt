the question on the worksheet was: "Explain what a pramāṇa is. List the pramāṇas accepted by the Nyāda school and briefly say what each one is. Then explain how Nyāya's list compares with that of the Cārvāka materialists, with the Buddhists, and with schools that accept more than four, and give the Nyāya reasoning for stopping at four."

here's the answer i have to grade:

"A pramāṇa is a reliable source or means of knowledge — literally that which produces pramā, true cognition, as opposed to error. The Nyāya school, one of the six orthodox schools, accepts exactly four. Pratyakṣa is perception, and for Nyāya genuine perception requires actual contact between the object and the sense organs, which is why hallucinations don't count. Anumāna is inference, the classic case being: there is fire on the hill, because there is smoke; wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen; this hill has smoke; therefore there is fire on the hill. Upamāna is comparison — the textbook example is someone who is told that a gavaya (a kind of wild ox) is like a cow, and who, on later seeing the animal, comes to know what the word 'gavaya' refers to. Śabda is verbal testimony, and for Nyāya this means any statement you understand, spoken or written — so when I learn a fact from a textbook or a teacher, that is śabda operating, since the sentence is graspable.

The Cārvākas accept only perception, rejecting inference on the grounds that establishing a universal connection (vyāpti) would require checking every case and leads to a regress. The Buddhists accept only perception and inference, holding that testimony is not an independent pramāṇa. Advaita Vedānta accepts six, adding postulation (arthāpatti) — e.g., a person known to be alive but not in the house must be out somewhere, since otherwise his being alive wouldn't make sense — and non-apprehension (anupalabdhi), knowing something is absent because you fail to perceive it where it would be visible.

Nyāya's argument for stopping at four is that the extra candidates, like postulation and non-apprehension, turn out on analysis to be forms of inference. And in the end all four reduce to perception anyway: testimony and comparison work through language, and language is something we originally had to learn through our senses. So at the bottom of the chain perception is doing all the work, and in that deeper sense Nyāya ends up agreeing with the Cārvāka more than it would like to admit."
