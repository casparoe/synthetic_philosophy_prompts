I've spent two years in a reading group where the evolutionary psychology wars get re-fought monthly, and the striking thing is how little the actual methodological dispute moves. Everyone on both sides now agrees on the platitudes: adaptationist hypotheses must generate testable predictions, byproducts and constraints are real alternatives to adaptation, and post hoc flexibility is a vice. Gould and Lewontin won the rhetoric of the 1979 spandrels paper in the sense that even the most committed adaptationists disavow Panglossian storytelling in principle — and yet the same fights recur over cheater-detection modules, mating-preference findings, and cross-cultural psychological claims, with nobody conceding anything. My hypothesis is that the surface methodological dispute masks a deeper one about default priors: each side agrees what would count as evidence in the abstract, but they enter every case with different background assumptions about how often complex, finely tuned psychological traits are likely to be adaptations versus byproducts, and neither prior is itself testable in the way both sides demand of hypotheses. Is that right, or is the real disagreement something else — divergent standards for what a 'prediction' is, disciplinary turf wars, or smuggled-in political commitments about human nature? Diagnose the structure of the dispute using what the parties actually claim, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a methodological innovation, a shared evidential threshold, or merely accepting that the disagreement is one about reasonable priors and not about science at all.
