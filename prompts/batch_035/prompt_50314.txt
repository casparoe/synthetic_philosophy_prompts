I'm a second-year taking a philosophy module on decision theory, and last Thursday after the seminar a few of us got stuck on the St Petersburg thing and argued about it for like an hour in the kitchen. I'll try to reconstruct the actual back-and-forth because I'd like someone to referee it, since I genuinely couldn't work out who was winning.

Quick setup for anyone who needs it: the game is you flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if heads first shows up on flip n you win £2 to the power n. So £2 for heads straight away, £4 for tails-then-heads, £8 for T-T-H, etc. Expected value is a half times 2 plus a quarter times 4 plus an eighth times 8... every term is 1, so it sums to infinity.

Round 1: Dan said the obvious conclusion is you should be willing to pay any finite price to play — £10, £10,000, a million, whatever, since any finite amount is less than infinity. Jess said that was obviously daft and shows the maths is broken somewhere. Dan said no, the maths is airtight, it's just a sum, so either decision theory tells you to do something insane or you have to say what replaces expected value here.

Round 2: Jess's first patch was the practical one: no actual casino has infinite money, the payout is capped by the house's bankroll, so the expected value is finite in any real situation, and once you do the sums honestly it comes out to something modest like a few hundred quid tops. She said the infinite EV is an artifact of pretending the counterparty has unlimited funds. Dan said fine, but that feels like dodging rather than solving — the maths says infinity, and "nature won't let the game be offered" isn't an answer to the mathematical game.

Round 3: Jess then went theoretical: she said money has diminishing marginal utility, so you don't value £2^n the way the naive sum assumes — the utility of doubling your money grows much slower than the money itself — and once you compute expected utility rather than expected money, the value of playing becomes finite and actually pretty small, which matches the small amount people would actually pay. Dan came back with a tweak: change the payoffs so they grow fast enough (instead of £2^n make it, say, £2 to the power of 2 to the power n, or whatever grows quicker than the utility function) and the expected utility is infinite again. So he said her patch doesn't kill the paradox, it just sets up a rematch — for any utility function you pick, I can build a nastier game. Jess said that's true but it tells you something real: the paradox is now about utility, not money, and maybe that's progress.

Round 4: Jess's last move was the one I couldn't shake. She said probabilities like one in a million billion basically don't enter into anyone's deliberation, and treating every positive probability as equally real is a modelling choice, not a law of nature — so decision theory as we've set it up just breaks when the numbers get extreme enough, and that's a fact about the framework, not about us being irrational. Dan said if your formal theory of rational choice returns "no answer" or "absurd answer" whenever numbers get big, that's the theory failing, and you can't fix it by shrugging at the math and saying humans don't think about tiny probabilities, because then what is the theory even for?

They basically called it a draw and went back to their reading. Can you go through the exchange round by round and say who's right about what? Especially: is Jess right that the bankroll cap dissolves the puzzle, or only the physical version of it? Does the payoff-tweaking move in round 3 actually work, or did Dan overstate it? And is there a principled answer to round 4, or does decision theory genuinely have nothing to say once the expected value is infinite? I'd also take a verdict on who was more right overall.
