I'm a novelist about four chapters into a book where my narrator, a retired myrmecologist named Marisol, becomes convinced that the colony of leafcutters she's been observing for thirty years is not just an organism but a mind in its own right — a someone, not a something. The whole plot hinges on whether the reader finds her position genuinely defensible or just the sad delusion of a lonely woman, and I want to lean toward defensible, so I need the actual philosophical spine to be solid, not hand-wavy. The obvious touchstone here is the old Hofstadter dialogue where an anteater chats with an ant colony named Aunt Hillary while the other characters sputter in disbelief, and I want Marisol to be able to give something like the anteater's argument but updated and more careful.

So here's what I'd like from you: make the best case you can that a single ant colony could literally be a subject of experience — have beliefs, feel pain, maybe even converse in some attenuated sense — and not merely be a nicely coordinated superorganism that we're pattern-matching onto personhood. The hard parts I need you to actually grapple with rather than wave past: (1) unlike neurons, individual ants are themselves agents with their own little interests and sensory lives, so the colony-mind would have to be a mind built out of miniature minds, and it's not obvious a mind can be constituted by things that are already somebody; (2) the parts keep dying and being replaced while the putative mind persists, which is maybe fine for a body but feels weirder for a self; and (3) the argument risks proving too much — if coordination across simple agents is enough for mentality, aren't markets, ecosystems, and cities also minds, which seems like a reductio. If you think one of these objections can't actually be answered, say so rather than fudging it, since Marisol is allowed to be a bit overconfident but I don't want her to be dumb.

One more wrinkle, since it's important for the character: Marisol is an atheist and a hard-nosed materialist, so I'd rather the argument not smuggle in any spooky vitalism or group-soul stuff — it should work, if it works, on entirely physicalist and functionalist grounds. And please don't give me a reading list or tell me who else has written on this; I can't go to a library just now, I just want the argument itself laid out. Thanks so much, this is my first time trying to write a philosopher-character who's suppose to be genuinely good at philosophy, and its terrifying.
