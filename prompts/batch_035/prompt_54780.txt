I am the secretary of my school's robotics club, and we are in the middle of what our faculty advisor half-jokingly calls a constitutional crisis. Our club constitution contains the following clause: "Any member who is absent from three consecutive regular meetings without prior excuse shall be removed from the active roster, and only active members may compete at regionals or run for office." Last month, one of our members missed three consecutive meetings because she was representing our school at a week-long state science and engineering fair. Before leaving, she emailed the faculty advisor explaining where she would be, and the advisor replied that it was fine. However, a separate bylaw states that excuses "must be submitted to the Vice President no later than 48 hours before the meeting in question," and she did not do this, since she assumed the advisor's approval was sufficient. The Vice President, who keeps attendance, has now removed her from the active roster, which disqualifies her from running for club president next month — an office nearly everyone expected her to win.

Our club has split into two camps, and I am caught in the middle because I keep the records. The first camp, led by our current president, argues that a constitution has to mean exactly what it says or it means nothing at all: she was absent from three consecutive meetings, her excuse did not go to the officer the bylaws name, so the consequence follows automatically, and if we let ourselves bend the rule because we happen to like the person, the document becomes worthless. The second camp argues that the clause obviously exists for a reason — to stop people from drifting away and losing interest in the club — and that using it against someone who was away *because* she was doing school-sponsored academic work defeats the entire point; they say punishing her this way is like a rule against running in the hallways being used against a student fleeing a fire. The president replies that everyone always has a reason the rule shouldn't apply to them, and that the moment you consult "the spirit of the rule," you are really just consulting your own preferences dressed up as interpretation.

I have to write up the minutes for the meeting where this gets decided, and honestly I am not sure which side is right — both arguments feel solid until I put them next to each other. Could you help me think it through? I want more than "there are two sides": tell me which side you actually come down on, given the specific wording of our clause and the facts I've described, and explain what really drives your answer — is it the text, the purpose, the fact that she notified *someone* official, or something else entirely? Please also tell me what would change your mind: what fact about the situation, the rule's history, or the arguments themselves, if it turned out to be true, would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong. I would rather bring a well-reasoned position to the meeting than a coin flip.
