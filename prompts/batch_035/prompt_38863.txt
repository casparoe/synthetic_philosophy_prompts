Write an essay on whether precommitment by machine can rescue the credibility of nuclear deterrence. The familiar paradox: a threat of massive retaliation is supposed to deter a first strike, but once the strike has landed, carrying out the threat serves no further purpose — so a rational adversary can discount the threat accordingly. Two remedies are on the table. One is hardware: the Soviet Union reportedly built, near the end of the Cold War, a semi-automated system (Perimeter, often nicknamed the Dead Hand) intended to launch a retaliation even if the entire leadership had been destroyed. The other, sometimes treated as its software equivalent, is Schelling's idea of deliberately leaving something to chance: keeping the process of escalation only partly under anyone's deliberate control. Assess whether such devices actually solve the credibility problem or merely relocate it — after all, someone must still decide to build the machine, keep it armed, and believe it will work, and the adversary knows all of this. Weigh both sides and reach a definite verdict; a qualified conclusion is acceptable only if you specify exactly which respects the remedy works in and which it fails in, and why.
