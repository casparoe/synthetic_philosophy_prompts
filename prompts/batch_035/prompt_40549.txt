I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting turns on a single argument scene. My protagonist is a retired actuary, a lifelong secularist now in her final months of illness, whose daughter has joined a small ascetic religious order; the daughter comes to the hospice and, gently but seriously, presses something like Pascal's wager on her mother — live as if the order's eschatology were true, observe its devotions for the months that remain, and in exchange accept a chance at an eternity that dwarfs everything else at stake. I want the mother to refuse, and I need her refusal to be intellectually honorable: the scene will fail if readers see the daughter as a fool or the mother as a coward. I could always rig it the standard way — invent rival sects with rival eternities so the many-gods objection does the work — but that has been done a thousand times, and I want the scene to have philosophical teeth even for readers who know the literature.

So I'd like you to construct the thought experiment underlying this scene, to the following specifications. The case must preserve the Wager's decision structure: the cost of wagering is modest but real — it genuinely reorganizes what remains of the protagonist's life, it is not a costless gesture — while the promised payoff is of a different order of magnitude from every other consideration in play. Since I am the author and may stipulate the background, build the case so that exactly one dominant-payoff hypothesis is in play and the many-gods objection gets no purchase. The probability of the payoff must be genuinely estimable and not insultingly small: the protagonist, being a professional, assigns it something like one in a few thousand — an actuarial-style figure she cannot in conscience dismiss, of the sort her trade prices every day. And yet she must be reasonable in refusing. Her refusal has to fall out of a principled decision-theoretic worry that a serious philosopher would recognize — something in the neighborhood of whether expected-value reasoning is even well-behaved when payoffs of this size enter the arithmetic — and not from the cheap moves: not from the probability being secretly zero, not from rival gods, not from the cost being ruinous, and not from a stipulated character flaw. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, keep it realistic, and let the fiction never reveal which way the world actually goes.

After the scenario, walk me through the machinery. Tell me what unrestricted expected-value maximization says and why; tell me how the most natural Pascalian repair — whether by dominance reasoning, by trimming the payoff down to something merely enormous, or by whatever route you think is strongest — fares in this specific case and where it leaks; and identify which single feature of the construction is doing the work, so I know what the scene is really about. Then tell me what happens if I remove that feature: does the case collapse back into the ordinary Wager, where refusing looks unreasonable after all? Finally, tell me how a defender of the wager would attack the case, and give me your honest judgment on whether the scene, built this way, will strike a philosophically literate reader as fair to both characters. I should add what the case is for, since it fixes what counts as cheating: the scenario has to generate the refusal from the decision theory, not stipulate it — if the only way to make the mother reasonable is to quietly tilt the setup, my readers, who are sharper than I am, will smell it.
