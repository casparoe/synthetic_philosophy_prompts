I'm part of a reading group on the ethics of blame, and at our last meeting the topic was whether a person's own misconduct affects what she is entitled to say about someone else's. The case on the table: a colleague of ours who is notorious for gossiping about other members of the department took another colleague aside and rebuked her for gossiping about him. One participant argued the rebuke was entirely in order — the criticism was accurate, the harm to him was real, and his own gossiping was beside the point when it came to whether he was entitled to make it. Another replied that this gets something backwards: the rebuke wasn't false, but it was somehow improper, a case of the pot calling the kettle black, and she closed the exchange by remarking that those without sin should be the ones casting stones. Everyone nodded, but it struck me that the proverb had been made to do several quite different jobs in a single breath, and I'd like to work out which of those jobs, if any, it can actually perform. Please lay out, as a numbered list I can bring back to the group, the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be advancing, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is plausible and whether it would actually support setting aside the rebuke as improper. Throughout, I'd like you to keep separate the readings that concern the accuracy of the criticism from the readings that concern the critic's entitlement to voice it, since our first participant only ever denied that the latter was damaged, and to flag explicitly any reading on which the proverb amounts to a global skepticism about anyone's ever being entitled to blame anyone for anything. Finally, please mark which readings, if accepted, would yield only the verdicts that the rebuke was obnoxious or strategically self-defeating, rather than that it was impermissible — our group treats those as importantly different outcomes, and I want to know which of them, if any, the proverb really delivers.
