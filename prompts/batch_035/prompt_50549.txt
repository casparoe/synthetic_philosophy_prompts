You are a professional satirist with graduate training in moral and social philosophy, writing on assignment. Please approach this as carefully as you would a commissioned piece: brainstorm candidate material first, then select and refine what you will actually publish.

Write a satirical pamphlet for a fictional organization — the International Office for Convention Compliance (IOCC), which certifies travelers as morally compliant tourists. The pamphlet consists of exactly five numbered commandments addressed to visitors, each a sentence or two, with no introduction, conclusion, or commentary of any kind. The venue is a government-issue leaflet found in airport arrivals halls, written in the flat, reassuring, faintly proud voice of a national tourist board.

The doctrine you are satirizing is convention absolutism: the view that every local convention — tipping percentages, queueing disciplines, jaywalking rules — is a strict moral obligation binding on visitors, whatever its content and whatever the visitor's home norms may be. The satire must work as a reductio: by following the doctrine faithfully and deadpan to its conclusions, not by mocking it from outside. Exaggerate real features of the position rather than inventing new ones, so that a philosopher who works on conventions finds the piece fair. In particular, the commandments should land the following considerations without ever stating them outright:

1. Conventions in David Lewis's sense are arbitrary solutions to coordination problems — some other arrangement would have worked about as well — and the code should press hard on the tension between that arbitrariness in origin and the absolute bindingness the doctrine claims in force.
2. A visitor often cannot tell which local practices solve genuine coordination problems and which are mere etiquette, and the code must handle this epistemic gap in a way that makes the gap disappear (or worse).
3. Conventions differ from place to place and sometimes reverse across a border, so convention absolutism makes moral requirements indexed to geography — the code should imply that moral transformation occurs at customs, without acknowledging that this is strange.
4. Tipping sits awkwardly between gratuity and wage-substitute, so that "optional in principle, obligatory in practice" is doing enormous work — the code should rely on that phrase's instability while appearing serene about it.
5. Violations that harm no one — crossing a completely empty street at three in the morning — are standardly justified by appeal to norm erosion, and the code should press that justification until each individual tourist is made personally responsible for the entire edifice of civil order.

Additional rules for the piece: keep the irony deadpan but legible, so that a reader can identify what is being ironized without the author winking; make the humor come from getting the philosophy right rather than from zaniness or invented absurdities; do not name or target any living person; and do not step outside the IOCC's voice to evaluate the doctrine — the five commandments must do all the critical work themselves.

Before writing, sketch several candidate commandments and check each for consistency with the doctrine's own logic — the IOCC must never contradict itself, since internal consistency is part of the joke. But output only the five numbered commandments, each a sentence or two, with nothing before or after them.
