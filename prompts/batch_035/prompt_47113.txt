I'd like your read on a running argument my friend and I have. She believes in karma, and her version of it is explicitly a theory of moral desert: roughly, the harm you are responsible for comes back to you in proportion to that responsibility, and the good you are responsible for comes back to you likewise. She has issued verdicts on cases as they have come up over the years, and I have kept a list:

1. A hunter who kills animals for sport and relishes it: heavy bad karma.
2. A committed vegetarian who, without knowing, is served a dish containing meat at a friend's house: no karma at all.
3. A slaughterhouse worker who does the job without relish because it is the only work available to him: bad karma, but far lighter than the sport hunter's.
4. An attentive driver who kills a child that darts into the road when stopping in time is impossible: no karma.
5. A person who, in genuine fear, shoves an armed mugger, who falls, strikes his head, and dies: no karma.
6. Someone who pays a third party to injure an enemy, doing none of it himself: bad karma, nearly as heavy as if he had done it with his own hands.
7. A donor who gives generously to charity chiefly to be admired, with little real concern for the recipients: some good karma, but much less than the same gift given anonymously and from the heart.

Four new cases are now on the table, and I want your prediction of her verdicts before we discuss them:

A. A man who spends his final decade nursing a grudge, drafting and refining an elaborate plan of revenge in obsessive detail, but who never acts on it and dies without having harmed anyone.
B. A juror who conscientiously votes to convict on testimony that later turns out to be perjured; the innocent defendant spends twenty years in prison.
C. An heir who inherits a fortune built on his grandfather's brutal exploitation of workers, harms no one, lives comfortably off the income, and donates generously from it.
D. A driver who kills a pedestrian while texting, is immediately and sincerely remorseful, and bore the pedestrian no ill will.

I care less about the four verdicts themselves than about which rule she is actually running. Her track record looks to me consistent with more than one principle — for instance, one on which the ledger tracks the quality of the agent's will, and one on which it tracks the agent's causal responsibility for outcomes — and the four cases seem designed (by fate, not by me) to pull them apart.
