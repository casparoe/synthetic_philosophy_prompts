As a journalist covering science and ideas, I've spent the last few years writing pieces that touch on value theory and moral psychology, and somewhere along the way I drifted into a set of positions I've never actually checked against one another. Before I commit them to print in a longer feature, I want a rigorous audit from someone with professional training in metaethics — ideally an expert on reasons, fittingness, and the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. Please think carefully and work through my commitments methodically: start by spelling out what each one commits me to, and only then compare them, rather than flagging everything that merely looks awkward.

Here is where I stand:

1. I accept the fitting-attitude analysis of value: for something to be good is for it to be a fitting object of pro-attitudes, and for a person to be admirable is for admiration toward them to be fitting.
2. I believe that being a reason just is being a consideration that counts in favor of a response — that's not a property reasons have on top of counting in favor; it's what makes them reasons.
3. I believe that the threats of Parfit's powerful demon — who will torment anyone who fails to admire him — give his victims genuine, even decisive reasons to admire him, such that admiring him can be the rational thing to do.
4. I believe the demon is not admirable at all, and that his threats make him no more admirable: whatever admiration he is due must track features of him, not consequences of admiring him.
5. I believe fittingness is primitive: whether an attitude is fitting toward an object is not reducible to the balance of reasons there are for holding that attitude toward it.
6. I believe that when we call an attitude fitting, we're saying the same thing as when we call it rational or justified — they're different words for one status.

My question: which of these can be held together, which are in genuine tension, and which apparent tensions dissolve once you look closely? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each real one say what would have to give and what I'd lose by giving it up. I genuinely don't know which commitment is the weak spot, so don't just target whichever seems easiest to attack — show me the derivations, including where a tension only arises if you read one of my claims in its strongest form, and note where a weaker reading would let me keep the set intact.
