I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so bear with me. I've been musing on something about how we treat git history at work and in open source, and I want someone to actually take a position on it for me.

Here's the thing. In basically every codebase I've touched, the commit log is treated as the official record of who did what. People run "git blame" (a command literally called blame!) to figure out who to ask about a weird line, promotions and conference talks get justified by commit counts, and changelogs are generated from commit messages. But the record is selective in ways nobody ever decided on, and the selections have weird consequences:

- Squash-and-merge is standard on a lot of projects, which means a junior dev's week of work can show up in history as one commit authored by the maintainer who clicked the merge button. The person who did the work is gone from the record; the person who approved it is in it forever.
- Review comments, design debates, the issue thread where someone laid out why the bug was happening — all that lives in a PR/issue tracker that gets wiped when a project migrates platforms, while the commit log survives. The thinking gets deleted, the typing gets archived.
- Force-push and rebase rewrite the past routinely, and nobody treats that as destroying evidence.
- Meanwhile Dependabot gets authorship credit like it's a person, and a contributor whose "reviewed-by" line made the difference gets nothing.

So my question: what view of authorship, credit, and record-keeping does this whole setup presuppose? Is the commit log working as an archive of who deserves to be remembered, and if so what's the implicit theory — something like "you are what survives as text attributed to you at the moment of the last rewrite"? Does the practice secretly encode a claim that review labor and upstream discussion don't count as work on the code?

I don't want a menu of possible interpretations. Commit to one: tell me what the implicit theory is, whether the different parts of the practice are consistent under it or pulling in different directions, and what one concrete change (something a team could actually do next sprint, not some tooling utopia) would follow if we took the theory seriously. If you think I'm wrong that there's any implicit theory at all, and it's all just path dependence from how git works, say that and defend it — but pick a side.
