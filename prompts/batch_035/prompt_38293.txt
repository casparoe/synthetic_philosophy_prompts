You are a professional philosopher who works on collective action and moral responsibility, and I'd like your help diagnosing a debate rather than winning it. I'm a high school junior in an ethics elective, and the same argument has now stalled out three times in our class: once about taking a flight for a family vacation, once about eating meat, and once about voting. The pattern is always the same. Someone says their one action makes no difference—one flight doesn't measurably warm the planet, one burger doesn't cause a cow to be slaughtered, one vote almost never decides an election. Someone else replies that if everyone reasoned that way, the harm would be enormous. The first person answers that they are not everyone, they are one person, and that "what if everyone did that" is a complaint about a pattern of behavior, not a reason addressed to them. Then someone brings in the idea of being the kind of person who doesn't take part in an unjust system, and the first person says that may be fine as a matter of self-image, but it doesn't show that their burger hurt anyone. At that point we circle back to the beginning. In three months, nobody has moved an inch, even though everyone agrees on the facts about factory farming, emissions, and elections.

I've been trying to work out what kind of disagreement this actually is, and I have a hypothesis I'd like you to take seriously but not simply agree with. Here is what I've pieced together on my own. Some defenders of individual obligations apparently use an expected-value argument: even if no single burger crosses a threshold, each purchase has some small chance of being the one that tips the supplier into ordering more animals, so you multiply the harm by the probability and get a genuine, if tiny, expected wrong. Critics respond that real supply chains don't have sharp thresholds of that kind, or else that each purchase does an imperceptible share of the harm, which raises a further question of whether imperceptible contributions can add up to something an individual is responsible for. Meanwhile, the voting case seems to run backwards from the other two: elections are occasionally decided by a handful of votes, so there the individual really can be pivotal, just rarely. So maybe we have been treating three different problems as a single debate. Or maybe the whole thing is a fight between two theories of what makes an action wrong—whether the action has to actually make a difference, or whether it can be wrong merely by participating in a collective practice that is bad overall—and neither side has noticed that this is what they are disagreeing about.

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement. Is it verbal, or factual, or a clash of normative starting points that people hold without being able to argue for them? Are the "my action makes no difference" side and the "what if everyone thought that way" side even answering the same question, or is one talking about consequences while the other is talking about something like consistency or fairness? Does the asymmetry with voting—where individual pivotality is real, if rare—suggest that what we call one debate is really several? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would genuinely count as progress: a distinction, a piece of information, a reformulated question—or whether we should expect this debate to stay stuck, and why. Before you answer, brainstorm the candidate diagnoses and explain why you settle on the one you do. Throughout, please keep clear about what is more or less settled among philosophers who work on these issues and what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus just because it happens to be common.
