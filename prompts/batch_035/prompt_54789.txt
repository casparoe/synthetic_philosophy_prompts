My mother died last spring, and a friend gave me a translation of Zhuangzi, the old Chinese Daoist, partly for consolation. I've been reading it slowly since, and one thing has struck me: he doesn't seem to have one single reason for not fearing death but at least five, and I can't tell how strong they each are, or even whether all five are meant seriously. As best I can retell them: (1) life and death are phases of one continuous natural process, like the seasons succeeding one another, so grief and fear mistake a small stretch of the pattern for the whole — this is how he explains calmly drumming on a tub after his wife died; (2) we simply don't know that death is bad — the dead might pity our clinging to life just as we'd pity a dreamer who can't wake, and he points to a young woman who wept on her way to becoming a great lord's bride but was glad once she'd arrived, as a hint that leaving life might be like that; (3) death might be actively better than life, with nobody above you, nobody below you, and no labor — the story where he chats with a skull that politely declines his offer to be restored to life; (4) when one of his friends is sick to the point that his body is twisting and contorting, the friend treats the change the way a potter treats clay — if this arm becomes a rooster, I'll use it to announce the dawn — so resenting a transformation merely because it happens to me is confused; and (5) near his own death, when his students wanted to give him a grand burial, he replied that heaven and earth were his coffin and being left for crows was no worse than feeding ants, which sounds less like an argument and more like a refusal to take sides between outcomes. Please rank these five from strongest to weakest purely as reasons not to fear death — as arguments, not as literature or spiritual exercises. Give exactly five numbered items, strongest first, a sentence or two on each, with nothing before or after the list.
