And here's what the student wrote:

"The nonidentity problem starts from the fact that many of our choices affect not just how future people's lives go but who those future people will be. Parfit's example is a 14-year-old girl who decides to have a child right away. Because she is so young, her child has a life that is not worth living — it goes badly for the child. But if she had waited even a few months, she would have had a different child, with a different father being involved too. So the child she actually has could not have existed if she had waited. The child therefore cannot complain that it was wronged by being brought into existence, since the alternative was never existing at all, and its life, though bad, is still better than nothing.

The same structure appears in the depletion case, where a generation consumes resources in a way that lowers the quality of life of people in the distant future, but because of the policy changes, different people end up being born than would have been otherwise. So the future people who exist because of depletion cannot say the depletion policy harmed them, because without the policy they would never have existed.

This is a problem for the person-affecting view because that view says an act is wrong only if it's worse for someone. In both cases there's no particular person who is worse off than they would otherwise have been — the 'otherwise' doesn't contain that person at all. So the person-affecting view seems to imply that the girl's choice and depletion are both permissible. Parfit accepts this conclusion for the girl, but for depletion he argues that the policy is still wrong because it lowers total welfare compared to the alternative, and that total utility is what really matters. His conclusion is that we should reject the person-affecting view and go over to a purely impersonal utilitarianism, which counts the value of lives without regard to who lives them.

One response to the problem is to say that we can wrong someone without making them worse off. For instance, we might say the girl wrongs her child because she had a child knowing it would be disadvantaged, in circumstances that fell below some threshold of responsible parenting. This keeps the intuition that the girl did wrong while granting the nonidentity claim itself. The problem with this move, according to Parfit, is that it's not clear what makes the act wrong if no one suffers any harm that they could have been spared."
