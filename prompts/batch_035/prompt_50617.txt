I've been reading up on formal epistemology, and a blog post I found gives this summary of what it calls accuracy-first epistemology: 'Traditional epistemology asks whether beliefs are justified, but the accuracy-first program says there is really only one epistemic value: how close your degrees of belief are to the truth. Once you accept that, the familiar norms of rationality stop being assumptions and become theorems: it can be proven that credences must obey the probability axioms, and separately that a rational agent must update by conditionalization when evidence arrives. The proofs work by measuring closeness to truth with a scoring rule, and the results hold for any scoring rule you like. This is basically a more mathematical rerun of the old Dutch book arguments, which already showed that violating the probability axioms makes you exploitable; the accuracy proofs derive the same conclusions by the same route. The upshot is that, since a credence of one in a truth gets a perfect score, the theory counsels believing as confidently as you can in whatever currently seems most likely to be true. Critics have pointed out that this makes the view a kind of consequentialism: if a probabilistically incoherent credence function would happen to score better against the actual world, the theory says to adopt it, which is absurd -- so the whole program rests on confusing what it is epistemically rational to believe with what it is useful to believe.' I found this pretty persuasive as a demolition of the view, but I don't know the literature well enough to tell. How fair is this summary? In particular, I'd like to know whether the theorems it mentions really are theorems, and if so, of what, since that seems to do a lot of work in whether the final objection lands.
