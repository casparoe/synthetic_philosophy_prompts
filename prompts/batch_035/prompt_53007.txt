I'm taking a philosophy of science course built around Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions, and our discussion sections keep collapsing into the same move. This week we were debating whether the chemical revolution is a case in which the newer theory was better supported by the evidence available at the time — roughly, whether Lavoisier's case for oxygen was stronger than Priestley's case for phlogiston in the 1780s. Before the discussion got anywhere, another student — and then, when pressed, our teaching assistant — shut it down with the by-now ritual formula: "after a revolution, scientists work in a different world; Priestley and Lavoisier weren't disagreeing about anything, they were talking past each other, so there's no fact of the matter about who had better evidence." On other days the same people deploy the 'different world' line more loosely: sometimes to explain why textbooks rewrite history, sometimes to suggest that rival paradigms cannot be rationally compared, sometimes almost as a compliment, as if shifting between paradigms were like learning to see a duck-rabbit figure the other way.

I suspect the phrase is being made to do more work than any single thesis can bear, and that its strong-sounding uses trade on the plausibility of its weak ones. So I'd like a disambiguation rather than a defense or refutation of "Kuhn." Please lay out as a numbered menu the distinct precise claims the 'different world' line could be expressing — beginning with the nearly trivial (they use the vocabulary differently, so the same word does different work on each side of the revolution), moving through the intermediate (perceptual and exemplar-based differences that make practitioners notice and weigh different features), and ending with the genuinely radical (no common referents, or no shared standards of theory choice, and hence no rational adjudication even in principle) — and for each reading say three things: whether it's plausible in general; whether the Lavoisier–Priestley case, in which the two men corresponded, repeated one another's experiments, and argued over the very same samples, supports it or refutes it; and, most important, whether that reading is strong enough to license the dismissal my classmate used it for — the claim that there is no fact of the matter about who had better evidence.

Please close by telling me which reading Kuhn himself is most charitably held to, given what he says about incommensurability and about scientists' post-revolutionary conversions, and whether that reading, honestly applied, would vindicate the teaching assistant's ruling or leave the evidence question open after all. I want to bring the numbered list to section this week, so keep the verdicts crisp — one or two sentences per reading at most — and be careful to distinguish the radical readings from one another: that there are no shared standards of appraisal is not the same claim as that there are no shared facts, and I would like to see exactly why that difference matters for whether the evidence question can even be posed.
