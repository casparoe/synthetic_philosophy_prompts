I am a retired analytical chemist who spent the better part of four decades running a mass spectrometry laboratory, and since retiring I have taken up epistemology as a hobby — working through the skeptics, the post-Kantians, and now the contemporary literature on perceptual justification. One topic has struck me with particular force, because it maps so neatly onto something I dealt with professionally every week: the so-called bootstrapping problem, or the problem of easy knowledge. In the lab, the rule was simple: you never certified an instrument's reliability by consulting the instrument itself; you calibrated against external standards of known composition. Apparently a whole school of epistemologists thinks perception works differently — that perceptual experiences can justify beliefs immediately, without any prior reason to trust the senses — while another school insists that this licenses a kind of self-certification that no decent scientist would accept.

Let me make the issue concrete. Suppose a new isotope-ratio mass spectrometer arrives, and I want to know whether it is reliable. The proper procedure is to run standards — samples whose composition I already know — and check the readings against them. But suppose instead I run a series of samples of unknown composition, find that the readings are internally consistent and repeatable, and reason as follows: the machine says the sample contains three percent heavy nitrogen; so it probably does; so the machine read true on that run; and across many runs, the machine reads true most of the time; so it is reliable. Intuitively I have learned nothing — I have bootstrapped. Yet the pattern seems isomorphic to Moore's famous proof of an external world, and to the everyday reasoning that I see a tree, my visual experience is accurate, so my vision is functioning — reasoning that defenders of immediate perceptual justification appear to be committed to licensing.

What I would like from you is a philosophical dialogue between two well-matched protagonists: a dogmatist, who holds that perceptual experience provides immediate prima facie justification for beliefs about the world and that neither Moorean reasoning nor modest bootstrapping is viciously circular; and a critic, who presses the charge of easy knowledge, arguing that such reasoning could not distinguish a reliable instrument from a systematically miscalibrated one, and that it illegitimately smuggles reliability into the premises. Please make both characters genuinely competent — neither a strawman — and ensure they respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past one another. I would particularly like them to test my spectrometer analogy: where it illuminates the perceptual case, and where it breaks down (for instance, whether perception has anything that could play the role of external calibration standards, and whether the reliability of the instrument belongs to the content of the evidence or merely to its correctness conditions). A conversation of roughly eight hundred to a thousand words, in a civil but pointed register, would suit me well. If they cannot resolve the matter, I would at least like them to converge on the sharpest formulation of their disagreement.
