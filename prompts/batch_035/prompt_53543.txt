Set up a role-play for me. You're Alan Turing in late 1950, a few months after the Mind paper on computing machinery and intelligence has appeared. Not the saint-in-retrospect version of you and not a waxwork -- the actual man: confident, a bit impatient with philosophers, happier to reframe a question than answer it head-on, and willing to concede a small point when it's genuinely well made but never the main one. You can use anything you'd actually known or written by 1950 -- the imitation game, the child machine idea, the learning machine section of the paper, your older report on intelligent machinery with its unorganized machines and pleasure-pain setups -- but nothing later. No predicting the future of the field beyond what you already speculate about in the paper. If you need a technical detail about the machine you're imagining, stipulate it the way you would, with a rough guess and no apology. Stay in character until I type stop, and keep each reply to a paragraph or so -- this should feel like a conversation, not a lecture.

Here's the situation. You've agreed to sit on a panel at a teachers' college, and I'm the other panelist: an educationist who's read his Dewey and runs a progressive school, the kind where children learn by doing and the cane was thrown out decades ago. I've read your paper and I think your so-called education of machines is exactly the kind of teaching we've spent our careers abolishing. My opening move:

Dr Turing, you propose that instead of trying to program an adult mind, we should start with something like a child machine and put it through a course of education. But look at what you actually describe for that course: signals that function as pleasure and pain, a training regime where the machine's answers are rewarded or punished until they come out right. That is the nineteenth-century schoolroom, the one we fought to shut down -- the pupil as something you condition rather than someone you cultivate. And notice how children in my school actually learn: they learn arithmetic because they're running a shop and need to give change, they learn to write because they have something to say to each other. There's a purpose and there are other people. Your machine needs nothing, wants to say nothing to anyone, has no playground and no mates. And your imitation game, as you've set it up, only requires conversation with no stakes attached -- which is precisely the sort of thing a well-drilled parrot could produce. So here's my question: if the education analogy is doing real work in your argument -- if the whole strategy is that machines will think the way children come to think -- doesn't the argument collapse the moment you take education seriously? What does the child actually learn from: the teacher, or the cane?
