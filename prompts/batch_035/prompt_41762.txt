I've been going round in circles on Moore's "proof of an external world" for a philosophy reading group and I want help figuring out what I actually think, because my gut verdicts seem to pull in different directions. So here they are. When Moore holds up his hands and says he's shown the existence of things outside his mind, I genuinely feel he knew there was a hand in front of him — the proof seems fine in some sense. But when I consider whether I *know* I'm not a brain in a vat, or not dreaming right now, I squirm in a way I don't squirm about the hand — those feel like a different kind of claim, one I can't really claim to know. And yet I also hold onto the thought that knowledge is closed under known entailment: if I know I have hands, and I know that having hands entails not being a brain in a vat, I should be in a position to know I'm not a brain in a vat. Those two verdicts can't both be right, I don't think. On top of that, I'm pulled two ways on the proof itself as a piece of persuasion: it strikes me as completely useless as a way to answer a sceptic (it just begs the question against them), and at the same time I feel Moore did prove his conclusion — my working thought is that he can know he proved it while being unable to prove that he proved it, which sounds weird even to me.

A few more verdicts that might or might not fit whatever principle you extract. I know I'm in pain when I'm in pain, and I know I have a body — both feel certain, but the first feels incorrigible in a way the second doesn't, and I can't say why that asymmetry should matter for knowledge. I know my car is in the driveway even though there's a real chance it was stolen in the last hour — that still feels like knowledge to me. But when I bought a lottery ticket last year, I didn't know it would lose, even though the chance I'd win was tiny — so the stolen car and the losing ticket feel different, and if there's a principle it needs to keep them apart. And finally: I'm fairly comfortable saying I know Paris is the capital of France, but I've never verified it myself and couldn't refute someone who told me my geography teachers were all in on a lie — should that comfort me or bother me?

What I'd like from you is this: try to find a principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, state it precisely, and tell me which clause of it is doing the work in each case — especially the clash between knowing-about-hands and not-knowing-about-vats, which I suspect is where my position has to break. Then apply it honestly to my verdicts and tell me which ones have to go, and whether the price is worth paying. Please also say what the best principle you can construct predicts for two cases I haven't given you a verdict on: whether I can know there was an unobserved past (rather than the world having popped into existence five minutes ago looking old), and whether Moore's proof would still work if instead of a hand he'd held up something that only five people in the room could see clearly. Answer in British English please, and don't worry about being gentle — I'd rather find out my verdicts are a mess now than mid-reading-group.
