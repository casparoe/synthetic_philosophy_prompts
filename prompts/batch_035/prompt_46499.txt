A guy on my trivia team, Marcus, and his friend Elena got into it after drinks on friday, and I was there for the whole thing. The topic was whether their friend Priya's new bakery will still be open in eighteen months. Marcus is doing a PhD in statistics and is a bit insufferable about it. He put the odds at 70% — great location, loyal morning crowd, best cardamom buns in the neighborhood. Elena put it at 35% — most new food businesses fold inside two years, and bakeries run on brutal margins. They argued for a while, and the argument drifted from the bakery to the arguing itself. Marcus said: "This is exactly what Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem is about. Two rational agents with the same priors can't disagree once their opinions are common knowledge. We've both walked past that storefront, we both know the base rates, so if we're this far apart, one of us is being irrational, and honestly I don't think it's me." Elena said: "That's not what it says. It needs a common prior and common knowledge of posteriors, and we're nowhere near either. It's a theorem about idealized agents, not about two people at a bar." Marcus came back with: "But haven't we gotten there? We've each said our number out loud like five times, in front of each other, with you as a witness. You know I'm at 70, I know you're at 35, you know I know, I know you know I know, and so on — that's literally the definition of common knowledge. So the theorem should kick in, and one of us should have to move." Elena answered: "It doesn't kick in, because every one of those announcements was a snapshot. The moment you heard my 35, you should have updated — moved up, if you think I'm rational and know things you don't. So your current opinion isn't 70 anymore, and I don't know your current number, which puts us right back at no common knowledge. If we kept trading numbers until they stopped moving, then it would apply, and we'd end up in the same place." Marcus said: "But we never converge when we argue, we just get tired and quit. And I read a blog post by some quantum computing guy working out that the amount of evidence you'd actually need to exchange to settle a disagreement like this is surprisingly small — a few dozen bits or something. So the fact that we don't converge means one of us isn't updating like a Bayesian at all, which was my original claim, just with extra steps." Elena's last word was: "Or it means we don't share a prior. I've watched a lot more storefronts die than you have, and no amount of number-trading fixes a disagreement that's really about what kind of world we each think we live in. In which case the theorem is beautiful and tells us exactly nothing about any argument I've ever actually been in, and maybe that's the honest conclusion."
