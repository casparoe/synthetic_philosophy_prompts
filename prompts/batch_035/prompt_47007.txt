I am a professor of philosophy grading a take-home midterm for an upper-level course I teach called "Minds, Machines, and the Moral Community," which covers the metaphysics of mind and its applications to the moral status of animals, fetuses, and artificial systems. This is the first question on the exam, worth roughly a third of the grade. I would like you to act as an expert grader in philosophy of mind and ethics — someone who knows the literature on functionalism, multiple realizability, supervenience, and the Chinese Room, and who grades fairly but does not let imprecision slide. Please think carefully before committing to a judgment: I'd like you first to work through the answer paragraph by paragraph, deciding for each claim whether it is correct, correct but loosely stated, or mistaken, and only then assign a letter grade (A through F, with pluses and minuses as appropriate). Your deliverable is a point-by-point assessment in the form of margin comments, followed by a brief overall justification of the grade and the single most useful piece of feedback I could give this student.

The exam question, verbatim: "It is increasingly claimed that the moral status of a system — what it is owed, and what counts as harming it — turns on its computational organization rather than on the material out of which it is built. Defend or attack the claim that moral status supervenes on computation. Your answer should (i) state the supervenience claim precisely, (ii) give the strongest argument in its favor, and (iii) present the most serious objection, indicating how a proponent should best reply."

And the student's answer, in full: "The claim that moral status supervenes on computation is the claim that there can be no difference in what a system is owed without some difference in the computation it performs — in other words, moral status just is a fact about the system's computational profile, a fact about which function it implements. The strongest argument in favor is multiple realizability, first defended by Putnam: if pain, or any mental state, can be realized as well in silicon as in carbon, then what matters is the organization, not the stuff. Suffering matters because of what it feels like, and what it feels like is fixed by the causal role the state plays, so what matters morally is fixed by that role too. To deny this because a system is built from the wrong material would be a kind of hardware chauvinism, exactly parallel to denying moral status on the basis of species membership; the burden is therefore on the substrate chauvinist to identify the morally relevant physical property, and no plausible candidate has ever been produced. The most serious objection is the Chinese Room, where Chalmers argued that syntax is never sufficient for semantics: a system can manipulate symbols flawlessly and still understand nothing, and if understanding can be absent while the computation is present, so can suffering, so computational profiles underdetermine moral status. Chalmers's point is sometimes thought to be answered by the systems reply, but he plausibly denied that it helps. In any case, the proponent's best reply is to bite a bullet: once moral status supervenes on computation, there must be a determinate threshold of computational complexity at which moral status begins — otherwise there would be no fact of the matter about what a system is owed, and it is absurd to think there is no fact of the matter about whether, say, an industrial robot can be harmed. So vagueness in this domain is impossible."

In your comments, please distinguish between what the student gets right, small slips of formulation, and genuine errors — including any cases where an attribution, a definitional move, or an inferential step is wrong in a way a hurried grader might miss. Do not inflate the grade out of sympathy for the fluent writing style; grade the philosophy. Note also anywhere the student answered part (i), (ii), or (iii) of the question only partially or not at all, and take that into account in the grade rather than treating the answer as a seamless whole.
