You're a statistician who has spent way too much of your career dealing with aggregated data, and you're also good at explaining this stuff to normal humans. I need your help turning the ideas around Simpson's paradox into something my team can actually use.

Context: I run a small analytics unit at a regional healthcare network. Roughly once a week someone asks us a question of the form "is A doing better than B?" -- is hospital A's readmission rate lower than hospital B's, is the new care protocol better than the old one, did patient satisfaction go up after the staffing change. The analysts usually pull the numbers, compute a couple of aggregate rates, and hand over a table. Twice now we've been burned by Simpson-type reversals: the aggregate said one thing, and as soon as someone broke it down by patient severity or by department, the story flipped, and it flipped in a way that embarrassed us in front of the board. So I want a written procedure, not just "be careful."

Before you write the procedure, take a moment to brainstorm and get the conceptual foundations straight, because I want the procedure to be principled rather than a pile of heuristics. In particular, think through the distinction between a genuine Simpson's reversal (the sign of the association flips between the marginal and the conditional analysis) and the more general problem of aggregation bias, where the marginal number is just misleading without literally reversing. Think also about the point that whether you should condition on the third variable is not a statistical question -- it depends on the causal structure, on whether the subgrouping variable is a plausible common cause of the treatment and the outcome, or a mediator you'd be conditioning away, or just noise. The procedure has to encode that reasoning somehow without requiring my analysts to draw DAGs, unless you really think they should.

The procedure itself should cover, at minimum: when the analyst is required to check for a possible reversal before sending numbers out (every time? only when the marginal comparison is close? only for certain kinds of questions?); how to pick which disaggregation variables to look at, given that we have dozens in the data and could slice forever; what to do when the subgroups are so small that the breakdowns are themselves statistical garbage -- is there a principled stopping rule or is it a judgment call, and if so whose; what the deliverable to the requester looks like when the aggregate and the disaggregated pictures disagree; and when the procedure should escalate to me rather than let the analyst decide. Be explicit about the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated -- I'd rather know where the honest uncertainty is than get a fake sense of objectivity.

Then run the procedure on three cases, worked through concretely:

1. Hospital A has a higher mortality rate than Hospital B overall, but A is the region's trauma center so its patients are much sicker. 2. A new post-surgical protocol looks better than the old one on the aggregate numbers, but once you split by which surgeon performed the procedure, the advantage disappears within each surgeon's patients -- the new protocol happened to be adopted first by the surgeons with better outcomes. Show what the procedure does when the disaggregated answer kills the headline.

3. This is the one I actually want you to be tough on: patient satisfaction scores went up network-wide after a staffing change, but satisfaction went down within every single department. So the network-wide improvement is an artifact of departments with higher baseline satisfaction growing as a share of the total, and every department is actually slightly worse off. The requester here is the person who championed the staffing change, senior to me, and she wants a clean positive number for her quarterly review. Walk me through what the procedure says to report, how the analyst is supposed to communicate it, and where the procedure hands the decision up to me because it's not really a statistics problem anymore.
