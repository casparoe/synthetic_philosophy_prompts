Until my retirement a few years ago, I worked as a condensed-matter physicist at a national research laboratory. Since then I have been leading a small seminar at a university continuing-education programme, this term on Schrödinger's views on consciousness and Vedanta. I set the take-home question below and am now grading the submissions. I am used to marking lab reports, not philosophical prose, so I would be grateful for help assessing one of the essays: please assign a letter grade with pluses and minuses, and justify it with a point-by-point account of what the student gets right, where they go wrong, and how much each error should cost them. The comments should be specific enough that I could defend them to the student in a face-to-face meeting.

The question I set was this:

"In his Cambridge Tarner Lectures of October 1956, published as Mind and Matter (1958), and in philosophical essays dating back to the 1920s and later collected in My View of the World, Schrödinger pressed what he called the arithmetical paradox: the scientific picture of the world presents us with an enormous number of observers and minds, yet the one mind each of us knows from within has no distinguishing feature that could explain why it is this one rather than another. He wrote that consciousness is 'a singular of which the plural is unknown,' and that 'the plurality that we perceive is only an appearance; it is not real,' illustrating the point with a many-faceted crystal that shows hundreds of little pictures of what is in reality a single object. He traced the doctrine to the Upanishads and their formula tat tvam asi ('this is you'). In an essay of about 800 words, explain what exactly Schrödinger took the Vedantic doctrine to claim; what status he assigned to it — scientific result, philosophical insight, religious conviction, or something else; and how he understood its relation to his own work as a physicist. Where in his writings do you find support for your reading?"

The student's essay, reproduced in full:

"Schrödinger's claim is that the multiplicity of individual consciousnesses is the deepest puzzle we face, and that its solution comes from the Upanishads. The puzzle is not that there are many bodies — physics handles that without strain — but that each of us is unavoidably acquainted with exactly one mind from the inside, while the objective picture of the world contains an untold number of minds, all, as far as we can tell, of exactly the same sort. Nothing in the objective picture explains why I am this one rather than another. Schrödinger's resolution is the Vedantic identification of atman with Brahman: the self you find within is not a private possession but the one universal consciousness itself.

His Advaita is essentially the droplet-and-ocean picture. Each separate consciousness is a genuine droplet of the single ocean of Brahman, individualised for a lifetime and reabsorbed into the whole at death, losing its separateness as a wave loses its shape when it subsides. On this reading both the unity and the plurality are real, the unity fundamentally and the plurality derivatively: droplets are genuinely formed out of the one substance. This is what tat tvam asi means — the consciousness now reading this sentence is that very Brahman, so that at death nothing of ultimate significance is lost.

It is worth separating this from the Buddhist doctrine of anatta: the Upanishads affirm a self where Buddhism denies one, and Schrödinger appreciated that Vedanta therefore offers a positive account rather than a merely negative one.

Was this a scientific claim? No. Schrödinger was explicit that physics had nothing to do with it. He set the view out first in What is Life?, his famous book on the physics of the gene, but the unity of consciousness is not derived there from any physical argument, and he never pretended otherwise. The scientific method cannot establish the unity, and he expected no future science to do so either: the insight is available to immediate experience in the Vedantic manner, and can only be accepted or rejected as a whole. It is a metaphysical conviction, held with a physicist's honesty, and the most that can be said for it is that no experiment could count against it — or for it."

Please evaluate this essay point by point, tell me how you would weight the strengths against the weaknesses, and give the grade you would assign.
