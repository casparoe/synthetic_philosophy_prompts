In December 1973 the American Psychiatric Association's board of trustees voted to remove homosexuality from the DSM-II, replacing it with a milder category for people distressed by their orientation; the membership confirmed the change by referendum the following year with a far from overwhelming majority, and the residual category survived in various forms until ego-dystonic homosexuality was deleted from the DSM-III-R in 1987. The change was driven largely by protest, internal APA politics, and shifting attitudes rather than by any new finding about the biological functioning of people attracted to their own sex. Analyze this episode through the naturalism–normativism debate in the philosophy of medicine. What should the naturalist say happened in 1973 — a correction of a classificatory error in light of better evidence about species-typical functioning, or a value-driven relabeling that a value-free account of disease is powerless to either vindicate or condemn? And what should the normativist say — is this her showcase case, or does it show that once disease judgments are hostage to social values, psychiatric categories risk being whatever pressure is currently strongest says they are? Where does Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction compromise come out of the episode — vindicated as capturing what both sides were right about, or exposed as unable to explain why ego-dystonic homosexuality deserved deletion while ordinary distress-based disorders stayed? Take a definite position: does this episode confirm normativism, confirm naturalism under a charitable reconstruction, or show that neither framework explains what actually changed in the diagnostic manual?
