I'm writing a term paper for a course on the philosophy of tradition and reform, and I came into the topic sympathetic to the Burkean side: the thought that inherited institutions embody accumulated knowledge that no individual reformer possesses, and that this puts the burden of proof on whoever wants to dismantle them. My TA, who I suspect is baiting me, walked me through the following argument in office hours and then refused to tell me where it goes wrong. (1) A reformer who does not understand a long-standing social arrangement has no standing to remove it; the burden of justification lies with the remover, not the preserver. (2) That burden can be discharged only by recovering the reason the arrangement was originally established — not by guessing at what it does now, since the whole point of the principle is that surface appearances mislead. (3) For most long-standing arrangements the original reason is unrecoverable: many practices arose by drift rather than design, the motives of their originators are contested or lost to history, and even well-documented origins tell us little about the function the practice serves centuries later. (4) So for most long-standing arrangements the burden can never be discharged, and removal is never justified. (5) Yet some long-standing arrangements — hereditary chattel slavery, the legal subordination of married women, the binding of daughters' feet — were plainly justified to remove, even at times when their origins and workings were subjects of genuine scholarly and popular dispute. (6) Therefore the burden-of-proof principle is false, and the Burkean is left with nothing.

I want to keep both (1) and (5), which means something between (2) and (4) has to give, but I can't get a clean grip on it. I half suspect my TA has quietly narrowed "understanding" in (2) — from knowing whether an arrangement serves any defensible purpose at all down to knowing the founders' actual intentions — and that this narrowing is what makes the burden look undischargeable in (3) and (4). But I also worry that if I widen (2) back out, I've hollowed the principle: it starts to permit removing anything a confident reformer claims to understand, and Burke's whole point was that such confidence is cheap. I've also noticed that the abolitionists themselves were told, by perfectly serious people, that they didn't really understand the institutions they wanted to destroy — which makes me wonder whether (5) is smuggling in a retrospective certainty that wasn't available at the time, and whether a fence principle should be evaluated from inside the epistemic situation of the reformer or from ours.

Please take on the role of a careful philosopher of political thought and work this through before answering. Tell me which single step or premise you would reject, naming the precise move — whether it's an equivocation on "understanding" between (1) and (2), a slide from "not justified without understanding" to "never justified" between (3) and (4), a hidden assumption in (5), or something I haven't spotted — and explain why that step looked so innocent. Then price the alternatives: if I reject (2), what exactly do I give up, and does the repaired principle still do any conservative work; if I reject or weaken (4) or (5), what does that cost the Burkean; and if you think the argument is in fact valid and (1) really must fall, say so outright rather than rescuing it for my sake. I want a definite verdict, not a tour of the options. Please write in continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists in your answer, and if you restate the argument's steps, fold them into sentences.
