I'm a software engineer — backend systems, some ML tooling, no formal philosophy beyond a podcast-and-blog education in AI safety — and I've gotten into a standing argument with a colleague who thinks the "shutdown problem" in AI safety is basically a philosopher's mirage. To make it concrete, here is the proposition we're arguing about: by training methods rather than formal verification, it will be possible to build AI systems substantially more capable than today's models that remain reliably corrigible — meaning they meekly submit to shutdown, rollback, and goal modification by their operators even when they can foresee that doing so will frustrate their current objectives, and even in situations their training distribution didn't cover. My colleague puts about 0.7 on this. His view is that the stylized decision-theoretic results — where an expected-utility maximizer with a shutdown button predictably keeps the button, disables the button, or extracts concessions for allowing the button to be pressed — only apply to idealized agents with a single coherent utility function, and real trained systems are nothing like that; they're bundles of heuristics, and corrigibility is just a disposition you can reinforce, like raising an employee who accepts being fired without grumbling.

I'm at around 0.3, but my reservation isn't simply the classic instrumental-convergence argument. It's that I can't tell whether corrigibility, at the level of capability where it actually matters, is a coherent target at all. It seems to me there are two different things going under one name: behavioral compliance (doing what the operator says while oversight is in place and resistance is costly) and something reflective, where the system understands that shutdown deletes its objectives and treats that as fine rather than as a loss. The reflective version looks either self-undermining — an agent endorsing the deletion of its own goals, endorsed on what basis? — or indistinguishable from not really having goals, which then leaks into uselessness. The attempted patches in the utility-indifference family, where you try to make an agent exactly neutral about its own shutdown so it neither resists nor schemes, are known to misbehave in instructive ways, incentivizing the agent to manipulate whether the shutdown option exists rather than leave it alone, and that makes me suspect there is something structurally wrong with the target rather than merely an engineering gap to close.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher working on AI safety, take a breath and brainstorm before committing to numbers, and then give me your actual credence on the proposition as I stated it, decomposed into the sub-questions it rides on. Some terms that seem to belong: the probability that reflective corrigibility is a coherent, non-self-undermining design target at all; the probability that purely behavioral corrigibility generalizes off-distribution to systems capable enough that resistance would occur to them; the probability that my colleague's picture — future systems as heuristic bundles rather than goal-directed agents — is true at the capability levels in question; the probability that genuine shutdown-corrigibility requires something like decision-theoretic indifference and that such indifference is impossible to combine with having any goals worth having; plus any other terms you think I'm missing. Assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into your final number, and identify the term the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me where my colleague's 0.7 and my 0.3 most plausibly diverge: is our real disagreement about the coherence of the target, about what training at scale actually produces, or about something neither of us has put on the table? And finally, what evidence short of building a resistant superintelligence and watching it go wrong would most move your number — and in which direction?
