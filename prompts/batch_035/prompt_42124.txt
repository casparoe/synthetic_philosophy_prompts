ok so some context: I'm a first-year PhD student and just got handed my own discussion section for the first time — intro course on rational choice, about 20 students, 75 minutes. My week's topic is Parfit's hitchhiker and the cluster around it: rational irrationality, commitment, credibility, why being (or seeming) irrational can pay. The topic also got weirdly personal for me last month: back in January I asked a collegue to cover one of my grading shifts because I was travelling, and sealed it by promising to cover her shift in April. April rolls around, its a gorgeous weekend, and I'm standing there realizing: past me made a promise that present me now has to eat the cost of, and if she'd known I'd be this rational about it in April she'd never have covered me in January. Apparently that's the whole point of the literature, but I felt it before I could name it, and that's what I want for the students.

Design me a game that gets them there. Requirements, roughly:

- No real money — points or tokens, maybe a silly leaderboard. But at the moment of paying, paying has to be strictly dominated: it costs you points and you get literally nothing back. They should feel the "why on earth would I" pull in their own heads, not just see it on a handout.
- Two roles, hitchhikers and drivers, multiple rounds so reputations can form. Drivers choose whom to take.
- Crucially: I want dispositions dealt like cards. Some students get a sealed card that says they keep promises even when it costs them; some get a card that says they're fully rational and will not pay once paying is pointless. They don't get to pick. I'm hoping the finding emerges on its own: the fully rational ones can't get rides.
- A cheap-talk phase — hitchhikers get 30 seconds to say whatever they want to the driver before the driver decides. I want to know if talk works, and whether it works differently for the two card types.
- A commitment device they can purchase: sign a binding card at a small upfront cost, and I act as the referee and enforce it. I want to see if demand for it only shows up after the first failure, which is the part I find most interesting.
- Don't reveal the philosophical point up front. No lecture, the name can come out in the debrief if at all.
- The debrief has to map specific moments in the game onto the distinction between the rationality of acts and the rationality of dispositions/characters, and onto one-shot vs repeated play. And it has to be honest about what the game can't reproduce — in the game your character is dealt to you, in life you can't just decide to be someone who keeps promises at a cost, or can you? I'd rather that part be said out loud to me than papered over.
- Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I've never facilitated anything before, so include the novice-facilitator stuff too: rules disputes, kids colluding outside the rules, the one person who thinks its hilarious to defect every round, me fumbling the explanation.
- One last thing: give me the payoff table as something I can just put on a slide, because I will absolutely botch explaining it out loud. And maybe a sentence or two on where this debate actually lives so I don't sound like an idiot when a student asks "wait, so is being rational bad?" — but keep it light, I don't want the debrief turning into a lecture on constrained maximization.
