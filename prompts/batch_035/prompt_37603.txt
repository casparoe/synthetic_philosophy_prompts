Exam question: Norman Malcolm argued that dreams cannot be experiences occurring during sleep. (a) Explain the steps of his argument and what he took its conclusion to be. (b) Explain how Daniel Dennett's model in his article "Are Dreams Experiences?" (Philosophical Review, 1976) both agrees with and departs from Malcolm's. (c) Is the dispute between them resolvable by empirical evidence? Why or why not?

Student's answer: Malcolm's argument in his 1959 book Dreaming draws on Wittgenstein's view that meaning is use: a statement's meaning depends on the criteria for asserting it. Someone who is genuinely asleep cannot make an assertion — if words come out of a sleeper's mouth we do not count them as reports, since a sleeping person is not a participant in the language game. So no one can report a dream while it occurs, and a waking report of a dream can never be checked against what actually happened. Malcolm concludes that dream reports are meaningless pseudo-statements, on the verificationist principle that a statement is meaningful only if it could in principle be verified. Since dream reports fail this test, they are neither true nor false, and dreams turn out to be nothing at all.

Dennett's reply, published in his 1991 book Consciousness Explained, accepts that we cannot observe dreaming as it happens, but argues that this leaves open an alternative empirical model: during sleep the brain composes dream content and loads it into memory without the content being experienced, and at waking the content is played back, producing what seems to be a memory of a long dream. Dreams, then, are real experiences after all — just very short ones, squeezed into the moment of awakening. Dennett supports this with cases in which an external stimulus, such as an alarm, gets woven into the dream's climax, showing that the content must have been composed on the spot at waking.

On part (c), the dispute is conceptual on both sides: Malcolm and Dennett agree that no experiment could settle whether dreams are experiences, since their models agree on all the observable facts. And since Malcolm's verificationism is false, the neuroscience of REM sleep has in fact settled the matter against him: dreams are experiences occurring during sleep.
