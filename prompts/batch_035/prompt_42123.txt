I am two hundred pages into a novel whose narrator, a woman of about forty, declines to have children and does so in openly philosophical terms — she cites Benatar's asymmetry by name, more than once, and a chapter of the book turns on her sister's furious response to it. I want her to be intelligent, and I refuse to let her hold a position I have misrendered, since a character arguing badly for a view her author never understood is a small act of dishonesty toward the reader. The trouble is that I came to this material late, and my only sustained account of it is a letter from my editor, who read philosophy as an undergraduate. She writes, in relevant part: "Benatar's asymmetry says that pleasure is good and pain bad, but that the two are not symmetrical in the way pronatalist thinking assumes. The badness of pain outweighs the goodness of any comparable pleasure — suffering counts for more than happiness does — and this weighting extends to the alternatives. The absence of pain is good for the person who is never born, even though she never exists to enjoy that absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad, because there is no one to be deprived of it. Since every life contains some pain, and the pains of existence are a real cost to the person created while the absent pleasures are no loss to anyone, coming into existence is always a net harm, and having a child wrongs the child. So the argument only bites when the life in question would be genuinely dreadful; a woman as contented as your narrator, who expects her child's life to resemble her own happy one, has no purchase on it at all — Benatar's asymmetry is really a claim about lives not worth living, which is why wrongful-life cases in law are its natural home."

I confess I do not know which parts of this letter are faithful and which are glosses, and the novelist's fear of a reviewer who knows the philosophy is not a dignified basis for choosing a character's convictions. Take the letter apart for me. Which sentences would a careful antinatalist accept as written, which are conflations — for instance, between the asymmetry itself and other things Benatar argues, or between a comparative claim about how bad things weigh and a claim about whom absence is good or bad for — and does the editor's final conclusion, that a contented woman cannot invoke the asymmetry against her own would-be child, actually follow from whatever accurate parts remain? I want the diagnosis to engage what Benatar himself maintains, including where he anticipates objections of exactly this shape, so that I know what my narrator is entitled to say. And since I will be putting words in her mouth for another two hundred pages: tell me plainly where your own reading is most likely to go wrong — whether some interpretive dispute about the asymmetry is live enough that your audit could be contested — and what would change your verdict, whether a passage, an argument, or a consideration I have not thought to ask about.
