I'm in a reading group that's slowly working through Hegel's Phenomenology (we're using the old Baillie translation, which honestly might be part of the problem). We finally hit the self-consciousness chapter, and I thought I was following it okay because I'd already read secondary stuff on the master-slave dialectic, but then we actually looked at the paragraphs right before the famous life-and-death struggle and I realized I don't understand them at all. Here's the bit that's confusing me, from around paragraph 178 and then the end of 184 into 185:

"SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or "recognized". The conception of this its unity in its duplication, of infinitude realizing itself in self-consciousness, has many sides to it and encloses within it elements of varied significance. Thus its moments must on the one hand be strictly kept apart in detailed distinctiveness, and, on the other, in this distinction must, at the same time, also be taken as not distinguished, or must always be accepted and understood in their opposite sense."

and then a few paragraphs later, after all the stuff about the "double process of both self-consciousnesses":

"They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another. This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes."

So here's what I can't figure out. In the first passage, recognition already seems to happen — the two self-consciousnesses apparently arrive at "mutually recognizing one another" at the end of 184, symmetrically, no fight needed. But then 185 immediately says we now have to consider how this "pure conception" appears as a process, and the process turns out to be the wildly asymmetrical master-slave situation where "one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes." My reading group split into two camps and neither convinces me: one guy says the mutual recognition in 184 is just Hegel describing the concept from the outside, "for us" the readers, and the actual consciousnesses on the ground have to go through the struggle to get anywhere near it (so 184 is like a preview of the end of the book, not something the characters achieve). Someone else thinks the symmetry in 184 is real but unstable, and the master-slave relation is what it collapses into when each tries to make the recognition it gives cheaper than the recognition it receives — like a kind of inflation of recognition.

Could you help me untangle this? Specifically: is the mutual recognition of 184 supposed to be an achievement of the two consciousnesses or just a description "for us"? And does Hegel give us any textual clue about why the symmetrical arrangement breaks down into the asymmetrical one, or is the slide from "mutually recognizing one another" to "one is merely recognized" something we're just supposed to accept as the dialectic doing its thing? I'd especially appreciate it if you could walk me through what "its process appears for self-consciousness" is supposed to mean — that phrase seems to be doing a lot of work, but I can't tell what work exactly. If it helps, feel free to explain using whatever terminology, I don't need it dumbed down, I just want the actual logical structure of the move.
