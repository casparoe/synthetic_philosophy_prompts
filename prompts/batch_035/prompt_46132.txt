My partner and I have been arguing about whether generations — Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z — are real kinds in any meaningful sense, and after months of going in circles we've decided to outsource the numbers. Her view is that the cohort labels pick out genuine structure in the world: people who come of age during the same formative period share dispositions, values, and sensitivities that persist across the lifespan, and the labels are just noisy names for that structure. My view is that they're mostly administrative artifacts: the boundaries (1965 vs. 1964, 1997 vs. 1996) are arbitrary lines drawn by marketers and demographers, the within-cohort variance swamps the between-cohort variance, and any predictive power the labels seem to have is really just period effects and age effects wearing a cohort costume — the classic identification problem, since every person's age plus their period fixes their cohort, so the three are never cleanly separable from one another. Our credences are roughly 0.7 (her) and 0.25 (me) that generations are real kinds.

Before we go further, I want your actual credence on the proposition, and I want the reasoning decomposed. To make it precise: I'm not asking whether birth cohorts are metaphysically possible, or whether early-life experiences matter (they obviously do). I'm asking whether the conventional cohort groupings — the ones with names and roughly twenty-year spans — are projectible: do they support robust, non-trivial generalizations that we couldn't get more accurately just from knowing someone's exact age and the calendar year? So please break the question into the sub-claims it depends on and assign a rough probability to each: that there exist sharp formative windows in early life during which shared period effects leave durable marks; that those windows are few and long enough that twenty-year groupings capture them rather than slicing across them; that the resulting clusters are homeostatic enough (like Boyd's homeostatic property clusters in biology, where the properties reinforce one another) to survive as kinds even as cohorts age and mix; and that the between-cohort differences are large relative to within-cohort differences. Feel free to add or discard terms — that list is my attempt, not a constraint — but tell me how your terms combine into the final number, and flag which term carries the most weight in getting you there.

Two more things. First, tell me which term you are least confident in and what would move it — not just abstractly, but concretely: what pattern in a cross-sectional survey, what kind of longitudinal result, or what argument about the age-period-cohort identification problem would shift that term most. Second, given your decomposition, tell me where you think the disagreement between her at 0.7 and me at 0.25 most likely lives: is it one of the sub-questions, or do we weight the same sub-questions differently, or does one of us make a compositional error the other doesn't? If you think our disagreement rests on a confusion that dissolves once the question is stated precisely, say so plainly — but only if you actually believe it, not as a way of dodging the number.
