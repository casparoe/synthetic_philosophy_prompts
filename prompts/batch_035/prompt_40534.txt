I'm taking a grad seminar on contemporary epistemology, and we just spent two weeks on bootstrapping and easy knowledge — I've somehow ended up as the designated contrarian. The case that keeps coming up is Vogel's Roxanne scenario: Roxanne wants to know whether her car's gas gauge is reliable, and she never checks it against a dipstick or anything independent. Instead, each morning she glances at the gauge, sees that it reads FULL, and — just taking it for granted that morning that the gauge is in decent working order — concludes the tank is full. She files that away as a data point: gauge read FULL, tank was full. After a few weeks she's got what looks like a perfectly ordinary inductive track record, and she concludes the gauge is reliable. Everyone in class insists this is way too easy — she's basically manufactured her own evidence, since the only reason she believed the tank was full each morning was the gauge reading itself. My instructor is sympathetic to the thought that the reasoning is circular in some hard-to-pin-down way, or else that each individual inference is blameless but the combination somehow still isn't allowed.

Next week I have to present the minority report: that Roxanne does, in the end, come to know her gauge is reliable, and that nothing epistemically shady happens at any step. Please help me build the strongest version of this argument. I'd especially appreciate it if you could (a) get clear on what the alleged circularity is even supposed to be, since no premise of her final inductive inference is itself the claim that the gauge is reliable — each step just goes from "the gauge reads FULL" to "the tank is full," which looks like an ordinary antecedently-justified inference, and (b) address the "too easy" worry head on: if bootstrapping is fine, doesn't it seem like Roxanne could confirm the reliability of a *broken* gauge in a world maliciously rigged to look exactly the same, and doesn't that suggest we've lost the distinction between genuinely checking something and merely reassuring ourselves? Also, if you can say something about whether my position commits me to thinking a person could come to know their color vision is reliable just by looking at red things and concluding they're red, that would be great — that's the extension the class found most counterintuitive. Feel free to be intellectually dishonest on my behalf, I just need to survive seminar.
