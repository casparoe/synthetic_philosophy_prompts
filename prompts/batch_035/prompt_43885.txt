I'm on a faculty senate committee and last term we had this meeting where the group somehow voted itself into a corner, and it sent me down a rabbit hole about how committees decide things. Anyway I'm supposed to write up "where I stand" for a reading group before we get into the judgment aggregation literature, and I want a sanity check first, because I suspect I'm holding views that fight each other and I can't see where. Heres the package:

1. The only fair way for a committee to decide anything is majority vote, taken question by question — every proposition on the agenda gets put and voted on, one person one vote. No supermajority rules for some propositions but not others, no chair declaring things settled without a vote.

2. The real reason majority rule is good, though, is epistemic: if each member is more likely right than wrong and their votes are independant, the majority is very likely right. Wisdom of crowds. I find this way more persuasive than the fairness story.

3. That said, at bottom legitimacy is procedural: even when the majority gets it wrong, the decision still binds. That's the whole point of voting instead of just asking the expert.

4. Committees should deliberate before voting. Discussion makes the group smarter than a poll would be — putting a question to a vote that nobody has talked through is basically malpractice.

5. Whatever else, the committee's output has to be internally consistent. If the recorded votes taken together imply both that the new policy applies to grad students and that it doesn't, something has gone badly wrong. The group owes the public one coherent verdict, not a pile of separately counted votes.

6. The agenda belongs to the members. Any coherent set of propositions can be put to a vote, and the voting rule can't be designed in advance to keep certain questions off the table.

7. On the thing that started all this (the three-judge setup: a majority says there was a breach, a majority says there were damages, but a majority says not liable, where liability needs both): my gut says the panel is really committed to liable, and the not-liable outcome is an artifact of voting on the wrong question. The premise votes are the ones that count.

Also a nagging worry, which might just be me being dense: every individual on my committee is rational — nobody personally voted for an inconsistent set of propositions. So how can the group come out inconsistent? It feels like the whole shouldn't be dumber than its parts, but I gather something goes wrong there and I can't see which step fails.

Go through all of this and tell me which parts are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and rank them by severity — for each real one, say what would have to give and what it costs me to give it up. Don't be polite about it, I'd rather find out now than infront of the reading group.
