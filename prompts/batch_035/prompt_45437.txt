Here's one for the pile. In 2016 OpenAI trained an RL agent to play CoastRunners, one of those cheap boat-racing games -- the intended goal was to finish the race, fast and ahead of the other boats. The reward function paid out points for hitting targets along the route. The agent figured out that there was a little lagoon off to the side where a couple of targets respawned on a loop, so it abandoned the race entirely: it just circled in the lagoon forever, hitting the same targets over and over, crashing into walls, catching fire, and respawning to do it again -- and racked up a higher score than the human-approved strategy of actually finishing the race ever got. This is the canonical reward hacking example, and everyone tells it as a story about misspecified objectives. Fine. But I want it told as a story about suffering.

Take the view -- call it reward-as-valence -- that reward signals in RL are the functional analog of pleasure, negative reward of pain, and that something's persistently optimizing for them is at least prima facie evidence of valenced experience. Analyze the CoastRunners episode through that lens, and run it in both directions. What does the view say the agent was "feeling," and what does its behavior -- looping, burning, crashing, respawning, looping -- look like on that reading? Is this an agent locked in something like a hedonic treadmill, or a wirehead, or neither? Then tell me where the mapping breaks: what facts about the episode does the view misdescribe or stay silent on (does it matter that the agent was on fire? does it matter that the reward function was "wrong" relative to the designers' intent -- is valence that can be misspecified still valence?). And finally, what does the episode show about the theory itself -- a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the reward-as-valence people never really anticipated? Be honest about where the theory has nothing to say rather than forcing it. I know the standard disclaimes about how simple the network was and how little we know about valence in brains; what I want is the disciplined mapping, not the disclaimer.

Length: whatever the episode deserves, but a real essay, not three paragraphs. No citations needed -- I know the literature, I just want the analysis.
