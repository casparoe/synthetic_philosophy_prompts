I'm a graduate student who has started attending a local AI-safety reading group, and I keep running into the one-liner that intelligence and goals are orthogonal — the so-called orthogonality thesis, in Bostrom's label. It gets deployed to end conversations: whenever someone suggests that a sufficiently capable system would outgrow, reflect on, or come to see through whatever goal we gave it, a senior member replies that intelligence and goals are orthogonal, as if that one sentence settles the matter. But when I pay attention, I notice the sentence is doing several different jobs in that room. Sometimes it seems to mean only that no amount of intelligence logically entails caring about anything in particular. Sometimes it means that raising a system's capabilities leaves its values untouched, so capability work and alignment work are independent dials. Sometimes it means that for any goal whatever, there is a possible mind of arbitrarily high intelligence pursuing it — that the smartest possible paperclip maximiser is a genuine possibility rather than a cartoon. And once it was stretched to imply that smarter agents won't as a matter of fact tend to be nicer, and that we therefore shouldn't bother making systems more capable, only safer.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind and action who works on motivation and reasoning in artificial agents. Before writing anything, think carefully — perhaps sketch some candidate readings and how they come apart — because these senses of the slogan are routinely conflated even by people who deploy it fluently. Then lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing: for instance, a claim of logical independence between intelligence and the content of final goals; a modal claim about which combinations of intelligence and goal are possible or coherent; a causal-psychological claim about what greater intelligence does to an agent's values as a matter of fact; and an engineering claim about what scaling up a system's capabilities does to its motivations in practice. For each reading, say whether it is a near-truism, substantive and defensible, or false, and note what would count for or against it. Finally, tell me which readings would actually support the uses the slogan was put to in my reading group: does any of them show that a sufficiently smart system could not outgrow a goal like maximising engagement, and does any support the leap from orthogonality to the conclusion that capability and value are independent engineering problems? Flag, too, which readings would, if false, be bad news for alignment work organised around the capability/values split. Present the whole thing as a structured list of readings with verdicts that I can bring to the next meeting.
