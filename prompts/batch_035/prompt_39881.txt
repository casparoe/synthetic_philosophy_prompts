Two epistemologists have been arguing for several years about epistemic akrasia — believing a proposition while believing that one's own evidence fails to support it, or disbelieving a proposition while believing one's evidence supports it. One holds that such akratic combinations are always irrational: rational belief must cohere with the subject's own view of what her evidence supports. Call this the Requirement camp. The other holds that there are cases in which an akratic combination is rational, typically because the subject's higher-order evidence — evidence about what her evidence supports, or about her own reliability — is misleading, or is outweighed by her first-order grasp of the matter. Call this the Permission camp. Draft the joint statement the two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should have three parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than diplomatic filler. Include what both camps accept about the normative force of higher-order evidence in ordinary cases, for instance cases in which a test result defeats a perceptual belief; about why Moore-paradoxical combinations cannot be rationally believed all-in; about the distinction between what one's evidence is and what one takes it to be; and about the failure of quick arguments on both sides — the observation that evidence of evidence is evidence settles nothing without a further story about weight, and the complaint that the Requirement camp's principle is self-undermining is itself an argument the Permission camp owes rather than an observation it is entitled to.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one remaining disagreement twice: once in wording the Requirement camp would accept as a fair statement of its own view, and once in wording the Permission camp would accept likewise. If you judge that some part of the dispute is verbal — for example over whether rationality is assessed from the subject's standpoint or from the assessor's, or over whether one's evidence includes everything available in one's environment or only what one has properly taken account of — say so explicitly, name that part, and explain why it is verbal.

Third, a cruxes section. Identify the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and for each, a case or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. At least one crux must be assessed against the following case, which both camps have stipulated is well-specified and probative:

The Ledger Case. An amateur logician has, for years, kept a private record of her own verdicts on a tightly defined class of puzzles — call them star puzzles. The record was kept conscientiously and she has no reason to think it inaccurate; it shows that she has been wrong on roughly 80 percent of star puzzles, while feeling at the time just as confident as she does now. Today she is presented with a new star puzzle. She works it, checks her solution three times by independent routes, and can articulate no flaw in it; yet the solution has the surface character that her ledger says has almost always marked her failures, and she has no independent check on the answer.

Record each camp's prediction about what rationality permits or requires of her here: whether she must suspend judgment on the puzzle's answer, may believe her checked solution, must believe against it, or something graded in between. For each prediction, state which feature of the case is doing the work. Then identify at least two further cruxes of your own choosing. Candidates worth considering: whether first-order evidence is ever transparent enough to outweigh higher-order evidence directed against it; whether the prohibition on akrasia and the requirement to respond to one's evidence can come into conflict, and what each camp thinks follows if they can; and whether the Permission camp's favorite cases covertly assume a subject who is in fact reliable at assessing the very thing her higher-order evidence is about, so that the cases prove less than they seem to. For each further crux, supply a case or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Throughout, use no citations, author-year references, or reading lists; state all positions descriptively. The standard of success is this: each camp, reading the final document, would recognize its own view in the wording attributed to it, would sign the agreed claims without crossed fingers, and would regard the recorded predictions as ones it is now committed to. That last condition is what makes the document worth having, since some of those predictions will eventually be shown wrong, and the document should make it possible to see, when that happens, which camp must move and why.
