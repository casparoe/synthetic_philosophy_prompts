I'm a third-year undergraduate in a course on the historical Jesus, and a debate in our seminar has been stalled for most of the term in a way I'd like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. One camp defends the criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence — as the only disciplined route to statements about Jesus that stand on historical rather than confessional ground. The other camp holds that the criteria are circular or worse: dissimilarity presupposes that we already know Second Temple Judaism and early Christianity well enough to recognize what departs from both; embarrassment presupposes that we can reconstruct what would have embarrassed the evangelists; multiple attestation presupposes that we can distinguish independent traditions from a single story circulating in parallel. Both camps read the same secondary literature and nobody has moved in weeks, which makes me suspect the disagreement isn't really about the criteria's internal logic.

I have a hypothesis, and I'd like it tested. I suspect there are three separable layers here. The first may be verbal: the camps may mean different things by authentic — the exact words of Jesus, the substance of his deeds, or something looser like characteristic patterns of teaching and conduct that survived memory and translation. The second may be methodological: one side treats the criteria as filters that certify a secure minimum core of material, while the other treats them as fallible signals feeding into an overall judgment of probability, so that the two sides are answering different questions — what can be established versus what is most probable given the total evidence — under a single vocabulary. The third, I suspect, is doing most of the work and is the least discussed in seminar: a difference in priors, in particular about what kind of explanations are admissible. The methodological naturalism built into the criteria enterprise, which by construction cannot certify anything supernatural, collides with the convictions some students bring to the texts, so each side ends up counting different material as evidence at all — and neither can argue the other out of it, because it's not a disagreement about the sources.

There's also a specific sticking point I'd want your diagnosis to address: several of us have noticed that the criterion of dissimilarity, applied strictly, defines Jesus by his difference from both his context and his followers, so the better it works the more it guarantees a figure who fits neither first-century Judaism nor the movement he founded. The defenders say this just shows the criterion sets a floor rather than a portrait; the skeptics say it shows the method manufactures its own conclusion. Is that a substantive disagreement or a disagreement about what a criterion is for?

So, my questions: is my three-layer hypothesis right, and are the layers actually separable, or does the verbal layer disguise a substantive one? Is part of this merely bedrock — intuitions about whether testimony to the extraordinary can ever outweigh its improbability — that no argument will move? And what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: agreed test cases where both camps commit in advance to what the criteria should yield, a shift to the social-memory approach some of our readings favor, or the admission that the question the criteria were built to answer — the authentic words and deeds of Jesus — is unanswerable at the level of resolution the debate demands? Please engage the positions as I've described them rather than surveying the field, and be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are well supported and which are conjecture. I'd also like you to say where your diagnosis is most likely to fail, and what consideration would lead you to a different one.
