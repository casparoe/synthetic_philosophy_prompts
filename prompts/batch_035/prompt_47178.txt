I've found myself pretty much bought into the hedonistic imperative — David Pearce's line that we should use biotech (germline engineering, designer drugs, whatever comes next) to phase out suffering not just in humans but in all sentient life, and not merely treat it but rebuild the reward architecture so future beings run on information-sensitive gradients of super-well-being instead of the old pain/pleasure axis. I'm not looking for a fan letter or a rehash of Brave New World objections. I want a genuine stress test: what would actually have to be true for this view to be wrong?

A few places I specifically want you to dig:

- The gradients move is doing a lot of work in my head. Pearce claims pain's signaling function — alerting us to noxious stimuli, organizing avoidance behavior — could be preserved by graded enhancements of well-being, so you'd still functionally respond to a hot stove without the nasty qualia. Is that move load-bearing for the whole project? What would have to be true about the neuroscience, or about what information-processing has to feel like from the inside, for it to fail? And is there actual reason to think it fails, or is it just unproven tech optimism?
- Am I secretly a utilitarian without noticing? I'm not one. Does the abolition of suffering only come out as the overriding priority under a fairly narrow hedonic axiology, or can the imperative survive under value pluralism — and if it can't, is that a defeater or just a scope limitation?
- The consent problem for nonhuman animals. Rewiring predators (or their prey) happens without anyone's agreement. What would count against the view there, and would a committed abolitionist just bite the bullet, or is there a principled response?
- Unfalsifiability check. Since the view mostly makes claims about future biotech, it can drift toward being insulated: any obstacle is met with "eventually the tech will get there." What near-term observations or findings — say, about hedonic set points, about whether well-being gradients can actually carry the motivational load, about consciousness itself — would genuinely lower your credence in it?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed abolitionist would concede count against them versus swat away, and be honest if your conclusion is that the view is actually pretty robust. End with a final line of the form: Verdict: [one sentence naming the single most dangerous consideration against the hedonistic imperative and whether it is fatal].
