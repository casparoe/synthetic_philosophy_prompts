I'm writing my undergraduate dissertation in philosophy of mind, and my supervisor keeps telling me the multiple realizability literature is the perfect place to make a contribution because it's been completely stuck for thirty years. I want to test that claim before committing, and I'd like your help diagnosing the stall rather than picking a winner. As I understand the shape of it: Putnam-style functionalists argued that if a mental state type can be realized by physically very different systems — mammalian brains, silicon, perhaps octopus nervous systems — then psychology cannot be reduced to neurobiology, and the special sciences earn their autonomy. Jaegwon Kim then argued that this gets things backwards: widespread multiple realizability means there is no single neural kind to reduce anything to, but it also means each realizer gets its own local, realizer-specific reduction, so the autonomy of the higher-level science dissolves rather than being secured. Then there's the empirical wing of the debate: some philosophers contend that careful neuroscientific work shows real multiple realizability is far rarer than the functionalists assumed, with nervous systems repeatedly converging on the same mechanisms, while others insist that cases like pain across radically different taxa, or convergent evolution of the same capacity in separate lineages, show it is ubiquitous after all. Nobody seems to have moved much since the early 2000s.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion, is that the stall happens because the two sides are quietly answering different questions under one heading: the functionalists are making a point about the individuation of scientific kinds and the semantics of higher-level explanations, while the critics are making a point about causal explanation and token physicalism, and the empirical skirmishes are fought over cases that each side can reclassify without loss. But I can also see two rival diagnoses: either there is a genuine hidden empirical question about the grain at which neuroscience carves its kinds, currently unanswerable because the neuroscience itself is unsettled, or the whole dispute is a metaphysical one about what it takes for two realizers to count as realizing the same thing — a verbal disagreement dressed up as a scientific one. My department wants the dissertation to say something about what would count as progress, so I can't just leave it as 'they talk past each other'.

Could you write a proper diagnosis? Go through what each side actually claims about realization, kinds, and reduction, tell me which of my three diagnoses (or some fourth one) you think is right and why, and be honest about whether anything — a conceptual distinction, a specific empirical finding, a reformulation of what the autonomy of the special sciences is supposed to amount to — could actually end the dispute, or whether it is genuinely intractable and my supervisor is wrong that it's fertile ground. Please answer in British English, and don't turn it into a survey of every paper in the area: I want a reasoned diagnosis, with the positions described only as much as the argument needs.
