I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about sunk costs by collecting my own gut verdicts on cases from work and ordinary life. Here they are:

Our team spent three years building an in-house build system, and a far better open-source alternative now exists that we could adopt in a month. My manager keeps refusing to switch, explicitly citing the three years invested. I think that's irrational, full stop.

I once spent five days debugging a gnarly concurrency bug in an old service rather than rewriting the module, which would have taken two. At the time, and still now, I think staying with the debugging was reasonable — not because the five days obligated me, but because by day five I had accumulated a mental map of the failure modes that a rewrite wouldn't inherit. I'd make the same call again knowing this in advance.

A friend paid ninety dollars for a concert ticket, and on the night of the show is exhausted and no longer wants to go, but goes anyway "because the money's spent." I think that's the textbook fallacy, and I think my friend is being irrational.

I abandoned a side project I'd worked on for two years the moment a clearly better-maintained library came out that made most of it redundant. Abandoning it felt obviously right, and I feel no guilt. But when I told a teammate last month to shelve his two-year side project for exactly the same reason, it felt wrong — like I was asking him to betray something — even though rationally the situations look identical to me.

During a production incident, we spent six hours attempting to restore from backups before someone proposed wiping and rebuilding the environment from scratch, which took three hours. I believe the right call would have been to switch to the rebuild at hour two, and the fact that six hours had already gone into the restore attempt made it psychologically harder to switch, which is the fallacy operating on the team.

My tentative principle is something like: honoring sunk costs is rational when, and only when, the accumulated effort itself has produced private information or local knowledge that a fresh start wouldn't have, so that the "sunk" investment changes the forward-looking comparison. I'd like you to test this principle against my cases. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the principle survives, needs repair, or should be replaced — and then give your reasoning. Don't restate my question or open with preamble. I want the principle (or the competing candidates) stated precisely enough that it has determinate verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause; I want its verdicts on at least two cases I haven't listed, ideally ones that would occur in software engineering; and I want an honest accounting of which of my verdicts above it simply cannot accommodate, with your judgment on whether the verdict or the principle should give way. Pay particular attention to the two side-project cases: I suspect my divergent feelings there are doing something I haven't fully understood, and I'd rather know if they're tracking a real asymmetry or just status quo bias dressed up.
