I am a retired experimental physicist who, after four decades of work on nonequilibrium systems, has spent the last several years reading philosophy of action, and I have become preoccupied with Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. What strikes me most about it is that, unlike the free-will debates I vaguely absorbed during my scientific career, it does not turn on whether determinism is true. The argument claims that to be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for how you are (at least in certain fundamental respects), and since you cannot be ultimately responsible for how you are — you did not choose the initial conditions of your own character — you cannot be truly responsible for what you do. What interests me as a former scientist is the dialectical shape of this: it proceeds by a regress rather than by an appeal to physics, and yet it is often presented as establishing something stronger than any laboratory result could — an impossibility in principle.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two characters: a defender of the Basic Argument and a skeptic who rejects its conclusion. The dialogue should be a genuine exchange, with each character responding to the other's actual points rather than talking past one another, and neither should be a strawman. The defender should press the regress itself, and should handle the obvious objection that indeterminism in the brain would supply the needed freedom. The skeptic should not simply deny the premises; she should mount a serious response — for instance, challenging the claim that responsibility for an act requires responsibility for character, or arguing that what the argument demands is an incoherent target rather than an unattained one.

In addition, I would like the dialogue to engage a specific worry of mine, which I hope you can distribute naturally between the two speakers. In my own field, self-referential systems are not automatically incoherent: feedback loops, self-tuning controllers, and systems whose dynamics alter their own boundary conditions are perfectly respectable objects of study. The regress in the Basic Argument, by contrast, seems to require something it declares impossible — a being that is, in some sense, the ultimate author of itself. I cannot tell whether this impossibility is supposed to be a logical point (like a square circle), a rational point (like demanding that a chain of justifications terminate in something that both is and is not a justification), or an empirical point (like exceeding the speed of light). The two characters should explicitly take up this question, with the defender of the argument explaining which kind of impossibility he has in mind, and the skeptic pressing him on whether the argument succeeds once the kind is made precise. If it helps, the characters may be given names and brief personas, but they should remain recognizably two thoughtful philosophers rather than caricatures. Please do not end the dialogue in a tidy conversion of either party; it may conclude with an unresolved but sharpened disagreement, provided the exchange along the way has moved the question forward rather than merely restating opening positions.
