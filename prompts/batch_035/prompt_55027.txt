You are a senior researcher in formal epistemology with a strong background in modal logic and the epistemic-foundations-of-game-theory programme, and you have supervised a fair number of logic-oriented doctoral students. I need your advice on choosing a dissertation direction, and I want a defended prioritisation, not a survey.

My situation: I am roughly two years into a PhD, with a background in modal and dynamic epistemic logic rather than economics. I have, realistically, about three years of research effort left before I need to be thinking about a thesis, and my comparative advantage is in constructing formal models and proving things about them, not in running experiments or doing empirical work. I have been reading around epistemic game theory — hierarchies of beliefs and type spaces, the epistemic conditions for iterated deletion of dominated strategies, the backwards-induction debate, Aumann's result on agreeing to disagree, and the impossibility theorems about complete and consistent belief structures that followed from treating self-referential belief schemas naively. My question is which open problems in this cluster are actually worth three years of a logician's time, and in what order.

Here are the candidate directions I can see, though I may be missing better ones, and I want you to feel free to replace parts of this list:

1. The backwards-induction puzzle: what exactly needs to be assumed about the players' epistemic states for the familiar equilibrium argument to go through? It seems increasingly unclear to me that full common knowledge (or common belief) of rationality is doing the work the textbooks say it is — some of the refinements in the literature seem to get the same conclusions from strictly weaker epistemic conditions, and I'd like to know whether the residual role of the common-belief assumption can be isolated and stated precisely.

2. The impossibility results: what the no-complete-type-space-style theorems really show about whether the hierarchies-of-belief framework can be both fully general and internally consistent, and whether the standard escapes (restricting the language, moving to non-well-founded structures, treating belief as a partial rather than total relation) are principled or merely repairs.

3. The gap between the idealisations and bounded reasoners: the epistemic programme assumes players can in principle entertain unbounded hierarchies of beliefs about beliefs, while the behavioural evidence suggests most people manage one to three levels of reasoning. One might dismiss this as a confusion of normative and descriptive projects, but I suspect it is not so easy — the idealised results are often advertised as explaining why equilibrium is rational to play, and an explanation that requires reasoning capacities nothing in the population has seems to be explaining something else. I half-want to write a dissertation on whether there is a principled notion of approximate or bounded common belief that preserves some of the classical conclusions in the limit, and whether the classical results can be understood as limit cases of a family of bounded results.

4. The interactive epistemology of disagreement and its economic corollaries (the no-speculation-style results): which consequences of common priors and common knowledge of beliefs survive weakening each ingredient, and how much of the interest of these results survives the weakening.
