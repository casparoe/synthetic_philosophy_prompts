My brother, who is thirty-one, has spent seven years failing to finish a novel while supporting himself with gig work he openly dislikes. He is not in denial about the pattern; he can recite the case against his life — the foreclosed savings, the relationships that ended over his unavailability — more crisply than anyone in the family could. Last month he told me that all our arguments are addressed to a version of him who wants different things, and that person does not exist: there is only him, who wants to write the novel, and you cannot argue someone into wanting something. He studied philosophy as an undergraduate, and I suspect he is half-remembering a view along these lines — that a reason only exists for you if it runs through motivations you already have. When I pressed him, he made a distinction that has bothered me since: he agreed he has reason to call our mother more often, since he hates disappointing her and calling is the cheapest way to avoid that, but he denied he has any reason at all to abandon the novel, since nothing he actually cares about is served by it — except finishing it, which is the problem.

My question has two parts. First, as a philosophical matter: is he right that our arguments are, as it were, misaddressed — that there is no fact of the matter about what he has reason to do beyond what flows from what he already wants — or are there considerations that simply apply to him whatever his current motivations, so that "he has reason to stop" can be true even if nothing in him connects to it? I am aware of the obvious retort, that he does care about things that conflict with the novel and is just refusing to deliberate honestly about the tradeoff, so please tell me whether that retort is all there is to say or whether the interesting cases are ones where the motivational set is not mistaken but merely unfortunate. Second, and more important to me: does the philosophical answer have any practical upshot? If he is right, it seems our only lever is to change what he wants — encouragement, guilt, waiting out the infatuation — rather than to show him reasons; if he is wrong, the right response is to keep making the argument in the hope that it eventually connects. I would like to know which picture of what I am doing when I argue with him is correct, or whether both miss something — whether, for instance, the philosophical dispute is real but makes no difference to what I should do next, or whether it quietly decides between patience and pressure. If it helps, I am not looking for a referee to declare a winner: I want to know whether "he has reason to stop" is something I can believe truthfully, or only a compressed way of saying I wish he would.
