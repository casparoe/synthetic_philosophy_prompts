I'm a retired geophysicist who got hooked on philosophy since leaving the lab, Mill especially, and I've run into a problem where I actually need him. I volunteer on the programing committee for a public science lecture series at our city's natural history museum -- maybe twenty talks a year, mostly retirees and curious laypeople in the audience, and we pick speakers in a one-hour monthly meeting. Every few months we have a screaming match about some controversial speaker. Last year it was a homeopath who wanted to give a talk on complementary cancer care in our health series, and right now it's an ecologist with real publications who wants to argue against GMOs, and whose science I think is bad but who isn't a crank. Right now we decide these things by whoever argues longest, and I'd like to do better. Can you turn Mill's harm principle into an actual procedure the committee could run in that hour, with people who have never read a page of philosophy? What I want: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says host the talk, where it says refuse, and where it has to admit the question is about our values rather than anything Mill can settle, and hand it to a vote. Mill's thing about how the same opinion about corn dealers is fine in print but not shouted at an angry crowd outside the dealer's house matters here too, since we have live audiences and Q&A, so the procedure should cover not just which talks to book but how to handle the live event if a speaker starts telling sick people to refuse treatment. Then run it on the homeopath and the GMO ecologist, and tell me honestly if it gives an answer I won't like -- I already suspect it might on the second one. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text, the way you'd write a letter to a colleague.
