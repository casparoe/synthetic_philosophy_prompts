I spent my career in physics — thirty years in condensed matter, retired for five — and I have spent the time since reading ethics with the seriousness of a late convert. I have arrived at a set of positions, each of which I find individually compelling, and I would like them audited for joint tenability. My worry is that each was adopted while thinking about small, finite examples, and that at least one cannot survive the picture of the world my own training tells me is true. I cannot locate the fault myself, and I would rather not discover in public, at the seminar I attend, that my position is a patchwork.

Here is the set:

1. I am a consequentialist. In a choice situation, the right act is the one whose outcome is best, and outcomes are ranked by total welfare over everyone who ever lives in them.
2. I hold that adding a life of positive welfare to a population makes the outcome strictly better, and subtracting such a life makes it strictly worse. I take this to hold wherever it applies, not only where counting is easy.
3. On physical grounds I believe, more likely than not, that the universe is spatially infinite: the evidence for flatness, together with inflationary cosmology, points to infinitely many galaxies and, presumably, infinitely many lives containing both suffering and well-being. Every outcome that any act of ours is compatible with therefore contains infinitely much of both.
4. I hold that an act can be wrong only if some alternative available to the agent would have made the outcome better. Where the available options are of exactly equal value, normativity goes silent: nothing is obligatory and nothing forbidden. Wrongness requires a difference in value.
5. I hold, as a plain matter of fact and not of attitude, that a physician who kills a trusting patient on a whim, when the patient would otherwise have lived decades of happy life, acts wrongly. On this point I am a realist, and I would not give it up on a bus.
6. I hold that the better-than relation is complete: for any two outcomes, one is better than the other, or the other is better than the one, or they are exactly equal in value. I regard appeals to incomparability as a cop-out.

My suspicion, which I would like tested, runs as follows. From my measure-theoretic training I know that adjoining a single point leaves an infinite measure unchanged. If total welfare against an infinite backdrop behaves like an infinite measure, then by (1) and (6) the outcome with the murdered patient and the outcome without him are exactly equal, and by (4) the physician did nothing wrong — contradicting (5). Yet it also seems to me that by (2) the outcome without the murder is strictly better, which would rescue (4) and (5) at the cost of contradicting something else. I cannot tell whether (2) and the combination of (1), (3), and (6) are the real antagonists here, whether the trouble lies elsewhere entirely, or whether part of the conflict is only apparent. It further occurs to me that a realist about value ought perhaps to be uncomfortable with there being no fact of the matter in infinite cases — though I am honestly not sure that follows.

Please treat the list itself as the object of the audit rather than as an invitation to lecture me on infinite ethics. Tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension; which only appear to be; for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what the revision costs elsewhere in the set; and, if there is one, which single revision would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. I would particularly like to know whether the completeness of better-than in (6) is doing more work in generating the trouble than its innocent formulation suggests — it is the principle I find most congenial, as I like total orders, so if it is the culprit, I will want the case against it made slowly — and whether the suspicion in my penultimate paragraph is right, half right, or aimed at the wrong pair entirely.
