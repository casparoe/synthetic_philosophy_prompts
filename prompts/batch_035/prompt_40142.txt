At a reading group on the metaphysics of mind, a friend defended epiphenomenalism and then pushed it a step further, to a conclusion the rest of us couldn't accept but couldn't locate a flaw in either. Here is the argument as he gave it, in numbered steps:

(1) Epiphenomenalism: phenomenal properties — what our experiences are like — are caused by physical states, but make no causal difference to anything physical.

(2) My judgment that my current experience is reddish is a physical event: a neural occurrence, together with the report I would sincerely give if asked. By (1), the reddish character of the experience is nowhere among the causes of that judgment; the judgment is fully fixed by prior physical states.

(3) So the judgment would have occurred exactly as it does whether or not my experience was reddish — indeed, whether or not there was any reddish experience at all. How things look makes no difference to what I judge or say about how things look.

(4) A judgment that would have been made just the same whether or not p is not knowledge that p; whatever agreement there is between the judgment and the fact, it is not earned.

(5) Therefore, on epiphenomenalism, I do not know that my experience is reddish; and the same holds for every judgment I make about what my experience is like.

(6) But I do know what my current experience is like. If there is anything I know, it is that.

(7) So epiphenomenalism is false.

What makes this hard for me is that, as he presented it, each step sounded like a restatement of the one before. (3) seemed to follow from (2) trivially, and (4) seemed to be nothing more than the old point that a lucky guess is not knowledge. I now have the nagging sense that some phrase means one thing on its first appearance and something stronger later, but I cannot say where, and I may be imagining it. If I have to give something up, I would much rather keep (6) than anything else in the argument, and I would like to keep (1) as well, since the case for (1) was the part I found most persuasive going in. The friend, for his part, does not flinch at (5): he says that if his theory costs him knowledge of his own experience while leaving the experiences themselves untouched, that is a price he is content to pay, and that we object only because we overestimate what the word "know" has to do.

I would like three things. First, tell me which step to reject, and explain why that step looked fine when it was given. Second, price the rejection: what does giving it up cost — not just for me, but for the position that accepts the step, and in particular does the friend's own way out, accepting (5) and denying (6), leave him with anything still worth calling knowledge of his own mind, or does it collapse into something he ought to find embarrassing? Third, if you think more than one step could be rejected, say which rejection is cheapest and why.
