Before the Critique of Pure Reason made him cautious, Immanuel Kant published, anonymously in 1755, the Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens. In it he proposed the nebular hypothesis, argued that the nebulae are "island universes" of their own, and insisted on teleological grounds that a cosmos in which most worlds sat empty would be a profligate waste inconsistent with divine wisdom. He even ranked the planets' likely inhabitants by distance from the Sun: beings on Mercury and Venus comparatively material and sluggish, humans middling, and the inhabitants of Jupiter and Saturn more refined, more spiritual, and closer to perfection. The essay closes on a mystical note about the "universal silence of nature" — though there Kant meant the quiet in which the immortal spirit listens inward, not the quiet that later astronomers would find in the sky.

Suppose you could sit Kant down in the present day and walk him through the Fermi paradox: hundreds of billions of stars in our galaxy, thousands of confirmed exoplanets, a strong expectation from the 1755 essay that rational life should be ubiquitous — and decades of SETI producing only silence. He would learn of the standard responses (the rare Earth hypothesis, the great filter, technological civilizations being short-lived, them existing but being uninterested or unreachable), and you would press him to react.

I want a committed answer, not a menu. Specifically:

1. Would the pre-critical Kant of 1755 treat the Great Silence as a genuine refutation of his teleological wager, or would he dig in — say, by arguing that purpose is served by worlds that develop without mutual interference, and that the distances separating inhabited systems are themselves part of the design?
2. Would the later, critical Kant — who in the Critique of Judgment treats natural purposes as regulative rather than constitutive — shrug off the silence entirely, on the grounds that teleology was only ever a heuristic for reflecting judgment and makes no predictions that evidence could defeat? Is that a principled retreat or an unfalsifiable dodge, and would Kant have seen the difference?
3. Kant's philosophy of history, in the 1784 essay on universal history and in Perpetual Peace, claims that nature's hidden plan drives rational beings, through their "unsocial sociability," toward an ever-larger federation of states. Does he owe us the prediction that technologically mature civilizations converge on a federation? Would he expect a galactic version, and if so, should he expect us to have heard from one?
4. Finally, take a stand: of the familiar Fermi paradox resolutions, which is the most Kantian — and is it one you find any independent reason to accept?

Give one overall verdict on what Kant would actually say, defend it from the competing textual evidence, and close by naming the strongest objection to your own reading and why it fails (or succeeds). Avoid the mere survey of possibilities; if the Kant of 1755 and the Kant of 1790 would genuinely disagree, say which one wins and why.
