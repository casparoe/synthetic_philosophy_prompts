I'm a novelist, and I've gotten weirdly obsessed with Moore's paradox, and I need someone to tell me which parts of the actual philosophy matter for my job.

Quick background: I write literary fiction, mostly close third person with a bit of first person. A while back I gave a narrator the line "It's raining, but I don't believe it" — she says it out loud at a funeral, meant as a moment of dissociation, not comedy. My editor flagged it as nonsense, a reader wrote to say it was her favorite line in the book, and when I went looking for why both reactions felt right I found out the sentence has a name: Moore's paradox. Since then I keep bumping into the open questions in that literature, and honestly I can't tell which of them matter for what I do and which are angels on pinheads. I dont beleive everything my narrators say, but I do need to know which of these to actually think about.

The questions as I've gathered them, roughly: (a) whether the absurdity is semantic or pragmatic — whether "p but I don't believe p" is somehow self-contradictory or just breaks a rule of assertion; (b) whether the omissive version ("p but I don't believe p") and the commissive version ("p but I believe not-p") are absurd for the same reason or different ones — my funeral line is the first kind, and a character who says "he loves me, and I believe he doesn't" would be the second, and those feel very different on the page; (c) whether self-deceived people are actually in Moorean states — i.e., whether a woman who believes her husband is faithful while knowing everything that should of changed her mind has contradictory beliefs or just an avoidance strategy — this one feels like it might be the whole ballgame for me; (d) something about first-person authority, why "it's raining but I don't believe it" is absurd while "it's raining but you don't believe it" is just an observation about a fool; and (e) whether the paradox generalizes to other attitudes, like hoping — I suspect that one doesn't matter for me but include it if you disagree.

There's also a thing I've noticed that I can't find a home for, so tell me which open question it belongs under: in free indirect style, a narrator can assert that p on behalf of a character while the scene has just shown the character acting as if not-p. No Moore-paradoxical sentence is ever uttered, but the reader gets, I think, the same jolt. And conversely, when a character in dialogue says something Moore-paradoxical, readers often hear it as lucidity or damage rather than absurdity — which makes me wonder whether the standard explanation, whichever it is, would even predict that. If the absurdity is just a norm of assertion being broken, then a broken character is simply a norm-breaker, fine, that's easy to write. If it's a genuine incoherence in the content, something else has to explain why "I love him and I believe I don't" reads as tragedy. Is that settled in the literature or open?

So here's the ask: rank these open questions by how much they matter for a novelist whose next book is about self-deception and unreliable narration, and defend the ordering. I want explicit criteria — which questions are upstream of the others for my purposes, which are tractable by reading and thinking rather than by formal work I'm not equipped for, and which are overrated for fiction even if they're the crown jewels of philosophy of language. Tell me what would move things up or down the list: does it matter whether the next book is in first person? Whether the self-deception comes through dialogue or through free indirect style? And if there's an open question in this area I haven't listed that you'd rank first, add it — you'd be doing me a favor. No reading list, no citations; I can chase papers once I know which chase is worth it. Assume I get maybe an hour a day for this between drafts, not a sabbatical.
