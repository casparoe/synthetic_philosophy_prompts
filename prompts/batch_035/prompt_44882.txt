I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a philosophy of mind course, and we've just finished a unit on naturalizing intentionality — the project of explaining, in non-semantic terms, how a physical state in a brain can be about something. Teleosemantics is the view I find most tempting, since it at least gestures at an explanation rather than just redescribing content in content-involving terms, but I'm aware it's also the view everybody loves to attack. In the seminar we spent a lot of time on whether it can handle misrepresentation and whether it assigns contents at a useful grain, so I feel like I have some sense of where the live disputes are.

The trouble is a passage I read outside the course, in a popular science book on the evolution of cognition by a cognitive scientist (I'd rather not name it since I'm about to be uncharitable if it deserves it). It summarizes teleosemantics roughly like this: just as the heart was selected for pumping blood, so that pumping blood is what the heart is for, our beliefs were selected for guiding successful behavior toward features of the environment, so a belief's content is the environmental feature it was selected to track; and a false belief is then exactly like a diseased heart — an organ failing to perform the function it was selected for — so the problem of error, which sank earlier causal theories, falls out of teleosemantics essentially for free. The book then draws two conclusions: first, that since natural selection has been tuning our cognitive systems to match the world for millions of years, teleosemantics predicts that human beliefs are overwhelmingly true; and second, that the view is nonetheless refuted by a thought experiment — imagine a lightning strike in a swamp assembles, by pure coincidence, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, with no evolutionary history at all; since its states have no selectional etiology, teleosemantics must say this duplicate has no beliefs whatsoever, which is absurd, and so the whole program collapses.

My instinct is that this is a hostile caricature dressed up as a neutral summary — the swampman case in particular is presented as a knockout blow when, from what I gather, defenders of the view treat it more like a bullet they've already decided to bite — but I'm honestly not sure how much of the earlier material is accurate either. Could you audit the passage for me? Specifically: which of its claims would a defender of teleosemantics accept as a fair statement of the view; where does it conflate things the view keeps apart (for instance, is it running together the function of a whole organ or system with the content of an individual state, and does that matter?); is the claim that error comes for free something teleosemanticists actually assert or their most persistent embarrassment; and is the "our beliefs are mostly true" prediction really a prediction of the view, given what selection actually optimizes for? Finally, do the book's two conclusions follow from the accurate parts of its own summary, or do they depend on the misrepresentations? I'd rather know what's genuinely weak about teleosemantics than score points against a strawman.
