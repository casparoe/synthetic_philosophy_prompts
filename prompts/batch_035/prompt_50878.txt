Douglas Hofstadter's Luring Lottery, run through his Scientific American column in 1983, worked roughly as follows: readers could enter the contest by mailing a postcard, and each postcard could bear any positive integer, counting as that many entries. The prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, paid to the sender of one randomly drawn entry. More entries meant a better chance of being drawn but a smaller prize; game-theoretic reasoning therefore pushes every entrant toward ever-larger numbers, and in the event many readers submitted astronomically large figures (some reportedly a googolplex), rendering the prize a negligible fraction of a cent. Hofstadter's proposed remedy was superrationality: the idea that perfectly rational agents facing a symmetric problem will converge on the same answer, so each should reason as if choosing a strategy for all of them at once, and enter exactly once.

Suppose David Hume — specifically the Hume of the Treatise of Human Nature, where he explains justice and property as human conventions rather than natural sentiments or promises — were given an accurate summary of this episode, including the outcome. Could he make sense of superrationality, and would he endorse it? The fit is tantalizing: Hume's paradigm of convention is famously illustrated by two men rowing a boat who fall into a shared rhythm without any agreement, merely because each expects the other to keep time, and by neighbours who drain a meadow together. Both Hofstadter's superrational thinker and Hume's conventional partners seem to reach cooperation through symmetry rather than contract. But there are serious disanalogies: Hume's conventions arise gradually, out of repeated interaction, observation, and convergent self-interest, among people who can monitor one another; the lottery was a one-shot, anonymous interaction between strangers who would never meet. And Hume's rowers need not suppose that they are cognitive duplicates of each other — each simply adjusts to what he expects the other to do — whereas superrationality seems to lean on the far stronger assumption that identical reasoners will land on identical choices.

Please construct Hume's likely position on superrationality and the Luring Lottery, and organize your response under the following sections, in this order:

1. The lottery and the theory (roughly 100 words): a brief, accurate restatement of the Luring Lottery's rules and outcome, and of superrationality, in your own terms.
2. Hume's machinery (roughly 200 words): the essentials of Hume's account of convention — the rowers, the common-interest expectations, the role of repetition and gradual emergence — presented so as to make it usable for the question.
3. Hume as ancestor (roughly 250 words): the strongest case that Hume would view superrationality as a natural extension, or even an anticipation, of his own account.
4. Hume as skeptic (roughly 250 words): the strongest case that Hume would reject superrationality as a philosopher's artifice — an unstable, one-shot imitation of convention lacking the conditions (repetition, observability, convergent expectations) under which his conventions actually form — and would treat the googolplex outcome as precisely what his theory predicts.
5. Verdict (roughly 200 words): your considered judgment of where Hume would actually come down, including how he would treat the point that conventionalists can coordinate without assuming they are reasoning-duplicates, and a sentence or two on how confident the textual record should make anyone in either direction.

Throughout, ground the reasoning in Hume's actual theoretical commitments rather than vague appeals to his temperament or biography, and do not attribute to him views that presuppose post-Humean developments.
