My decision theory reading group has an argument that, if it works, dismantles expected utility maximization, and I cannot locate the seam. The setup is the mugging named for Pascal's wager: a stranger stops Pascal on the street and offers to return tomorrow with an astronomical benefit in exchange for his purse, and the benefit is whatever the stranger needs it to be. The argument:

(1) An act is rational only if it maximizes expected utility relative to one's credences.
(2) However extravagant the promise, one's credence in delivery should be nonzero: the stranger could be a time traveler, a demon, or an honest emissary of a vastly richer civilization, and none of these hypotheses has probability zero.
(3) No promise is too large for the world to contain, and no finite cost outweighs an arbitrarily large benefit.
(4) Therefore, whatever price the stranger names, there is a promise for which the expected utility of paying exceeds that of keeping the purse: some nonzero credence remains, and the payoff can be raised without limit.
(5) Hence rationality requires paying at any price; and since a second stranger can always appear with a still larger offer, it requires Pascal to remain on that corner surrendering everything indefinitely.
(6) By parity of reasoning, the theory also requires devoting all resources to any hypothesis with a vast payoff and negligible probability — including pairs of mutually incompatible promises, so it recommends contradictory actions at once.
(7) A theory of rationality with these consequences is not one, so (1) is false: rationality must discount tiny probabilities as such, regardless of the payoff attached.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I can see three escape routes — credence that falls faster than the payoff rises, bounded utility, and amending (1) itself — and I want each priced, including what it gives up on ordinary cases: what bounded utility does to the value of averting billions of deaths at tiny odds, what a complexity penalty on credences does to exotic but real risks. Note that steps (5) and (6) may not follow even from premises you accept, so tell me which of them are genuine consequences and which are overheated. For my part, I would most like to keep unbounded utility and the nonzero-credence requirement in (2); step (7)'s blanket discounting of small probabilities strikes me as an ad hoc fix. Finally, be explicit about what would change your verdict: which premise's diagnosis rests most on a contestable assumption, and where in your own reasoning are you most likely to have gone wrong.
