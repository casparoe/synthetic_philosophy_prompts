Think carefully before you answer: brainstorm internally, consider the strongest version of the position you are auditing rather than a strawman, and organize your response before committing to a verdict.

The position I want audited rests on a single analogy: natural selection is a designer. In the artifact case, a knife is for cutting because the knifemaker intended it to cut, and it malfunctions — fails at its function — when it cannot cut, even if it serves admirably as a paperweight. In the biological case, the heart is for pumping blood because pumping is what ancestral hearts were selected for doing, so a heart that does not pump is diseased rather than merely useless. So far the analogy seems to earn its keep: it explains how there can be malfunction — a normative-looking notion — without any intentional mind behind it. But my professor then takes a further step. Since selection gives us biological functions, and since beliefs look like things that can be true or false — can, so to speak, function properly or misfire — we should be able to get content out of the same machinery. A state means whatever it was selected for carrying: roughly, the worldly condition that, in the environment where the state was selected, allowed it to do its evolutionary job. A frog's snap reflex is about flies because tracking flies is what selected for that reflex, and a false belief is like a blunt knife — an off-token of a type that normally does its work. The conclusion drawn is that intentionality is no mystery after all: aboutness is just selected function all the way up.

Audit this analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table in detail — designer, intention, blueprint, artifact malfunction, workshop, planned obsolescence, and anything else that suggests itself — mapped onto the evolutionary counterparts. Then say where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the content conclusion and those that do not. In particular, I want your judgment on at least these pressure points. (1) A designer can hold a determinate specification in mind, while selection history may underdetermine what a trait was selected for — the sort of worry people raise with frogs that snap at flies and at BB pellets, or with any trait whose ancestors' survival was served equally well by several different effects of it. Is this fatal to the step from function to content, or merely a refinement? (2) The swampman case — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate assembled by chance, with no selection history — appears to show that content does not supervene on selection. How bad is that for the analogy, and does it tell equally against the heart case, or only against the content case? (3) Can the designer analogy even in principle get us from malfunction to misrepresentation? A blunt knife is not about anything; a false belief seems to be about the very thing it gets wrong. What has to be added to selected function before there is a bearer of truth, and does the addition reintroduce precisely the intentional notions the analogy was supposed to replace?

Finally, give a verdict: does the conclusion — content naturalized via selected function — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if so, what is the honest, restricted version of the claim? If it does not survive, say what the best fallback is for someone attracted to naturalizing content but unpersuaded by the teleosemantic route.
