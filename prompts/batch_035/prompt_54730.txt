I direct the conceptual arm of a small research program on welfare ethics and moral uncertainty at a grantmaking organization. Next funding cycle I can support about four researchers for two years on the cluster of issues surrounding lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain — theory and formal work, not empirical welfare science, though I care about where the two touch.

By lexical threshold negative utilitarianism I mean the family of views on which there is some severity of suffering such that no amount or duration of positive experience can outweigh it — where preventing a single instance of that severity lexically dominates any quantity of pleasure — while leaving open whether milder pains remain commensurable with pleasures in the ordinary additive way. I am not asking you to defend or attack the view itself. I am asking which of the open questions in this cluster actually deserve the funding, and in what order.

Some candidates, roughly sketched, though I want you to feel free to restructure, add, or discard:

1. Coherence: whether lexical thresholds can be stated so that they are formally stable — whether the threshold must be sharp or can be vague, whether lexicality survives aggregation across persons and probabilities, and whether a view with a lexical component can avoid collapsing into either standard additive utilitarianism or an absurd view on which the tiniest chance of threshold-level suffering dominates everything else.

2. Measurement: whether anything in how pleasure and pain are actually measured — categorical severity scales, ordinal reporting, dissociations between intensity and duration — could ever license a commensurability claim in either direction, or whether the metaphysical question is simply insulated from the empirical one.

3. Justificatory genealogy: whether the appeal of lexical intuitions has an respectable evolutionary or contractualist pedigree, or whether it is better explained as a scope-insensitivity heuristic misapplied to the moral domain — and whether this matters for how much we should trust the intuitions.

4. Practical surrogacy: if the theory cannot be settled within decades, which decision-relevant proxy (e.g., treating severe suffering as carrying an enormous but finite weight, or lexicographic weighting only under resource-allocation contexts rather than utility comparisons proper) should effective altruist-style cause prioritization use in the meantime.

For each question you keep on the list, tell me why it ranks where it does: how many other questions in the cluster depend on it, whether it is tractable for philosophers with formal training versus needing collaborations with welfare scientists, whether it is neglected or already crowded, and what developments over the next few years would move it up or down. I am less interested in a survey than in a defended ordering, and I specifically want to know which of the popular framings in this debate — pinprick-style arguments, appeals to the asymmetry between creating happy and unhappy lives, the inference from suffering's urgency to its lexical priority — are doing real work versus which are overrated rhetorical scaffolding.

Two requests on the epistemics of your answer. First, be explicit about your confidence in each of the major claims you make, not just the overall ordering. Second, single out the point in your ranking you are least sure of, say what would make you flip on it, and indicate where you suspect you may be privileging a framing that the existing literature has conditioned you toward rather than one an outsider would find natural.
