I run a philosophy of mind reading group — a mix of grad students from other departments (a historian, a comp lit student, a couple of neuroscientists) — and for the applied half of an upcoming session I want us to read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through Daniel Dennett's ladder of minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, where each kind is a more sophisticated way of letting error kill a hypothesis instead of the organism — hard-wired moves, trial-and-error learning, tests run in imagination before the world gets a chance to punish you, and finally minds re-engineered by borrowed mind-tools like language, notation, and maps. The creature's education seems almost suspiciously well suited to this. He works out by experiment which foods sustain him and that fire both warms and burns, which reads as the Skinnerian stage; his long campaign with the De Laceys — learning their language while hidden, rehearsing his appeal, choosing the blind father as his safest first contact so that his hypothesis of human acceptance can be tested with the least exposure — looks like Popperian let-the-hypothesis-die-in-your-stead reasoning; and once he acquires language and devours Plutarch and Milton, he can manipulate abstractions, describe himself as an outcast and a fallen angel, plan deceptions, and torment Victor by calculated proxy rather than blind rage, which is the Gregorian ascent. My question for the group, and for you: as a reading of the novel, does this taxonomy map onto identifiable episodes, and where does Shelley put the thresholds between stages?

I'd also want the reading to press where the lens strains, because I suspect it strains in an interesting place. Dennett's ladder presupposes a Darwinian substrate — millions of years of innate, selected design under the learning — whereas Shelley's creature is a Lockean blank who seems to start with nothing and yet climbs to articulate selfhood, moral theory, and strategic revenge in under two years. Is that the novel's failure to take seriously what minds require, or is it the novel arguing, ahead of its time, that the social and linguistic tools can do more of the work than Dennett's bottom rung allows? And the question I'd most like the group to end on: is the creature's turn to elaborate, book-fed vengeance simply a Gregorian mind using its tools, or does the novel suggest the tools did something to him — that the ladder of error-avoidance is also a ladder of new and worse ways to err, since his self-interpretations as fallen angel and injured outcast, borrowed from the books, seem to close off paths his earlier, less articulate self might still have taken? Please take both the novel and the taxonomy seriously rather than reducing one to an illustration of the other, and flag the places where a group like mine will be tempted to force the analogy too hard.
