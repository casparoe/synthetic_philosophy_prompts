write me a short story about quantum immortality, but i want it to actually grapple with the decision theory, not just use the idea as spooky flavor. here's the setup i have in mind:

the protagonist is a physicist (or a grad student, whatever feels right) who survived something that should've killed her — say, a plane crash she walks away from as the sole survivor, though pick whatever works. afterwards she can't stop running the numbers. if the many-worlds picture is right, she reasons, there were branches where she died, and the only branches she can ever find herself in are ones where improbably many things went right. so from the inside, survival isn't evidence of luck; it's just what finding-yourself-alive looks like. fine. but here's the twist i actually care about: she starts to wonder whether the same logic gives her *permission*. if every risky action has branches where she walks away, is she allowed to treat her own future as insured? the story should turn on the gap between "i will always find myself alive" and "i should act as if i can't die" — the difference between an anthropic observation and a decision rule.

and she should eventually notice the part nobody puts on the poster: the reasoning that makes survival unsurprising also makes every future version of her that's maimed, or locked-in, or brain-damaged, equally guaranteed to be found-from-the-inside. quantum immortality promises you a branch where you survive; it says nothing about the *condition* you survive in, and most of the measure of "surviving" is not what people mean by the word. that noticing should land somewhere in the middle of the story, not the end, and it should hurt a little.

a few constraints:

- don't resolve whether many-worlds is actually true. she doesn't know, the reader shouldn't know, and honestly the story is better if her reasoning cuts against her either way. if many-worlds is false she's just a lucky survivor rationalizing; if it's true she's a thin sliver of herself congratulating the sliver. the story should hold both readings at once.
- no "and then she gambled with her life and the universe winked" ending. im bored of those. if she takes a risk, or refuses to, the interesting part is how she justifies it to herself in her own words — maybe a scene where somebody who loves her pushes back and she genuinely can't tell whether she's doing philosophy or talking herself into something.
- i'd like at least one scene where the argument is laid out properly, as she'd actually say it — assumptions stated, even the ones she's smuggling in. she's a physicist, she'd catch the smuggling eventually, and that moment of catching herself should be one of the best beats in the story.

length: whatever a short story needs, maybe 1500-2500 words. and don't explain the philosophy after the story ends — let the last scene carry it.
