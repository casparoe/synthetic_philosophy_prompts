I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on my first paper in value theory, and I'd be grateful for comments on the argument sketched below before I invest a term in writing it up. Specifically, please tell me: (a) whether the central argument works or where it breaks; (b) whether I'm representing the standard positions I criticize fairly, or trading on caricatures; and (c) whether, if it works, the paper would be genuinely interesting or just a relabeling of familiar moves. I'm not looking for citations or literature pointers — I have a bibliography plan already — I want the argument itself stress-tested. At the end, please state how confident you are in your overall verdict and single out the point (either in my argument or in your own objections) that you are least sure about.

The topic is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. Hedonism says a life goes well to the extent that it contains a favorable balance of pleasure over pain. But it's notoriously hard to say what all pleasures have in common. The pleasure of an ice-cold drink on a humid afternoon, of finally cracking a proof after three days, of schadenfreude at a rival's downfall, of sinking into a warm bath, of being moved by the last movement of a symphony — introspectively these seem to share nothing but the name. If there's no common property, the "summing" in the hedonist's formula is mysterious: what quantity is being added up across such heterogeneous episodes? And if there is a common property, it seems too thin and generic to be the whole substance of a good life.

My paper considers the three standard answers and finds each wanting. First, the phenomenological answer: all pleasures share a distinctive felt quality. This looks dead on arrival, since no one can point to the common quale without just gesturing at the heterogeneous instances again. Second, the attitudinal answer, associated with Fred Feldman: what unifies pleasures is not how they feel but that they involve taking a certain favorable attitude toward their objects. This handles heterogeneity elegantly, but at what seems to me the wrong price: attitudinal hedonism has simply changed the subject. What I care about for my own welfare is how my life feels from the inside, not how I'm disposed to appraise it — a depressed person can take attitudinal pleasure in the thought that things are going well while feeling wretched. Third, the desire-based answer in the spirit of Sidgwick: pleasure is the kind of consciousness that, when had, is desired for its own sake and desired to persist. This ties pleasure to motivation rather than feeling, but it inherits the counterexamples that plague desire theories generally — masochistic desires, adaptive preferences, desires for experiences one is not currently enjoying.

The constructive move I want to make borrows the realizer/role distinction from philosophy of mind. The heterogeneity problem assumes that if pleasure is a unified property, it must be unified by a shared intrinsic feature — a quale, an attitude type, a mode of desire. But a property can equally be unified by the role its bearers play. My proposal: pleasure is whatever state occupies a certain motivational-attentional role — roughly, a state that is sought and reinforced, that holds attention without effortful maintenance, and whose termination motivates recovery of it. On this reading, the heterogeneity of pleasures is no more troubling for hedonism than the heterogeneity of pain-realizers is for the identification of pain with a functional state: it is just multiple realization. The felt diversity of pleasures is the diversity of realizers; the unity relevant to hedonism's arithmetic is the unity of role.

The objection I anticipate, and where I suspect the paper is most vulnerable: role-hedonism is either circular or extensionally wrong. Circular, because specifying the role in terms of what is "sought" and "reinforced" imports desire, and I've already rejected desire-based accounts. Extensionally wrong, because some states occupying the role don't seem to be pleasures at all — think of flow states, or the grip of an addictive loop — while some paradigm pleasures seem to occupy it only dubiously, as with calm, effortful pleasures that one doesn't reflexively seek to prolong. I tentatively reply that the first horn shows only that the role must be specified without intentional-attitude vocabulary, not that no role specification is available, and that the second horn is a disagreement about cases rather than a refutation — though I'm aware that "disagreement about cases" can be a euphemism for having nothing to say.

Is the functionalist rescue a real contribution, or have I reinvented something that failed years ago under a different name? And is the circularity objection fatal or merely a research constraint? Those are the two questions I most need help on.
