I'm a novelist working on a chapter set in a near-future international order, and I need a satirical document for my protagonist to read. The novel's conceit is this: after a century of inconclusive historiographical warfare over who started the First World War, the world's governments finally solve the problem the only way left to them — by marketizing it. War guilt is now a tradable asset class. Nations commission audits of their historical responsibility, issue guilt-backed securities, offset unacknowledged atrocities by purchasing absolution credits from historically blameless states, and a body called the Versailles Clearinghouse publishes a quarterly Guilt Index. My protagonist is a junior analyst at a historical-consultancy firm, and the document I need is the market's flagship annual outlook report.

Please take the role of a writer who is equally at home in political philosophy and in parodying investor-relations prose, and draft that report — roughly 700-800 words, in the plausibly dull voice of a genuine financial-market publication (a prospectus crossed with a central-bank outlook), with section headings, a small glossary sidebar, and at least one forward-looking disclaimer. This is satire, but it must earn its laughs by being philosophically exact, because the novel's whole argument lives in the fine print. Concretely:

- The report's tone should never wink. The comedy comes from the market apparatus silently precisifying the ambiguities philosophers have fought over for decades: causal contribution gets priced separately from moral blameworthiness; the blameworthiness of the state gets unbundled from the liability of individual citizens; backward-looking guilt and forward-looking reparations obligations become distinct product lines. A reader who knows the collective-responsibility literature should recognize each real distinction at the moment it is corrupted into a line item.
- Somewhere in the report there should be a market-moving event that is a thinly disguised version of the Fischer controversy: a historian's monograph arguing that a great power deliberately seized a war it had long prepared, triggering index volatility, threats of academic downgrades, and a proposed rule change about whether premeditation and mere risk-acceptance belong in different compliance buckets. The report should treat all of this purely as a pricing event.
- I want at least one clause that quietly inherits the logic of the Versailles war-guilt clause — the 1919 treaty article assigning Germany responsibility for the war's damage as the legal basis for reparations — and at least one commentator quote that raises, without ever naming it, the oldest objection in the whole debate: that a nation is not the kind of entity that can intend anything, so someone, somewhere, is holding a bag that belongs to no one.
- The closing disclaimer should make the protagonist's problem concrete: the market prices responsibility without ever deciding who, if anyone, is blameworthy — which is either the market's fatal flaw or its entire value proposition, and the report cannot tell which.

Before you write, think carefully and brainstorm first. Lay out for yourself the distinct senses of "responsible" in play — causal contribution, moral blameworthiness, legal liability, and the forward-looking obligation to repair — and for each, decide how a market would commodify it and what exactly would be lost in the conversion. Also consider what happens to the distinction between holding a nation's government responsible and holding its people responsible, generations later, when both are securities. Then write the report so that a specialist in collective responsibility finds it fair — every joke an exaggeration of something real in the debate, nothing invented from whole cloth — while a reader with no philosophy background finds it simply funny in a dry, mildly alarming way.
