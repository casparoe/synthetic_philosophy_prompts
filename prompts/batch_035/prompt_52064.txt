I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and my dissertation is on metacognition — mostly type-2 signal detection and trial-by-trial confidence ratings. While writing my background chapters I've been trying to bring in the consciousness literature, and higher-order theories strike me as the natural bridge: they hold that a first-order mental state is conscious only when there is a further, higher-order representation of that state, which sounds like it should be exactly what my confidence judgments are tapping.

Here is my problem. The metacognition literature I work in shows that people are often only modestly accurate at monitoring their own cognitive processes — sometimes barely above chance — and that confidence frequently tracks factors that had nothing to do with how the process actually unfolded. If higher-order theories are right, doesn't this commit us to saying that we are conscious of far less of our mental lives than we take ourselves to be, and that most introspective reports are confabulations stitched together after the fact? And I've also seen it claimed that the higher-order representation can outright misrepresent the first-order state, which sounds to me like saying you can be conscious of a state you never had — if that's allowed, I don't see what the theory is ruling out anymore.

More practically: my experiments ask subjects to report their awareness on every trial, and I had been planning to treat those reports as direct evidence about consciousness. If the higher-order story plus the metacognition findings are right, it seems those reports are about as trustworthy as eyewitness testimony — and I'd need some correction procedure before I could use them. Before I build my dissertation on this, I'd like a careful account of whether the tension I'm describing is real, and if not, where exactly my reasoning goes wrong.
