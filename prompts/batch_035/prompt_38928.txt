I've become drawn to what Dennett calls the Stalinesque position on the timing of consciousness, and I want a serious map of where it is exposed.

The case that moved me is color phi: two stationary spots, one red and one green, flashed in quick succession at different positions, are experienced as a single moving dot that changes color partway across the sweep. The puzzle is that the change of color is experienced as happening before the green spot arrives, which means the experience carries information it shouldn't yet have. On the Orwellian reading, the subject first consciously experiences red-here, then green-there, and the memory of that sequence is promptly revised so it seems there was a smooth color change in between; consciousness is veridical but short-lived. On the Stalinesque reading, the brain holds the signal back, composes the edited narrative -- dot moving, turning color midway -- and only then delivers it to consciousness, so nothing conscious is ever overwritten. I find the Stalinesque reading cleaner: it posits no annihilated experiences, and it fits the general picture of perception as a construction delivered with a delay rather than a live feed.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. A few I've half-formed myself, which I'd like evaluated rather than merely supplemented: (1) a case where the pre-edit content is recoverable by behavior -- say a fast manual response, given within a window too short for post-experiential revision, that reliably tracks the unedited sequence, while slower verbal reports track the edited one. Would that vindicate the Orwellian, or would the Stalinesque theorist just say both responses reflect different drafts, none privileged as conscious? (2) Conditions like blindsight and change blindness, where subjects demonstrably carry unreported or over-written-looking content: do these favor one reading over the other, or are they neutral? (3) The cutaneous rabbit, where taps at the wrist are experienced at intervening locations before those locations are stimulated: does the fact that the illusion is spatially resolved in advance tell for Stalinesque delay, or does it cut the other way, since the effect is spatial rather than temporal?

Two structural questions matter to me as much as the empirical ones. First, does the Stalinesque view make any prediction its Orwellian rival doesn't? Dennett's own move, as I understand it, is to argue that the two accounts are not just empirically but in principle indistinguishable, because there is no further fact about when consciousness "really" happened beyond what all discriminations and memories show. But if that is so, my worry is that I haven't adopted a theory at all -- I've adopted a preference between two labels for the same data, and the "no further fact" claim is doing all the work while being insulated from any finding. Second, is that insulation a defect for a view of this kind, or is it the expected and acceptable upshot of a scientific theory in a domain where the appearance of a further fact is precisely what is in question? I can see it argued both ways, and I don't trust my own judgment here.

Please take a committed position rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose: tell me which putative defeaters a consistent Stalinesque theorist should concede as real costs, which they can absorb, whether the view in its defensible form still deserves the label, and what you yourself would conclude if the fast-response experiment above came out the way I described. Please write in American English.
