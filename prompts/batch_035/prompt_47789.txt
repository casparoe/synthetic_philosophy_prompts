I'm a novelist three-quarters through a draft about a research lab's language model quietly wrecking things nobody intended it to touch, and I've persuaded my writers' circle — six people, no technical background, all of us saturated in a decade of AI cinema — to give me one ninety-minute session on the two ideas my book needs to get right: the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence. I don't want the ideas themselves from you; I want the design of the session. What has to be in place before instrumental convergence can register as a claim about the world rather than as atmosphere, which single invented case should carry both ideas (it must give the system a goal the room finds mundane rather than sinister, and it must not collapse back into the machine-wants-power story we all grew up on), the misconceptions the circle will bring and the order they will surface in — intelligence curdling into malice, sentience as the precondition for having goals at all, the model 'choosing' self-preservation — with the point in the sequence at which each should be taken apart, one question I can put to the room at the end that would reveal whether they can now spot lazy anthropomorphism in a manuscript rather than merely nod along, and what I should openly concede lies beyond a single session and decline to teach.
