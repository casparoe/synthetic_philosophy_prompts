Last night a friend and I argued for hours about forgeries and the value of originals, and by the end she had issued verdicts on a long series of cases. Her rulings, as best I can reconstruct them: an undetectable forgery of an actual Vermeer — visually indistinguishable, with a fabricated provenance — loses essentially all of its value and even looks worse to her once exposed; a "newly discovered Vermeer" that is really an original composition by a gifted forger, beautiful and seemingly innovative, suffers the same collapse; a Renaissance altarpiece designed and retouched by the master but largely executed by workshop assistants retains its full value; a painting so heavily restored that most of the visible surface is modern conservator's paint also retains its full value; but an artist's own exact replica of her most celebrated painting, made later in life with complete sincerity and skill, is worth considerably less than the first canvas. This morning she messaged me the case we never settled: suppose the genuine Vermeer from the first ruling is destroyed in a fire, the forger's duplicate survives and passes as the Vermeer for a century — shaping painters, hanging in a museum, becoming the beloved image of that work — and is then exposed. I'm less interested in the answer than in the reasoning: which rules fit her five verdicts (at least two seem to, and they should diverge on the new case), what each predicts about a duplicate that becomes culturally foundational while the original burns, what she will most plausibly say and why, and which further case I should put to her to tell the surviving rules apart.
