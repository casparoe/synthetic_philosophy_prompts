I'm a graduate TA for a 200-level philosophy of mind course, and for the section on emotion I run a course blog where each week I post a "which view do you actually hold?" diagnostic quiz before the seminar on that topic. We've done personal identity and free will; next week is emotion theory, covering roughly: judgment theories (emotions as evaluative judgments), perceptual theories (emotions as perceptions or appearances of value), pure feeling theories (emotions as feelings of bodily change), and attitudinal theories (emotions as evaluative stances toward objects that are correct or incorrect rather than true or false), with motivational/action-readiness views as a possible fifth position.

Please write me a quiz of about ten questions. Each question should be a concrete case with two or three answer options, and the cases must be ones on which these positions genuinely come apart, so that readers are sorted by what they say about the case rather than by which label they find attractive. The following cases must appear, since I plan to use the quiz results to structure discussion: recalcitrant fear (e.g., standing terrified on a glass skywalk while fully believing the structure is safe); emotional response to fiction (weeping for a character you are certain does not exist); a phobia about which the person has no evaluative belief at all (pure avoidance of dogs despite judging this dog harmless); diminished reported emotionality after spinal cord injury; and alexithymia-like dissociation, where bodily arousal is present but the person reports no emotion and cannot identify any. Beyond those five, add whatever further cases you think best discriminate among the views — candidates I'd consider include infants or non-human animals lacking the relevant evaluative concepts, identical bodily arousal differently identified as anger or elation, guilt that persists despite the agent's judgment that she did nothing wrong, and anger that strikes one as unfitting even while the underlying beliefs are accurate.

Constraints on the questions: no question may ask directly whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, feelings, or attitudes; every question must be answerable by someone who has never read any emotion theory. And the options should describe what is actually going on in the case (e.g., "the fear is real but misrepresents the situation" versus "the fear is just the body's response, the belief is what's doing the evaluating"), not name any view.

After the quiz, include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the positions, including a judgment theory with the recalcitrant-emotion problem built in as a live possibility (i.e., the key should allow the reader to land there rather than rig the quiz against it). Where two or more of the positions cannot be separated by the quiz — for instance, if perceptual and attitudinal theories predict the same answers on all ten cases — say so explicitly rather than inventing a fake discriminating question, and explain in a sentence or two what further case would be needed to pull them apart. Also note any coherent combination of answers that corresponds to no standard position, with a sentence on whether you think it's unoccupied because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

The response should begin directly with the quiz — first question first, no title sequence, preamble, or commentary on the assignment — with the scoring key and any discussion of undetachable positions and unoccupied combinations coming afterward.
