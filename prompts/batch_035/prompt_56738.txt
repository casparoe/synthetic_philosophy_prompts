Last night a friend and I argued for hours about who was to blame for the First World War, and I want to work out what I actually think rather than let it end in a stalemate. Here is the shape the argument took.

She began from the Fischer controversy: Fischer's case, built on the archival record of German war aims — the September Programme of 1914 and the decision to hand Austria-Hungary the so-called blank cheque — for treating Germany as the one power that deliberately risked, and to a substantial degree wanted, a general European war. She said she no longer believes any of that, citing the newer multi-causal accounts of the sort made famous by Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers: all the great powers accepted grave risks, none wanted the war that actually came, and the crisis was driven by Balkan entanglements, alliance machinery, mobilization timetables, and mutual misreading. From this she drew a two-step conclusion. First, the empirical step: the Fischer-style thesis is false, so Germany was not uniquely responsible. Second, the conceptual step: since no state is uniquely responsible, blaming a nation for the war is a mistake — and in any case nations are not the kind of thing that can be blamed, since they have no mind, only members do, so talk of a guilty nation is either a category error or loose shorthand for a list of blameworthy officials.

I pushed back on both steps, and I want to see how far the pushback goes. On the conceptual step, I suspect my friend is assuming that responsibility requires sole authorship — that unless one agent caused the war, no one is responsible for it. But we do not ordinarily treat blame as all-or-nothing. Where several people each take a reckless risk that jointly produces a disaster, we ask not who authored the outcome but who could most easily have prevented it, who had the clearest view of what was at stake, who had a legitimate reason for running the risk, and whose contribution the outcome was most sensitive to. Those are matters of degree, and they could distribute responsibility unequally across the powers without making any of them the sole author. There is also something suspicious about the rhetoric of shared responsibility: a finding that blame is everywhere can function as a finding that it is nowhere, and the party with the most power to stop a catastrophe is often the one that benefits most when the catastrophe is declared nobody's fault.

On the empirical step, I want to know whether the multi-causal view really redistributes responsibility evenly, or whether it just changes what responsibility tracks — from intentions to risk-taking. If several powers accepted comparable risks for comparable stakes, even-handedness follows; but if one power ran an enormous risk for illegitimate stakes while holding the brake and refusing to use it, the multi-causal picture may leave differential responsibility intact.

Write an essay that works through this dispute and takes a side. Structure it with these sections, in this order:

1. The Fischer thesis and its rivals (roughly 350 words). Set out the strongest version of the Fischer-style case and the strongest multi-causal rejoinder, as claims about decision-making in July 1914 rather than as slogans. You need not attribute every point to a named historian.

2. From causation to responsibility (roughly 400 words). Does rejecting the Fischer thesis dissolve responsibility, redistribute it, or merely change what it tracks? Consider the preventability, stakes, and risk-based dimensions of responsibility above, and say whether any of them could single out one power even under a multi-causal description of the war.

3. Can a nation be blamed? (roughly 400 words). Take seriously my friend's individualism: states have no mind, only members do. What would a defender of nation-level responsibility have to say — shared intention, institutional agency, decades-long policy traditions, later populations inheriting the gains — and does any of it survive, or is nation-blame at best shorthand for blaming long-dead officials?

4. Verdict (roughly 250 words). Take a position on what it means, or fails to mean, to say that a nation is responsible for a war, and say which power or powers, if any, come out bearing how much responsibility under your account. Do not end in neutrality.

Please do not pad the essay with a bibliography or historiographical name-dropping for its own sake; argue the substance. And if my own moves above are weak, sharpen them rather than rescuing them — I would rather find out now than at my friend's kitchen table.
