I'm a novelist planning a book written as one continuous present-tense monologue: the narrator reports his own thoughts and perceptions as they occur, sentence by sentence, with no retrospection and no interruption. Readers and reviewers treat those reports as authoritative in a way they treat no other character's speech — nobody inside the novel, and no reader, can correct the narrator about what he is thinking. The technique strikes me as a descendant of Descartes's cogito, but the convention seems to claim more than the cogito proves: as I understand it, the cogito establishes at most that thinking is occurring and that the thinker exists, whereas my monologue seems to presuppose that the thinker is incorrigible about the content of his own thoughts. Now my problem: I want the novel's final turn to reveal that the narrator has been self-deceived throughout about the motive driving the monologue — that what he has been reporting as his reasons were rationalizations all along. So: what theory of first-person authority does the technique of interior monologue actually presuppose? Is it genuinely Cartesian — a domain of facts about one's own mind that the narrator cannot be wrong about — or is it something weaker, such as an asymmetry of access rather than infallibility, that would leave room for pervasive self-deception? Reconstruct the implicit theory the convention rests on, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and tell me whether my planned ending honors the contract that makes the monologue intelligible or quietly breaks it. Finally, in the spirit of full disclosure, tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to fail, and what consideration — for instance, an account on which the narrator's self-reports function as expressive performances rather than as assertions — would change your verdict. Please write in American English.
