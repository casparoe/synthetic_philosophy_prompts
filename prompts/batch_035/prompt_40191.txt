I should say at the outset who is writing this: I am a retired evolutionary biologist who spent a career measuring demography and life histories in wild populations, and who has come rather late in life to moral philosophy. Bentham has been my introduction, and I have been working through an essay he composed around 1785 on what he called offences against oneself — an argument for ceasing to treat consensual homosexual conduct as a crime, which he withheld from publication during his lifetime for fear of the reaction it would provoke. Since it happens to be from a text Bentham never published, I will paraphrase his reasoning rather than quote it. As I understand him, he held that the only primary mischief of a non-procreative sexual act between consenting parties would be a tendency to diminish population, that this tendency is negligible in any populous nation, and that nearly all the real harm done by such conduct is produced not by the acts themselves but by the law's punishments and by the social infamy attached to them; he also dismissed, on what look to me like sound grounds, the claims that such conduct weakens the men who practice it or threatens marriage.

I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about when the criminal law should reach sexual conduct, by recording my verdicts on cases and looking for the principle underneath them. My verdicts are these:

1. Consensual homosexual conduct between adults in private: no business of the criminal law. This is Bentham's own case, and I agree with him.
2. The same conduct performed in public, in a place where unconsenting passers-by are likely to encounter it: properly a matter for the law, at least as a minor offence. The unwilling spectator makes a difference to me.
3. Adultery: a genuine wrong to the betrayed spouse, and yet I would not criminalize it. The legal cure seems worse than the disease.
4. Incest between two adult close relatives who are infertile — say, a post-menopausal woman and her brother — so that no offspring are possible and both consent. Here I falter. My working view is that it should remain criminal, or at minimum that it is wrong in a way that the conduct in (1) is not, and I cannot see how to square that with (1).
5. Bestiality: my gut is that this should be punished, and punished more readily than adultery, although on Bentham's own reckoning there is no human victim other than the disgusted observer, and Bentham remarked that we do far worse to animals in the slaughterhouse. This verdict too sits badly beside (1).
6. A stable arrangement in which three informed adults live as mutual partners, all consenting: nothing here for the criminal law, so far as I can see.
7. Obtaining sex from a woman by a promise of marriage that is made insincerely and never intended to be kept: I would treat this as at least a civil wrong, and I am tempted to criminalize it. Yet I notice that this begins to look like the law protecting a person's judgment about her own consent — a kind of paternalism that my other verdicts seem to condemn.
8. Commercial sex between consenting adults: decriminalize.
9. Any case involving minors I regard as settled — they cannot consent, and that is the end of it — so please set such cases aside.

My demographic training makes me fairly confident of Bentham's population claim, at least in the direction he needed it: in a nation with surplus births, the fraction of unions that are non-procreative is lost in the noise, and I do not think my verdict in (1) rests on a doubtful empirical premise.

So my question, put plainly: is there a single principle that captures these verdicts? My tentative candidate is that the criminal law may properly reach sexual conduct only where it harms a non-consenting party — with unwillingly imposed disgust or offence counting as a real but minor harm, as in (2) — and that the law may never act on repugnance alone. But this candidate plainly accommodates (1), (2), (3), (6) and (8) while being embarrassed by (4), (5) and possibly (7). I would like you to do for me what I cannot yet do for myself. Please state, as precisely as you can, the principle or the small set of candidate principles that best fits the verdicts, naming for each clause the case that motivates it. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the resulting principle cannot accommodate, and whether they should be accommodated at the price of complexity — perhaps by admitting repugnance as a weak but non-zero ground, or by distinguishing victimless wrongs from wrongs that are merely victimless in the narrow sense — or simply abandoned. And please tell me what the principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: a brother and sister, infertile as before, who conceal their relationship entirely from everyone; and a person who, while sound of mind, gives written consent to a partner for an act upon her body after her death, which the partner then carries out without any living person knowing of it. I would rather know where the principle leads than be spared the answer.
