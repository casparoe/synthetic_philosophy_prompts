So I'm a novelist and I've gotten obsessed with this argument from a philosopher named Scheffler (I think it's in a book about death and the afterlife, the exact details are fuzzy for me tbh). The idea, roughly: human extinction is supposed to be this ultimate catastrophe, but when you ask people why, they mostly say "all those deaths." But imagine the deaths aren't the issue at all -- imagine everyone now living gets a full lifespan, but for some reason no new children are ever born, or a comet takes out the planet exactly fifty years after the last current person dies naturally. Nobody alive loses a minute. Scheffler's claim is that even in that scenario, a lot of what we value would quietly come apart -- cancer research, planting vineyards, writing novels, saving for grandchildren, even music somehow means less if it's the last music. It's not that we love future people we'll never meet, it's that our projects are structured around an assumption that there will be a "later" that we're not part of.

Anyway I want to write a story about this but I keep writing essays instead of fiction, and I want to test whether the premise can actually carry a narrative rather than just be the premise. So: write me a short story, maybe 1200-1800 words, set in a world where everyone learns, with scientific certainty, that humanity ends exactly thirty years after the death of the last currently-living person (no one's lifespan is shortened, just the species has a hard stop after them). No explanation is ever given for WHY it ends, and make that matter -- I don't want a disaster movie, I want the epistemic weirdness of a guaranteed, causeless terminus.

The story should follow two central characters whose reactions diverge: one who keeps doing long-horizon work (I've been imagining an archivist digitizing a library collection that will outlive its readers, but you can pick something else -- a cathedral restorer, a breeder of a slow apple variety, whatever), and one who stops, or who pivots hard to pleasure or to their immediate family. Don't make either one the sane one. The interesting question I want dramatized, not answered in dialogue: does the archivist's work actually retain its point, or are they just failing to update? And crucially -- this is the part I keep fumbling -- I want some moment where a project's meaning flickers. Like, someone plants an oak knowing full well when the last person who could ever see it mature dies, and the story has to sit in that without being sentimental or smug either way.

One more constraint: no deathbed scenes, no apocalypse porn, and don't let any character explain the philosophy out loud like they're a talking paper. If it can be said in a sentence, show it instead. And please, no twist ending where it turns out the prediction was wrong -- that's the cheap exit and I've already written it twice.
