context for this: i'm taking a social choice course and we just covered Sen's impossibility of a paretian liberal. i can reproduce the theorem fine, but for my term paper what i actually want to figure out is which of the two conditions should give way when they conflict — and my hunch is the answer depends on the case, which is annoying but maybe the interesting part.

so build me two scenarios. both have exactly two roommates and a household understanding that certain things are each person's own business (their half of the flat, their body, their stuff). each scenario has two such personal features, one per roommate, giving the four social states, and a preference profile that actually triggers the paradox — show the cycle explicitly, step by step, marking for each social preference whether it comes from someone's decisiveness over their own pair or from weak Pareto. if the profile that works forces someone to rank their own exercise of the option somewhere weird (like their worst state being the one where they alone do the thing), don't hide it — motivate it, and every ranking needs to be something a normal person could actually have, with a one-line reason behind each.

the two scenarios have to be a matched pair that differ only in the kind of spillover. in scenario A the features' effects cross the wall physically — one roommate's drumming through thin walls, the other's incense, that sort of thing — so the "personal sphere" designation looks shaky, and i'd bet most readers resolve it by shrinking the sphere (siding with Pareto). in scenario B the spillover is purely through the other person's sensibilities — a visible tattoo one roommate sincerely hates, the other eating meat in the flat when the first is genuinely (not performatively) against it — nothing crosses any wall, and here i'd bet readers side with the liberal condition and want Pareto weakened or restricted to preferences that aren't about the other's private sphere. the catch, and this is what i care about most: the two scenarios must be formally identical — same four states up to renaming, same profile shape — so any flip in people's verdicts can only be tracking the content of the sphere, not the logic. and don't get the flip by stipulation: both household arrangements have to be ones a reasonable flatshare agreement could actually have, no declaring one sphere assignment "obviously wrong" in a footnote.

after the scenarios, walk through the cycle in each (it should literally be the same cycle), say which difference between A and B you think does the work — physical vs. psychic spillover, body/identity vs. lifestyle, whatever — and ideally probe it, e.g. check whether the drum case flips if the objection is to the music itself rather than lost sleep. then tell me how a defender of the condition that loses in each scenario would attack the case, and what happens to the verdicts if you delete the working feature. honest hedging about whether the intuition flip would really happen is better than a tidy story — if you don't think it would, say so and propose a pair that would. also no name-dropping of the literature in the walkthroughs, i want the cases to carry the weight on their own.
