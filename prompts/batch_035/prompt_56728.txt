I am a novelist, and for the past nine years I have been at work, on and mostly on, upon a single book. My first novel did respectably — enough to buy me time and, apparently, enough to make me believe I could attempt something far larger: a three-part chronicle of one family across most of the twentieth century. Each year I have rewritten substantial portions of it; twice I have had encouraging interest from editors, each of whom wanted it to become, in some respect, a different book, and each of whose vision I pursued partway before retreating into my own. My agent, patient beyond what I deserve, has lately suggested that I set the manuscript aside and write something smaller and quicker. Her argument is the familiar one: whatever years I have already given this book are given regardless, and they should not dictate how I spend the next five. I understand the logic perfectly, and yet something in me refuses it, and I would like to know whether that something is wisdom or merely the pathology it resembles.

A friend who teaches psychology once described to me a classroom experiment that has stayed with me since. An ordinary dollar bill is auctioned to the highest bidder, with one twist: the second-highest bidder must also pay whatever they bid and receives nothing in return. Bidders, he told me, routinely push well past a dollar — because once you have bid ninety cents and someone bids ahead of you, quitting means losing ninety cents with certainty, while one more bid might still leave you the winner. The only move that is clearly rational, he said, is the refusal to enter the auction at all. I have come to suspect that I am standing in the middle of some version of that auction, though I cannot say precisely which one.

Here is what I would like help sorting out. Is my situation genuinely this auction, or does it only resemble it? The nine years are sunk, admittedly — but unlike the classroom game, the prize I am bidding for is not fixed in value in advance: the more I invest, the better the book arguably becomes, and no rival bidder takes it away if I pause. Or does something occupy the rival's role — the younger writer I once was, or the smaller books those nine years might otherwise have produced? If it is a genuine case of the auctioneer's trap, what would the counsel to stop bidding look like when translated into a working novelist's decisions — not the platitude about sunk costs, but the concrete point at which further years become bids against my own earlier ones? And is my agent entirely right that past investment should carry no weight at all, or does she overlook ways in which a decade of accumulated structure, voice, and half-solved problems genuinely changes what the next year of work is worth? Finally, I want a serious account of the difference between the obstinacy that produces the books we revere and the obstinacy that merely loses with dignity — because I can no longer tell, from the inside, which of the two I am practicing.
