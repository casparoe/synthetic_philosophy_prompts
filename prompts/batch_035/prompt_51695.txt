You are a professional philosopher of science with a strong grounding in physics; think carefully about the exchange below before adjudicating it, and begin your answer with a brief brainstorm of the different senses of "structure" and "object" that might be in play, so that your verdict is explicit about which sense it turns on.

Some background before I pose my question. I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has spent the last few years reading philosophy of science, and I recently hosted a dinner at which two guests spent the entire evening arguing about what physics says the world is made of. One guest, a former colleague of mine from my research days, has become enamored of the view that physics progressively strips away objects and leaves only structure — that what really exists are relations, and that the objects of ordinary and even of scientific discourse are just positions in a relational structure. The other, my son-in-law, who teaches philosophy, was having none of it. I took notes afterward because I wanted to remember the details, and I would like your help sorting out who was right about what. Here is my reconstruction of the argument, move by move.

(1) My colleague opened with particle physics. He said everything we know about an electron is exhausted by its place in the theory: its mass, spin, and charge are labels of how it transforms under the relevant symmetry groups, and the classic case study in this literature — the one where Fresnel's equations survived the transition to Maxwell while the ether they were about did not — shows that what survives scientific revolutions is precisely the mathematical structure, not the things it was allegedly about. So the rational attitude is to believe in the structure and stay agnostic about any relata underneath it.

(2) My son-in-law responded that this conflates epistemology with ontology. That our best descriptions of particles are given by symmetry groups shows, at most, that we know the structural properties of things; it does not show that the things are nothing over and above those properties. A railway timetable exhausts what you can say about the schedule and tells you nothing about the trains, but the trains still exist.

(3) My colleague replied that this is where modern physics actually delivers the stronger conclusion: for identical fermions, the formalism is symmetric under permutation of the particles, and there is no fact of the matter about which electron is which after an exchange — no individuality, no haecceity, nothing "over and above" the structural facts. Nature herself, not just our theories, has deleted the relata. If even the paradigm objects of physics turn out to be structure all the way down, the timetable analogy concedes the game: the trains were never anything but slots in the timetable.

(4) My son-in-law made two replies. First, he said the quantum case at most shows that particles are not individuals in an old metaphysical sense; it does not show there is nothing there — indeed physicists happily say there are 10 to the 23rd electrons in the room, and counting requires that there be things counted. Second, he raised what he called the Newman problem: if all you commit yourself to is that the world has some structure or other, you have said almost nothing, because any collection of things whatsoever can be endowed with relations of arbitrary cardinality, so pure structural claims become trivially true and empty. To avoid triviality, the structuralist must say which structure, and the moment he does, he is making substantive claims that need something to make them true — which smuggles the relata back in.

(5) My colleague's final move was that needing a truthmaker is a demand no one can meet anywhere: the demand for a ground for the ground either regresses forever or stops at something primitive, and the structuralist stops at structure, exactly as the objector stops at objects; primitiveness is not a special embarrassment for one side. He also suggested that my son-in-law's counting point was confused: what gets counted are excitations of fields, and an excitation is a state of something structured, not a little object.

(6) My son-in-law closed by saying that "field" is exactly the object doing the grounding that his colleague kept denying existed, and that a view whose relata keep reappearing one level down — first particles, then fields — is not structural realism but object realism with worse publicity.

They agreed to let me find a referee, and I confess that, despite a career in physics, I could not fully adjudicate it myself. Please referee it properly: go through the six moves in order, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating. Then tell me who is more right overall, whether either of them made a strong point that the other never actually answered, and what, if anything, both of them are jointly missing — for instance, whether the dispute might partly turn on which of the several things one might mean by "structure" or by "object," and whether an unresolved disagreement of that kind is substantive, verbal, or a mixture of both. I would also appreciate your candor about which of the moves depends most on contestable physics, since I am better placed than most to check that part.
