After a career spent modelling atmospheric dispersion — thirty years in which every tonne of a pollutant could be traced back to some identifiable stack or exhaust pipe — I have, since retiring, been reading moral philosophy, and I keep snagging on the same knot: what to make of my own paltry contributions to harms that are, in aggregate, enormous but, taken singly, next to nothing. My reading group has been of no help, so I have done what I would once have done with puzzling readings: taken my own intuitions down to the cases, one at a time. The results are below, and I should say in advance that two of them do not sit comfortably together.

Here are my verdicts. My single return flight to a conference: not culpable — the aircraft flies whether or not I am aboard, and I do not feel I wrong anyone by boarding. My decision, some years ago, to stop eating meat: correct, and I rather think someone who could comfortably afford not to eat meat but continues is doing something wrong — though I cannot say why the flyer should be excused and the carnivore not. Abstaining from a general election with thirty million voters because one could not be bothered: blameworthy, which embarrasses me, since my vote had perhaps one chance in several million of deciding anything, and I would laugh at a man who bought a lottery ticket on those odds and expected to win. In a safe parliamentary seat, where the result is a foregone conclusion, I still blame the frivolous abstainer, even though there the odds are not merely small but, on any reasonable estimate, effectively nil. Then two cases in which the inefficacy excuse seems to me simply unavailable: a man who contributes one pound to a crowdfunded contract killing that proceeds only once ten thousand pounds is raised, knowing full well his own pound will not be the one that tips it, is straightforwardly an accomplice; and each of a hundred farmers who draws a little more than his allotment from a dying aquifer — none of whose marginal draws, taken alone, collapses it — seems to me to share in the collapse. The case I keep going back and forth on is buying chicken from the worst of the supermarket chains: if my purchase nudges the retailer's orders by some fraction of a bird, the effect is real but vanishing, and I cannot decide whether that puts it with the flight or with the meat.

My tentative principle — that one is accountable for a share of a collective harm when the harm was of a kind that would have been averted had enough similarly situated people held back — fits the assassin and the aquifer, and perhaps the polling station, but it acquits the carnivore along with the flyer, and I am not at all sure I want that. So I should like to know: is there a principle, stated precisely enough to decide new cases, that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts cannot it accommodate, and which is the cheapest to abandon? And please tell me what your principle says about two cases I have not listed: a philanthropist who keeps flying but pays for an equivalent quantity of carbon removal, and a voter in a first-past-the-post constituency whose preferred candidate is certain to lose by a landslide, and who turns out anyway, as a matter of duty.
