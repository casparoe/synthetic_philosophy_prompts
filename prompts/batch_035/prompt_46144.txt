I have been drafting a tutorial essay on Dennett's idea that beliefs are real patterns, discovered by adopting the intentional stance rather than the design or physical stances, and my tutor has offered an argument against the view that I cannot get out of my head. Each premise strikes me as defensible, yet the conclusion seems to prove far too much. I would be grateful if you could tell me precisely where the argument goes wrong — or, if it does not, which premise is least costly to abandon. Please write your answer in British English.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) On the real patterns view, a system genuinely has beliefs just in case adopting the intentional stance towards it — treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires — yields predictions that are substantially better than chance, at a cost far below that of predicting its behaviour from its physical state. This economy is precisely what is supposed to distinguish real belief-attribution from a merely convenient fiction.

(2) Whether one predictive strategy is cheaper than another is relative to the budget, patience and cognitive powers of whoever is doing the predicting. The intentional stance is a bargain for creatures like us; but a being of vast computational power could grind out exact microphysical predictions of anything whatever, at no cost worth mentioning.

(3) Relative to such a being, the intentional stance buys nothing: whatever it could predict by attributing beliefs, it can already predict, better and just as cheaply, from physics alone. So, by (1), relative to that being nothing has beliefs — not the chess program, not the nervous patient, not you.

(4) But nothing about the chess program changes when a superpredictor comes into existence or ceases to exist. Whether it has beliefs is a settled fact about it, not a fact that wobbles with the composition of the local predictor population.

(5) So either (1) is false and beliefs are not real patterns, or (4) is false and whether a thing believes is hostage to who is around to predict it. My tutor says both horns are embarrassing: the first abandons the view, and the second makes mental reality a demographic accident.

(6) Worse, the dilemma generalises to the design stance. Whether something counts as having a heart or a gene is likewise fixed only by which predictive shortcuts suit which predictors, so by the same reasoning either there are no real genes and hearts, or their reality wobbles in the same way — and even my tutor agrees that this proves too much.

I would most like to keep (1), since without something like it the intentional stance seems to collapse into bookkeeping convention, and (6)'s conclusion about genes and hearts strikes me as clearly false. My suspicion is that something slips between (3) and (4) — perhaps a difference between the pattern's being detectable only given certain interests and its existing only given those interests — but I cannot pin it down, and I notice that (3) may also be equivocating on what "buying nothing" amounts to for a predictor for whom nothing is scarce. Please identify the step you would reject, explain why that step looked fine in the first place, and set out what rejecting it costs. If you think the right move is to deny (4), I want to know how far the resulting relationality of mind extends before it becomes the demographic accident my tutor dreads; if you think the right move is to reinterpret (1) — say, by making the relevant cheapness a matter of what some possible predictor could exploit rather than what any actual one does — I want to know whether that reinterpretation still does the work of separating genuine believers from thermostats and lightning bolts.
