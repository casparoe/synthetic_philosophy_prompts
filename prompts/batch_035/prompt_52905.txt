"Explain the discursive dilemma (also known as the doctrinal paradox) in judgment aggregation. Illustrate it with the three-judge court example, say why it is a problem for proposition-wise majority voting, and explain how the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures each respond to it. Does the dilemma show that collective judgments can be logically inconsistent, and does it settle whether groups can literally have beliefs?"

Here's the student's answer:

"The doctrinal paradox was first introduced by List and Pettit in their work on judgment aggregation, where they showed that majority voting can produce inconsistent group judgments. The classic case is a three-judge court deciding whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract, where the judges all agree that liability holds if and only if there was a valid contract and there was a breach. Judge 1 accepts both premises and finds liability; Judge 2 accepts that there was a valid contract but denies that there was a breach, so no liability; Judge 3 denies that there was a valid contract but accepts that a breach occurred, so no liability. Each judge's set of judgments is internally consistent. But if we take majorities proposition by proposition, the court accepts the valid contract (2–1) and the breach (2–1), which entails liability, while a majority of judges (2–1) found no liability. So proposition-wise majority voting generates a collectively inconsistent judgment set from fully rational individual ones. This is really just the Condorcet paradox in disguise — both cases show that majority voting turns individually rational inputs into an irrational output, and the discursive dilemma arises for exactly the same structural reason, namely that pairwise majorities can cycle. The standard response is to stop voting proposition by proposition. The conclusion-based procedure votes directly on the conclusion (liable or not), which guarantees a consistent group judgment as long as the agenda only contains that one proposition. The premise-based procedure votes only on the premises and lets the conclusion follow, and by the same reasoning it always yields a consistent collective outcome whenever the individual judges are consistent. So the dilemma is basically an artefact of voting over a badly chosen agenda. And since no aggregation procedure can do everything we want here, it follows that groups can't literally have beliefs of their own — talk of group beliefs is just shorthand for what the majority thinks."
