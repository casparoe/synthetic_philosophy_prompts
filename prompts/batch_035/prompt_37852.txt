Here's something I keep chewing on. Forecasting platforms score their forecasters with Brier-style accuracy scores over hundreds of questions, and the public leaderboard has basically become the face of "good judgment" — tournaments, prizes, op-eds citing top-ranked forecasters as experts. What epistemology does that practice take for granted? I can see something like "credences should be accurate" in the background, but I want to know how much more specific it gets: does ranking people by one aggregated number presuppose that epistemic value is a single scalar quantity, additive across whatever questions happen to land on the site? Does it presuppose that a credence is the kind of state that can be scored at all, rather than, say, a whole stance toward a question? And heres a tension I can't resolve myself: the same community that celebrates calibration also rewards sharpness — a forecaster who says 0.9 when 0.55 was defensible gets a big payoff when the bold call lands, so the practice seems to praise epistemic humility and reward epistemic boldness in the same breath. Also, proper scoring rules are supposed to make honest reporting of your true credence the best strategy, which sounds like a fact about incentives rather than about epistemology — so is the leaderboard actually committed to epistemic utility theory, or is that just an accidental byproduct of the scoring design? Reconstruct whatever implicit view makes the leaderboard make sense, tell me which rival epistemologies it quietly rules out and which ones it could live with, and what would have to change if you swapped in a view on which suspension of judgment is sometimes an achievement rather than a failure to commit. And please flag how confident youre being about each claim, and single out the point youre least sure of — especially on whether properness really ties the practice to accuracy-first epistemology or whether I'm overreading a design choice.
