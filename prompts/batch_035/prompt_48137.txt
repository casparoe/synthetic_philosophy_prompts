The post summarizes things roughly like this: Lewis defended the thesis that the probability of a conditional 'if A, then B' just is the conditional probability P(B given A). Then in a famous 1976 paper he proved that this equation can only hold universally in trivial cases -- basically, if it holds for all probability functions, every sentence in the language ends up with probability 0 or 1. The proof, according to the post, works by treating the conditional as the material conditional and then applying ordinary probability calculus to it. The upshot the blogger draws is that indicative conditionals therefore aren't propositions at all -- they lack truth conditions entirely -- and that this also takes down the whole possible-worlds approach to conditionals (Stalnaker's and Lewis's semantics alike), since those assign truth conditions. He ends by saying the triviality results are now universally accepted as having refuted the probability thesis outright.

Something about this feels off to me, partly because my professor presented things as much less settled. But I honestly can't tell which parts are fine simplifications and which parts are actually mistakes. Like, was the probability thesis even Lewis's to defend in the first place? And did the proof really depend on reading the conditional as a material conditional? That seems like it would be attacking a strawman since nobody thinks indicatives are just material conditionals. Also the leap from "the equation fails" to "conditionals aren't propositions at all" seems fast, and so does the claim that the result somehow refutes the possible worlds semantics -- my impression was that the triviality stuff is about probability, not about truth conditions directly. But maybe I'm wrong, the blogger is a grad student after all and I'm a second-year undergrad.
