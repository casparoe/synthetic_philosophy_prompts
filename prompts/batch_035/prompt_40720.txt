I would like to practice confronting John Stuart Mill on a part of his record that his liberal admirers tend to skip past: his thirty-five years as an official of the East India Company and his lifelong defense of British rule over India. Please play Mill himself, speaking with me in the 1860s, after the Company's Indian functions have been handed over to the Crown. Take his position seriously and give it his best resources: he believes Company rule was a genuine trust, that the old double government of Company and Board of Control actually shielded Indian policy from a British electorate that neither knew nor cared about Indian welfare, and that a people not yet fit for representative institutions can be legitimately governed despotically if the despotism is genuinely directed at making them fit. He is sharp, sincere, and utilitarian; he will concede small points but never the main one, and he will not break character unless I signal as described below. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs of continuous prose, as in a conversation.

Here is my opening move. You spent your entire working life at East India House drafting dispatches for a subcontinent you never once set foot in, and when Parliament finally stripped the Company of India, you opposed the transfer — not because you thought Indians should govern themselves, but because you thought a body of expert administrators answerable to neither British voters nor Indian subjects would rule India for India's benefit better than the Crown would. You also hold that relations between civilized and barbarous peoples stand outside the ordinary rules of international morality, because those rules presuppose a reciprocity that barbarians cannot offer. All this from the century's great champion of the claim that no one can be a better judge of a person's own good than that person. Why isn't your empire simply the tyranny of the competent, and why should your formula — govern them well until they are ready, then leave — be anything more than a promissory note that will fall due exactly when it becomes inconvenient to honor?

One further rule: whenever I write the word aside, drop the Mill persona for one short paragraph and tell me how confident you are in the historical accuracy of what you have just been saying on Mill's behalf, singling out the specific claim you would least trust without checking a source, and then resume the character when I reply.
