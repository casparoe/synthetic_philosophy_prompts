I'm defending my dissertation next month before a committee split between political philosophers — mostly rights-based theorists sympathetic to the person-affecting restriction — and two economists who work on social discounting. My thesis is that climate duties to future generations should be grounded neither in future people's rights nor in an impersonal duty to maximise aggregate welfare across time, but in what I call a constraint on unilateral background authorship: no generation may irreversibly rewrite the ecological conditions within which all later moral agents act, choose, and constitute their lives. On this view, the non-identity problem is a symptom of asking the wrong question — the duty was never owed to particular future individuals in the first place — and the economists' discounting dispute is largely orthogonal, since the constraint is lexical rather than welfare-comparative, holding even when abatement is expensive and the beneficiaries are indeterminate.
