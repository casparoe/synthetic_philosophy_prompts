My flatmate is a phil major and she's got me stuck on something about moral realism. Her argument:

(1) Natural selection shaped our moral beliefs — we believe things like "loyalty is good" and "harming your kin is bad" because creatures who believed stuff like that left more descendants.
(2) Selection picked those beliefs for fitness, not for tracking moral truth.
(3) If the process that produced a class of beliefs didn't select for truth in that class, then the beliefs are unjustified, unless we can verify some of them through a process that wasn't itself shaped the same way.
(4) Every faculty we'd use to "verify" moral beliefs — intuition, reflection, whatever — was itself shaped by the same selection pressures.
(5) So none of our moral beliefs are justified.
(6) And if they're not justified, it's irrational to assert that anything is actually wrong, so the moral realist can't even get started.

Then the "proves too much" bit, which is where I come in. I told her the same argument runs on math:

(7) Our mathematical and logical beliefs were also produced by selection, and selected for fitness (creatures good at reasoning about quantities of predators survived better), not truth.
(8) Any verification of math would use those same evolved faculties.
(9) So by (3), none of our mathematical beliefs are justified either.
(10) But that's insane — I'm not irrational for believing modus ponens.
(11) So the argument proves too much, and something in (1)-(5) must be wrong.

She has a reply I can't quite pin down as wrong but that feels slippery. She says the cases are disanalogous: for math, we can tell a story where fitness-tracking and truth-tracking coincide, because believing true things about "three predators vs two" was fitness-enhancing, so evolution would install roughly truth-tracking math faculties. For morality, the realist claims there are mind-independent moral facts, and there's no reason why fitness would track *those* — what's fitness-enhancing (kin loyalty, reciprocity) and what's morally true could come apart completely, and evolution would have shaped us the same way either way. So (7) has a truth-tracking explanation available that (1) lacks, and that's why the debunking hits moral beliefs but not math ones.

Something about this still bugs me. Isn't the availability of the "coincidence story" for math itself something we can only judge using our evolved faculties? And it feels like her move also implies we're justified in trusting perception of the external world (veridical perception is fitness-enhancing) but maybe not our perception of, like, colors or other people's intentions — is she okay with that? Also she says we'd have believed in loyalty even if loyalty weren't good, but why couldn't a realist just deny that — maybe true moral beliefs happened to be the fit ones? I can't tell if her reply is just the standard answer and I should accept it, or if it smuggles in an extra premise somewhere.

So: does the math parallel actually break the debunking argument, or is her disanalogy reply enough to save it? If her reply works, what exactly is the extra premise doing the work, and is it one a moral realist is entitled to? And if the reply doesn't work — if the debunking really does generalize to math — is that a reductio of the argument, or should we bite the bullet and get more skeptical about math than we're comfortable with? For the record I'd rather keep (10) than (5), but I want to know what accepting her reply costs me.
