I help run a discussion group at my local community centre — mostly retirees, teachers, a nurse, an engineer, and me; not one of us has any philosophy training, and we have been meeting monthly for years to argue about whatever is in the news. One argument keeps coming back in new costumes, and I have finally worked out what it is: we disagree about whether a political leader can ever be right to do something that is, by that same leader's own lights, simply wrong. Torturing a suspect to stop a bombing, lying to the public to prevent a panic, betraying an ally to buy time in a war, firing an innocent person to hold a coalition together. When I read around on my own, I gather the philosophers call this the problem of dirty hands, but every article I can follow seems to assume the reader already knows which sub-question is at issue, and I want to stop our group from sliding between them.

What I would like is a decision tree, formatted as a handout I can print on two sides of A4. The tree should start from a concrete case — our standing example is a mayor who falsely promises a witness protection to extract testimony that will prevent a riot, but I am open to a better one — and should reach its leaves by asking a sequence of questions, where every question is one a person could honestly answer without ever having heard the philosophical labels. From listening to our arguments, I believe the branching questions include at least these, though you may add or reshape them: first, whether the leader actually did wrong at all, or merely chose the lesser of two bad outcomes and has nothing on his conscience — some of my group insist that choosing the smaller evil stains nothing, and I have never known how to argue with that; second, whether the act only becomes forgivable if the good outcome actually arrives, or whether a sincere, reasonable attempt that fails is enough — one member says the mayor is a monster if the riot happens anyway, another says that makes morality a lottery; third, whether the leader must feel guilt afterward for what he did, or whether the guilt is optional sentiment; and fourth, whether we can ever say the leader was justified in acting, or whether the most we can say is that we won't punish him.

The leaves should include, at minimum: the view that there really are dirty hands — a genuine wrong was done, the leader was nonetheless right to do it, and the guilt is the proof that morality wasn't simply suspended; the view that there is no such thing, because all the leader ever did was weigh outcomes and pick the least bad one, so his hands were clean from the start; the view that the leader must keep his hands clean at any cost, and if the price of clean hands is the riot, then that is the price — his job is not to save people by doing wrong but to refuse; and a fourth view I can only describe as the cold one — the wrong was real, the act was right, and the guilt is a luxury the leader owes it to the public not to indulge. If there are other positions that fit naturally, add them, but build the tree out of the questions first and only then tell me which named views, if any, sit at the leaves — several of our members hold combinations I could not find names for, and I want to know whether those combinations are unoccupied because they are incoherent, unattractive, or simply rare.

Two further requests. First, wherever two positions end up on the same leaf because no honest answer to your questions separates them, say so plainly rather than inventing a distinction — my group will notice. Second, somewhere at the bottom of the handout, give us three fresh cases, described in two or three sentences each, chosen so that different members of the group will end up at different leaves of your tree depending on their answers — the point of the exercise is to find out what we each actually believe, not which of us can recite a name. Please write it so an intelligent person with no philosophical vocabulary can follow every word; if you need a technical term, define it in a line the first time it appears.
