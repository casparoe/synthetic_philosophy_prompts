I am putting together a unit on the ethics of belief for my introductory epistemology seminar, and I invited a colleague from the psychology department to give a guest lecture on how the James–Clifford exchange looks from the vantage point of contemporary research on reasoning. She was careful and charming, but at the end of her handout she compressed the whole debate into a paragraph that I now find myself uneasily circling. It reads, in substance, as follows: Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" argued that it is always wrong, for anyone and in any circumstance, to believe anything on insufficient evidence, because a belief formed that way corrupts our character and, once the habit sets in, we will end up credulous about everything; and since we cannot avoid acting on some beliefs before the evidence is in, Clifford's rule would paralyze ordinary life. James's "The Will to Believe" was the direct rebuttal: since we can never have sufficient evidence for anything that really matters, and since believing something often makes us happier and can even make it true, each of us is entitled to believe whatever we like, so religious faith comes out automatically rational. The handout closes by observing that what the exchange really shows is an old quarrel between reason and emotion, and that the psychology of motivated reasoning has now settled it decisively on Clifford's side.

I suspect the paragraph is unfair to both men, but in different ways and to different degrees, and I would rather not correct my colleague with a wave of the hand. Could you go through her summary claim by claim and sort it into three piles: what a careful defender of Clifford would accept, what a careful defender of James would accept, and what is genuinely wrong or subtly off — paying particular attention to how the phrase "believe whatever we like" sits with what James actually licenses, whether the leap from "wrong to believe on insufficient evidence" to "paralyzed in daily life" is a slide James or Clifford would own, and how the psychology of motivated reasoning bears on the cases James had in mind rather than on the wishful thinking he was careful to disclaim. Finally, tell me whether her closing verdict — that the debate reduces to reason versus emotion and is now empirically settled — follows from the parts of her summary that survive correction, or whether it depends on the parts that do not.
