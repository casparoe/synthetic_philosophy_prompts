I keep coming back to Charles Whitman, the guy who climbed the tower at UT Austin in 1966 and started shooting people. The night before, he stabbed his wife and his mother, and he left a note asking doctors to examine his brain after he was dead, because he'd been having violent impulses and headaches he couldn't explain — and sure enough the autopsy found a tumor pressing on his amygdala. Everyone pulls this case out in free will debates as "his brain made him do it," but I want to run it through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument instead. As I understand it, the argument is: you do what you do because of the way you are, so to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, which would mean you somehow made yourself, and nobody can do that, so nobody is ever ultimately morally responsible. If that's right, doesn't Whitman come out as just an unusually vivid illustration of the human condition, not an edge case at all? Walk me through what the Basic Argument actually says about him, then the other direction: does his case give the argument anything it didn't already have, or does the fact that basically everyone feels the tumor *should* exculpate him (more than a bad upbringing would, say) expose a bullet the argument has to bite? And tell me where my framing breaks — I might be misreading Strawson, or building the whole thing on a shaky analogy, and if so say so rather than playing along.
