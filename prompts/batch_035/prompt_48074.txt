I am assembling cases for a graduate seminar on Dennett's Real Patterns and the three stances, and I need a thought experiment engineered to stress a specific claim in that framework. The claim: the stances are often treated as nested, with design patterns somehow underwriting intentional ones — an artifact's purposes and a creature's functions are supposed to be what make it apt for intentional-stance prediction. I want a scenario in which that underwriting is absent yet the intentional stance still earns its keep.

Construct a thought experiment in which the intentional stance is the best predictive instrument available for some system, while the design stance has no genuine purchase on it. The critical twist: this must happen not because the system is irrational (the usual direction of failure) but because nothing in the system's causal history was selection-for. No designer intended it, no evolutionary or market-style process differentially retained variants, and no feedback loop shaped it during formation — the system's competence must be a matter of one-shot luck permitted by ordinary physics, yet its rationality must be broad and robust enough that the intentional stance is genuinely the cheapest way to predict it, not merely a coincidence we narrate after the fact.

Constraints: the scenario should be one paragraph and involve no exotic physics, no demons, and no perfect predictors; the system's rationality must emerge from the description rather than being stipulated; and the process that produced it must not involve any iterative winnowing, however indirect. After the scenario, do the following. First, show what each of the three stances predicts about the system, attending to the cost of prediction under each — which stance compresses its behavior most economically, and why the design stance comes up empty. Second, identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work of breaking the nesting (the selection-free genesis), and state what happens to the verdicts if you add that feature back — for instance, if the lucky system were one of many generated and the successful ones were kept, does the design stance return, and does the case collapse back into an ordinary designed artifact? Third, consider the strongest counterattack available to a defender of the nesting thesis: the anthropic point that observers only ever encounter the lucky systems, so an implicit observer-selection is smuggling design back in. Say whether you think this move saves the thesis or merely relocates the selection from the system's genesis to our sampling of systems. Finally, and most importantly, address the question in Dennett's own terms from Real Patterns: on the criterion of predictive compressibility, is the intentional pattern exhibited by your system a real pattern, and if it is, what does that commit Dennett to about whether real intentional patterns require any grounding in design at all? Do not end non-committally — defend a verdict on whether the case forces a revision of the nesting claim, or explain exactly where it fails to bite.
