After a career in condensed-matter physics, I spent my final decade fitting predictive models to data, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of religion. The fine-tuning argument strikes me as suspicious for a professional reason I would like you to examine. In model selection, a hypothesis that accommodates the data only because a hidden parameter has been nudged into a narrow range is treated as defective — overfitting, ad hoc tuning — and parameter-rich models are penalized for it, as in the Akaike criterion. The fine-tuning argument, however, seems to run this penalty in reverse: because the physical constants must occupy a narrow life-permitting range, and a designer who wanted life would have placed them there, the very narrowness that would elsewhere mark an overfitted model is offered as evidence for the hypothesis that contains the dial. My question is where, precisely, my analogy breaks down. My own guess is that it is a matter of bookkeeping: in the argument, the constants' values function as the data rather than as parameters being fitted, and the rival hypotheses are compared by likelihood rather than by effort or parameter count. But I then cannot see how the design hypothesis earns any determinate likelihood unless the designer's preferences are specified in advance, and that specification looks like exactly the free parameter I would penalize. Is the argument just overfitting in formal dress — a penalty converted into a bonus — or is there a principled answer to my worry? Please be explicit about what plays the role of data, model, and free parameter, since I suspect the disagreement between the two camps is ultimately a fight over that assignment.
