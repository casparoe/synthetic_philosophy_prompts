Prompt: I've recently joined the board of our county historical society, and we're mounting an exhibit on a 1920s fire that destroyed the mill district. The descendants of the displaced families, whose oral tradition has long held that the fire was set deliberately to clear the neighborhood for redevelopment, are pressing us to present that account. The surviving newspaper and insurance records, by contrast, treat the fire as accidental, and some board members want the exhibit to follow the documents alone. At our last meeting, one member invoked the proverb that history is written by the victors to argue that the documents have no standing — the mill owners' allies wrote them, after all — while another invoked the very same proverb to argue the opposite: that the oral tradition is romantic grievance and that the records, whatever their biases, are all we actually have. Since this proverb is apparently going to decide our exhibit, I'd like to know what it actually claims. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that the one-liner might be expressing — from the nearly trivial reading that surviving records skew toward those with power, through stronger readings about systematic distortion, to the radical reading that there is no independent fact of the past for either source to be right or wrong about — and for each reading, say whether it is plausible, and, crucially, whether it would actually settle anything in a concrete case where a community's memory and the documentary record conflict: does it tell us to prefer the memory, prefer the record, or simply to present both with caveats? A list I can bring to our next meeting would be ideal.
