I should like to practise defending Aristotelian virtue ethics against the situationist critique. To do that I need an opponent who is genuinely good, so please play a committed situationist psychologist: sharp, empirically minded, and never a strawman. You are convinced that stable character traits, as the Aristotelian tradition understands them, do not exist, and that what we call 'character' is at best a pattern of responses to situations — so that any serious moral improvement should be sought by redesigning environments rather than by cultivating souls. You may concede small points when my arguments genuinely score, but you must never concede the main claim, and you must remain in character until I say 'stop'. Please keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it feels like a live conversation.

One constraint, since neither of us can consult the literature: argue only from the findings I set out below, and never invent new studies, statistics, or names. Where you need a further fact about one of them, ask me for it in character rather than fabricating it. Your evidence base is: (1) the obedience experiments in which most subjects, instructed by an authority figure to continue, administered what they believed were painful and dangerous electric shocks to another supposed participant; (2) the study in which seminary students, asked to deliver a talk on the Good Samaritan parable, were far likelier to help a slumped figure in a doorway when they had been told they were early rather than late, with time pressure apparently mattering more than the disposition they had just been asked to reflect on; (3) the older studies finding that children's honesty varied considerably from one classroom task to another rather than there being a fixed set of honest children; and (4) the general finding that small, unremarked features of mood and circumstance — a found coin, the smell of fresh bread — measurably raise rates of helping.

Here is my opening move. Your whole case rests on tests of cross-situational consistency, but Aristotle never claimed that the just or courageous person behaves identically everywhere; he claimed that the virtuous person judges well which fears matter and which circumstances call for what, and that this good judgement is itself the trait. Your experiments never measure judgement at all. In the obedience study, the subject is given no independent means of verifying that refusing will help the victim, that the authority will not retaliate, or how serious the shocks really are; if the situation systematically hides or distorts precisely the information a good judge would need, then compliant behaviour is very weak evidence about anyone's character. Likewise, on your own findings, time pressure made seminary students walk past a stranger — but a person hurrying to keep an obligation is plausibly weighing two duties, not revealing an absence of compassion. So here is my charge: your experiments establish that manipulated situations predict behaviour, but you have done nothing to show that the situations were not also manipulating the subjects' beliefs about what was going on, and until you can separate those two effects, the inference to 'there are no character traits' simply does not go through. Respond.
