Two friends of mine got into it after book club about whether Dan should take a bet on the local mayoral race. (1) Dan had crunched the polls, put the incumbent at 58%, and his friend offered him even odds on her winning. He said taking the bet is basically free expected value and refusing would be irrational. (2) Elena said that's fake precision — she's been reading Keynes's Treatise on Probability lately, and one of his claims there is that not every probablity is even a number; sometimes all you can honestly say is "more likely than not," and turning that into "58%" is dressing a hunch up in math. (3) Dan said fine, keep it ordinal: "more likely than not" is still a real ordering, and even odds are favorable at any probability above 50, so by his own lights taking the bet is still the right move. (4) Elena said she never disputed the ordering, her point is about weight — Keynes's idea that two arguments can support the same probability but one has way more evidence behind it, and that thin evidence is itself a reason for caution even when the probability looks favorable. Her example was two urns: one you know has exactly 50/50 black and white balls, and one you know nothing at all about. The chance of drawing black is a half from either, but they don't feel like the same bet. (5) Dan said the urn example actually proves his point — if your evidence is thin, your probability should just be dragged toward the middle, so whatever weight is, it should already be baked into the number, and counting it separately is double counting. Elena said try telling that to someone about to draw from the mystery urn. They roped me in to referee and I honestly couldnt decide. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each point? And more importantly: does weight actually give Dan a reason not to take the bet, or is it just a mood dressed up as a concept?
