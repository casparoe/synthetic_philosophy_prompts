I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, but I got into a huge argument with some friends at a hackathon about who "owns" an idea, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe by going through cases where my gut gives a clear answer. Here's where I've landed. Reading a blog post that describes a caching technique in prose, then implementing it myself from scratch: totally fine, that's just learning. Copy-pasting a 50-line function from someone's GitHub repo into my closed-source work project, with the license file deleted: obviously not fine. Reverse-engineering a competitor's product to figure out their clever data model and then building my own version of it: fine, honestly, even though it feels a little dirty. Verbatim reimplementing the exact algorithm from an academic paper, same variable structure and everything: fine, papers are meant for that. A friend pitches me an app idea at a party, I say "cool," and a year later I build something pretty similar: not fine, and it's not fine even though I only took the concept and none of the code. And here's one I genuinely can't decide: I take a take-home interview assignment, don't get the job, and months later I notice their actual product shipped with something that looks suspiciously like my take-home solution. Part of me says that's fine (they paid for my time, sort of, by interviewing me) and part of me says it's theft.

So what's the actual principle here? My first instinct is it's about ideas vs. expression -- you can't own an idea, only the specific form you gave it -- but that can't be the whole story, because the party-idea case bugs me and there's no code or expression being taken there, just the concept. Maybe it's more like: what's being taken is the *planning*, the part where someone already did the work of deciding what to build, and copying that is freeloading regardless of the form? But then reading a paper and reimplementing it should feel wrong too, since the authors did all the hard design work, and it doesn't. There's also a consent dimension that keeps butting in -- the paper author consented to sharing, the party friend sort of did, the repo owner explicitly did via the license I violated.

Can you pull this into one coherent principle, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it has to throw out? Also curious what it says about two cases I didn't list: (1) training a code model on millions of public repos where the individual lines clearly get "used" but no human ever reads them, and (2) me deliberately avoiding reading a competitor's patent so I can later claim I reinvented it independently, which feels fine to me in a way that reading it and then implementing does not. Go easy on the jargon if you can, I've never read a word of Locke.
