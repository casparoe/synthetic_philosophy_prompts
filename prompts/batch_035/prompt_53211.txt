Next month I'm speaking at an interdisciplinary workshop on machine welfare, and I want a prep sheet for the question period. My thesis is that in the reinforcement-learning systems we actually train today, the reward signal cannot be the vehicle of valence: the reward number is a parameter of the training process, not a state of the agent. It never appears inside the forward pass — it enters only through the gradient update — so there is nothing in the running system that could instantiate a felt quality; the reward plays the role that food or injury played in shaping our ancestors' learning, which is the role of a selection pressure, not of an experience. My argument sketch has three legs: (1) the architectural point just stated; (2) a disanalogy with the dopamine system, where the prediction-error signal is broadcast inside the brain and modulates ongoing processing, unlike an external loss term; and (3) a caution from animal-welfare science — since pain behavior persists without pain experience (decorticated rats show nociceptive reflexes), behavior alone can't settle the question in either direction, so the right response is uncertainty plus empirical work, not confident denial. Two concessions I plan to make up front, because the audience will find them anyway: critics and value functions do compute reward-like quantities inside the forward pass, and I lack a principled account of why those are different in kind rather than just smaller in degree; and I have no criterion for valence that doesn't already assume something about implementation, which makes my architectural argument look question-begging against functionalists.

The audience is about forty people, and I can describe the factions precisely. The largest group is computational neuroscientists who think of reward as whatever controls the dopamine system, and who will invoke the wanting-versus-liking literature — the point that dopamine drives pursuit independently of pleasure — both as support for my disanalogy and as a trap: if wanting and liking dissociate, that cuts against the claim that any single scalar carries valence, but it also suggests valence research needs inner-state evidence I can't provide for machines. There are several philosophers of mind, mostly functionalists, who will say that if a signal plays the valence role in the right causal organization, it is valence, and that my "outside the forward pass" line just relocates the dispute to a favored implementation. There are ML engineers from an AI lab who mostly want to know what they would have to build or test to be safe, and who will push on whether my position has any practical upshot or just licenses inaction. And there are two people from animal-welfare science who distrust behavioral criteria in their own field and will be watching for me to lean on behavioral evidence exactly where I told them not to.

Prepare the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to my thesis, each phrased in the vocabulary of the subgroup likely to raise it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Assume I know the standard positions; what I need is the mapping between what this particular audience cares about and the specific soft spots in my three-legged argument.

Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — name the objection most likely to sink the talk and the one-line strategy for handling it — then give the sheet, with no preamble and no restatement of my setup.
