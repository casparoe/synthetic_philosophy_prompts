I'm a retired neuroscientist -- 30-some years in comparative neuroanatomy, mostly comparing cortices across mammals -- and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole. The question that keeps me up now is whether brain size is morally relevant at all. My gut from the lab says total size is a terrible proxy (whale brains dwarf ours, and shrew cortex beats ours on neuron density), but I also suspect you can't just throw magnitude out entirely, since whatever grounds moral status presumably runs on some quantity of some neural stuff or other. If I've got maybe two years of retirement to spend thinking about this properly, which open questions should I tackle first, and in what order? The ones I've managed to formulate myself, roughly: (1) what the relevant measure even is -- total neuron count, cortical neuron number, something about connectivity or synchrony, or whether the whole size framing is confused and it's really about specific circuits; (2) how moral weight should scale with whatever measure we land on -- linear, logarithmic, threshold, or not at all; (3) whether cephalopods with their wierd distributed nervous systems are a genuinely informative test case or just a fun distraction; (4) whether at the small end (insects, larval zebrafish) the scaling question collapses into something almost binary. Rank these or add others I've missed, but defend the ordering: what's upstream of what, what's actually tractable for someone with my background and zero ethics training, and what developments would make you bump something up or down the list.
