I cover cognitive science and AI for a general-interest magazine, and over a decade of writing about minds — animal, human, and lately machine — I've drifted into a settled philosophical position that I'd like stress-tested before I build my next long-form feature on it. The position is Dennett's, or close enough to his that the differences don't matter here: belief-desire talk, on the intentional stance, is not a useful fiction but a way of picking out real patterns — patterns as real as genes or centers of gravity are, real in roughly the sense that positing them buys you predictive compression that no other vocabulary delivers. The physical stance and the design stance get at genuine structure, and the intentional stance does too; it isn't a placeholder for a mechanistic story we don't yet have. I've gone further in my own columns, and this is where I want the pressure applied: I've claimed that large language models are the strongest test case this position has ever faced, because for the first time we have systems where complete mechanistic access exists in principle — every weight inspectable, no skull to seal it off — and yet in practice we predict and manage their behavior using deliberately intentional vocabulary. We speak of what a model wants, what it's trying to do, what it believes about its situation, because it works. That, I've argued, is the real-patterns view winning on its home turf: the pattern is there to be found precisely where we have the best possible mechanistic alternative.

Tell me what would count against the view, not just what standard critics say. I want the defeaters ranked, from fatal to merely awkward. Concretely: if mechanistic interpretability eventually delivers what it promises — full predictive and steering power over these systems with no intentional terms doing any load-bearing work — does that defeat the position, or does it merely show that the compression the intentional stance buys is an artifact of our ignorance, and is that concession fatal or survivable? What should I make of the objection that the reality of a pattern shouldn't depend on whether anyone bothers to take up a stance toward it — that this makes belief-attribution observer-relative in a way genes aren't — and would a committed Dennettian concede that as counting against them, or dismiss it as a misunderstanding? Are there cases the position simply cannot describe — I've wondered about minds so alien that no predictive compression at the intentional level is available, or minds whose behavior is fully predictable without it but only via a physically infeasible model — and does the second case reveal that the position quietly trades on tractability rather than compression? Which of these would a defender of the view actually grant as evidence against it, and which would they wave off, and in each case would they be right to? Finally, an honest assessment of the insulation question: the position seems built with escape hatches — every apparent victory of mechanism can be redescribed as the pattern holding at a different grain — and I want to know whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that's a vice for a metaphysical claim of this kind or an acceptable feature of a claim that was always about what earns its keep rather than what is fundamental. I should say what I'd personally find most persuasive: a worked case, in advance, of a finding about LLMs or brains that would make me drop the view, so that I'm not just admiring its resilience from inside.
