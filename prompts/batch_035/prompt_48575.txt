In my seminar on the French Revolution, the professor and several of the readings keep leaning on the old adage that revolutions devour their children — invoked to explain why so many of the men who made 1789 were on the guillotine by 1794 — and it usually arrives bundled with a story on which the constitutional settlement of 1789–91 was the real revolution and the Terror its betrayal. I've come to suspect this bundling is doing quiet historiographical work: it fits the revisionist reading associated with Furet, on which the radical phase is an ideological overflow of 1789's political culture rather than a response to war, invasion, and the Vendée, whereas the classical social interpretation we also read (Lefebvre, Soboul) treats 1793 as continuous with 1789 — the same class forces unfolding under new pressures. So I would like an audit of the metaphor itself, not another recital of the debate. First, the mapping: what corresponds to the parent, who exactly are the children (the first generation of leaders? the moderate faction? the constitutionalist ideals themselves?), and what plays the role of the appetite that does the devouring? Second, where the mapping breaks — my guess is the breakdown turns on two points: whether the metaphor presupposes that a revolution has a natural, legitimate course from which it can then deviate, and whether it casts the killing as internal competition when much of the driving pressure was external and contingent. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion everyone draws from it — that 1793 was a pathology of the revolution rather than a stage of it — is something the adage can actually support, or whether the metaphor was carrying the interpretation all along.
