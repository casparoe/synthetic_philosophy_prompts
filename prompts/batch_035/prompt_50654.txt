I spent the better part of four decades running an electrophysiology laboratory, and now that I have retired I have been working through Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations. The passage about the diarist who records a recurring private sensation with the sign "S" has been bothering me, though perhaps not for the usual reasons. My professional reflex upon encountering anything putatively unobservable is to instrument it, and I want to explore whether that reflex, applied to the diarist, actually dissolves the problem or merely relocates it. Please construct the thought experiment for me.

The case I have in mind: suppose the diarist is wired to a decoding device, of the kind my own field has genuinely produced for other neural states, that displays a signal whenever it detects the occurrence of the diarist's sensation. The diarist writes "S" at will, and the machine's display independently lights up or stays dark on each occasion. I want to know whether the machine supplies the diarist with a standard of correctness that Wittgenstein claimed was missing.

Constraints on the construction:

1. One paragraph, set in an ordinary research lab. No perfect mind-reading: the device is a statistical classifier with a nonzero error rate, calibrated in the way such devices actually are, and there is no independent specification of the state it tracks apart from its own output.
2. The sensation in question must be genuinely unnamed — introspectively accessible to the diarist, but not describable in existing public sensation vocabulary without loss. The diarist's sign must not be translatable into ordinary words like "pain" or "an ache."
3. The scenario must generate the verdicts rather than stipulate them. That is, after presenting it, show why the diarist can now be wrong on a given day, what exactly the criterion of correct use is, and whether that criterion is a criterion of the sensation's occurrence or merely a correlated symptom. If your honest judgment is that no criterion is forthcoming despite the machine, say so and show why.
4. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work — I suspect it is the public readability of the machine's display — and then run the subtraction: what happens to the case if the display is visible only to the diarist, or if the diarist must instead read and interpret the raw physiological trace. Does the argument collapse back into the original diary case, or does something survive?
5. State plainly what the case is designed to isolate: whether Wittgenstein's target is the inaccessibility of sensations to third parties, or the absence of any standard of correctness, which the machine case is meant to pull apart. A construction that cannot keep those two apart has failed, whatever else it achieves.
6. Finally, give me the strongest Wittgensteinian rejoinder. I anticipate something about the calibration and interpretation of the machine being themselves public practices that presuppose agreement in judgments, and perhaps the point that statistical reliability is a poor substitute for a criterion. But argue it rather than gesturing at it, and then tell me, as one career experimentalist to another, whether I should conclude that my instrumentation reflex has genuinely been defeated here, or only that it complicates the dialectic without resolving it.

Please be rigorous about the distinction between a device that corroborates a judgment and one that constitutes the standard by which the judgment is assessed; I suspect the entire case turns on it.
