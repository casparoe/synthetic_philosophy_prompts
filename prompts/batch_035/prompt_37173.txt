I'm a science journalist putting together a piece on what neuroscience actually says about free will, and my editor forwarded a clip from another magazine that sums up the Libet experiments like this: 'In the 1980s, Benjamin Libet wired people up to an EEG and found that a readiness potential began building in the brain about half a second before they felt any urge to move their wrist — so the brain had already made the decision, and the conscious "choice" was just the self being notified after the fact. Libet proved that when you feel like you're deciding, the deciding is already done, and he himself accepted this and gave up his earlier belief in free will.'

Most of this sounds plausible to me but the last bit smells off — I half remeber Libet arguing somewhere for a conscious "veto" power? Before I touch this stuff in print I want a proper audit, since I can't tell a subtle distortion from a howler. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro or conclusion: go through the claims in order and say what a neuroscientist working on volition would accept, what's an oversimplification (e.g. does the readiness potential precede the action or the reported intention, and does that distinction matter), and what is just wrong — and make the fifth point a verdict on whether "free will is an illusion" actually follows from the corrected version of the findings.
