I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology and I keep a (very low traffic) blog where I try to convince people they already have views about the lottery paradox, they just havent noticed yet. Next post I want a "which solution to the lottery paradox do you actually hold?" quiz. Specs:

- Around 8 questions, each a concrete case where the reader has to report what they believe, not what they'd endorse after reflecting. Work in at least these cases somewhere: holding one ticket in a million-ticket fair lottery; the professor writing a preface admitting the book probably contains at least one error while standing behind every individual chapter; fifty independent weather forecasts, each saying 97% chance of no rain in a different city, where the reader has to say whether they believe all fifty; and a bridge case where each particular hand is astronomically unlikely but some hand is guaranteed to be dealt.
- Answer options should be phrased belief-first ("I believe this ticket loses", "I believe the conjunction of all fifty forecasts but no single one on its own", etc.) — no labels, no isms.
- The scoring key maps answer patterns onto: the Kyburg-style line that rational belief just isn't closed under conjunction, so you get to keep all the individual beliefs and dump the conjunction; the no-lottery-belief line, where high probability is never enough on its own and you believe none of the individual claims; the skeptical option of giving up the individual beliefs in lottery-like cases; the knowledge-first move where rational belief requires knowledge and the odds destroy knowledge; and the credences-only option that binary belief is a fiction and there are only degrees. If you think a real position is missing from that list, add it.
- Flag any two positions the quiz can't cleanly separate and say why, and note at least one answer pattern nobody would realistically give, with a sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unpopular.
- No question may just ask "do you accept closure of justified belief under conjunction" — the whole point is sorting readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

One thing I want to get right: the preface and the lottery questions should do different work, because the preface gives you evidence of your own fallibility while the lottery only gives you odds, and I suspect alot of people split their intuitions between the two cases without seeing it. Make sure at least one question pair exploits that asymmetry.

Keep the whole thing, questions plus scoring key, under 700 words.
