I am a novelist working on a novel set aboard a generation ship launched in the 2070s, and a central character is its chief systems architect, a woman of fierce moral seriousness who has spent her career arguing that humanity is not merely permitted but obligated to become a multi-planet species. To prepare for writing her, I have arranged a long-form interview with an ethicist who defends precisely this position — that dispersing humanity across worlds is a moral duty grounded in the reduction of existential risk — and I would like your help drafting the questions.

I want eight to ten questions that engage the substance of her position rather than inviting her to restate it, and for each I would like a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. To that end, please build the interview around the following pressure points, which correspond to the places where I expect her position is most vulnerable:

First, the dispersal argument itself: the claim that settlements on other worlds insure humanity against catastrophe. She should be pressed on the common-cause objection — that the most probable catastrophes (misaligned artificial intelligence, engineered pathogens, physics gone wrong) would not respect planetary boundaries, so a backup on Mars may insure against the least likely disasters only. Her likely reply will distinguish risks that are genuinely localized from those that are not; the follow-up should ask her to quantify, even roughly, how much of the expected benefit survives once the common causes are subtracted.

Second, the transition from permission to duty: even granting that a backup world would be good to have, what converts that prudential hedge into an obligation, and who bears its costs? I anticipate she will appeal to the vast number of future people whose existence the backup secures; the follow-up should ask whether duties owed to merely possible beneficiaries can bind present people to enormous sacrifice, and how her answer avoids the standard objections to aggregating across potential lives.

Third, consent aboard the generation ship itself: the colonists' grandchildren will be born into a sealed vessel mid-voyage, committed to a project they never chose, in service of a destination they will never see. This is not a hypothetical for my novel — it is the condition of nearly every character in it. She will likely reply that no one consents to the circumstances of their birth on Earth either; the follow-up should test whether that symmetry holds, given that a generation ship forecloses exit in a way terrestrial life does not.

Fourth, the moral status of whatever is already there: I want a question on whether her case for settlement changes if the target world hosts microbial life — and, in the follow-up, whether her treatment of that life as an obstacle to be managed reveals that the duty she defends is really a duty to human beings rather than to life or mind as such.

Finally, I want one question on the settler-colonial analogy, since my character is aware of it and dismissive of it. She will say the analogy fails because there are no indigenous inhabitants; the follow-up should ask whether the wrongness of historical colonialism really resided only in the presence of inhabitants, or also in the extractive and unaccountable structure of the enterprise — and if the latter, what accountability she would accept for a colony that is, by construction, beyond anyone's reach.

A note on tone: my interviewee is formidable and disdains softball framing, so the questions should be courteous but unflinching, and should presuppose familiarity with the philosophical terrain rather than asking her to define terms. If you think there is a pressure point I have missed that would surface in a real exchange with a sophisticated defender of the duty to colonize, please include it as well, with its follow-up.
