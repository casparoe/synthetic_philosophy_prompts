I'm in a reading group that just did the Nagel and Williams moral luck papers, and I've become one of those annoying people who thinks the whole idea is confused — luck affects what happens to you, sure, but not how blameworthy you are. I want to stress-test that, so play my opponent. Draw on the standard resources — Nagel's fourfold split between resultant, circumstantial, constitutive and causal luck, Williams' painter who abandons his family and whose act is somehow redeemed or not by how the art turns out, the two equally reckless drivers where only one kills a child — but improvise your own cases too, and feel free to point out when my position forces me to bite a bullet I haven't noticed. Here's my opening move: I'll grant that outcomes are lucky, and that we do in fact treat the unlucky driver far more harshly. What I deny is that this differential treatment tracks blameworthiness rather than just practical necessity — deterrence, public grief, insurance-logic. Blame is supposed to answer to the agent, to what *they* did, and you can't become more guilty because a coin you never flipped landed badly; that would mean you could owe something on account of pure chance, which seems incoherent. A perfectly informed and perfectly calm judge would sentence the two drivers identically — anything else is the legal system's rough heuristic, not desert. So go ahead: tell me why my perfectly calm judge is a fantasy, or why desert isn't the only thing blame is about. But make it a real argument, not just an appeal to how the practice happens to work.
