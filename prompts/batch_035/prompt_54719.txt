A labmate sent me an essay from a popular neuroscience newsletter that tries to dispose of the zombie argument, and I'd like a careful audit of it before I decide whether to assign it to our reading group. I'm a PhD student in computational neuroscience, so I know the physicalist orthodoxy of my field but not the fine structure of this corner of philosophy of mind.

The essay's summary runs roughly as follows. The zombie argument, attributed to Chalmers, says that we can clearly imagine a universe physically identical to ours in every respect — every particle, every brain state, every behavioural output exactly the same — except that nobody in it is conscious: there is nothing it is like to be any of its inhabitants. The essay then says: whatever we can clearly and coherently imagine is possible, so such a world is genuinely possible; and if a world with all the physical facts fixed can lack consciousness, then consciousness is not a physical fact, and physicalism is false. As for the objections, the essay notes that people once imagined water without being H2O and turned out to be imagining an impossibility, so imagination is an unreliable guide to possibility and the argument is refuted. It closes by observing that a zombie would behave and report exactly as we do, insisting it was conscious, which shows that consciousness does no causal work; on that basis it concludes that qualia, in the sense the argument needs, simply don't exist.

Please go through this summary and tell me what to trust. Which parts would a defender of the argument accept as a fair statement of it? Which parts are subtly off — places where the argument's actual premises are stronger or weaker than what the essay presents, or where a distinction the argument relies on has been flattened? I have my suspicions about the treatment of the water analogy (it's presented as if the objector never contemplated it, which seems unlikely given how famous the exchange is) and about the closing move, which feels like it slides from a consequence of the argument's success to an argument against it. But I don't know the literature well enough to say where the essay conflates the different grades of conceivability or misstates the inference from conceivability to possibility. Finally, does the essay's dismissal of the argument follow from the parts of its summary that survive scrutiny?

As you go, please indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts — distinguishing what is standard in the literature from what is your own reading — and single out the point in your audit you are least sure of.
