Draft the joint statement that an epistemic structural realist and an ontic structural realist could agree to sign after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration on scientific realism. Both enter the collaboration accepting that the historical record of theory change undermines standard scientific realism's attitude toward the full ontological furniture of past theories, but they differ on what that record supports: the epistemic structural realist reads it as a claim about the limits of our knowledge (there are entities in themselves, but only their structural properties — roughly, their relations and the equations governing them — are knowable), while the ontic structural realist reads it as a claim about what there is (relations are ontologically primitive, and objects are at best metaphysically thin nodes in a web of relational structure, with no individuating essence over and above the structure).

The document they produce should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including claims about what the historical record does and does not show (e.g., the familiar case of Fresnel's equations surviving into Maxwell's theory while the ether did not), about the challenge of underdetermination for any realism that goes beyond structure, about what the retention of structure means in mathematical terms (preservation of dynamical equations and symmetry groups across theory change, and the significance of dualities relating empirically equivalent formulations with superficially different ontologies), and about the shared diagnosis of where both plain scientific realism and the pessimistic induction over the history of science go wrong.

2. A disagreements table — one row per issue, with columns for: the issue; the epistemic structural realist's position; the ontic structural realist's position; and the collaborators' joint judgment as to whether that residual disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or an artifact of equivocating on terms such as object, individuality, or grounding. Candidate rows include whether anything metaphysical follows from physics' silence on individuality, and whether the notion of a bearer of properties doing no work beyond bearing them is coherent.

3. A cruxes section — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. These should include: whether the permutation invariance of quantum states of identical particles and the diffeomorphism equivalence of distinct models in general relativity show that individuality is conferred by structure (as the ontic side claims) or merely that our physical theories happen to individuate objects non-haecceistically, leaving room for individuation we cannot know (as the epistemic side maintains); whether the ontic side's claim that relations are ontologically basic has content beyond a redescription of the epistemic side's position, or is surplus metaphysics; and how each side should respond to a hypothetical future physics that reformulates everything currently stated in terms of objects purely in terms of relational structure — as a discovery about what there is, or as a change of representational bookkeeping. For each crux, state the kind of argument, case, or scientific development both sides agree would genuinely bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, so that a reader could later check who was right.

4. A closing section identifying any parts of their dispute the collaborators have come to regard as verbal rather than substantive, and explaining precisely why.

Constraints on the drafting: every formulation of a disagreement must be worded so that the side it attributes a view to would accept it as a fair statement of their own position; agreed claims must be substantive enough that a naive scientific realist or a pure empiricist could not also sign them; and residual disagreements must be made exact rather than gestured at. Do not include citations or reading lists. Where a scientific case is invoked (e.g., permutation invariance, diffeomorphism equivalence, or a duality between two formulations), describe it in enough detail that the document is self-contained.
