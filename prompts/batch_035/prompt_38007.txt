I'm a philosopher of mind, and in a fortnight I'm giving a job talk at a large European cognitive science department that's one of the big centres for enactive robotics — think a building full of people who build sensorimotor agents and consider Rodney Brooks a folk hero, plus one grizzled old-school symbolic AI type who's been there since the eighties. My paper argues that the symbol grounding problem, as Harnad originally posed it, is badly framed for today's multimodal models. The old framing treats grounding as a discrete, all-or-nothing thing: either a symbol is connected to the world through sensorimotor loops or it's just meaningless squiggles being shuffled around a Chinese Room. My claim is that grounding is really about the richness and structure of the constraints under which a system can use its symbols — and that multimodal training supplies constraints that are rich enough to put these systems well up the spectrum, even if full-bodied sensorimotor loops would add more. I call this "grounding by constraint" in the paper, which I gather some of them have already seen and aren't thrilled about.

So what I need is a proper Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections this particular audience will actually raise, in the order they'll probably come, phrased the way an enactivist phrases things (they'll say meaning is *constituted* by sensorimotor engagement, not merely correlated with it, and that training on captioned images is still just statistical covariation all the way down) and the way the roboticists will put it (wait a decade and I'm sure they'll have some variant of "my robot's visual system closes the loop, your model just predicts pixels; calling both 'grounding' waters the concept down until it does no work"). The old symbolic AI guy I expect to come at it sideways with something about how I've just smuggled behaviourism back in. For each objection: a two-minute reply I can actually deliver, what I should flat-out concede, and where my reply is soft — I'd rather know the weak spots than be surprised by them on stage.

One more thing: I suspect my weakest joint is the counterfactual divergence problem, i.e. that a model's internal constraints on the word "dog" can quietly come apart from ours (the classic worry that it latches onto textures or co-occurrence patterns rather than the thing itself), so it's *predicting like* a grounded user without the underlying profile.
