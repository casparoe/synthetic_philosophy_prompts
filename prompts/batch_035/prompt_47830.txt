I sit on the steering committee for an annual Turing-test-style competition — think of a more serious, academically-run cousin of the old Loebner prize, with philosophers and NLP people as judges. We have a rules dispute for next year's cycle and I need to circulate a written opinion recommending a decision before our meeting in two weeks.

Background: the current format is the classic setup. Each judge gets a 25-minute text-only chat with one human and one machine contestant, blinded, and has to say which is which. It's been this way since the beginning.

The proposal on the table: one committee member wants to add a "slow correspondence round" — a six-week letter exchange between judge and contestant where the judge can introduce arbitrary topic changes at any moment with no warning (like "by the way my father died last week... anyway what did you make of the game?"), reply whenever she feels like it, and go back to threads from weeks earlier. Her thinking is that short-chat indistinguishability has gotten cheap: a statistical mimic can glide through 25 minutes on plausible continuations of whatever the last few turns were, but weeks of judge-initiated drift, callbacks, and emotional stakes would expose anything that's just pattern-matching locally.

The counterargument, from another member: this is goalpost-moving with no principled stopping point. Either behavior is the criterion for mind or it isn't. If it is, then in principle even the slow round is passable by something with no more understanding than a lookup table — he brought up Ned Block's old Blockhead thought experiment, the machine with a pre-stored reply for every possible conversational history, which would pass any behavioral test you care to design if you granted it enough storage. On that view, stretching the test just makes it more expensive to fool, not conceptually different. And if behavior *isn't* the criterion, then the whole competition is theater and we're quibbling over stage directions.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete decision — adopt the round, reject it, or adopt with conditions (and if conditions, spell them out) — with reasoning both sides could actually engage with rather than a survey of the literature. Take the Blockhead objection seriously, don't hand-wave it; but also probe whether it's doing less work here than it seems (the standard "no table that size could fit in the universe" reply — does that actually help us, given our judges only have six weeks and the table only needs to cover *these* conversations?). Tell me what the slow round would be testing that the short chat doesn't — whether it's tracking something like integration over time, judge-controlled novelty, or stakes — or whether our objector is right that it's just the same test with worse ergonomics. One more thing our mission statement commits us to "operationalizing behavioral criteria for machine intelligence," so if the memo concludes the slow round is a better operationalization it should say why, and if it concludes no behavioral test can do that job, it should say what we tell the funders.

A couple of practical notes: judges are volunteers and we can't ask much more of them than we already do, the competition runs on a modest grant, and whatever we decide sets precedent — three other members have proposals waiting in the wings (a spoken-round-with-voice option, an embodied robotics round, a year-long round). So the memo should also say something about where the stopping point is, if we start down this road at all. Keep it to something I can actually read out loud in a meeting.
