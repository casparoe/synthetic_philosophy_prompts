I'm writing a chapter for a book on practical rationality aimed at managers and policy people, and the chapter is meant to end with a set of crisp recommendations about escalation of commitment — the phenomenon the dollar auction was invented to exhibit. Before I draft it, I want to know whether the position I've drifted into over years of teaching this material is actually coherent, because my recommendations need to come from somewhere. Here is the position, in six theses. First, the core of rational decision-making is that only prospective costs and benefits matter: the money, time, and effort already spent on a project are reasons for nothing, and a rational agent's evaluation of a project should be independent of how the project came to be under consideration. Second, in the dollar auction itself, once you have made a serious bid and been overtaken, it is often rational to keep bidding: quitting guarantees the loss of your standing bid, while a small further bid buys a real chance of recouping it, and two bidders who each see this can rationally drive the price to several times the value of the prize — the auction shows escalation being generated by rationality itself, not by a lapse from it. Third, there is a defensible policy of finishing what you start: given that our assessments of floundering projects are noisy, and that quitting under pressure is exactly when our judgment is least trustworthy, a standing rule that treats accumulated effort as creating a mild presumption in favor of continuing can be the optimal rule to adopt, even though it would be irrational to reason that way case by case. Fourth, real-world organizational escalation — the failed IT system that absorbs its third rescue budget, the war fought another year to justify the dead — is mostly a bias, a predictable failure of the human decision machinery, and drilling managers on the sunk cost principle is among the most valuable things a decision course can do. Fifth, commitment devices are paradigmatically rational: burning bridges, posting public stakes, hiring the trainer who charges you for cancelled sessions — deliberately making your future options worse can be the smartest move available. Sixth, whether a course of action is rational should not depend on the agent's history: two agents facing the same choice with the same options and the same information should choose alike, whatever paths brought them there.

I believe all six, but I suspect they cannot all be true, and I can't see where the problem is well enough to fix it before the manuscript is due. Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look to be, and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them uniformly. For each genuine one, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me — which recommendation in the chapter would have to be softened or retracted. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write the audit as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold text. And go deep on the two or three tensions you think are real rather than giving equal airtime to everything.
