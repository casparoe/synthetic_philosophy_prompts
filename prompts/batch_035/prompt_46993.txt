I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form piece about what the philosophy of language can tell us about the limits of translation — including, eventually, machine translation. For background I'm working through chapter 2 of Quine's Word and Object (1960), and there is one stretch of the argument I cannot get straight, so I would like your help understanding it.

The passage, as I read it, runs roughly as follows; I am paraphrasing from memory, so treat this as my reconstruction rather than a quotation. A field linguist with no prior contact sets out to translate a native language from scratch. A rabbit scurries by, the native says "gavagai", and the linguist tentatively records it as equivalent to our word for the animal, subject to testing in further cases. Quine then observes that all the behavioral evidence the linguist could ever collect is equally consistent with taking "gavagai" to mean undetached rabbit part, or a momentary rabbit-stage, provided the rest of the translation manual is adjusted to compensate — so no observable fact decides between the manuals.

Here is what I cannot resolve. It seems obvious that the linguist could settle the matter by interrogation: pointing at a rabbit seen yesterday and one seen today, ask the native, "Is this the same gavagai as that one?" On a whole-animal reading the answer should pattern one way, on an undetached-part or stage reading another, and the manuals would finally diverge on something checkable. My edition seems to anticipate this objection, but the move it makes — that the native's word for "same" is itself up for translation, so the answer only disambiguates relative to a prior choice among manuals — strikes me as question-begging: the test appears to fail only because we permit the rival manual to rewrite the test. Please explain whether that really is Quine's reasoning, whether the regress ever bottoms out, and where exactly, if anywhere, the argument depends on his behaviorism (the premise that everything about meaning must be extractable from dispositions to assent and dissent) rather than on anything about language as such. I want to understand the machinery well enough to describe it accurately in print, not to referee it.
