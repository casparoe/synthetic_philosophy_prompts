At a recent rationality meetup — I've been in this community long enough to have seen versions of this argument many times, though never run this deep — two members got into a sustained disagreement about whether evolutionary debunking arguments succeed against moral realism. I was asked to referee, and honestly could not. I'll reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can, in numbered moves.

(1) Dara opened with the debunking setup: our moral dispositions were shaped by natural selection for reproductive fitness, not for tracking moral truth. Had the selection pressures been different — had they favored more nepotism, or less altruism toward strangers — we would hold systematically different moral beliefs with just as much confidence as we now hold ours. So the genealogy of our moral beliefs gives us no reason to think those beliefs are true, and the realist owes us a story about why evolution would have pointed us at moral facts rather than merely at fitness. Absent that story, we should distrust our moral beliefs wholesale.

(2) Farid replied that this proves too much. Evolution also shaped our perceptual systems, our memory, and our disposition to trust elementary logic, all for fitness reasons; if fitness-shaped origins were sufficient to debunk, all cognition would be debunked, including the premises of the debunking argument itself. In some domains, accuracy is fitness-enhancing, so selection can and does build reliable faculties; nothing in the genealogy alone tells you which domain you're in.

(3) Dara granted that but insisted on a domain-specific asymmetry. For perception and logic, we can independently explain why accuracy would have been selected for: perception is causally constrained by the world it reports on, so inaccurate medium-object perception gets you eaten. Moral truths, by contrast, are causally inert — if there are stance-independent moral facts, they don't push particles around — so fitness considerations are simply insensitive to them; fitness varies independently of moral truth, and that independence, not the evolutionary origin per se, is what does the debunking. The 'proves too much' reply therefore misses its target.

(4) Farid made two countermoves. First, he said the causal-inertness premise just is non-naturalism: the debunking argument presupposes the very conception of morality it is supposed to be testing, so realists of a naturalist stripe can shrug it off as begging the question. Second, he offered a third-factor response: suppose survival and moral truth are correlated — for instance, that damage is bad and that creatures which avoid damage survive more. Then the same selection process that favors fitness also, incidentally, favors sensitivity to moral truth, exactly as it favors sensitivity to tissue damage. The pain system gets vindicated by this structure, he said, and there is no obvious reason the moral case cannot run the same structure.

(5) Dara answered that third-factor vindications are epistemically circular in a way the pain case is not. To identify the third factor and certify its correlation with moral truth, you must deploy the very moral judgments that are under suspicion — you are certifying the instrument using the instrument's own readings. The pain case works because the correlation is certified by independent, non-moral observations of injury and behavior; in the moral case, no such independent check exists, so the vindication is only as good as the beliefs it was supposed to vindicate.

(6) Farid's final move was to change the status of the question rather than answer it: even if the genealogy defeats any positive justification for moral beliefs, beliefs acquired by processes we cannot currently indict still merit epistemic conservatism — one may rationally retain them absent a specific defeater. Dara shot back that asking where a 'specific defeater' could come from, if not from exactly the genealogy he had described, was precisely the question — and there the exchange ended, both convinced they had won overall.

Please adjudicate the six moves in order: for each, say what is right in it, what is mistaken or overstated, and where the burden of proof should be taken to lie at that point. Then give an overall verdict on who, if either, won the exchange. Finally, if there is a distinction or a standard move in this literature that both of them missed and that would have restructured the debate, I'd be grateful to hear it — I suspect the disagreement kept sliding between debunking the origin of a belief and debunking its present maintenance, but I could not get clear on whether that distinction would help either side.
