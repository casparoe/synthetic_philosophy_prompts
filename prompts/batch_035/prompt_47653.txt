I've been running a reading group where two members have spent the better part of a year arguing about epiphenomenalism, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement they should have produced. One is a dualist who takes the conceivability of functionally identical beings without experience seriously and holds that phenomenal properties, while lawfully correlated with brain states, do no causal work in producing behavior or judgment. The other is a physicalist who thinks phenomenal judgments are ordinary cognitive events with evolutionary histories, fully explicable at the neural level, and regards the dualist's position as self-undermining for a specific reason: if experiences cause nothing, then our reports about our experiences are produced entirely by the neural side of the correlation, so the reports can't count as knowledge of the experiences — this is the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The dualist replies that lawful covariation suffices for reliability, much as a barometer's readings can be reliable evidence about a storm the barometer doesn't cause.

Produce the document itself, not advice about how they might proceed. Include the claims both can sign — about the neural machinery of report, about dissociation cases like blindsight where behavior and experience come apart, about what any theory of consciousness owes an account of. Then state their exact residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording: I suspect one turns on whether reliability requires causal connection or only covariation, and another on whether my zombie duplicate makes the same judgment I do when it says it is in pain — but work out the precise formulations yourself, and take a definite position on whether any of these disagreements are merely verbal (e.g., about what "judgment" or "reliability" means) rather than substantive. Finally, for each substantive crux, give a concrete case or thought experiment both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance; feel free to devise one, such as a hypothetical pharmacological intervention that inserts a systematic delay between neural events and the experiences lawfully correlated with them, and ask what each side predicts about the subjects' retrospective reports. Close with a short section in which you, as drafter, commit to which side currently has the better of the argument and why — don't leave me to weigh the options myself.
