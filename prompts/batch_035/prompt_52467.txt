Two of my coworkers, Priya and Dan, got into a debate at lunch on friday and I couldn't follow it well enough to referee, so I'm bringing it here. A bit of context: our org ran this one-shot prisoner's dilemma exercise last month as part of some offsite team thing — random anonymous pairings across departments, people you'd never met and would never meet, single round, no communication, real money split at the end depending on both choices. Afterwards participants were allowed to compare notes, and that's where it started.

Dan defected and said obviously so: whatever the other person does, defecting pays more, so it's not even a decision, it's arithmetic. Priya cooperated, and Dan told her she'd basically donated money to a rando.

Priya said she didn't do it out of niceness. Her reasoning was: whoever she got paired with was, like her, sitting in the same building, having read the same instructions, thinking about the same choice — and if she can talk herself into defecting, so can he, and if she cooperates he probably does too. Her phrase was that she and the partner are "two instances of the same loop running on the same input."

Dan said that's the gambler's fallacy in a nice outfit — your choice can't reach across the building and change his. Two processes, two outputs, no connection.

Priya said she's not claiming her choice *changes* his, it's evidence about his — like, if you flip a coin you suspect is biased and it lands heads, that's a reason to expect heads again. She and the partner are draws from the same pool, so whatever tips her toward cooperate probably tips him the same way.

Dan granted the evidence point but said evidence isn't a reason to cooperate. If you both lean cooperative, the smart play is still defect and collect his cooperation for free — the setup rewards it, and it's not like he'd ever know.

Priya's comeback was that this only works if she gets to treat "what I do" and "what he does" as two separate knobs she can set independently, and she doesn't think she can: if everyone reasons the way Dan says is smart, everyone lands on defect, including with the person Dan got paired with. "I pick the row, and the row picks the outcome for both of us, that's just what symmetric means here."

Dan's last word was that this only works if you and the partner are literally the same computation, and that she snuck that in with the "same loop" line without ever defending it.

They asked me to call it at the time and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what — where each of them conceded too much or too little, whether the coin analogy is doing legitimate work or not, and what, if anything, they're both missing? I write backend code for a living so feel free to use whatever formalism helps, but I have zero philosophy background, just so you know where I'm starting from.
