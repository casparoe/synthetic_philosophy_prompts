okay so I'm a novelist — midlist, don't worry about it — and I'm stuck on a chapter. my book has this character, Dr. Ines Marchetti, a decision theorist who didn't get tenure (the episode with the chair's coffee and the whiteboard is a whole other subplot) and now writes an advice column for a regional newspaper. the column is called "Expect the Best" and it's her whole deal: every letter gets answered with expected-value arithmetic. someone writes in about whether to take a new job, she computes. someone asks about a third date, she computes. she is serene about it. it's how I'm dramatizing the whole rationalist self-help thing, the idea that every life question is a box you can put numbers in.

the chapter I need is her final column, the one that breaks her. it should read like an actual advice column — 550-650 words, three or four letters with replies, deadpan throughout, the satirical target being the assumption that every choice has an expected value sitting there waiting to be maximized. the earlier letters can be her at her most confident, doing genuinely coherent EV reasoning (a funnier-if-you-know-it version of normal decision theory, not a strawman — I want decision theorists to find her competent right up until she isn't). then the last letter is from a reader who's been offered a curious game at a county fair: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if that first heads happens on flip n, the payout is (-1)^n · 2^n / n dollars, so roughly +$2, -$2, +$1.33, -$1, and so on, sign alternating, magnitude shrinking. the reader asks whether to play and what entry fee would be fair.

and here's where I need you to get the math right, because the joke only works if it's real: the expected value of that game is the sum of (-1)^n / n over all n — an alternating series that converges under the standard ordering but can be made to sum to basically anything, or diverge, if you group or reorder the terms (Riemann rearrangement stuff, first-year analysis). it's the Pasadena paradox in carnival form. the payoff isn't infinite like its older cousin, it's *undefined*, and that's worse for Ines — with an infinite EV she'd at least have something to say.

so her reply should show her trying her usual moves and watching them fail: she computes, the partial sums swing above and below, she tries truncating ("just ignore the last flips, no one flips forever anyway") and the truncation choice silently determines her recommendation, she notices that whether she tells him to play depends on the order she happened to add the terms in that morning. the satire should come from her treating the undefinedness as a personal shortcoming — one more self-help guru with a method that only works on questions that were never really open. but don't let the column end in despair or a neat moral, the editor at the paper wants a definitive answer for the readers and Ines gives one, confidently, for reasons she can't reconstruct afterward. that last beat is the thing I can't write myself.

no real people in the letters, all correspondents invented. and please dont have anyone say "math is hard" or wink at the reader — she never breaks character, that's what makes it sad and funny at the same time. if you could keep her voice consistent across the letters (slightly formal, fond of the word "dominant"), that would really help the chapter. also dont worry about newspaper formatting conventions, just letters and replies.
