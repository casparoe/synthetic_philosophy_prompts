I work as an environmental consultant for a regional wildlife agency, and a dispute has just landed on my desk that has turned a question from my undergraduate philosophy of biology course into a professional emergency. A genetic study argues that a protected salamander population, listed as a distinct species for thirty years, is connected by continuous gene flow to a common, unlisted one — the sort of gradient you find in ring species, where each neighboring population interbreeds readily but the populations at the two ends do not, so that any boundary you draw across the chain reflects a decision rather than a discovery. If we recommend delisting, the population loses its protection; if we don't, we are drawing a line through a continuum and presenting it to the public as though nature had already drawn it for us. My agency's lawyers want a recommendation, and I've noticed that my hesitation depends on a philosophical view I have never actually examined: whether there is a fact of the matter about how many species exist in that valley at all.

So I'd like your credence that species realism is true — that "species" carves nature at something like a joint, such that a completed biology would settle, without arbitrary convention, how many species live there — and I'd like the reasoning decomposed rather than a bare number. Some terms I can already see in the mix, though feel free to add or restructure: the probability that there is exactly one correct species concept rather than several equally legitimate ones tailored to different explanatory purposes; the probability that speciation typically leaves objective, discoverable discontinuities in the world rather than continua that only extinction and time chop into countable units; and the probability that the tree of life is mind-independent and jointy enough to ground realism even where hybrid zones, reticulate evolution, and lateral gene transfer tangle the branches into something more like a web. Assign each a rough probability with a line of justification, show me how they combine into your final number, and then tell me which term you are least confident in and what evidence would move it most.

One further thing, since my report is due either way and I can't submit a credence as a recommendation: if your overall confidence in species realism comes out modest, tell me whether you think the defensible move in a legal and regulatory context is to treat "species" as a useful fiction that we are nonetheless obligated to administer — and, if so, what that should commit me to in how I frame the report, as opposed to the framing where I write as though the boundary were simply there to be found.
