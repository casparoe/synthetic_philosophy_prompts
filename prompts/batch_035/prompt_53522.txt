ok so, background: I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since maybe 2011 — Overcoming Bias, then LessWrong, mostly a lurker — and the thing that's survived all those years is my friend Maya's verdicts. Same reading group for six years now. Whenever some "does the big thing cause the small thing" question comes up, she rules on it immediately, and she's never once reversed one. I've kept a list. Your job is to extrapolate from it.

Act as a philosopher of mind who has actually read Dennett, Kim, and Woodward on causation, but write for me, not for a journal — and please brainstorm the candidate rules before committing to one, instead of grabbing the first thing that fits.

Her rulings so far. Format: does the macro-level item push the substrate (the micro-level stuff it's made of, or that it acts on) around?

1. A buggy line of firmware in an infusion pump → the exact voltage spikes it produces in the memory chips: NO. Her words: the circuit does everything, the bug is just our name for a pattern in the circuit — I'll happily say the bug killed the patient, that's about blame, but it doesn't shove electrons around.
2. An enzyme's overall shape → which individual substrate molecules get captured: YES. The shape is a fact about the whole molecule and it steers individual molecules; bond-by-bond physics doesn't tell you why that molecule got caught.
3. A gene "wanting" to spread → the individual chemical steps when an organism dies for its kin: NO. Metaphor. Dawkins is allowed the metaphor and she isn't.
4. The market index falling → the timing of one particular day-trader's sell keystroke: YES. The macro signal reaches down and moves his hand; his brain state alone doesn't fix the timing without it.
5. The reward scalar in an RL training run → the individual weight updates: YES. It's computed outside the network, read back in, and does the work. No reward, no updates.
6. A glider in the Game of Life → the cells that switch on as it passes: NO. The rule table shoves everything, the glider rides for free.
7. Maya deciding to type a sentence → the individual keystrokes: YES, and she got genuinely annoyed at the guy who pushed on this — I am not a passenger in my own body, she said. But when he asked the follow-up, "so does the decision push your individual motor neurons around?", she said "hm, ask me later" and changed the subject. I suspect that hole in the record is definately the shape of the next question.

Which is this. I've been rereading Hofstadter, and the new case is his careenium. Picture a huge table where billiard balls careen around forever, colliding. Certain stable, repeatable patterns in their collective motion — he calls them simmballs — act like symbols: when enough balls cluster in one region, that simmball fires, and the firing redirects the balls so they stream out toward the regions of the simmballs it's connected to, sometimes setting those off in turn. (I'm paraphrasing him, and badly, but that's the setup.) The question, in her format: do the the simmballs push the individual billiard balls around, or are they just patterns the balls drag along for free?

Predict her verdict. Honestly I care less about the yes/no than about the machinery, so structure your answer in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

- "Candidate rules" (~200 words): the two-to-four rules that fit her whole record, and where they agree and diverge.
- "The careenium under each rule" (~120 words).
- "My prediction" (~150 words): your best guess, your reasons, and which of her rulings is the closest analog.
- "The case that would settle it" (~80 words): a further case we could put to her that would split whatever rules are still live.
- "Where I'd bet wrong" (~100 words): what argument or consideration would flip your prediction, and which step in your own reasoning you trust least.
