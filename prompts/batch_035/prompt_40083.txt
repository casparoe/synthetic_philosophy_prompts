I'm a fourth-year PhD student in sociology, and this semester I'm running the discussion sections for a large social-theory lecture. Next week's section (18 undergraduates, 75 minutes, movable desks) covers the Campbell and Manning typology of honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures, and I don't want to just walk through the three boxes and let students file them under "obviously dignity culture is the good one." What I'd like is a structured simulation in which students play out the *same* incident three times under three different institutional protocols — labeled neutrally as Protocol A, B, and C, with the actual names withheld until the debrief — so that they run into the framework's central and least intuitive claim themselves: that victimhood culture isn't just "dignity culture gone wrong" but a distinctive hybrid, combining something like honor culture's sensitivity to slight with dignity culture's habit of appealing to third parties, under conditions where the third parties are informal and public rather than formal and legal. Concretely, I imagine a fixed offense (a written grievance card handed out by a designated role-player, so no one insults anyone for real), and three rounds in which only the surrounding rules change: in one round, status is entirely reputation-based, private redress or confrontation is the only channel, and there are no authorities; in another, each player's standing is a fixed, non-negotiable endowment that slights do not touch, with a costly formal appeal mechanism for serious matters; in the third, there's a broadcast channel and an audience of third parties who can be mobilized, and being demonstrably wronged carries some kind of spendable moral capital. I'm told by the professor to avoid real money, so I'd want a token or point economy, and I'd like the payoff structure to make the *behavioral differences* visible in aggregate even if individual students game it.

Please design the full thing: the exact rules for each protocol, the point/token accounting (including what it costs to appeal, to confront, to publicize, and to forgive or ignore), how the audience/third-party role works so it doesn't just devolve into everyone watching, a round structure that fits 75 minutes with the debrief included, and the debrief itself, which should map specific moments students will remember onto the framework without my lecturing at them. Two constraints I care about a lot. First, the debrief must separate the descriptive question (which behaviors does each protocol elicit) from the normative one the section is really about — whether any of the three cultures is *morally superior*, or whether each is a locally rational adaptation to a particular setting: dense reputation-based networks with weak or absent formal authority versus anonymous, legally ordered settings versus networked publics with cheap broadcast. Students should leave unsure whether superiority is even a well-formed question when the cultures are solving different problems. Second, be honest and concrete about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — my guesses include participants finding the grievance cards funny rather than offensive, the third-party audience being inert, students pattern-matching to the real-world label early and performing their political priors instead of playing the rules, and the "victimhood capital" mechanic accidentally teaching a cynical caricature rather than the analytic point — and tell me how to handle each. Also flag what the simulation fundamentally cannot reproduce that I'll have to supply in the debrief (I'm thinking the actual risk of violence in honor settings, the fact that people don't choose their culture, and the selection effects that make real aggrieved parties non-representative of everyone who is wronged), so that the debrief doesn't quietly claim more evidential weight than a classroom game can carry. Lead with the single most consequential design decision in your first sentence, then give the full plan with no preamble and no restatement of these instructions.
