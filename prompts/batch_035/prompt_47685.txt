I teach an upper-level seminar cross-listed between philosophy and computer science, "Minds of Machines," and I'm writing the final exam. I want one 90-minute essay question on whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer in virtue of their reward signals, and it has to defeat the two stock answers my students have memorized: the easy yes ("reward is just pleasure and pain, so a big negative reward just is suffering") and the easy no ("RL agents lack phenomenal consciousness, so nothing is experienced, end of story"). The question should instead force them to work on the machinery. Three framings in particular: (i) the difference between reward functioning as a mere input to a learning rule — a teaching signal the agent need not itself represent, let alone feel — and reward functioning as something represented in the agent's internal model with valenced content; (ii) the finding, stable in affective neuroscience, that the systems driving pursuit ("wanting") and the systems generating hedonic experience ("liking") dissociate, so that an agent could be powerfully driven by negative reward without anything pain-like occurring in it; and (iii) the sharper proposal that valence should be tied to reward prediction errors rather than reward per se, so that suffering tracks the gap between what the agent expected and what it got rather than raw punishment magnitude.

The question should also push toward practical upshot, because that's where the three framings earn their keep: each predicts something different about the moral status of design choices. Consider an agent whose reward channel is invisible to its own world-model versus one that self-monitors its own training signal; or a robot trained with dense, frequent, small negative rewards versus one trained with rare, large shocks; or an agent whose value function has fully converged (so prediction errors have gone to zero) versus one still being surprised constantly during training. A strong answer will use cases like these to argue for or against the different framings rather than treating the framings as three ways of saying the same thing — because they are not. They locate suffering (or its absence) at three different points in the architecture, and they can come apart: on some combinations of views, the frequently-shocked agent is worse off than the harshly-shocked one, or vice versa, or neither is worse off than a thermostat.

Please write the question itself (a paragraph, addressed to the students), a model answer of about 600–700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points total that names five must-hit moves, what each is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically fumble each. The A/B distinction should turn on whether the student sees that the three framings are three different candidate locations for where suffering could be — or fail to be — in an RL agent, and can generate a case where two framings yield opposite verdicts; a B answer will instead treat the framings as accumulating evidence for a single conclusion and merely assert it with more citations. An answer that deploys the analogy to pain asymbolia — nociception retained while the affective dimension is gone — in a load-bearing way should be flagged in the marking scheme as one legitimate route to the top band, but not a required one, since there are other equally good routes (e.g., arguing from homeostatic regulation, or from the self-monitoring design difference).
