My brother-in-law sells used cars, and his rule of thumb is that as long as every word you say is true, you're not lying — the customer draws their own conclusions, and their conclusions are their own business. We've been arguing about this for about a year now, mostly on his porch, and somewhere along the way I started keeping a list of situations in a notebook with my honest gut reaction to each. My hope was that my gut would confirm one of two clean pictures: either that lying is distinctly worse than merely misleading, or his view that there's no real difference once you've decided to deceive someone at all. Instead my gut refused both, so now I want someone to tell me whether there's a single principle underneath my reactions or whether I'm just inconsistent. Here are the cases, numbered. (1) Selling his truck, asked whether it had ever been in an accident, he answered "it's never had bodywork," which was true only because he'd swapped out the crumpled fender himself with a junkyard part in his garage. Nearly as bad as lying outright, I think. (2) Asked in a job interview why I left my last job, I said "I was ready for a change," when actually I'd been fired for chronic lateness. Bad, though maybe a shade less bad than a flat "I quit on my own terms." (3) If he had simply said "no accidents ever," a plain lie — the worst of the batch, but honestly not by much more than case 1. (4) The buyer never asks about the truck's history and he volunteers nothing. Feels far less bad than case 1, maybe not even wrong. (5) Saying "I've told you a thousand times" or "this line at the DMV has been here since dawn" — false, and everybody including the listener knows it. Not wrong at all. (6) At a yard sale, telling a haggling buyer "I've had another offer" when I haven't, in a setting where everyone treats haggling talk as expected puffery. Basically fine. (7) My grandmother, who trusts me completely, asks whether I'm doing okay financially, and I say "I've been eating fine" — true, and I'm two months from being evicted and I know exactly what conclusion she'll draw. Feels worse than the lie in case 6.

The two things I can't square: my verdicts don't seem to track the lying/misleading line at all — case 1 (all true words) scores nearly as bad as case 3, and case 7 beats case 6 — and they also don't track my own pet explanation, which was my brother-in-law's point turned around: that with a misleading-but-true statement the listener does part of the work of deceiving themselves, and there's something less bad about a deception that routes through someone's own reasoning. That explanation predicts the engineered technical truth in case 1 should be *better* than a lie, since the buyer "concludes" on his own, but my gut says the airtight technical truth is worse than a lazy lie, because it feels like a trap built with more care. And I can't decide whether the gap between case 1 and case 4 is because silence leaves a false belief alone while speaking arms it, or something about whose job it was to raise the topic in the first place.

So here's what I'd like: state a principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that it delivers a verdict on each of my seven cases; tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it can't save and why I should give those up rather than complicate the principle; and apply it to two cases I deliberately left off my list — a nurse telling a frightened patient "the large majority of people with this condition recover fully," which is true even though the nurse knows this particular patient is in the unlucky minority and is counting on the comfort; and a seller who advertises "ask me anything, I answer everything straight," then answers a direct question with a technically-true dodge. At the very end, give me a line in the form \answer{...} containing a short name for your principle (or "no single principle"), then a semicolon, then either "all seven verdicts stand" or the numbers of the verdicts that must go, then a semicolon, then "nurse case: fine" or "nurse case: not fine."
