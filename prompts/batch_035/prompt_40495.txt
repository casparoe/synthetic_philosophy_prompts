I'm on a small working group for our city's participatory budgeting program — we advise the city council, and in about six weeks they'll vote on how the next funding round (something like 4 million for local infrastructure and community projects) gets its recommendations made.

Quick background on the fight. Last round we used the classic open town-hall format: anyone who shows up can propose and vote on projects. About 130 people came, median age well above the city average, hugely overrepresented from the neighborhood associations and one extremely organized cycling lobby, and the same twenty faces who show up to everything. One faction on our group, led by my colleague, says the fix is obvious: pick the next recommending body by lottery. Stratified random sample of maybe 350 residents, compensated for their time, meeting over several weekends. No more self-selection, no more rewards for having a flexible Tuesday evening. The other faction, mostly the finance office people, says this is naive twice over: (1) randomly selected residents are mostly disengaged and will just rubber-stamp whatever the professional staff put in front of them, so we're not democratizing anything, we're laundering staff decisions through a lottery, and (2) motivated volunteers, whatever their biases, at least know the neighborhoods. They want to keep town halls and just do better outreach.

I have to write the recommendation memo the council will actually read before the vote. Please draft it for me.

A few things I want in there:

- A clear recommendation — full sortition, keep town halls, or some hybrid — with a decision the council can act on, not a menu of options.
- Concrete design conditions attached to the recommendation: panel size, what to stratify on and why those variables rather than others, what happens when someone drawn by lottery doesn't show up or stops coming, how much the panel can go beyond staff-prepared options. If your recommendation is a hybrid, say exactly which parts are random and which are open.
- An honest treatment of the losing side's single strongest point. My colleague's lottery faction has a genuinely good argument about self-selection bias (open formats systematically select for spare time, extroversion, and pre-existing organization — a point about *who* gets rewarded, not just who shows up). The town-hall faction has a genuinely good argument about the agenda (if staff frame the options and the panel only ranks them, random selection decorates an unelected process rather than changing it). Don't strawman either.
- Something about the competence worry that goes deeper than "trust the people": if a big random panel is on average only weakly informed about infrastructure costs, what exactly are we hoping the aggregation does for us, and under what assumptions would that hope actually be justified? I remember there's a classic result along these lines about juries and majorities — I don't want the memo to misuse it.

Some context on me: I'm a mid-level planner, not a philosopher, but the council members have heard of deliberative mini-publics and will spot hand-waving. So play the role of a political theorist who's spent a career studying democratic institutions and has watched a few of these citizen-assembly-type bodies succeed and fail up close — please actually think through the design tradeoffs before locking in the recommendation, like, maybe sketch the realistic failure modes of each option first and then decide which failure mode this specific city can least afford, given what I described about who currently dominates the town halls.

Also, if it helps the memo, you can note the deeper point that keeps bugging me: a lottery doesn't reward anyone for anything — it just ignores motivation entirely. Whether that's a bug or the whole feature seems to be the philosophical crux of this fight, and I'd love the memo to be honest about it rather than pretending there's a technocratic answer.

Keep it usable — council members read about two pages before their eyes glaze over. Something like a real decision memo: recommendation up top, reasoning under it, and the strongest objection answered rather than buried.
