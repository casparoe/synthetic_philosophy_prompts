I'm really into family history, and there's something about the census that keeps bugging me. Here in the UK your individual census return — the actual schedule with your name, your household, your occupation, who slept where — gets sealed for a hundred years. The 1921 one only became public in 2022, and the 1951 one comes out in 2052, by which point I'll almost certainly be gone. So the state compels everyone to answer, uses the data immediately for planning and statistics, but promises nobody gets to look at MY sheet until everyone who could possibly have known me is dead.

What theory of privacy is that arrangement actually resting on? It clearly can't be that the information is secret — they use it straight away. It seems to be something about identifiability, or about there being no one left alive to be harmed or embarrassed. But then why a flat hundred years rather than, say, death plus a margin? Someone who was ninety in 1921 was almost certainly dead within a decade, yet their return sat sealed for decades longer than that, while a five-year-old on the same page had a decent chance of still being around when it opened. And the sheet isn't only about one person anyway — it records lodgers, servants, a neighbour's child staying that night, children born outside marriage, people enumerated in asylums and workhouses. Whose privacy does the seal even protect?

So: can you reconstruct the implicit view of privacy here? Is it a harm-based view (privacy matters because living people can be damaged by exposure, and death dissolves the claim), a control view (the record is mine while I'm alive to control it), or something about the flow of information between contexts — fine for a statistician now, fine for a genealogist in a century, never fine for a landlord or an insurer? And please actually take a position rather than laying the options out and leaving me to choose. I want you to say which reading best explains why we do it this way, whether the round hundred-year figure genuinely follows from that reading or is just a compromise none of the theories really supports, and what would have to change in how census records are handled if your reading is the right one. It's not urgent or anything, I've just been chewing on it and want someone to give me a straight answer.
