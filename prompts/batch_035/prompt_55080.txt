I teach phil of mind, and for years I've defended the view that the badness of pain is just constituted by its phenomenal character -- what makes pain bad for you is nothing over and above how it feels, that awful qualitative character itself. My grad students keep needling me with pain asymbolia: patients who say they feel the pain, can locate it, rate its intensity, but seem completely unbothered -- they don't withdraw, some even laugh when pricked hard. If the feel is supposedly intact while the badness is gone, doesn't that suggest the badness was never in the feel to begin with, but in some aversive attitude or motivational component that can come apart from sensation? I've got my stock replies (maybe the feel isn't really intact, maybe it's a partial dissociation, etc.) but I want a proper map of what would actually count against my view. Which possible empirical findings about asymbolia would genuinely hurt me -- say, what would careful introspective reports have to show about whether the qualitative character itself is subtly altered? Which objections should I just bite, and which can I legitimately explain away? And be honest with me: has my position been qualified so many times, with distinctions between sensory and affective components and ever-finer qualia, that it's become unfalsifiable -- and if so, is that fatal for a view of this kind or just par for the course in philosophy of mind? I'd also like to know whether the attitude-based rival really does better on these cases, or whether it has symmetrical problems of its own. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
