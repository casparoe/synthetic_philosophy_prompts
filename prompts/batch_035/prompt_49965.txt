I do hobby forecasting on a public prediction platform — maybe twenty questions a month, things like election results, product launch dates, sports outcomes — for points and bragging rights, not money, and I keep a calibration spreadsheet because I'm trying to get better. A couple of years ago I read a blog post about Bayes' theorem and it changed how I work: pick a prior, watch for evidence, update. The problem is that the blog quietly assumed the evidence arrives certain. Mine never does. Last month a friend who works at a company told me their flagship product would slip a quarter; I treated "delayed" as a settled fact, moved my forecast from 35% to 85%, and it turned out he was mixing up two product lines. This week I'm staring at a blurry leaked screenshot that's maybe a coin flip to be genuine, and at a poll from an anonymous account that is usually right but occasionally just invents numbers. So what I actually have isn't evidence — it's a tip about the evidence.

I gather there's a real rule for this, named after a philosopher (Jeffrey something): instead of pretending the evidence is certainly true, you decide how probable the tip makes it, then take a weighted average of what you'd believe if it were true and what you'd believe if it were false. I can follow the formula on paper but I can never run it in the moment, under deadline, with numbers I mostly don't have. There's also a wrinkle the formula seems to ignore: sometimes the leak's mere existence is informative — companies about to cancel a product seem to leak more than ones that aren't — and I don't know whether the rule still applies when the source's behavior depends on the answer.

What I'd like is a procedure I can actually execute in about ten minutes at my keyboard, written for someone whose statistics education is one blog post. Please give me the questions to ask myself in order, rough rules of thumb for putting numbers on source reliability when I have nothing but a vague sense that someone's "usually right," guidance on when the arithmetic is worth doing in the spreadsheet versus estimating in my head, and an honest marker on each step that is really a judgment call wearing a mathematical costume. Then work through the two live cases for me — the fifty-fifty screenshot and the friend who's been right about three of the last four comparable things — showing the numbers you'd plug in and why. And please include at least one scenario where the procedure forces an answer I won't like: I know I jump too hard on exciting leaks, so if the honest output is "move your forecast by four points, not forty," I want that stated bluntly, along with whatever the procedure simply cannot settle and I have to decide with my gut.
