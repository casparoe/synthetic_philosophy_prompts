In my psychology class we just started consciousness, and my teacher explained the global workspace theory with an analogy: consciousness is like a spotlight on a theater stage. There are dozens of actors backstage and in the wings doing their work in the dark — memory, language, vision, motor planning — but at any moment the spotlight picks out just one of them, and what the spotlight illuminates is broadcast to the whole crew so everyone can coordinate around it. She then used the analogy to argue two things: that consciousness is basically selective attention plus publicity (being conscious of something just means the spotlight is on it and the whole system can use it), and that there is no special extra "spark" of consciousness beyond the spotlight's workings themselves. But when I described this to a friend who reads a lot of philosophy, she said the analogy is exactly the target of Dennett's "Cartesian theater" critique — that any theater picture secretly puts a spectator in the audience who watches the lit stage, and that this spectator is precisely the mysterious inner self the theory was supposed to explain away. So my question: how far does the spotlight analogy actually hold for global workspace theory? Please give me a correspondence table of what maps onto what (the actors, the spotlight, the backstage crew, the audience, the broadcast), say where the mapping breaks down — in particular, whether there is anything in the analogy playing the role of the spectator, and whether a theater could run with nobody watching — and then tell me whether my teacher's two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether one of them needed a stronger picture of the spotlight than the analogy supplies. If there is a better everyday analogy for the theory than the theater, I would like to hear it.
