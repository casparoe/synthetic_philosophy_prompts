I've been reading Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae (Prima Secundae, question 108, article 1), where he considers what the New Law of the Gospel prescribes and forbids with respect to external acts. The following excerpt from that article has left me genuinely puzzled:

There are some which are necessarily in keeping with, or in opposition to inward grace consisting in faith that worketh through love. Such external works are prescribed or forbidden in the New Law; thus confession of faith is prescribed, and denial of faith is forbidden. On the other hand, there are works which are not necessarily opposed to, or in keeping with faith that worketh through love. Such works are not prescribed or forbidden in the New Law, by virtue of its primitive institution; but have been left by the Lawgiver, i.e. Christ, to the discretion of each individual. And so to each one it is free to decide what he should do or avoid; and to each superior, to direct his subjects in such matters as regards what they must do or avoid. Wherefore also in this respect the Gospel is called the "law of liberty": since the Old Law decided many points and left few to man to decide as he chose.

In the same article, replying to the second objection, he elaborates:

Accordingly the New Law is called the law of liberty in two respects. First, because it does not bind us to do or avoid certain things, except such as are of themselves necessary or opposed to salvation, and come under the prescription or prohibition of the law. Secondly, because it also makes us comply freely with these precepts and prohibitions, inasmuch as we do so through the promptings of grace.

I would appreciate help on three fronts.

First, a plain-language reconstruction: what exactly is the twofold connection Aquinas draws between external acts and grace, what does it mean for an act to be "necessarily" in keeping with or opposed to faith working through love, and why does he think the non-necessary cases are properly left to individual discretion and, in some settings, to a superior's direction?

Second, and this is where I most need your judgment, I want to know how this passage bears on the concept of supererogation. The standard genealogy of that concept runs through precisely this scholastic apparatus of precepts and counsels: the counsels of poverty, chastity, and obedience are recommended but not commanded, and they are the paradigm of acts that are praiseworthy to perform yet not blameworthy to omit. But when I read the excerpt closely, it seems that the acts Aquinas describes as "left to the discretion of each individual" are works that are not necessarily in keeping with charity — that is, works love of God does not itself demand. That sounds less like a sphere of heroic, especially meritorious conduct and more like a sphere of morally permissible variation, where equally acceptable ways of life diverge. On the first reading, the passage supplies supererogation; on the second, it supplies something importantly different. Which reading does the text better support, and why does the difference matter for someone interested in whether supererogation is a genuine moral category?

Third, some contemporary philosophers argue that supererogation is unstable: if an act is truly better than its omission, then omitting it must be criticizable in some way, however mildly; and if the omission is beyond criticism, the act cannot really be better. Does Aquinas's framework, as represented here, escape this dilemma? Notice that his discretionary zone is defined relative to what is necessary for salvation, and his law of liberty has two distinct grounds — the small number of binding precepts, and the grace-infused freedom with which one complies. Consider whether either ground could make sense of acts that are better, though not obligatory, without the alternative being criticizable; and whether a secular analogue of his position is even possible, given that his discretionary zone appears to be carved out by a legislator's decision about what to command, something secular moral theories arguably lack. I am not looking for references or further reading — just your best reasoning about the passage and the questions it raises.
