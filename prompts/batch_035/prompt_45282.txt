I co-direct a working group of four historians and four philosophers, meeting monthly over two years, on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history. The motivating tangle: historians routinely explain an event by citing a trigger — say, the assassination at Sarajevo — against a background of standing conditions, several of which look individually sufficient for some version of the outcome (alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, imperial rivalry). It is unclear when, if ever, the trigger does genuine explanatory work rather than merely fixing the timing and shape of what the background would have produced anyway; and it is equally unclear whether the philosopher's notion of overdetermination, built on toy cases with two bullets, transfers to situations with dozens of partial, reinforcing, jointly-sufficient background causes. Which open questions in this area should our group prioritize, and in what order? I care more about the reasoning than the list: for each question, explain what makes it upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for a mixed group whose comparative advantage is worked historical cases rather than formal causal machinery, and what two years of monthly meetings could realistically settle versus what is generational. Also flag the questions you would deprioritize — including ones popular in the literature — and say why their answers would not change much downstream, whether in how historians should argue or in how philosophers should model historical causation.
