I am a novelist — sixty-one, with a modest, respectable career behind me — and I have stumbled into a situation that seems to me less a legal matter than a philosophical one, though I intend to pursue it as both, and I would like help seeing its structure clearly.

In 1993 I finished my first novel. It was rejected by fifteen houses. The last rejection was the one that stung, because it came from the publisher of Isabel Corvin, the writer I then revered above all others; my then-agent let slip that Corvin herself had read the manuscript and admired it before the house passed. I put the novel in a drawer and wrote other books. Corvin died in 2018. This spring her estate announced a lost novel, found among her papers, supposedly written in the rented Devon farmhouse in the mid-nineties. It was published to great acclaim — reviewed as the finest thing she ever did.

It is my book. Perhaps one sentence in ten has been touched; the rest is word for word what I wrote. I have the dated drafts and the rejection letters. But here is what keeps me circling, and why a lawyer does not seem to be the whole answer. The words are the words; they are on the page and always were; they moved no one in 1993, and they are moving thousands of people now. The only substantial change Corvin made was to add a final paragraph to an ending I had deliberately left unresolved, and it is that paragraph the reviewers single out as the best thing in the book — the one part that is not mine. So the readers seem to have been given something true (every word was truly written, by someone, thirty years ago) and something false (that the someone was the woman whose grief they are reading into it), and I cannot decide whether the false part touches the book at all, or only the occasion of reading it. What I want to know is this. If I prove authorship, what exactly will I have established was stolen — the text was never taken, it sits in my attic — and what exactly will the readers have been deceived about? Is the novel they love the words, or the event of the words being written, and if the event, whose event is it, mine or hers, given that the passage they love best is hers? I have been invited to speak about the late Corvin at a centenary conference in the spring, and I have until then to decide whether to stand up, and, more troublingly, what to say the wrong consists in — since it is not obvious that the right description is that she stole my book, and it is not obvious that the right description is that everyone is deceiving themselves.
