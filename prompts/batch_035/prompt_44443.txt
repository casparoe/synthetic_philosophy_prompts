I am a novelist — twenty years of writing fiction, mostly long novels told in close third person — and my training has left me with an occupational habit: when a character "takes on a life of their own," refusing a plot turn I had planned, or when readers write to me about a minor figure as though that person went on existing after the last page, I nod along, because this is simply what the work feels like from the inside. My cousin, who did a philosophy degree, has lately been pressing on this. He tells me the philosophy of mind has vindicated our craft: on the account he favors — Daniel Dennett's, as he presents it — the self is a "center of narrative gravity," a fictional character the brain spins out of its own storytelling, real in something like the way a novel's protagonist is real: an abstraction we posit because it organizes the data, not a thing sitting in an inner theater. My cousin then draws two conclusions. First, that the fear of death rests on a category mistake: a character is not harmed when the novel ends, and Doyle's grudging later revival of Holmes rescued no one from anything; so a self's ceasing should trouble us about as much. Second, that anxieties about authenticity — "am I living my real life, or a life I merely narrate?" — are confused in the way it would be confused to ask whether the deerstalker is Holmes's "true" hat: there is no fact of the character over and above the telling, so there is no fact for the anxiety to be about.

Here is my difficulty: I know fictional characters from the inside of the craft, and they do not seem to me to be self-like in the ways the argument needs. A character is all surface and no interior — I invent what passes for their depth, but there is nothing it is like to be them, whereas there is something it is like to be me, drudging through a Tuesday. Characters are finished or abandoned at my discretion, read or unread, quietly retconned in later editions, and their inconsistencies across a long series are forgiven by readers in a way a person's are not. Yet I can also see real points of contact, which is what unsettles me: the distinction I live by between what a character does and how it is narrated (Holmes versus Watson's narration of him) suggests a self-versus-narration divide too; the "unreliable narrator" device gives me a teller who cannot be trusted to report the very person he is; and my own experience of a character "resisting" me is, I confess, epistemically indistinguishable from talking to myself about myself. So please audit the analogy properly. Lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the author, the text, the reader, the canon, the drafts, the ending — then say where the mapping breaks, and, crucially, separate the disanalogies that matter for my cousin's two conclusions from those that do not (that characters are less detailed than people seems irrelevant; that they lack a point of view may not). Tell me whether either conclusion — the one about death, the one about authenticity — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way. And if the novelist's toolkit contains a better model for the self than the character — the narrator, perhaps, or the act of reading rather than the thing read — I would like to know which it is, and why.
