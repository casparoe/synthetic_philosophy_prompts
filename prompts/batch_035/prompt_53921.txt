Quick disclaimer up front: I'm a philosopher by trade (ancient, mostly Aristotle's ethics and some Presocratics), but the free will / moral responsibility literature is not my patch at all, and its terminology defeats me, so bear with me if this has some obvious name I've just never run into. Here's the thing I keep noticing, in myself and in others. When someone wrongs me, I resent them — fine, normal. But then at some point I learn the details of how they came to be the way they are, and the resentment just evaporates, without any decision on my part. And the odd part is that the new information doesn't function as an excuse. Nobody, me included, would say "given that background, anyone would have done it." The wrong is still a wrong, the harm still happened, and I still think they shouldn't have done it. What changes is that the act stops looking like something aimed at me and starts looking like the output of a mechanism — as if I'd been injured by weather. Even odder: this works completly piecemeal. My abstract belief that everyone's actions have causes is decades old and never deflated anyone's resentment by an inch; but a specific backstory deflates resentment at one particular person while my identical resentment at a second person, whose story I never learned, is untouched. Same me, same theory, very different attitudes.

Three instances so you get the shape. A colleague in another department who was reliably vicious in committee meetings — the kind of person I'd cross the hallway to avoid. Then at a dinner someone mentioned in passing that he'd spent his thirties caring for a dying parent while holding a full teaching load. Being awful in meetings isn't grief, so this was not an excusing fact, but the dread and the resentment were simply gone afterwards, replaced by something closer to pity. Second: an old friend who cut me off during a bad stretch years ago, a grievance I'd been carrying like luggage; a mutual friend filled me in on what her life looked like at the time, and the grievance dissolved — retroactively, somehow, as if it had attached to a different person. Third, and this is the one that really bothers me: with a relative, the effect wears off. If I go a while without thinking about her backstory, the resentment creeps back, and I have to consciously re-tell myself the story to deflate it again, like pumping up a tyre. That one makes me doubt the deflation is any kind of discovery about her; it looks more like something the narrative does to me.

I've tried the obvious nearby shelves and none of them seem to be it. It's not what the lawyers call diminished responsibility, because that requires the cause to be of an excusing kind (illness, coercion) and to lower culpability, whereas my judgement of culpability doesn't obviously change at all. It's not the free will vs. determinism debate, because my theoretical commitments were already in place and did nothing — it's specifically the detail that does the work. It's maybe somewhere near moral luck, but I can't see how, since nothing about her or her act changed, only my information. So: is there an established concept for this piecemeal, backstory-triggered deflation of resentment? I don't want a bare label — I want to know what the detail is actually doing. Is the deflation a rational update (I've learned she was never the right target for resentment) or a psychological seduction, where a sad story mimics the cues of an excuse without being one? If the literature has no exact match, tell me the nearest concepts and how they miss, and then coin a term with a definition and real edges: does it require the backstory to be sad, does it work for third parties (should a juror's blame work this way?), does it survive the story wearing off — and note the mirror case, since my admiration of "self-made" people deflates exactly the same way when I learn about the advantages they started with, so whatever this is, it can't be specific to blame.
