THE EXAM QUESTION

Background facts provided to students:

1. The UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 recognises animals as sentient beings for the purposes of government decision-making, and extends that recognition to decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters, shrimp, prawns) and cephalopod molluscs. This followed an independent review commissioned by the UK government and conducted at the London School of Economics — Birch, Burn, Schnell, Browning and Crump, 2021 — which drew on over 300 scientific studies and concluded that there is strong scientific evidence that decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs are sentient.

2. The Act does not itself regulate farming practices. It establishes an Animal Sentience Committee that reports on whether government policy has had due regard to the welfare of animals as sentient beings, with ministers answerable to Parliament. The enforceable welfare rules that exist in UK law — the welfare duties owed under the Animal Welfare Act 2006 and the welfare-at-slaughter requirements — apply to vertebrates; farmed decapods fall outside them.

3. Farmed shrimp is by far the largest crustacean aquaculture sector. By count of individual animals, recent estimates suggest that more farmed shrimp are killed for food each year than any other farmed animal, exceeding both farmed fish and chickens by a wide margin.

4. Eyestalk ablation — the removal of one or both eyestalks of female broodstock shrimp in hatcheries in order to accelerate ovarian maturation — remains routine worldwide, and no commercial alternative has yet replaced it at scale. Commercial devices for the humane electrical stunning of crabs and lobsters exist, but they are little used in practice.

5. Welfare-at-slaughter rules for farmed fish exist in the EU and Norway in principle, though their stringency and enforcement vary. The once-contested question of whether fish feel pain has been largely resolved: most welfare scientists now accept that they do.

The question:

Given that decapod crustaceans are now recognised as sentient in UK law but receive essentially no welfare protection in farming, should aquaculture welfare regulation be extended to decapods on the same model as farmed fish? Or does residual scientific uncertainty about crustacean experience justify a different, weaker, provisional regime? Defend your answer, making your use of the precautionary principle explicit and addressing at least one concrete practice (for example, eyestalk ablation or slaughter methods).

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

The question is whether decapod welfare in aquaculture should be regulated on the same model as farmed fish, or whether residual scientific uncertainty justifies a weaker regime. I will argue for the first option, and in fact for a stronger claim: the law already points that way.

First, the scale. Farmed shrimp is by far the largest crustacean farming sector, and by individual count shrimp are plausibly the most numerous farmed animals on the planet, yet their welfare receives almost no regulatory attention anywhere. That asymmetry is hard to defend given what we now know.

Second, the science. It is important to distinguish nociception from pain: nociception is the detection of and reflexive response to noxious stimuli, which can occur without any felt experience, whereas pain is a state of feeling. The old controversy about whether fish feel pain was largely resolved in favour of fish pain: fish have nociceptors, they make trade-offs between pain avoidance and other motivations, and they self-administer analgesia when given the chance. The LSE review extended similar standards of evidence to decapods and concluded that there is strong scientific evidence that they are sentient. So the residual uncertainty is small.

Third, the law. The Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 now recognises decapod crustaceans as sentient beings and requires government to have regard to their welfare in policy-making. Given this, comprehensive regulation of decapod aquaculture follows more or less automatically: the legal groundwork is already done, and all that remains is implementation. It would be irrational for the government to recognise these animals as sentient on Monday and decline to protect them on Tuesday.

Fourth, the moral principle. As Tom Regan has argued, we owe equal consideration of interests to every sentient being: where a being can suffer, its suffering counts equally, regardless of species. Since decapods can suffer, their interests in farming contexts must weigh equally with the comparable interests of fish. And because both are aquatic animals, the same protections can be transposed more or less unchanged: stocking-density rules, water-quality standards, and humane-slaughter requirements, with electrical stunning before killing, as devices already available for crabs and lobsters. Where a practice has no fish analogue, as with eyestalk ablation in shrimp hatcheries, the same standard of humane treatment applies by direct extension.

Finally, for any remaining uncertainty, the precautionary principle settles the matter: where the evidence is uncertain, regulate as if the animals were sentient. And since the protection of possibly-sentient beings is a matter of right rather than of cost-benefit balancing, the stringency of the rules cannot be watered down on grounds of expense to industry. On this view there is no room for the weaker provisional regime the question contemplates.
