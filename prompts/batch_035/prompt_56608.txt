Write a dialogue between two decision theorists, Mara and Dev, debating whether the St. Petersburg paradox is dissolved once you notice that the game must be truncated in practice.

Mara's line: the paradox rests on an illegitimate infinite idealization. Since no backer can pay out more than its capital, the game actually on offer is a truncated one, and she should compute the expected value of the standard game when payouts are capped at one trillion dollars (just under 2^40), so that the dialogue contains the actual arithmetic. The result — roughly $40, growing only logarithmically with the backer's capital — should carry her case: the real game demands no enormous entry fee, and our reluctance to pay large ones is thereby vindicated.

Dev's counter: truncation relocates the problem rather than resolving it. The expected value of the truncated game still rises without bound as the cap rises, so for any entry fee one might be reluctant to pay, there is a possible backer for whom expected-value reasoning says one should pay it; the "fair price" is thus hostage to the size of the largest balance sheet in the world, which is a poor foundation for a theory of rational pricing. He should also press Mara on the observational side: people decline entry fees well below the truncated expected value, so something beyond truncation — risk aversion, diminishing marginal utility — is needed, and he can add that plugging in a utility function brings fresh trouble: if utility is unbounded, a payoff table can be rigged to re-create the paradox, while bounding utility avoids it only by buying other anomalies.

Keep both characters technically competent: have them actually do the sums rather than gesture at them, and make sure each responds to the other's specific points rather than talking past them.
