My wife and I are trying to decide whether to have a child, and the conversation has hardened into a philosophical argument of hers that I cannot refute, even though I am confident it should be refutable. We are both in our late thirties, so this is not academic for us, and the immediate trigger was the birth of my brother's son, who has a painful chronic condition that will require surgeries his whole life. I want to be clear at the outset: I am not asking for comfort or for a mediator. I am asking whether her argument is sound.

As best I can reconstruct it, her position rests on four claims. First, if we have a child and its life contains suffering — as every life does — that is bad for the child. Second, if we have a child and its life contains happiness, that is good for the child. Third, if we do not have a child, the absence of any suffering is a good thing, even though there is no one around to enjoy that good; she says the relief we would feel is on behalf of the child who never exists, the way one is relieved on behalf of a friend who narrowly missed a plane that crashed. Fourth, the absence of happiness, if we do not have a child, is not bad at all, because there is nobody who is thereby deprived. From these she concludes that not creating a child can be better than creating one, but never worse — and therefore that having a child cannot be justified for the child's own sake, only for ours. She grants that if we both deeply want one, that is a reason, but she finds it decisive that the child can be wronged by being brought into a life with suffering, and cannot be wronged by never being brought into anything at all.

I have three objections in various states of disrepair, and I would like to know how good they are.

My first is about grief. A friend of ours miscarried at ten weeks, and both of them grieved not just their own loss but the life their child would have had. If my wife's fourth claim is right, that grief was misplaced in a strong sense: there was nothing bad about the happiness that child will never have. But we do not treat such grief as a confusion, the way we would treat someone who grieves the non-existence of every possible child. She replies that the miscarried child at least existed, even if briefly, so the two cases come apart; I am not sure that difference can carry the weight she puts on it.

My second objection is that her argument proves too much. Suppose a drug existed that, taken before conception, guaranteed a child a life of extraordinary happiness with only the mildest suffering. On her four claims, there would be no reason at all to take it — not even a weak one — because the absence of that happiness deprives no one. She accepts this without flinching, which I find alarming. But I notice that I myself accept something like the reverse when it comes to suffering: if a drug guaranteed a life of agony, I would think there was a real reason not to take it, and not merely for our sake. So my own intuitions appear to be asymmetric in the same direction as hers, and I do not know what to do about that.

My third objection is an attempted diagnosis. I suggested that her third and fourth claims quietly change the meaning of good and bad: in the first two claims, good and bad are good or bad for someone, while in the third, absence of suffering is good in some impersonal way, and in the fourth, absence of happiness is not even impersonally bad. She turned this around and said that if I grant that the absence of suffering is impersonally good, then her conclusion follows, and if I deny it, then I owe her an explanation of why we feel relief in the crashed-plane case that is not merely self-interested.

What I would like from you is a careful adjudication rather than a verdict on the marriage. Are her four claims jointly consistent, and do they entail what she says they entail? Do any of my three objections succeed, and if so, at which claim do they strike? If the asymmetry must be rejected, which of its four legs should go, and what happens to the intuitions — like the crashed-plane relief and the feeling that there is no obligation to have children — that made it attractive? If it can be preserved, does it really support not having a child, or only something weaker about not owing existence to anyone? I would also like to know whether my second objection, if pressed, forces a choice between her view and the idea that a guaranteed-happy child would be a reason to procreate, and which side of that choice is better defensible.
