So I've got to write a proposal for my MA dissertation (roughly 12,000 words, one semester) and what I have so far is honestly a mess of half-thoughts, so I'm hoping you can turn it into something that reads like an actual research plan. The topic is moral foundations theory and what, if anything, it can tell us about whether the left or the right (or neither) gets morality right.

The background, as I understand it: MFT says people's moral judgements draw on a handful of evolved foundations -- care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation (plus liberty/oppression as a later addition). The empirical claim, going back to Haidt's survey work, is roughly that people on the political left activate mostly the care and fairness foundations while people on the right spread their judgements across all the foundations. And then everyone immediately jumps from this psychology to a normative punchline. My lefty friends basically use it as a debunking argument: look, right-wing morality is downstream of disgust responses and tribal instincts, it's arational sentiment dressed up as principle. Meanwhile Haidt himself leans the other way, suggesting the left is morally colour-blind, deleting half the moral palate, and that a wiser morality would use the full set.

My half-formed idea is that both of these moves are way too fast, and for the same underlying reason. A psychological explanation of why someone holds a belief only debunks that belief if the causal process producing the belief was insensitive to the relevant truth -- otherwise you're just explaining how someone came to believe something true. So the interesting question isn't "what foundations does each side use" (that's the empirical part, and I'll just take the psychology as given for argument's sake), it's whether there's any asymmetry in whether the foundations are truth-conducive. And that question might have a boring answer: maybe every debunking argument in this space is symmetric and they all cancel out, leaving MFT normatively silent. Or maybe there's an asymmetry, but a fragile one that only survives certain assumptions about what moral truths are even like. That boring-or-maybe-not answer, and the conditions under which it's one rather than the other, is what I want the dissertation to nail down.

What I want from you, in the proposal document: state the research question precisely (my supervisor will grill me if it's vague); explain why the standard left-debunks-right and right-completes-left positions both leave it open -- I suspect they smuggle in assumptions about truth-conduciveness that they never defend; lay out the method, which I think has to be mostly conceptual work on what makes an aetiology of a moral judgement truth-tracking versus not, using the empirical psych at arm's length rather than running any studies; say what a successful outcome would look like and, importantly, what would count as the project failing; and be honest about whether this is too big for one semester and how you'd cut it down to fit (I'm guessing the liberty/oppression foundation and the metaethics of what "moral truth" even is are the first things overboard).
