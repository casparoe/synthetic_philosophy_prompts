So I'm a 2L and for our legislation seminar we had to write up a personal statement of interpretive method — like, what do I actually think a judge is doing when she construes a statute. Mine came back with the comment that it "contains a lot of moving parts that may not all be pointed in the same direction," which, thanks, very helpful. Here are the parts. (1) The meaning of a statute is the ordinary public meaning of its words to a competent reader at the time of enactment — meaning is fixed when it's enacted and doesn't drift with later usage. (2) I'm basically on board that "legislative intent" is a fiction: Congress is thousands of people with wildly inconsistent goals, and there's no fact of the matter about what it intended, over and above what the enacted text communicates. (3) When the text is genuinely ambiguous, I'm fine with judges turning to statutory purpose, and I like the purposive canons — construe exceptions narrowly, avoid readings that raise constitutional problems, don't interpret a statute to intrude on the states unless it says so clearly. Those all seem like common sense to me. (4) I also think a judge can refuse to follow text that produces an absurd result, because pure literalism eventually turns into a parody of itself. (5) The core reason I self-describe as a textualist rather than a purposivist is that I hate judicial discretion — interpretation should be rule-like and predictable enough that two judges with opposite politics usually land in the same place. And (6) for what it's worth, I think Holy Trinity was wrongly decided — the Court looked at a statute barring the importation of foreign laborers under contract, decided Congress obviously didn't mean ministers, and just rewrote the reach of the act. That's the judge rescuing Congress from its own drafting, and I don't think that's the job.
