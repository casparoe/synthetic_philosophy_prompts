A colleague in my psychology department, a committed Bayesian, has an argument that she takes the replication crisis to make decisive against significance testing. It runs as follows.

(1) Under a point null, run a two-sided test at the .05 level and keep sampling until the result is significant; as the maximum sample size allowed grows, the probability of eventually obtaining p < .05 tends to one, because the test statistic wanders without settling, and under the null its first crossing of the significance boundary points each way about half the time.

(2) A method whose output can be produced at will, with the null true, by nothing more than patience is not extracting evidence from data.

(3) The p-hacking behind the replication crisis — peeking at the data, stopping when a result suits, trying further outcome measures until one works — just is this procedure carried out informally; so the crisis is not a failure of scientific character but the test doing, under patience, exactly what it was always going to do.

(4) A likelihood-ratio or Bayes-factor analysis of the same observations is untouched by the stopping rule: the same data support the same inference whether or not the researcher meant to stop. The near-certain significance under optional stopping and the .05 guarantee under a fixed design are therefore both properties of procedures, not of data.

(5) Since a procedure can be arranged under which significance arrives almost regardless of whether the hypothesis is true, procedure-level guarantees cannot be evidential properties at all.

(6) So the published literature is the output of a machine that tracks patience rather than truth, and the remedy is to replace tests with likelihoods.

The argument seems to me to prove too much. Pre-registration, power analysis and nearly every reform the replication movement actually proposes are themselves procedure-level guarantees, so if (5) is right they measure nothing either. And if (2) is right I cannot see why her Bayes factors escape: their good behaviour is likewise a fact about procedures, not about any particular data set, yet she insists the likelihood remedy is untouched. Identify the premise or step that should be rejected, explain why it nevertheless looked unassailable, and set out what rejecting it costs — including the price on her side, if the fault lies there. Please answer in British English.
