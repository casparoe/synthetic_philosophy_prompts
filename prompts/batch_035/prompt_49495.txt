I am a retired evolutionary biologist — a behavioral ecologist by training, most of my career spent on contest behavior in animals — and in the past few years I have been reading my way into philosophy. I have drafted the outline and core argument of an essay, and before I attempt a full write-up I would be grateful for comments from someone versed in the philosophical literature. The essay concerns David Lewis's account of convention and whether evolutionary biology vindicates, revises, or quietly destroys it.

My thesis is this: Lewis analyzed a convention as an arbitrary solution to a coordination problem, sustained by common knowledge of a system of reciprocal expectations, and he took rational agents as his subjects. I want to argue that a surprising amount of his analysis survives translation into a population with no rational agents at all. The centerpiece is the old work by Maynard Smith and Parker on asymmetric contests: in the hawk-dove game, if there is an uncorrelated asymmetry between contestants — most famously the difference between the current owner of a resource and a latecomer, which confers no advantage in fighting ability — selection can stabilize a "bourgeois" strategy of respecting possession. Either convention (owner keeps, or finder yields) would do as well; which one wins is a matter of historical accident. That, I want to say, is Lewisian arbitrariness without a single believer in the population. From there I generalize: what Lewis secures through nested expectations — I expect you to conform, expect you to expect me to conform, and so on — natural selection secures through feedback, since a mutant deviating from the local convention is simply outcompeted. And what Aumann calls a correlated equilibrium, with the asymmetry serving as a correlating signal, looks to me like the right game-theoretic home for Lewis's examples: the signal is public enough, and no one gains by ignoring it. I close with a bolder claim: that the normativity philosophers insist is missing from this picture — the sense in which a convention gives us a *reason*, even an *obligation*, to conform — is a later cultural addition laid over a machinery that ran for eons without it, and is no more essential to convention than paint is to an engine.

I am aware, from conversations and reading, that Brian Skyrms has treated Lewis's convention explicitly with evolutionary game theory, and I do not want to reinvent his book. So my specific requests are these. First, accuracy: are there errors or anachronisms in the way I deploy the Lewisian apparatus — especially in my paraphrase of the common knowledge condition and the arbitrariness condition? Second, and most important to me: does the uncorrelated asymmetry material actually track Lewis's arbitrariness? In the biological cases the asymmetry is sometimes genuinely uncorrelated with payoffs, but sometimes it is correlated, and I worry that I have been coasting on the clean examples. Where does the analogy break? Third, is my replacement of recursive common knowledge by selective feedback philosophically respectable, or does it change the subject — would Lewis be entitled to say that whatever populations of animals have, it is not convention but something convention presupposes? Fourth, on normativity: I have read that Margaret Gilbert presses something like this objection to Lewis himself, and I am inclined to dismiss it as anthropocentric squeamishness, but I suspect a philosopher will tell me I am being too quick, and I would genuinely like to know whether my dismissal is defensible or merely a biologist's habit of treating "it is selected for" as the end of explanation. Finally, tell me plainly whether there is a distinct contribution left in my essay once Skyrms is taken into account, and if so, where I should dig in to make it. I would rather learn now that the paper is a footnote than discover it after submission.
