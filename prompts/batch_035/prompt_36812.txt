Bit of background: I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, about to commit to a dissertation topic, and I've gotten weirdly attached to the utility monster — Nozick's point that utilitarianism doesn't just permit sacrificing everyone for the one being whose utility scales off the charts, it positively demands it. The thing I keep running into is that almost every interesting question about the monster isn't about the monster at all. It's about upstream stuff in aggregation, and my department has nobody working on that, so I'm basically going to be self-directed for three years. My background is decent on formal social choice (I took the econ sequence, comfortable with Arrow/Sen-style stuff), weaker on philosophy of mind.

So here's my actual question: which of the candidate upstream questions should I work on first, and why? The way I see it the options are roughly: (a) whether interpersonal utility comparisons are even coherent, and what they could consist of — because if they're not, the claim that one being's utility outweighs everyone else's might not be a well-formed thought, and the monster debate quietly dissolves; (b) whether the bite depends on additive aggregation specifically, or whether every aggregative view — prioritarian, egalitarian, the lumping vs. splitting debates in population ethics — gets its own monster variant; (c) whether the objection is ultimately just an intuition pump about concentrated benefit, and whether it survives the move to person-affecting views; and (d) a newer angle my advisor mentioned in passing — whether the whole issue mutates when the candidate monster isn't some biological creature but an AI system claiming astronomical utility that we can't verify, where being wrong in either direction is catastrophic. A friend in econ thinks the entire puzzle is an artifact of treating utility as cardinally measurable and says I should drop it, which honestly stung a bit.

I don't want a survey, I want a defended ordering. Which of these is upstream of which? Which are actually tractable for someone with three years and my skillset versus fashionable dead ends? And what would move things up or down — e.g., if someone produced a convincing account of interpersonal comparisons tomorrow, does the rest of the literature become pointless, or more urgent? I care more about the reasoning than the list itself, since I'll be stuck with whatever I pick for a good chunk of my late twenties.
