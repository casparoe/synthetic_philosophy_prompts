Write a dialogue between two members of a hospital's clinical ethics committee: a committed luck egalitarian, shaped by the Dworkin–Roemer tradition of distinguishing brute luck from option luck, and a committed relational egalitarian, shaped by Elizabeth Anderson's critique that luck egalitarianism humiliates the unfortunate and mistakes the point of equality. The committee has been asked to recommend whether the hospital should adopt responsibility-sensitive triage for elective procedures — that is, whether patients whose conditions are partly attributable to their own choices should be deprioritized. The two of them meet before the formal session to try to settle their own disagreement first.

The dialogue should meet the following requirements:

1. Both interlocutors must be equally competent and argue in good faith. Neither should cave to the other's rhetorical pressure; neither should be given weak or obviously confused arguments.

2. There must be real back-and-forth. Each must respond to the other's strongest point, not merely to a weakened version of it — for instance, when the relational egalitarian objects that responsibility-tracking in medicine expresses contempt and creates hierarchies of deservingness, the luck egalitarian should engage with that objection at its strongest, perhaps by distinguishing between denying aid and asking for a contribution, or by denying that queue position is a judgment of character.

3. The dialogue must work through at least three concrete cases on which their verdicts diverge, drawn from something like the following: a longtime smoker needing a lung resection who has tried and failed to quit several times; a rock climber with a repeat climbing injury whose first surgery was entirely covered; and a patient whose untreated condition reflects a combination of a genetic predisposition, poverty, and long working hours rather than any discrete choice. The addiction case in particular should be used to stress-test the brute/option luck distinction.

4. Include at least one moment where a character's own theory delivers a verdict she is reluctant to accept — for example, the luck egalitarian confronting the harshness objection (that her view abandons the imprudent to the consequences of their choices), or the relational egalitarian conceding that she has difficulty telling the committee anything determinate about the climbing case, where equal standing alone seems not to settle who is next in line.

5. The characters may name real philosophers and characterize their general positions, but nothing should appear in quotation marks as a citation from a real text; paraphrase freely.

6. The ending should be honest: either they converge on a specific recommendation (in which case the dialogue should make explicit what each had to give up to get there), or they reach a genuine stalemate, in which case the dialogue should state precisely which moral premise is doing the work and why it cannot be resolved by the committee's evidence. Do not manufacture consensus, and do not have them simply agree to disagree without identifying the exact point of divergence.
