I'm a philosopher (mostly philosophy of physics, with an unfortunate side habit of reading Indian philosophy badly) and I've got a workshop paper due in March on Schrödinger's lifelong Advaita commitments and whether they did any actual work in his physics. I've drafted the central section and I'd like your comments before I embarass myself in front of the physicists and the Indologists simultaneously.

Quick context: Schrödinger spent decades reading the Upanishads, starting well before quantum mechanics was even a going concern (blame Schopenhauer, as usual), and in "My View of the World" he's fairly explicit that he took the identity of Atman and Brahman seriously -- not as poetry but as, in his own way of putting it, the most reasonable hypothesis about the nature of consciousness. He also has this move he calls the "arithmetical paradox": there appear to be many minds in the world, but consciousness itself is one, and he thinks Vedanta is the only tradition that takes that seriously without flinching.

OK here's the draft section:

"Let me suggest that the measurement problem as Schrödinger saw it in 1935 was not primarily a problem about physical systems but a problem about observers. If consciousness is singular, and the physical formalism only ever assigns states relative to a cut between observer and observed, then each new experimental arrangement literally creates a new subject-object split, a new maya. The cat is not an objection to indeterminacy; it is an objection to the assumption that the observer's role can be discharged by a Geiger counter. Something must unify the definite outcomes in experience, and for Schrödinger that something cannot be a further physical object. His famous discomfort with Copenhagen is thus the discomfort of a Vedantin being told that the one thing that is not maya -- consciousness itself -- enters the theory only as a classical boundary condition, i.e., as though it were one more object in the world."

Specifically I want comments on three or four things:

1. Am I being fair to him historically? My worry is that he never actually held the view that consciousness collapses the wavefunction -- that seems to be Wigner's later hobbyhorse, not his -- but my paragraph reads like he's one step from it. If the answer is "he kept the metaphysics and the physics in separate drawers," that guts the paper, so tell me now.

2. The timing problem, which I suspect is fatal but can't quite work out. He'd absorbed the Upanishads long before 1926, so when he later got uncomfortable with the measurement situation, was the Vedanta actually shaping the discomfort, or was it just the vocabulary he reached for afterwards to express a discomfort he had for boring ordinary reasons (realism about the wavefunction, distaste for a special role for measurement)? Those come apart and I think my draft conflates them.

3. The last sentence of the draft ("one more object in the world") feels like it's doing something sneaky. Would an Advaita-friendly reader actually accept my claim that consciousness "cannot be a further physical object," or is there a reading on which that's too quick -- e.g., one where the whole subject/object vocabulary is what's supposed to be generated at the level of maya, so that demanding consciousness be kept off the object side already concedes too much?

4. Is the maya framing earning its keep or is it decoration? Be honest. One of the organizers is exactly the kind of person who counts Sanskrit terms in the abstract and holds it against you.

Be as brutal as you like, I have time to rewrite. Also, if I've garbled what's in "My View of the World" versus the Mind and Matter lectures versus the cat paper itself, please flag it -- I've been citing loosely from memory and the page numbers in my edition are a disaster.
