Function talk is something I got opinionated about through old LessWrong threads on ev-psych — the idea that hearts have the function of pumping blood *because* pumping is what hearts were selected for, not just because pumping is what they happen to do in a system, always seemed obviously right to me. That's the etiological account, and I've basically treated the causal-role people (Cummins-style, where a function is just whatever a part contributes to some capacity we've chosen to analyze) as changing the subject to systems biology rather than answering the question. But I've been trying to apply the whole "write down what would change your mind" discipline to my beliefs, and for this one I honestly struggle to find much, which is either evidence the view is right or evidence I've insulated it. Some things that nag at me: if we ever build a genuinely de novo organism from synthesized parts with no evolutionary history at all, and it still makes sense to say one of its parts is *for* something and can malfunction, does the etiologist just bite the bullet that artifacts-by-design get their functions from design intent, and does that smuggle too much in? And the newly-coopted-trait problem — a trait that drifts into a beneficial new role, how many generations of selection before that becomes its *function*, and is the vagueness there a load-bearing feature or a bug? So, what would actually count against the etiological view of function? Give me exactly five defeaters, ranked from most to least fatal, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion. For each one say whether a commited etiologist would concede it genuinely counts against them or would dissmiss it as a misunderstanding, and be honest about whether the view has been patched with enough epicycles at this point that nothing short of a formal collapse into the causal-role view could touch it — and whether that insulation is a problem for a position of this kind or just what metaphysics looks like when its done.
