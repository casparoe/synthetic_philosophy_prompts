for the last session of my phil of language seminar on lewis on convention i want to close with a poem, and i'd like the form to do some of the argumentative work. write a sestina where the six repeating end-words themselves are the convention: chosen arbitrarily (no reason for that word rather than another) but made binding by nothing but repetition and everyone's expectations. so the poem enacts the idea that a convention is an arbitrary solution to a coordination problem that gets locked in by common knowledge — everyone conforms, expects everyone else to conform, prefers to conform given the others do, and everyone knows this all the way down. maybe set it with drivers on a road who have to settle on a side, or with the first speakers fixing which sound means water, your call. the envoi should circle back to the first line in a way that suggests the arbitrariness is now invisible — nobody remembers there was ever a choice to be made. someone who knows lewis should recognize the account (arbitrariness, coordination, reciprocal expectations, contrast with explicit agreement) without it being name-dropped, and someone who doesnt should still get a good poem out of it. bonus if you can gesture at why conventions are stable even tho nobody enforces them, just by how the end-words behave by the last stanza.
