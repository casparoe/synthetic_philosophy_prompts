I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of language that this semester has been circling the semantics of slurs, and I've noticed something that the rest of the class seems fine with: the debate appears to have completely stalled, and I'm starting to suspect the stall isn't really about slurs at all. Maybe some background: as far as I can tell the main positions on offer are (a) that the derogation is part of the literal, truth-conditional content — using a slur literally says something like "this person is contemptible because of their group," so "X is a [slur]" is false when X isn't contemptible; (b) that what's said stays descriptive and neutral, but the slur conventionally signals a contemptuous attitude the way "therefore" signals inference — a layer attached to the word but not part of what's truth-apt; (c) that slurs don't really describe or signal anything but express contempt, more like booing; and (d) that it's all more context-sensitive, with the offense arising from pragmatic reasoning about what the speaker is doing. Everyone in the seminar agrees on the raw data, which is what gets me: embedded slurs (under negation, in conditionals, inside attitude reports) still can't be uttered in a job interview even by someone reporting them neutrally, quoted or mentioned uses are weird in ways other quoted words aren't, and in-group reappropriated uses seem to flip the valence without changing the word. Yet nobody has moved an inch in weeks, and each side seems to treat the same data as vindication.

My working hypothesis — and I want to stress it's just a hypothesis — is that this is partly a disguised disagreement about where the semantics/pragmatics border sits and what "what is said" is *for*, which we'd have to settle anyway, and partly two groups answering different questions under one heading: one group is doing linguistics (what does the word contribute to compositional meaning), the other is doing something closer to normative pragmatics (what does a person do to their hearer and their social world in uttering it). If that's right, the stall is structural and not just hard. But I might be way off, and there's a nagging counterpoint: the people in the debate don't *seem* to be talking past each other; they directly attack each other's accounts with the same cases, which is what you'd expect from a genuine substantive dispute.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, substantive-but-hard, or two research programs with different success criteria? Please diagnose it using what the sides actually claim (as I've sketched them), tell me what, if anything, would count as real progress given your diagnosis — a new kind of case, a distinction that needs drawing, a decision to split the question, or nothing and why — and along the way say how confident you are in each step of the diagnosis and flag the single point you'd be least sure about. I'd rather hear "this part is a guess" than get a confident rundown that papered over exactly the thing I'm confused about.
