I co-organize a small interdisciplinary working group -- mix of comparativists, a legal scholar, two political theorists, me -- that's been meeting monthly for almost two years, and our two most senior members have been politely circling the same fight the whole time. One, a comparativist, thinks democratic backsliding is a real and identifiable syndrome: democracies now die slowly and legally, through elected incumbents capturing referees, tilting the media environment, and stacking the playing field, rather than through tanks in the street, and Hungary after 2010 is her paradigm case, with poland and Turkey close behind. The other, a theorist, is basically a skeptic about the concept: he thinks "backsliding" smuggles in a telos, gets applied selectively by whichever side happens to be losing, and that a lot of what gets labeled backsliding -- court fights, constitutional hardball, the escalating US cases -- is just ordinary adversarial politics that partisans redescribe as decay when it goes against them. After our last session they finally agreed to try a genuine adversarial collaboration, and they asked me to draft the joint statement, which I now actually have to do, so I'm outsourcing the hard part.

Write the document. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign -- about what factually happened in Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, about the difference between incumbents bending rules and abolishing them, about what the American cases do and don't share with those, and at least one methodological claim (e.g., what democracy indices built from expert judgments can and can't establish); (2) the precise disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording -- in particular I want the observer-dependence issue stated in a form the comparativist can sign ("the concept is elastic in use" rather than a flat "there's no fact of the matter," or however you think these two would actually land it); (3) the cruxes -- my hunch is one is whether there's a stable mid-point between ordinary polarization and regime change, as opposed to backsliding always being transitional toward something; another is whether the *sequence* of moves matters or only the equilibrium you end up at; and there's something about whether indices are measuring the phenomenon or measuring the consensus of a particular class of observers -- but add any crux I'm missing; and (4) for each crux, a test, case, or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. They've both bought into the idea that a good joint statement records falsifiable predictions, so make them concrete enough that in five years the group could actually check who called it.

A few constraints. Nobody gets to win: if a section has one side conceding the other's framework, you've gone wrong -- the comparativist should come out with a real research program intact and the skeptic with a real objection intact, and no hedged mush that neither would object to because it says nothing. Keep the Hungary section from swallowing the document -- oddly they fight about the US more, so give the American cases real attention (think escalation dynamics around courts and election administration, not January 6 alone). Long is fine; this is meant to be the working group's main deliverable. And at the very end, flag for me which residual disagreements you think are verbal rather than substantive -- I suspect at least one is, but I genuinely can't tell which, and I'd rather know before we all sign our names to it.
