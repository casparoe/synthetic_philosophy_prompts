A guy on my team keeps arguing that the whole process-supervision vs outcome-supervision thing in LLM training is fake, and I can't find the hole in it. His argument, as he lays it out: (1) Supervising a model just means giving it a training signal derived from some evaluation. (2) Outcome supervision evaluates the final artifact — did the math answer come out right, does the code pass the tests. Process supervision evaluates intermediate steps — a grader, human or model, judges each step of the reasoning on its own. (3) But "final" and "intermediate" are relative to where you draw the boundary: a whole solution is itself one step in the larger trajectory of training runs, and each "step" is the outcome of the prefix that produced it. (4) So the two regimes differ only in where you place the evaluation boundary and how often you fire it, and boundary placement and frequency are engineering choices, basically hyperparameters. (5) Therefore there is no deep process/outcome distinction, only sparse vs dense reward on the same underlying quantity, and all the talk about "supervising the reasoning itself" is vibes. This proves way too much for me. It would mean grading a student's proof line by line is the same pedagogical thing as grading only the final exam, just at finer grain. It would mean theres no interesting difference between scoring a chess engine per-move for positional soundness and scoring it only per-game for wins — but those clearly come apart in practice. And here's the thing that really bugs me. I know of setups where the per-step reward is itself computed by rolling out from that step all the way to the end and checking whether the problem eventually gets solved. On my colleague's scheme that counts as process supervision, since it fires per step. But the property being evaluated is still just eventual success. Meanwhile a grader judging each step's local correctness — does this follow, is it a legitimate move — is evaluating something eventual success only correlates with, and the two can diverge: a sloppy step that happens to sit on the winning path, a clean step that gets unlucky downstream. So sometimes the same step gets opposite labels depending on which function you use. He waves this off as noise that averaging over rollouts washes out, and says any remaining difference is about label cost, not about what's being supervised. I'm fairly sure the problem is somewhere between (3) and (5) but I can't name it precisely. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost — is he right that it all reduces to granularity, or is there a real distinction hiding in what the evaluation function measures rather than when it fires, and if so where exactly does his argument smuggle that away?
