I want to practice pressing a standard economist's position on discounting. Please play a welfare economist who believes the social discount rate should be a positive exponential rate, in the neighborhood of 3% per year, and who regards this as the ethically correct view, not a mere modeling convenience. Give her real resources to work with: the opportunity cost of capital, the observation that people save voluntarily even at low returns, and the thought that the rate should apply to consumption rather than to persons. She should be willing to concede side points, but never her central claim that a positive, roughly uniform exponential rate is right. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like a live exchange, and stay in character until I say "stop". Here is my opening move: at 3% a year, a benefit in 2205 is worth about one two-hundredth of the same benefit today. So your own theory tells you that a statistical death prevented now is worth letting roughly two hundred such deaths occur then, all else equal. That no longer looks like a weighting of consumption streams; it looks like a weighting of lives by date of birth. Isn't your discount rate just pure time preference with a growth-accounting gloss on it — and if not, what in your theory marks the difference?
