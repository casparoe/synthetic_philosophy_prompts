My flatmate is a stats PhD student and a bit of a troll, and he's got an argument that you should always switch envelopes in the two-envelope setup, before you even look inside. In case you don't know it: there are two envelopes, one holds twice as much money as the other, you picked one at random, and you can keep it or swap for the other. Here's his case:

(1) Since you picked at random, your envelope is exactly as likely to be the bigger one as the smaller one.
(2) Write X for the amount in your envelope. Then the other envelope contains 2X with probability ½ and X/2 with probability ½.
(3) So the expected value of the other envelope is ½(2X) + ½(X/2) = 5X/4, which beats X no matter what X turns out to be.
(4) So switching is strictly better, and you don't even need to open your envelope first.

And then he twists the knife. (5) Once you've swapped, the same argument applies to your new envelope — it too is equally likely to be the bigger or the smaller of the two — so you should want to swap back, and he and I could stand there paying each other for the right to exachange the same two envelopes forever. (6) He also claims the ratio doesn't matter: if the envelopes differ by a factor of r instead of 2, the other envelope comes out worth (r + 1/r)X/2, which is bigger than X for any r ≠ 1 — so his argument says you should switch even when one envelope holds €100,000 and the other €100,010, a difference of ten euros. (7) And he says it follows that before looking, you'd happily pay a fee of up to a quarter of what's in your envelope just for the *opportunity* to swap, which he claims casinos will be delighted to hear about.

I know this is a famous puzzle and I know the folk response is "switching can't help, because you'd just switch back," but that's not what I'm after — I can say that much myself. What I want to know is exactly which numbered step above is the rotten one, why it *looked* fine when I first read it, and what it costs to reject it (he insists premise (1) is unassailabe, symmetry being the one thing in the setup we actually know; my own hunch is that the cheat lives somewhere in step (3)'s arithmetic, but I can't for the life of me say what's wrong with doubling, halving, and averaging). So: where's the con, and what do I have to give up to escape it?
