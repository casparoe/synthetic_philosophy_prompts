I've been reading my way into early Chinese philosophy out of plain curiosity — no academic background on my side, just podcasts, a used beginner's guide, and a lot of thinking on my own during my commute. At a family dinner, my brother-in-law, who took one semester of Chinese philosophy in college, explained the famous Mencius–Xunzi debate to me roughly like this: 'Mencius taught that humans are born good — a newborn already possesses real virtues like compassion and honesty, which is why anyone would instinctively feel alarm at a child about to fall into a well. Xunzi taught that human nature is evil: we're born selfish and greedy, so morality is something people must be forced into through strict laws and punishments, and in that respect he was really a Legalist wearing a Confucian robe. So Mencius thought education and self-cultivation are unnecessary, since we're good already, while Xunzi thought people are too corrupt for moral education to accomplish anything — basically the same split as Rousseau versus Hobbes in the West.' Something about this feels off to me — it seems strange that two self-described Confucians would end up disagreeing about whether moral education works at all, since honoring teachers and ritual seems central to the whole tradition — but I don't know the texts well enough to pin down where he's oversimplifying.

Please take on the role of a careful scholar of classical Confucian thought, think through each element of his summary before judging it, and give me an accuracy audit I can actually rely on. Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Spread the five items across: the parts of the summary a scholar would endorse as basically accurate; the subtle distortions, such as what each thinker actually claims we possess at birth (full virtues versus something more like tendencies that need cultivation), or what Xunzi's word for our innate condition really amounts to; whether the Legalism and Rousseau/Hobbes comparisons hold up; and whether the brother-in-law's overall picture of the debate as a simple pessimist-versus-optimist clash survives once those corrections are made.
