A few of us in my reading group have been going round in circles about what's usually called the Reflection principle (van Fraassen's thing from the 80s): roughly, that your current credence in a claim should line up with what you expect your future self to believe about it. So if you're sure that tomorrow you'll be 80% confident it'll rain, you should be 80% confident now, and if you expect your future self to refuse a bet, you shouldn't be eager to take it today. The obvious pitch for it is that violating it makes you incoherent across time, in something like the way believing a contradiction makes you incoherent at a time — and that a bookmaker who knows both your current prices and your anticipated future ones can milk you. The obvious pitch against it is all the cases where you can rationally expect your future opinions to be skewed: you know the wine will seem more charming after a few glasses, or you're deciding whether to swallow a pill that will make you overconfident that the pill is safe — and in that case you'd be a fool to endorse in advance the opinion the pill will produce.

Could you write a dialogue between two characters — one who thinks Reflection is a genuine requirement of rationality, on a par with not contradicting yourself, and one who thinks its at best a policy of trusting your future self, a policy a sensible person can rationally decline in the right circumstances? To make it concrete, let them fight over at least these: the pill case, where the defender has to say something about whether you take the pill and about what happens to Reflection once you've swallowed it; a variant where instead of your future self you defer to a trusted friend, where it's clearer the deference can rationally break (say the friend is systematically overoptimistic about one topic, or you know they have a blind spot), and whether the self case is genuinely different or just the friend case with worse introspective data; and whether violating Reflection is more like flat-out believing a contradiction or more like having a bad but consistent plan — like resolving to order the next round no matter what even though you know you'll regret it. Neither of them should be a pushover: the defender shouldn't cave the first time the pill comes up, and the critic should have to say what, if anything, survives of Reflection once all the exceptions are carved away.

Please write the whole thing in British English, and dont worry about making it too long — I'd rather the back-and-forth feel real than have them agree at the end.
