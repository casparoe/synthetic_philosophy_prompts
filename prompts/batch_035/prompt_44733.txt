For thirty-two years I was an environmental chemist, and since retiring I have been reading epistemology the way I once read the literature on trace-metal analysis: slowly, in order, and with a growing suspicion that two research groups are publishing incompatible conclusions from the same data. The two groups, in this case, are the epistemic contextualists, who hold that the truth-conditions of ascriptions like "she knows the well water is safe" shift with the conversational standards of the ascriber, and the pragmatic encroachers (or subject-sensitive invariantists), who hold that the meaning of "knows" stays fixed while the subject's own practical situation — the costs of being wrong for her, in her circumstances — shifts how much evidence suffices for knowledge. I have read enough to see that each side takes itself to have a decisive objection to the other, and not enough to be confident either is right. In my old lab, when two groups had competing models of the same spectra, we made them sit down and write up what they actually agreed on before letting them publish another round of rebuttals. I want that document here.

Draft the joint statement that a committed contextualist and a committed pragmatic encroacher could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what the ordinary-language data are (the bank-case and airport-case patterns, lottery propositions, the behavior of "knows" under embedded report and under challenge), about what a theory of knowledge ascriptions owes us, and about what neither side is claiming.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice: once in wording the contextualist would sign, once in wording the encroacher would sign. Where one side thinks the other has simply misdescribed its position, the correction belongs in the table, phrased so the corrected party accepts it.

3. A cruxes section. I have in mind at least these three, though include others if they matter: (a) the divergent case — an ascriber in low-stakes circumstances judges a subject in high-stakes circumstances, where the two theories make opposite predictions about the truth of the ascription; (b) the disagreement and retraction data — when a subject's stakes rise, does the earlier low-stakes ascription become false, or did it say something else, and which predictions each side makes about whether ordinary speakers would retract it; (c) the ideal-language question — whether any residual disagreement would survive restating every claim without the word "knows," i.e., whether the dispute is at bottom about semantics or about the epistemology of practical reasoning.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For crux (a) I want a specific scenario worked into the document, and I will supply it: a technician at a water-quality lab measures a contaminant in a municipal well and finds it just under the regulatory limit; that afternoon her supervisor, filing a routine report, says "the lab knows the well water meets the standard"; that evening the mayor, deciding whether to truck in bottled water for the town that week on the basis of the same reading, says "no, the lab doesn't know that — not for this decision." Both parties should state, in advance, what their theory predicts about whether the supervisor's afternoon ascription was true, and the document should be explicit about what would count against each theory if the predictions came apart from competent speakers' judgments.

Where the two camps suspect each other of smuggling in an assumption — for instance, that the encroacher is really offering a theory of assertion or action rather than of knowledge, or that the contextualist is really offering a theory of conversation rather than of the property ascribed — the suspicion should be stated as a disagreement the accused party would agree is a fair statement of what they are accused of, followed by their reply.

The response should begin with the joint statement itself: no preamble, no commentary, no restatement of this brief. I want a document the two parties could put their names to, not a verdict on which of them is right — my own verdict, if I ever get one, should have to be earned against a text this exact.
