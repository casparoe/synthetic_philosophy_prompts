Literary editors are in a weird position about authorial intention. When an author explicitly wants their unpublished stuff destroyed — Kafka's whole thing with Max Brod asking him to burn the manuscripts unread — editors publish it anyway, in fancy critical editions even. But those same editors will agonize over manuscript variants and treat "that's just a printer's error, not what the poet wrote" as a knockdown argument that a line doesn't belong in the text. So the practice seems to be: an author's intentions about which works should exist can be overridden without a second thought, while their intentions about what a given work says are treated as nearly sacred. What theory of textual meaning does that combo actually presuppose? Is it intentionalism about the work but not the corpus, or something else? Could a consistent intentionalist defend publishing anyway — like, is destroying your own manuscript a final act of interpretation we're entitled to ignore — or does the whole practice only make sense if a text's meaning is partly independent of what the author wants done with it? And spell out where this leaves the editing itself: if we're fine overriding Kafka's clearest wish, why should anyone care about restoring what the poet "meant" instead of just keeping whatever the printer happened to set?
