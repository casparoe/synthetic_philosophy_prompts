I'm a retired machinist, and I've been asked to give the eulogy for my closest friend, Walt, who was declared dead last Tuesday at sixty-eight and — this is the part that makes it complicated — is currently stored in liquid nitrogen at a cryonics facility in Arizona, where he'd been signed up for thirty years. Walt wasn't a kook about it and he wasn't rich; he was a refrigerator repairman who liked to think hard about what it means for a thing to be alive, and his favorite argument was the spore analogy: a bacterial spore just sits there for decades with no metabolism and no response to anything, and nobody calls it dead — so why is a brain whose fine structure has been carefully preserved dead just because the heartbeat stopped? He used to say that the question of whether something is alive has no answer until you pick a definition of life, and he had picked his. His son and daughter think the whole thing is a sad fantasy and are grieving a father; the local cryonics group, maybe fifteen people, will be at the memorial and they genuinely use the word patient rather than deceased; and I'll be standing up in front of both. For what it's worth, I never finished high school and I've never given a eulogy, so I want you to take this on as a serious writing job.

I'd like you to act as an accomplished eulogist with a philosopher's feel for definitional questions. Before you draft anything, please think through what can honestly be said to both halves of the room: which sentences about Walt are true under his definition of life, which are true under the ordinary medical one, and — this is the ground I want the speech built on — which are true under both. My instinct is that the words that fall in that overlap are the ones that can carry grief and hope at the same time, but I can't find them on my own. I also don't want a lecture on definitions; the philosophy should do its work underneath the surface, in what the speech declines to claim, not in anything that sounds like an argument. And I'd rather the speech not come down on whether the preservation will ever work — nobody in that room knows, and Walt himself always said he was buying a lottery ticket, not a ticket home.

Please write me something around five minutes when spoken, plain-spoken, warm, and a little funny, that lets his daughter cry for a father without the cryonics people feeling the eulogy quietly ruled against them, and lets his friends keep their hope without the family feeling insulted. If you can work in one analogy Walt would have appreciated — he loved frozen frogs, dormant seeds, viruses, anything that sat in the gray zone — all the better. End it on something that, under any definition of life you pick, he would have agreed was true.
