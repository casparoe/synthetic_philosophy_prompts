I'm a retired chemist who got into philosophy a few years back when my book club pivoted from novels to philosophy of mind, and lately I've been reading about the experimental philosophy work on intentionality judgments — the side-effect cases with the CEO and the environment. Old habits die hard, so instead of just reading I've been running my own little n=1 vignette study on Frank, my old labmate, at our weekly coffee. His verdicts so far: the classic case where the CEO harms the environment as a side effect of a profit program and doesn't care at all — Frank says he intentionally harmed it. The mirror case where the program helps the environment and he doesn't care — not intentional. A pilot who foresees that an unavoidable maneuver will injure a passenger's back, flies it perfectly, and does everything humanly possible to soften the jolt — not intentional. And a chemist who runs a synthesis knowing it will destroy the lab's expensive detector, takes every precaution, but has literally no other route to the compound she needs — not intentional. Next week I've got three new cases ready and I want to walk in with actual predictions, like a proper scientist. Case A: a nurse breaks a hospital rule during an emergency to save a patient, foreseeing that a rule-following colleague will take the blame for the rule being broken. Case B: a new postdoc violates a lab safety custom nobody ever told him about, and as a side effect someone down the hall gets a minor chemical burn. Case C: a researcher skips signing the equipment log — a rule Frank himself has complained is pointless for years — and as a side effect another group gets blamed for the hours rung up on the machine. Predict Frank's verdict on each. More importantly, tell me what rule he seems to be running, which other rules would fit his four verdicts just as well (I can see at least two live rivals but I suspect there are more), which one you'd bet on and why, and what single further case I should add to best seperate the surviving rules. Treat it like designing an experiment: I care as much about the discriminating case as about the predictions.
