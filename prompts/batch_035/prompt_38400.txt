I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and next term I'm running a section on teleological notions in biology for an introductory course. Most of the literature's showcase cases for the divide between etiological accounts of function (the Wright–Millikan–Neander lineage, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected) and causal-role accounts (in the line of Cummins, on which a trait's function is its contribution to some capacity of the containing system) fall into two familiar patterns: either the accounts disagree about whether there is a function at all, as with novel traits that have never been selected for anything, or they disagree about which of two effects is "the" function while both effects are still being produced, as in the standard exaptation stories. I want a case of the second kind but with a twist, and I want it designed to order rather than excavated from the literature.

Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. First, it should concern a single trait of a single species, where the species is fictional but biologically plausible: you will need to invent the species and narrate its evolutionary and ecological history, and the history must be one of ordinary evolutionary dynamics — no genetic engineering, no pathology, no artificial selection by humans, and no decree that "the function changed." Second, the trait must have been selected for producing an effect that it no longer produces at all: the selection episode is over, the effect is now absent for a stated ecological reason, and the trait must not have gone vestigial or idle — it should be active and doing something biologically useful. Third, what it now does should be a different effect, one that clearly contributes to a capacity of the organism, but one for which the trait was never selected; and you must close off the etiological theorist's usual escape route by building population-level facts into the scenario — say, no heritable variation in the new effect, or too few generations for selection to have acted on it — that prevent the new effect from having been selected or being on its way to selection. Fourth, the scenario itself must stay neutral: it may describe selective histories, causal structures, and population facts, but it may not contain the word "function" or otherwise stipulate the verdicts — those have to be derived by the theories, not announced by the case. Don't recycle the textbook menagerie (hearts, eyes, wings, feathers, the panda's thumb, sickle-cell).

After the scenario, do the walkthrough. State what the etiological account says the trait's function is and why, what the causal-role account says it is and why, and note explicitly that the case puts the two verdicts in an unusual orientation: the etiological function is an effect the trait never produces anymore, while the effect the trait produces now is not its function on that account — the reverse of the easy direction, in which a trait acquires a causal-role function while having no etiological one. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work (I expect it is the gap between the selection episode and the present, but I want that argued, not asserted), what happens to the verdicts if that feature is deleted, and finally how each camp would attack the case from within: what the etiological theorist says to deny the current effect the title of "function" (presumably demoting it to a use or a mere effect), and what the causal-role theorist says to deny that the selected-for-but-never-produced effect deserves the title (presumably pressing the point that a function nothing performs is a function for nothing). If you think the two theories can be brought back into agreement on this case by some principled amendment rather than by stipulation, say what the amendment is and what it costs.
