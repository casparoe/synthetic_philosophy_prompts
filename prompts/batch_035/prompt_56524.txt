The exam question was: "Explain the disagreement between Malcolm (Dreaming, 1959) and Dennett ('Are Dreams Experiences?', 1976) about whether dreams are experiences had while asleep. What is the received view both are reacting to? What exactly does each philosopher hold? And if Dennett's alternative model were correct, would that show that dreams are not experiences at all?"

Here's the student's answer:

"The received view, which Dennett identifies at the start of his paper, is that dreams are experiences that unfold in real time while we sleep, and that the report we give on waking is a memory report of those experiences. Malcolm attacks this in his 1959 book. His point is basically epistemic: because the sleeper is asleep, there is no way to check up on what, if anything, is going on in their mind, so the waking report can't be evidence for anything that happened during the night. The sleeping mind is private in a way the waking mind isn't. So Malcolm's conclusion is that we can't know what our dreams were actually like — though he does concede that we dream, of course, he just thinks their content is unknowable.

Dennett agrees with Malcolm that the inference from waking report to sleeping experience doesn't work, and he offers a positive alternative. On his composition-on-awakening model, the dream narrative is written up by the brain either during the moment of waking or during the onset of sleep, and the feeling of having had the dream beforehand is an illusion of memory — the waking impression is the product of the composition, not a record of anything experienced earlier. So Dennett has shown that dreams are never experiences: they're confabulations put together after the fact, and the received view is refuted.

As for whether Dennett's model would show dreams aren't experiences at all, the answer is no, and in any case the empirical evidence ends up favoring the received view anyway. In the classic experiments from the fifties, researchers sprayed sleepers with water or played tones during REM, and the sleepers reported dreams of rain or doorbells, which shows the dreams incorporated the stimuli as they happened, in real time. Dennett never really addresses these incorporation results. And think of Maury's guillotine dream, where an entire elaborate dream about the French Revolution builds up to the blade falling, and the dreamer wakes because the headboard struck his neck — a dream like that must have been composed in real time to fit the stimulus. So at the end of the day the received view comes out looking solid."
