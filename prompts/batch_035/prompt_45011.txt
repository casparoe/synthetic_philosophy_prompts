Every thanksgiving my brother-in-law (the stats one) corners me about money and rationality, and this year he thinks he finally got me. His setup: option one, you get $1 million guaranteed. Option two, a lottery that pays $5M with 10% chance, $1M with 89% chance, nothing with 1% chance. I take the sure million. So does he. So does everyone he's polled at work. Then he reruns it, except now both options start with an 89% chance of nothing baked in, so it's either an 11% shot at $1M or a 10% shot at $5M. I take the 10% shot at $5M. So does everyone. He says some french economist built exactly this in the 50s to show that everybody fails a basic consistency test, and then he laid out why that matters:

(1) Rational preferences have to satisfy independence: if you prefer X over Y, you have to prefer "p chance of X, otherwise Z" over "p chance of Y, otherwise Z," for any Z and any p.
(2) My two answers together break independence — the first pair and the second pair differ only in what happens in the other 89% of cases, and I flipped my ranking when that 89% went from "$1M no matter what" to "nothing no matter what."
(3) Preferences that break independence can't be represented as maximizing the average of one fixed set of numbers you attach to outcomes, and anything that can't be represented that way can be Dutch booked: a bookie can construct a sequence of trades that leaves you strictly worse off than where you started no matter how the world turns out, the same way he can with rock-beats-paper-beats-scissors-beats-rock circular preferences.
(4) So my preferences aren't just quirky, they're pumpable, and pumpable means irrational — this is math, not taste.
(5) And the same holds for every other departure from being a pure expected-value maximizer with stable numbers: valuing certainty as such, or caring how you'll feel about having gambled away a sure thing, all of it gets you booked the same way.
(6) So valuing the sureness of a sure thing is exactly like valuing the pretty felt on a card table — a preference for the packaging instead of the contents — and that's irrational, full stop.

He's real smug about (6) and I can't quite say why. I tried the "I just really don't want to be the guy who had a million dollars locked in and threw the dice anyway" line, and he said that just proves his point: $1M-for-sure and $1M-after-you-survive-a-1%-risk are the same money, so valuing them differently is valuing the wrapper. I have a gut feeling the leap from my two survey answers to "a bookie cleans me out no matter what happens" is doing more work than he admits — when I pushed him on the actual trades he started waving his hands about randomizing devices and mixing lotteries together and I lost the thread. So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? And separately — is there any version of my position where my two original answers are just... fine? Because I'd like to keep those if its even coherent to keep them.
