In a reading group on property theory, a colleague laid out an argument that copyright cannot be justified by appeal to harm, and I cannot decide whether it is a proof of a conclusion he welcomes or a proof of far too much. (1) A legal prohibition on some conduct is legitimate only if the prohibited conduct harms someone. (2) An act harms a person only if it leaves that person worse off than they would otherwise have been. (3) When someone copies and distributes my unpublished manuscript, nothing is taken from me: I keep the manuscript, my talents, and my capacity to write more. (4) The obvious reply — that I am worse off by the sales I lose — compares my position to a baseline in which I hold a right to those sales, that is, a baseline that already contains the very copyright law whose justification is in question, and generating the harm from the law we are trying to justify is circular. (5) So, measured against a baseline that does not presuppose copyright, unauthorized copying harms no one. (6) So copyright cannot be justified by appeal to harm. (7) By parity of reasoning, fraud, defamation, and the publication of private letters cannot be justified by appeal to harm either, since each leaves the victim with everything they physically started with and costs them only what an existing legal entitlement would have secured. My colleague accepts (7) as a bonus conclusion; I take it as evidence that something has gone wrong. I would rather keep (1) and the parity claimed in (7) than any other pair of claims in the argument. My working suspicion is that the phrase "worse off than they would otherwise have been" in (2) shifts its reference between steps — sometimes meaning "had this act not occurred, under the law as it stands" and sometimes "in a world without the law at all" — but I have not been able to state the two readings cleanly enough to see whether the shift really does the work, and I also notice that if the argument is valid, then rejecting it seems to force a choice between saying harms must always be measured against law-free baselines and saying that some genuine wrongs are not harms at all. Which premise or inference should I reject, why did it look acceptable when I first accepted it, and what does rejecting it cost me when I turn to the cases in (7) — do those cases genuinely travel with copyright, or is the parity only superficial?
