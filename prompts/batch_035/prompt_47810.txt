You are a professional philosopher specializing in population ethics and the aggregation of claims, and a careful, even-handed referee of arguments. Please think the exchange below through before rating anything, and resist the urge to resolve it by appeal to authority.

At a reading group I sit in on, two members spent an hour arguing about whether numbers count in rescue cases, in a Taurek-style setup: you can save David, who will otherwise die, or five strangers who will each otherwise die, but not both. Marta, taking the minority side, opened by denying that saving the five is even better: no individual among the five has a stronger claim to rescue than David does, since each faces exactly the loss he faces, and there is no one for whom five deaths are worse than one — losses, she insisted, don't sum across persons. Ezra replied that no summing is needed: each of the five gives you an additional reason of precisely the same strength as the reason David gives you, and reasons can be weighed together in deliberation without there being anyone who experiences their total. Marta answered that additive reasons are just aggregation wearing a thinner mask, and pressed him with the harder case: suppose the choice is between saving David's hand and saving five strangers from each losing one finger. If numbers count, then some number of fingers outweighs a hand; if Ezra denies that, he owes a story about why the death case differs in kind rather than degree. Ezra drew a distinction: five deaths are five instances of the very same loss, so counting them needs no common metric beyond what each individual loses, whereas fingers and hands are different losses, and comparing them requires an independent scale that does not exist — which is why he can count deaths without being committed to any finger-to-hand exchange rate. Marta's last word was that like-for-like counting still leaves David entitled to ask, of the verdict against him, worse for whom? — and that a verdict which cannot answer any individual who asks that question has not been grounded in anyone's interests after all.

They asked the group to referee, and I honestly lost the thread. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; note any genuinely decent point that was left unexploited by the side that owned it; and say what, if anything, both of them are missing about the structure of the dispute. End your reply with a final line of the form "Verdict: [name], on the balance of the argument as actually run."
