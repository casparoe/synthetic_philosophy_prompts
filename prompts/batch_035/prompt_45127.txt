I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe about our duties to animals, and in particular whether cruelty to animals is wrong because of what happens to the animal or because of what it does to the person doing it (the Kantian line, roughly, that our duties regarding animals are indirect -- cruelty is wrong because it damages our capacity to treat humanity with respect). So instead of reading more, I'm going to go by my gut on cases and see what pattern comes out.

Kicking a stray dog for fun: wrong, no hesitation. A vet setting that same dog's broken leg without anesthesia because the clinic ran out and waiting a day would make it worse: fine, though grim. Gassing the rats in the barn quickly: fine. Setting the kind of glue trap where the rat dies slowly over days when the quick kind was on the shelf: wrong, and I notice I'm annoyed at the person even though they'd say the outcome was the same either way. A researcher giving mice a painful condition when there's a validated non-animal alternative: wrong. Same researcher, same condition, when there isn't one: I let it pass, uneasily. A hunter who shoots cleanly and eats what he kills: fine. A hunter who takes wing shots he knows will only wound, because he likes the challenge: wrong. My kid pulling the cat's tail because he's two: not wrong, but I stop him anyway. A grown man doing the same thing on purpose: wrong.

Here are the two cases that are bugging me. First, I'm pretty sure I think someone stranded alone on an island for life, who will never see another person again, does something wrong if he spends his days torturing the animals there. But if the duties are indirect -- if the wrongness comes from corroding his dispositions toward humans -- that's strained, since there are no humans around for it to matter for, and he dies without ever harming one. Second, I've read about a person who was reportedly gentle and generous with every human he met and brutal to animals in private, and my reaction is that the private brutality somehow makes the public kindness worse, not that it's a coincidence, which fits the indirect view; but I have basically the same reaction to the island case, where there's no public anything.

So: is there a principle (or a couple of candidates) that captures my verdicts, stated precisely enough to rule on new cases? If the indirect-duty view is the best fit, tell me which of my verdicts it has to overturn and whether that's a price worth paying. And either way, tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't decided on: a lab that anesthetizes its animals during procedures and euthanizes them painlessly, but does lethal work on them routinely, in a world (unlike ours) where no humans would ever be harmed by the practice.
