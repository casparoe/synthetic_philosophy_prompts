I'm running a ninety-minute session on the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion for a mixed group of second-year economics and philosophy students who have had exactly one week of expected utility theory. Please don't explain the paradox to me — plan how I should explain it. The plan should address the following, in whatever order you think soundest:

1. The prerequisite structure. My working assumption is that the paradox cannot bite until students accept that a subjective probability is a betting rate — something they reveal in choices — so that "I don't know the probability" is a description of their state of mind, not an escape from having one. Tell me whether that's the right thing to secure first, whether it's better secured through a de Finetti-style Dutch book sketch or through a simpler coherence argument at this level, and whether anything else has to be in place before the urn is even shown.

2. The carrying example. I'm inclined to use Ellsberg's one-urn, two-color problem (a single urn containing red and black balls in an unknown proportion) rather than the more famous three-color version with its two-urn contrast, because the students can hold the whole setup in mind at once. Tell me whether that simplification costs me anything essential — in particular whether the sure-thing or dominance structure that generates the paradox comes through with only two colors, and if not, at which point I should switch examples. I've also considered replacing the urn entirely with a pair of bets on tomorrow's temperature versus a lottery with known odds, on the theory that weather feels like a natural domain of ambiguity; tell me whether that substitution imports confounds I'm not seeing.

3. The misconception map. The ones I can predict: (a) students will assimilate ambiguity aversion to ordinary risk aversion and think a concave utility function handles it; (b) they will treat "the probability is unknown" as a third category of probability rather than as ignorance about a probability; (c) they will infer too quickly that any violation of expected utility is a refutation of it as a normative standard, without identifying which premise actually fails in the Ellsberg choices; (d) at least one will propose that the experimenter's freedom to set the composition is what drives the preference, which sounds like a debunking explanation but may actually be the phenomenon. For each, tell me where in the sequence it is best headed off and with what move — and tell me which misconceptions I haven't predicted.

4. The comprehension check. One question I can pose at the end whose correct answer would show the distinction between risk and ambiguity has actually landed, rather than that students can merely recite the urn setup.

5. The explicit refusals. What belongs at this level and what I should openly decline to cover — I assume axiomatizations of maxmin expected utility and smooth ambiguity models are out, and I want to know how to mark the boundary honestly without making it sound like the omitted material would rescue expected utility.

Finally, since this is a plan rather than an explanation, hold yourself to it as one: tell me where the plan is most likely to fail (which step rests on the shakiest assumption about this particular audience), and describe what evidence about the students during the session would lead you to restructure the sequence on the fly.
