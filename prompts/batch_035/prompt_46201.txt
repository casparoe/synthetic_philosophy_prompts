I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a new museum exhibition of Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE charts — the wall-sized pictorial statistics he built with Gerd Arntz and Marie Reidemeister for Vienna's Museum of Society and Economy in the 1920s. The audience will be genuinely mixed: design historians who can date every variant of the Vienna Method, a delegation from a philosophy-of-science institute, museum patrons, and a general public that has never heard of the Vienna Circle and knows Neurath, if at all, only as the man who wanted to rebuild the ship of knowledge while at sea. Write me five to six minutes of polished remarks that do three things. First, explain to the lay visitor why a fierce anti-metaphysician concluded that the way to fight superstition was to draw rows of identical stick-figure workers and sheep rather than to argue in print. Second, confront openly the charge the curators know will be whispered: that a picture language built for Red Vienna's housing campaigns — one that insists, as Neurath put it on his office wall, that words divide and pictures unite — is a propagandist's instrument wearing an educator's clothes, since a chart that needs no caption is also a chart that permits no rebuttal. Third, honour Neurath's own demand that a good ISOTYPE chart be legible without verbal commentary, by never once explaining an individual chart on display; the speech must say why there are charts at all, not what they show. Please close on the sailors rebuilding their ship on the open sea, but let the metaphor be earned by the argument rather than merely quoted.
