I'm a third-year biology major taking a seminar on genetics and society, and my final assignment is a policy memo for a hypothetical legislative research service. The scenario our instructor set up: during hearings on a universal pre-K bill, a witness argued that because twin studies put the heritability of IQ and educational attainment somewhere between 0.5 and 0.8, making environments more equal is largely futile — whatever differences remain after equalization will still track genes. My job is to write the appendix that untangles what actually follows from a heritability estimate and what doesn't, but I have room for at most two or three points done properly, and I keep going back and forth on which misreadings do the real work in that argument.

Please rank the candidate points below by how much distortive work they do in the futility inference, and defend the ranking. My candidates: (1) heritability is a property of a population within a particular range of environments, so it can be high precisely because environments are relatively uniform, and it predicts nothing about what happens when the range of environments changes; (2) high heritability is compatible with large environmental malleability, the classic examples being myopia, which is highly heritable and fully correctable with glasses, and phenylketonuria, a genetic cause removable by diet; (3) within-group heritability is silent on between-group differences, since the genetic and environmental decomposition of variance does not transfer across populations; (4) twin-based estimates confound genetic similarity with shared environment and with gene–environment correlation, whether passive, evocative, or active, to the extent that identical twins are also treated more alike; and (5) the everyday sense of the word genetic — fixed, inevitable, essential — versus the biometric sense, a ratio of variances with no such implication built in. I'm also unsure whether to bring in the gap between twin-study estimates and what genome-wide association studies recover, or whether that would just be a distraction for this audience.

So: which two or three points earn the space in a one-page appendix written for readers with no statistics beyond a single college course? I want the selection criteria made explicit — how load-bearing each misreading is in the futility inference, how hard it is to convey correctly in plain prose, and how likely the correction is to backfire or be misread as conceding the witness's point. I'd also like to know which of these points are upstream of which: does getting (1) across make (2) easier to accept, or do they have to be argued independently? Finally, the memo is supposed to close with a short list: tell me which alleged implications of high heritability you would explicitly declare do not follow, and which do follow but not in the direction the witness assumes.
