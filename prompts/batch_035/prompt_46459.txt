I have been collecting my verdicts on cases of self-locating uncertainty — situations where what is uncertain is not what the world is like, but where or when I am within it — and I cannot find the principle behind them. I would like someone to tell me whether my verdicts add up to a coherent view, and if they do not, to force the trade-offs into the open. Here is everything, honestly reported.

1. The standard case. A fair coin is flipped on Sunday. Heads: Beauty is woken once, on Monday. Tails: she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, a drug erasing the memory of each waking before the next. The protocol is common knowledge. On waking, my credence that the coin landed heads is 1/3, and I am fairly firm about it. The halving answer feels like it is throwing away information she plainly has.

2. The informed variant. Same protocol, except that each morning she is told the day upon waking. Once she learns it is Monday, I say her credence in heads is 1/2 — the disclosure seems to tell her nothing about the coin.

3. The duplicated variant. Instead of waking her twice, the lab wipes her memory on Sunday night if the coin lands tails and grows a duplicate of her; the original and the duplicate wake in indistinguishable rooms, neither knowing which she is. I say 1/3 again. Re-waking and duplicating seem, from the inside, to be the same thing.

4. The presumptuous philosopher. Two cosmological theories start at equal prior credence and agree about all observable local physics, except that one posits a trillion times as many observers. If my reasoning in (3) generalizes, I should become near-certain of the larger theory without running a single experiment. My verdict: no, absolutely not. Armchair reasoning cannot settle cosmology.

5. The doom argument. Reasoning from my early birth rank among all humans to a high probability that few humans will follow me. My verdict: also no. My birth order is not evidence about the future.

6. The multiverse. One universe whose constants are fixed once, versus a vast multiverse with randomly varied constants. I find myself in a life-permitting universe. My verdict: this really does seem to favor the multiverse, and I would resist being argued out of it.

7. The sleep study. A lab flips a fair coin to decide whether to enroll 1 subject or 100; I am enrolled and wake in a windowless room. When I actually imagine waking there, my gut says the 100-subject world is overwhelmingly more likely. On reflection I notice, uncomfortably, that (7) is structurally (4), which I just rejected.

Is there one principle that delivers all of these verdicts? If not, I want the candidates stated precisely: which clause earns which verdict, what each says about the others, and what the principle recommends in a case I have not listed — an absent-minded driver who must exit at the second of two identical intersections, whose memory is too poor for him to tell which intersection he is at, for whom exiting at the first costs hours and missing both is worst, and who must settle on a probability of exiting. And I want an honest accounting of which of my verdicts cannot all be saved. If something has to give, I suspect it is the multiverse verdict, but I would rather keep the thirding; tell me what each of those preferences costs.
