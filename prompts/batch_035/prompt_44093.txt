I'm a novelist, currently at work on a multi-generational family saga set in a fading agricultural town, and I've run into a recurring craft problem that I've come to suspect is actually a philosophical problem. Again and again I find myself writing sentences of the form "the reason the family did X was to Y" where no character in the book holds that reason, ever held it, or could articulate it. The festival is held when it is held because that's when it has always been held; the narration, however, wants to say the festival exists to get grain into the hands of the poorest households before winter, even though not a single character — not even the priest who organizes it — would offer that explanation, and the origin of the date has something to do with a tax collection calendar nobody remembers. I gather that philosophers of mind and biology have a name for this kind of thing — reasons that exist without any reasoner, as in the pattern on a moth's wing existing "in order to" hide it from predators, a rationale nobody designed or believes in. I want a procedure, not a lecture.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who works on teleology, functions, and explanation, and who has a pragmatic sympathy for working writers: someone who believes theory should end up as a tool that fits in a pocket. Before writing the procedure, please do some visible brainstorming: sketch the distinctions available from the philosophy of functions and reasons-without-reasoners — designed reasons, selected reasons, drift, accidental benefit — and say which of them a novelist plausibly needs and which are theoretical machinery that would only clutter the page. Then translate what survives into a step-by-step decision procedure I can run in fifteen minutes at my desk, mid-chapter, whenever I'm tempted to write one of these attribution sentences. It should tell me, in order: what to ask about the practice in question, what would count as a sufficient basis for the narration claiming a rationale no character holds, when the claim must instead be voiced by a character (and thereby become fallible and arguable), and when I must drop the rationale entirely and let the practice sit there, unexplained, the way most of what people do actually sits.

I'd like the procedure to be honest about the point where it can't decide for me, since at bottom the question of whether a given attribution is true of my fictional world is a question about my own creation, and no procedure can settle what only I can enact on the page. Please flag that hand-off explicitly.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my drafts, chosen to stress it:

1. The harvest festival described above, where the narration wants to attribute a redistributive rationale that no character holds and no one designed, but where the town's actual history — as I've written it — plausibly shows the festival surviving because it performs that function, decade after decade, whatever anyone believes about it.

2. An old prohibition on whistling after dark, which the narration wants to explain as having kept children indoors at night, though the characters believe it's about waking the dead, and I have written no selection history for it at all: no scene where families without the custom fared worse, nothing. It's just there.

3. The family's silence about the dead grandfather, which the narration is tempted to explain "in order to" protect the mother from grief — a rationale that would be attributed to people who demonstrably do have beliefs and reasons of their own, which strikes me as a different beast entirely, possibly the case where the procedure should refuse and redirect me to characterization.

I want at least one of these applications to end in an answer I won't like — my editor suspects the festival sentence is the best line in the book, and if the procedure kills it, say so plainly. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of attribution sentences it was never built to judge, and what I should do when I meet one. Feel free to propose a short list of diagnostic questions I can ask of any draft sentence of this type, phrased so I could tape them above my desk. Please think carefully and work through the brainstorming before committing to the procedure; I would rather see your reasoning change shape once than see a tidy answer you'd already decided on.
