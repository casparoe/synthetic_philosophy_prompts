I want a test case that pulls apart two reductionist positions about the self that are usually treated as allies: the Buddhist no-self view, on which a person is ultimately nothing over and above a causally connected stream of physical and mental events, and Parfitian reductionism, on which personal identity is nothing over and above psychological continuity and connectedness, but where it is psychological connectedness that grounds rational future-directed concern and the practical claims we make on our own futures and pasts. Both deny a deep further fact of selfhood. What I want is a case where they nevertheless issue in different practical verdicts — and where the common-sense view of persistence issues in a third.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The scenario must be realistic and set in an ordinary institutional context — a courtroom, a hospital, a workplace, a family dispute over a promise — and no longer than a single paragraph. It should feature one agent whose psychological connectedness to an earlier self of theirs has been drastically thinned while the causal continuity of their organism and mental stream remains intact: dementia, a conversion experience, brain injury, or a profound character change, whichever you can give plausible psychological detail. Hang on the scenario one concrete practical question that turns on how the agent relates to that earlier self — whether they owe an apology or restitution for a wrong committed by their earlier self, whether they are bound by a commitment it made, or whether someone else owes them something because of what was done to that earlier self. Choose one and make the stakes specific.

Then walk through the verdicts of three views: first, the common-sense view on which the person simply persisted through the change; second, the Parfitian reductionist, for whom what matters is psychological connectedness, so that thinned connectedness should correspondingly thin the practical claim; and third, the Buddhist no-self theorist, who grounds conventional personhood in causal continuity of the stream rather than in degree of connectedness, and who should therefore deliver a verdict that diverges from the Parfitian's on your case. If you find you cannot make the Buddhist and the Parfitian diverge, say so plainly and diagnose what blocks it, rather than forcing the divergence.

The verdicts must be generated by the theories, not stipulated by the design: you may not hand one side an absurd premise or the other an obviously right one. After the walkthrough, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work in producing the divergence, and state what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed or weakened. Then give the strongest available counter to whichever view comes out worst — in particular, consider whether the Buddhist can invoke the two truths and argue that at the conventional level the practical verdict survives untouched even though the ultimate analysis dissolves the person, and whether that maneuver is consistent with their reductionism or quietly reinstates the self they denied.

As you go, indicate how confident you are in each attribution of a verdict to a view, since I care more about where the reasoning is shaky than about a tidy table of positions. In particular, flag rather than smooth over any point where you are unsure whether a view — for example, Parfitian reductionism on whether it is connectedness rather than continuity that grounds concern — is actually committed to the verdict you assign it, and at the end single out the claim in your answer you are least sure of.
