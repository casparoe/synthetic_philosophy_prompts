I teach a second-year moral philosophy seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and next week is on prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection. They've already done the basics of utilitarianism and distributive justice, so they know what a welfare function is, but they haven't read Parfit yet and I don't want them to know the name of any of this going in. Design me an in-class activity where they run into the problem themselves before it gets named.

Roughly what I have in mind: they play a health funding board for a fictional country with two regions, allocating a fixed budget across rounds, and the rounds are set up so that at some point they have to choose between (a) raising the worse-off region by a little, (b) raising total welfare by a lot but widening the gap, and (c) a policy that makes the two regions equal but lowers everyone. I want a round where equality improves because something bad happens to the better-off group — a blight hits the richer region's farms, say — and they have to vote on whether the country is now in one respect better off, and then argue about what their vote commits them to. If you can work in a round where the participants might themselves end up as residents of one of the regions (some random assignment at the end), even better, because I want them to notice afterwards whether their allocations changed once it was their welfare on the line — that seems like a nice way to muddy the distinction between believing the worse off count for more and just being scared of being the worse off.

Please give me: the materials (nothing requiring real money, ideally just index cards or counters), the exact round structure with numbers that are simple enough to do in your head but sharp enough to force the trade-offs, a timeline that fits 90 minutes including the debrief, and the debrief itself — mapping specific moments in the game onto telic egalitarianism, the priority view, and the leveling-down objection, and introducing the actual terms only at the end. I also want an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students gaming the numbers, someone checking their phone during round three, the math breaking down — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which part of the philosophical issue the activity cannot possibly reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief: my worry is that with clean numbers on a whiteboard, the interpersonal aggregation at the heart of the whole debate feels easy when it shouldn't, but tell me if you disagree or if there's a worse failure than that.

One format thing: whatever you write back, please keep it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text — I want to be able to read it start to finish like a letter from a colleague, and I'll rework it into my own notes afterwards. A few of my students read better from plain paragraphs too, and I might just hand them parts of it directly.
