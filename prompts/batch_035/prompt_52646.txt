In my graduate seminar this term we spent three weeks on the Grundrisse, and two of my students — Vera, who is writing a dissertation on the Autonomist reception of Marx, and Ben, whose work is in value-form theory — ended the term in a genuine standoff over how to read the section everyone now calls the Fragment on Machines. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move, since I was never able to settle it to my own satisfaction.

For context: the passage in question, from Marx's 1857–58 notebooks, is where he imagines an automated industry in which the worker comes to stand beside the process of production as its supervisor and regulator, and where he writes that "the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time." The contemporary hook was a recent fashion for fully automated post-capitalist visions, which Vera favors and Ben resists.

Their exchange ran as follows:

(1) Vera claimed that the Fragment shows Marx concluding that capitalism, at the limit of its own development, abolishes the basis of the value form: if wealth ceases to be measured by direct labour time, then surplus value becomes a residual, and a fully automated capitalism is one that has destroyed the measure of the very thing it accumulates. On her reading, Marx is describing a structural tendency toward the system's self-undermining, and the current enthusiasm for post-work politics is simply this tendency becoming visible.

(2) Ben objected that this is a category mistake. Value, for Marx, is not a substance that machines can be more or less productive of; it is a social relation of exchange under generalized commodity production. Machines transfer the value embodied in them; they do not create new value. What automation actually does, on Ben's account, is expel living labour from production — generating unemployment, a rising organic composition of capital, and profitability crises — while the value form persists untouched. A fully automated capitalism would still produce commodities, sell them, and accumulate; it would simply be one in which most labour is design, maintenance, and supervision.

(3) Vera replied that the mechanisms Ben lists are the same tendency viewed from the other side: the falling rate of profit is just the value-side expression of the Fragment's claim, so in the limit case Ben concedes to her, profit falls to exactly zero. And the Fragment adds something Capital does not: knowledge — Marx's general intellect, social knowledge as a direct force of production — becomes increasingly hard for the wage relation to capture and meter, which is precisely the Autonomist point. The more production becomes scientific, the less of it can be counted in labour hours, and the more the wage relation becomes an anachronistic hold on an already post-labour process.

(4) Ben answered on three fronts. First, the Grundrisse is a rough draft, written in haste, unpublished in Marx's lifetime, and set aside for two decades while he wrote Capital, which never makes the value-abolition claim; cherry-picking the notebooks over the published critique is methodologically backwards. Second, the falling rate of profit is a contested mechanism even within Marxism — counter-tendencies, the cheapening of constant capital, and the like — so resting an entire systemic-collapse claim on it is precarious. Third, and decisively in his view, the idea that capital as such, separately from labour, creates value is one Marx explicitly mocks in those very notebooks, attributing it to Lauderdale; Vera's reading, Ben said, turns Marx into exactly the theorist he was ridiculing, and dresses up a Ricardian technological determinism as a critique of it.

(5) Vera's rejoinder was that Ben's Lauderdale point proves too much. Marx's mockery there is directed at the claim that machines create value now, within the labour theory of value; her claim is historical — that the value form is a transient mode of production whose material basis large industry dissolves, and that Marx's own method, stated in his 1859 preface, requires that a mode of production develop the productive forces it can accommodate before giving way. So a tendency-to-the-limit reading is not determinism; it is what Marx's stated method demands. And the general intellect is no unpublished anomaly: it reappears in Capital as modern science applied to production and in the figure of the collective labourer.

Please adjudicate: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, and identify precisely what, if anything, each party is missing — in particular whether either has noticed that the whole dispute may turn on a distinction Marx himself insists on between wealth and value, and on whether the Fragment is a claim about capitalism's collapse or rather about the material conditions for a successor society. Conclude by saying who, if anyone, is more right overall.
