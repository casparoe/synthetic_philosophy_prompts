Hi there. I'm a retired physical chemist (semiconductor surfaces, mostly, if you must know) and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy, in a slightly undisciplined way — one minute it's Popper, next it's this big strange Hofstadter book, Gödel, Escher, Bach, that my daughter bought me. There's a bit early on with the little formal system MIU that I've been chewing over for weeks and I'd like your help thinking through what it actually amounts to, because I suspect the pop-science version I keep hearing oversells it.

Quick recap so we're on the same page (paraphrasing, not quoting): you start with the string MI and you want to derive MU. You've got four rules for growing and shrinking strings — you can stick a U on anything ending in I, you can double whatever follows the M, you can trade any III for a U, and you can delete a UU. No amount of fiddling ever gets you MU. The trick is that none of the rules can take a number of I's that isn't a multiple of three and turn it into one that is, and MI starts you off the wrong side of that fence, so MU is unreachable. Hofstadter's name for noticing this kind of thing is jumping out of the system — JOOTSing — and he sees it everywhere, from Achilles talking back to the tortoise to, if I follow him, people refusing to be mere cogs.

What I want from you: the most important implications of JOOTSing, but the real ones, not the woo. Specifically:

1. First, a nagging doubt I'd like you to take seriously rather than wave away. The MU argument is just ordinary number theory about remainders — there's no self-reference, no diagonal trick, nothing Gödelian in sight. So what, if anything, does the MU puzzle have to do with incompleteness? Is JOOTSing one phenomenon or are people conflating two: (a) reasoning about a system from outside, which mathematicians have always done, and (b) the specific self-referential sentences that force you to reason about the system from inside? I want to know whether the connection is deep or whether Hofstadter has smuggled a pun past me.

2. The AI question, but handled carefully. The Penrose-style move is that because humans can jump out of any formal system and see its limits, we're not formal systems ourselves. My understanding is that this doesn't follow — the machine can do the meta-level too, and then there's a meta-meta-level, and the human advantage evaporates into an arms race nobody wins. Please spell out the implications of JOOTSing for that debate properly, including where the anti-mechanist argument actually goes wrong, because I keep half-believing Penrose when I read him and then feeling silly about it.

3. Something more practical and closer to my own working life. In forty years of lab work the decisions I most regret were all made inside a system whose rules couldn't generate the right answer — grant cycles, safety procedures, a committee that could only optimise what it already measured. Was JOOTSing a real thing I was bad at, or is it just a fancy name for the mundane fact that models have domains of validity, which any chemist who's pushed a model outside its temperature range knows in her bones? Is there any actual difference between jumping out of the system and simply noticing you've left the model's domain?

4. And the thing I most want your view on: there's no rule inside MIU that tells you when to look for an invariant. From inside, endless string-fiddling and a search for an impossibility proof look like the same activity for quite a while. What are the implications of that for the ethics of advice-giving and argument generally — when I lecture my grandson about his career, am I offering him a new rule from within his system or pretending to a God's-eye view I don't have?

If you could keep it grounded and tell me when a claim is metaphor stretched past its breaking point, I'd be grateful. I've had a lifetime of physicists-turned-philosophers being confidently wrong and I'd rather you flagged the shaky joints than produced something quotable.
