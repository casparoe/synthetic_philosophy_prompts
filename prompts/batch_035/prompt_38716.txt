I write fiction for a living, which mostly means deciding how much agony to inflict on people who don't exist, and lately I've been trying to get clear on what actually makes pain bad — partly curiosity, partly because I'm sketching a novel where a character has something like pain asymbolia and I need to know whether her pain counts as suffering on the page. (For those who don't know it: pain asymbolia is a documented condition where patients report that the pain is fully there, they can rate its intensity, but it doesn't register as threatening in any way — they don't pull away, they'll joke about it while it's happening.) So I went through a stack of cases and wrote down what my gut says about each, and now I want someone to tell me whether there's a principle under there or just vibes.

My verdicts. The asymbolia case itself: someone feels a clear pain, shrugs, says sure it hurts but who cares — I think that pain is basically not bad, barely a harm at all, even though the pain quality is fully present. A chronic back-pain patient who's had it a decade, says it doesn't get to her anymore, it's just backround noise, she doesn't rearrange her life around it: still genuinely bad, maybe as bad as ever. Someone on a good dose of morphine who says the pain is still there, clearly, but far away, like weather: not bad while medicated, bad again as it wears off — and I can't articulate how this differs from the asymbolia case, yet my gut treats them differntly. A soldier with a bad wound under adrenaline who doesn't notice the pain for an hour: bad only once he notices. A woman with survivor guilt whose grief-ache is exactly the sort of pain she'd refuse to have removed — she thinks she deserves it, and it quietly structures her whole week: bad, and bad in a way the morphine case isn't, even though she isn't suffering in the ordinary sense either. Pain in a dream so vivid I wake up gasping: bad while I'm in it, counts for nothing afterward, like it happened to a different guy. And a character with congenital insensitivity to pain who burns her hand badly and never notices: terrible for her, even though she never hurts once.

So: is there a principle that gets all or most of these? I keep circling something like pain is bad insofar as it moves you — grabs attention, steers behavior, gives you reasons — which handles the asymbolia and adrenaline cases, but then the survivor-guilt woman's ache moves her plenty and I still call it bad in a different way, and the chronic back pain supposedly doesn't move her and I still call it bad. Maybe my morphine verdict or my chronic-pain verdict is just wrong and should go. Tell me what principle you'd actually build, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up (be honest, don't rescue all of them with kludges), and what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: a patient who, because of a memory condition, forgets each pain within seconds, so it never accumulates into dread and every sting is brand new; and my protagonist question — whether a pain that fully reframes itself as meaningful, the burn of hard training welcomed the whole way through, is good, bad, or just neutral.
