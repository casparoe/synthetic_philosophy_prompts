I'm enrolled in an upper-level seminar on forecasting and political judgment, and I help run our department's student forecasting group, which submits semester-long probability estimates on geopolitical questions to an inter-university tournament. Nearly all our disputes come down to the reference class problem. When we forecast whether a particular regime falls within ten years, one member uses the class of personalist dictatorships, another uses post-colonial states in that region, a third uses "states with ongoing insurgencies," and a fourth refuses classes altogether and argues from the inside view. We've started calling this reference class tennis, and I want a way out of it.

Please write a decision procedure the group can actually run in about twenty minutes per question, with no software beyond a calculator and no time to look anything up. It should tell us: how to generate candidate reference classes without cherry-picking one that begs the question; what makes a class acceptable (size, how similar its members really are on the causally relevant dimensions, and whether any of us can actually estimate its base rate from general knowledge — with a rule for what to do when nobody can); when to abandon the outside view entirely and reason from the inside; and how to combine the two when both are available, including whether averaging probabilities across defensible classes is a legitimate default or an evasion. It should also mark honestly the points where the procedure runs out and the forecaster just has to judge.

Then run it on three of our actual tournament questions: whether the EU retains roughly its current membership through 2040; whether a given personalist regime survives the death of its current leader; and whether a long-standing constitutional democracy slides into competitive authoritarianism within a decade. At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like, and you should say which one. Finish with the failure modes: where the procedure is most likely to give us confident garbage, and what would tell us it's misfiring. No citations or reading lists — just the procedure and the reasoning behind it.
