I'm writing my final paper for an animal ethics seminar in which we've just finished the animal-rights material in Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia — his side-constraint framework, his hesitation about how animals fit into a rights structure, and his suggestion that we might treat animals under something closer to a utilitarian balancing while reserving side constraints for persons. I want to spend the last section on a predictive question, and I'd like your help thinking it through.

Assume Nozick were alive today and confronted cultivated meat — real, indistinguishable-at-the-table meat grown from animal cells, with live animals only minimally involved as cell donors. Sketch the most likely Nozickian position, paying special attention to two tensions: (1) whether his framework would treat switching to cultivated meat as morally required, merely permissible, or supererogatory, given that side constraints forbid actions rather than demand maximization; and (2) whether he would permit the state to do anything about it — a ban on conventional meat, or subsidies for cultivated meat — given his account of the minimal state and his insistence that persons may not be coerced even for good ends. In other words: if animals fall under something weaker than rights, but the taxpayers and diners are persons, what does his theory actually license?

Please ground your answer in his stated commitments rather than generic libertarianism, distinguish the places where you are extrapolating from the places where the texts speak directly, and end by noting the strongest objection someone could raise to your prediction — ideally one that draws on some part of his system (the experience machine, his later work, or his remarks on the separateness of persons) that pulls in a different direction.
