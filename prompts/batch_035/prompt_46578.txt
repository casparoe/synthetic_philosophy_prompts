Please argue that the right escape from Sen's liberal paradox is a restriction on which preferences count as inputs to social choice, rather than a weakening of either minimal liberalism or the Pareto principle. For context: I am a recently retired physicist who has spent the past two years reading my way into social choice theory, and I have come to read the paradox the way my old field reads no-go theorems — as an instruction to examine the framing rather than to reject one of the named premises. In thermodynamics, an impossibility result typically dissolves once you notice that a quantity everyone treated as fixed was really a choice of ensemble. My suspicion is that the liberal paradox works the same way: the tacit guilty assumption is that every preference in the profile is a legitimate input to the social ranking, including meddlesome preferences whose entire content concerns what happens inside another person's protected sphere. A society that simply refuses to aggregate such preferences — not merely discounts them, but excludes them from the profile — should be immune to the theorem, and this exclusion arguably expresses exactly what a protected sphere was supposed to mean in the first place. Take seriously the obvious circularity objection: identifying a preference as meddlesome presupposes an assignment of decision spheres, so the restriction presupposes rather than vindicates minimal liberalism. I want an actual answer to that, not a hand-wave about informal norms. Somewhere in the argument, please engage Gibbard's resolution via alienable rights, on which individuals can trade away their spheres — I want to know whether my proposed escape is genuinely different from his or merely a relabeling of it. Sen's prude and libertine deciding who should read a certain risqué book is a fine working example, and you may be as formal as the argument demands; I am not afraid of notation.
