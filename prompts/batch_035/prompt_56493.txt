Every summer my reading group devotes a month to the classic lectures on names and necessity — the book-length transcript of the 1970 Princeton lectures, published in 1980 — and by now one member has become our unofficial referee. I'll call her Mira. She issues verdicts on sentences along three axes: truth or falsity, necessity or contingency, and a priori or a posteriori status. Since she's about to move away, I have started keeping a ledger of her rulings, and next week three final cases are on the docket. Before the session, I'd like your help predicting her verdicts, reconstructing the rule she is implicitly running, and designing the one further case that would settle which rule it is.

Her ledger so far, in the order the cases reached her:

1. Hesperus is Phosphorus. — True, necessarily; known only a posteriori. Astronomers had to discover it, but no world in which both names are in use makes it false.
2. Cicero is Tully. — Same ruling: necessary, a posteriori.
3. Water is H2O. — Necessary, a posteriori. She added that on a Twin Earth whose lakes run with a superficially indistinguishable liquid XYZ, that liquid is not water, however watery it looks and however well it quenches thirst.
4. Gold is the element with atomic number 79. — Necessary, a posteriori.
5. Heat is the motion of molecules. — Necessary, a posteriori.
6. Aristotle might have died at age two. — Allowed. Aristotle might also never have gone into philosophy at all; in those scenarios he would still have been Aristotle.
7. The teacher of Alexander might never have taught Alexander. — This one she refused to rule on cleanly. She said she could hear it as false, almost self-undermining, but could also hear it as true, and she could not say what switches between the two readings.
8. The standard meter stick in Paris is one meter long. — True, and, strikingly, a priori: that is how the term was fixed. Yet contingent: in other conditions, or had history gone otherwise, the stick could have differed in length.
9. 2 + 2 = 4; every bachelor is unmarried. — Necessary, a priori.
10. Hesperus is Hesperus versus Hesperus is Phosphorus. — Both necessary; the first trivial, the second informative. She insists this pair involves no tension in her rulings.

Next week's cases:

Case A. Pain is the firing of C-fibers. She has ruled natural-kind and other theoretical identities (water, heat, gold) necessary a posteriori. Does the same rule carry over to this identity, or does she have a principled reason to hold phenomenal kinds apart from natural kinds? Note that her ruling on heat suggests the extension, while something about the feel of pain apparently pulling the other way is exactly the kind of pressure she tends to notice.

Case B. Suppose archival evidence emerged that the incompleteness theorems were in fact the work of a reclusive mathematician, Schmidt, whose notebooks the man we call Gödel obtained and published under his own name. In that scenario, is "Gödel proved the incompleteness theorems" true, false, or referentless? Her verdict here should interact in an instructive way with items 6 and 7.

Case C. The actual teacher of Alexander, whoever it was, did teach Alexander. — Necessary or contingent? A priori or a posteriori? Note that she stalled on its un-rigidified cousin, item 7, and that the rigidified description seems designed to force the issue she dodged there.

What I would like from you, in order:

1. Identify two or three candidate rules that each fit her ledger so far — for instance, a strict rule on which proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid while bare descriptions are not, a more liberal rule on which descriptions in modal contexts are often heard referentially, and any others you can construct that accommodate all ten rulings. Say what each rule predicts on A, B, and C.
2. Give your best guess at her verdict on each of A, B, and C, and explain what recommends that guess over the alternatives — including, where relevant, what her own precedent is the closest analog to the new case.
3. Name the single further case we could put to her after these three, before she leaves, that would best discriminate between the live rules — a case on which they diverge cleanly, so that her answer, whatever it is, would eliminate at least one candidate rule.

Please treat the ledger as authoritative: your job is to extrapolate from her record, not to grade it against the lectures themselves.
