The exam question read as follows: "Rosenthal introduced the centipede game in 1981. Set out the game's structure and the backward induction argument that the unique subgame perfect equilibrium has the first mover take at the very first node. Explain why this result is widely described as paradoxical, and critically assess the argument: what assumptions about rationality and common knowledge does backward induction require, are those assumptions consistent with what players would learn if a non-equilibrium node were actually reached, and does the paradox show that the backward induction reasoning is unsound?"

The student's answer, reproduced in full: "In the centipede game two players alternate moves; at each node the mover can take a slightly larger share of an increasing pot, ending the game, or pass the pot across the table, where both piles grow. In the hundred-round version the last mover takes, since passing yields nothing more; anticipating this, the mover at the second-to-last node takes, and the argument iterates back through the tree until the first mover takes at the very first node. This is the unique subgame perfect equilibrium, and the reasoning is equivalent to iterated elimination of dominated strategies, since cooperative play is strictly dominated at every node, as in the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, where defection likewise dominates in every period. The result is paradoxical because both players end up with tiny payoffs when, if they could somehow pass along the way, both would be much better off. The paradox is usually blamed on the knowledge assumptions. Aumann, in his 1995 paper, proved that common knowledge of rationality is inconsistent in games like this — the premises of the induction cannot all be true at every node — which is why the argument is unsound. But this dissolves once we think about what a player learns during play: if my opponent passes, I have learned she is irrational, so the rational response is to pass as well and collect later. Hence rational players will pass for many rounds, the backward induction prediction fails, and the so-called paradox is really just a false prediction. Experiments confirm this: most subjects pass, and they earn more than they would by following the equilibrium."
