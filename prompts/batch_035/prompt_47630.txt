I help run a tiny grant program that can support about three early-career philosophers for two years each, working on epistemology broadly — specifically the cluster around what people call the value problem for knowledge, the old Meno puzzle: if a true belief gets you to Larissa just as well as knowledge does, what's the extra value of knowledge? My board has asked me to put together a shortlist of the open questions in this area that we should actually fund, and honestly I'm torn about how to rank them. Some candidates I keep going back and forth on: whether the value of knowledge over true belief is instrumental or somehow constitutive; whether understanding is a different kind of good than knowledge (some people think the Meno problem is easier or harder for understanding, I forget which way that goes); whether virtue-theoretic "credit" accounts really solve the problem or just relocate it; and then there's the more practical angle — whether the whole debate has any implications for how much we should care about testimony, expert deference, or education, or whether those connections are oversold.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Pick what you think the main open questions actually are (feel free to drop or add to my candidates — I don't trust my own list), rank them, and explain the ranking: which ones are upstream of the others, which are tractable for two-year projects by people early in their careers, which are neglected vs. already crowded, and which are overrated in the sense that settling them wouldn't change much else. For the ranking criteria themselves, tell me which you're using and where they conflict — e.g. if the most important question is also the least tractable, say how you'd weigh that. Also flag which of the alleged practical implications (education, testimony, whatever else you think people claim) genuinely follow from the debate and which don't.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the ranked list, no preamble and no restating my question back at me — then give the reasoning. As you go, indicate how confident you are in the various claims, and at the end single out the point you're least sure about; my board will want to know where the reasoning is on solid ground and where it's a judgment call, since the whole shortlist is going to rest on it.
