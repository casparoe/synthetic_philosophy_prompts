Quick bit of background so you know what I'm asking about: I help run a reading group that's mostly history grad students plus a couple philosophy people, and we've spent over a year on the objectivity-in-history question. In that time I've come to realize nobody has moved an inch — not the empiricists, not the Carr people, not the one narrativist holdout — and I'm no longer interested in who's right. I want a diagnosis of why this argument, which is like 150 years old at this point, never goes anywhere.

The positions, so you can engage the actual views and not caricatures:

1. The Rankean empiricists. The past happened, it left traces, the archive is the arbiter. Ranke's slogan was that history should be told "wie es eigentlich gewesen" — usually rendered as showing the past as it really happened, though there's a whole running sub-argument about whether his word eigentlich means something closer to "essentially," which, if you need proof this debate has calcified, is itself something people still argue about. For this camp, objectivity means fidelity to sources, footnotes are the method, and historians who ignore that are just writing fiction with a bibliography.

2. The Carr side. Carr's point was that there are no facts without selection: before the historian arrives, the past isn't a story, it's a warehouse, and every book is the product of a particular person asking particular questions for particular reasons. So the Rankean ideal isn't a method, it's a fantasy — and the honest response is to size up the historian before you size up the facts.

3. The narrativists, going back to Hayden White. Their claim isn't about research at all — they'll happily concede you can establish what happened. The claim is about composition: once you have your established facts, arranging them into a story — this one as tragedy, that one as progress, the same events plottable as comedy — is a literary and moral choice that the evidence doesn't settle. On their telling, the story-level of a history book is never answerable to the archive in the way the footnotes are.

And then there's the silent majority: working historians who think the whole fight is beneath them, keep doing archival research, and answer the theorists by publishing another monograph.

Here's the specific way I think it's stalled, and what I want diagnosed. After the theory wars of the eighties and nineties, the camps basically stopped engaging — the theorists write for journals historians don't read, the historians respond with more sources. When they do meet, it's the same three moves forever: the empiricist says the difference between them and a denier is that they have the sources and the denier doesn't; the narrativist says they're conflating research with writing; the Carr people ask who's asking, and why. No one ever concedes anything, and no one can say what would make them.

My own guess, offered as just a guess: the word "objectivity" is bundling together three seperate questions — (a) can we know what happened, (b) can the historian keep their own perspective out of the account, and (c) is the narrative shape of a history book answerable to evidence at all. The Rankeans care about (a) and think they've won it outright; Carr was always really about (b); and the narrativists are only ever arguing about (c), usually while conceding (a) and (b) for the sake of the argument. If that's right, most of the "debate" is people answering different questions under one word, and the only genuinely open fight is the small one about whether emplotment is evidence-sensitive. But I could be wrong — maybe it's a real substantive disagreement about what counts as the data, because the two sides are literally studying different objects: for the working historian the data are traces in the archive, while for the narrativist the data are finished books. At that point it's not even one discipline, and it's no wonder they can't settle anything.

So, questions for you: What kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, substantive, two disciplines accidentally sharing a name? Ground the diagnosis in what each side says, not in the textbook version of them. And then tell me honestly what would count as progress, if anything. I keep circling one episode where the whole thing got cashed out operationally: the Irving/Lipstadt trial in London around 2000, where a court had to draw the line between legitimate reinterpretation and falsification — and afterwards both camps claimed vindication, which is either evidence that the question got settled and nobody noticed, or evidence that a court can decide a fraud case without ever deciding the philosophy. Is there a distinction, a kind of case, a piece of evidence, that would actually move someone? Or should I conclude the sides are answering different questions, stop trying to referee, and let the reading group split into two?
