I'm a senior software engineer at a company that builds dispatch and routing software for a fleet of autonomous delivery vehicles, and I sit on our Architecture Review Board. I have no formal philosophy training, so please keep the memo readable for a technical audience and briefly explain any philosophical terms of art you use.

A proposal is before the board, and we're deadlocked. Background: our current planner module, given a batch of pickup and delivery requests, enumerates candidate routes and returns the cost-minimizing one. Under peak load it misses its 250 ms planning deadline (p99 latency is around 900 ms), which forces fallback heuristics everyone dislikes. The proposed replacement is a satisficing module: instead of searching for the best plan, it returns the first plan whose estimated cost comes in below an aspiration threshold initially set at 85% of the estimated optimum. The proposal also includes what it calls aspiration annealing: the threshold is lowered step by step as elapsed planning time grows, so under time pressure the system commits to progressively more mediocre plans rather than miss the deadline. A post-hoc regret audit would log every case where a cheaper plan was discovered after commitment, for later threshold tuning.

The board splits three ways. The performance engineers support it on straightforward grounds: a good-enough plan delivered on time beats the best plan delivered late, and the numbers back them. Our safety lead opposes it on principle, not on the numbers: he argues that a system which knowingly settles for a worse plan when a better one was available at modest extra cost is making a normative error, not just an engineering tradeoff — he calls the design an institutionalized bias toward mediocrity — and he points out that aspiration annealing means the definition of good enough silently degrades exactly when it matters most, with the audit only telling us after the fact. A third member thinks the whole dispute is confused: on his view, satisficing search is just optimization with a search-cost constraint folded in, the threshold is the constraint, and therefore there is no philosophical question here at all, only an engineering one about where to set the constraint.

For this task, please take on the role of a philosopher of rational choice and practical reason who has advised engineering organizations on decisions of this kind, and who writes decision memos for a living. Think carefully before drafting — I'd like you to reason through the problem in a preliminary brainstorm (you can do this in your own working, or show it to me briefly before the memo): lay out the considerations for and against, identify the single strongest consideration on each side, and decide which should win. Then draft the memo itself.

The memo should:

1. Give the board an actionable bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — and if the recommendation is conditional, the conditions must be concrete and checkable (e.g., specifics about how the threshold is set and adjusted, what the regret audit must trigger, what gets monitored, on what schedule), with a time frame for each condition coming into force.
2. Engage the safety lead's strongest point honestly rather than dismissing it, and say what, if anything, would have to be true for his position to be right.
3. Settle the third member's deflationary claim, which the rest of us aren't equipped to adjudicate: does satisficing collapse into constrained optimization once search costs are priced in, such that the only question is where to set the threshold — or is there a residual normative difference between accepting a plan because it is good enough and accepting it because, all costs considered, it is optimal? I'm told this is a genuine live question rather than a settled one, so please give both readings a fair hearing and explain in plain language what turns on it for our decision.
4. Address the aspiration annealing mechanism specifically: whether a standard that weakens under time pressure is a defensible form of bounded rationality or a design defect, and what the memo's recommendation implies for it.

Please keep the memo to roughly 700–900 words, formatted as a document the board could circulate before next month's meeting, and close with a one-paragraph plain-language summary I can send to colleagues who won't read the whole thing.
