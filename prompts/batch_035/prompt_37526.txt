I've come to accept the moral hedging picture of decision-making under fundamental moral uncertainty: when you hold credences over first-order moral theories, you should choose the act with the highest expected choiceworthiness across those theories, with the theories' verdicts made commensurable by intertheoretic value comparisons — the machinery behind proposals like variance normalization or Borda-style aggregation. I'd like a serious map of what would count against this position. Not only the familiar worry that intertheoretic comparisons of value are themselves ill-defined, but also the deeper structural problems: that some theories in one's mixture may deny that the rival theories have genuine choiceworthiness values at all, so that there is nothing to take an expectation over; that hedging seems to be ruled out by certain theories in the mixture (a deontological view that says uncertainty about the true theory is no excuse for violating its side constraints); and that the decision-theoretic analogues of the view have known failure modes around averaging incommensurable quantities. Tell me which intuitions the view stands or falls with — for instance, the intuition that moral compromise is like ordinary compromise between people, versus the intuition that one should simply act on one's best-guess theory as if it were true. Which of the potential defeaters would a committed hedger concede as real problems, which would they dismiss as resting on a confusion, and which would they dissolve by qualifying the view — for example, by restricting the framework to theories that are already cardinalist and additive? Finally, give an honest assessment of whether the position, once qualified in these ways, has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a fatal flaw for a normative framework or an acceptable feature of theorizing at this level. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about what observations or arguments would move you most if you were the hedger.
