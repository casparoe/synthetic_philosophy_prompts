My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Putnam's Twin Earth, and I want to know whether the position I ended up defending is actually defensible. As I understand the setup, an Earthling and her molecule-for-molecule duplicate on a planet whose lakes are filled with some other clear, potable liquid are psychologically identical, yet their word "water" refers to different stuff, so meaning must depend partly on the environment rather than entirely on what is in the head — and, importantly, this held even before chemistry identified water as H2O, so the difference in reference can't be chalked up to a difference in what the two speakers know. Here is where I got into trouble. I argued that if what my words refer to is fixed by my surroundings, and no two speakers ever occupy exactly the same surroundings, then each of us attaches a slightly different meaning to every word: strictly speaking, no two people have ever meant the same thing by "water," "arthritis," or anything else, and every successful conversation is really a lucky overlap of near-identical but distinct meanings. My friend said I had proven too much — that Putnam's whole point was that meanings are public and shared, and that my reading turns externalism into word-by-word solipsism, which is closer to the opposite of what the thought experiment was supposed to establish. We never resolved it, and I can see how she thinks I've smuggled back a private language through the environment. So, settle this for me: does semantic externalism, properly understood, imply that individual speakers never mean quite the same thing by the same word, and if it doesn't, where exactly does my inference from "reference depends on the speaker's environment" to "meaning varies from speaker to speaker" go wrong? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
