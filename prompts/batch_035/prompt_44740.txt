Next week I'm leading the practical session of my philosophy of mind seminar, on whether reinforcement learning agents could suffer — specifically, whether a reward signal could constitute valence rather than merely steer behavior, as against the picture suggested by Silver and Singh's reward-is-enough hypothesis, on which reward is just a control signal. There are fourteen of us and seventy-five minutes, and the tutor wants an activity rather than a presentation. My idea is to make the participants be the agent for a while: navigating some kind of staged environment in which the only information they receive about what they are doing is a scalar score rising or falling, so they can feel the difference between the score increasing and something good happening to them — and then examine whether that felt difference tells us anything at all about the RL systems the seminar is about. Design the activity for me in exactly five numbered items: (1) the rules, materials, and running of the environment, including exactly what is hidden from the players; (2) the moment at which the experience is supposed to do the philosophical work, and why; (3) the debrief, mapping what happened in the room onto the reward-as-valence versus reward-as-control-signal question, including where a Dennettian functionalism and an illusionist view of pain would each press on the analogy; (4) the deepest disanalogy between the players and an actual RL agent that the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply verbally; and (5) the most predictable way it will fail the first time it is run, with how I should respond. Each item should be one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, and make definite choices throughout rather than laying out alternatives for me to pick between.
