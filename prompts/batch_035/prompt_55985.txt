A few friends and I were arguing about how appellate courts actually work, and we hit something I can't get out of my head. Apparently this kind of situation has genuinely come up in real litigation: a three-judge panel hears a contract dispute, and finding the defendant liable requires answering yes to two questions — first, was there a valid contract at all, and second, did the defendant breach it. Judge A says yes and yes, so liable. Judge B says there was a contract but no breach, so not liable. Judge C says there was no valid contract, but that if there had been one, the defendant's conduct would have counted as a breach, so not liable. Now count noses: a majority (A and B) thinks there was a valid contract, and a majority (A and C) thinks the conduct amounted to a breach. So premise by premise, the court as a body believes both things needed for liability — yet a majority (B and C) votes not liable. Depending on whether you aggregate over premises or over the bottom line, the same panel produces opposite outcomes.

What struck me is that legal systems seem totally unbothered by this and happily use both aggregation styles in different places. Juries in criminal cases usually return a single general verdict. But there are also special verdicts and verdicts with interrogatories, where the jury answers a list of factual questions one by one and the judge draws the legal conclusion from their answers — which is basically premise voting. And in a fully reasoned appellate opinion, the court is supposed to state its reasons, which again treats the court as if it had one coherent set of premises supporting one conclusion. So my question: what does this whole bundle of practices presuppose about what a multi-member court even is? Does the special-verdict practice presuppose that the panel is a single rational agent whose collective view has to hang together logically, while the general verdict presupposes something more like the court being just a device for registering the verdicts of the individuals on it? And can both presuppositions be true of the same institution at the same time?

A few things I'd want an answer to actually address. First, is there a real difference between saying the court has beliefs and saying the court is just three people whose votes get crunched by a rule — and does either aggregation method strictly require one of those pictures, or would both be compatible with either? Second, does anything here change once you notice that judges aren't just expressing preferences, they're giving public reasons that get written down and become precedent — does the practice of writing precedents presuppose a corporate reasoner in a way raw voting doesn't? Third, tell me where the practice is just philosophically underdetermined, i.e., spots where several different views of collective judgment would all license what courts actually do, so you can't read a single implicit theory off the institution. I don't need any case names or citations, just the reconstruction of what the institution is implicitly committed to.
