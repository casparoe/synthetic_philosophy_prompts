I'm preparing a unit on self-knowledge for a graduate seminar and want to put Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day to work as a test case for the debate over the reliability of introspection. The confabulation literature in psychology — split-brain work, Nisbett and Wilson on verbal reports of mental processes — shows people fluently narrating motives that their reports misdescribe, and philosophers in this area often distinguish unreliability about occurrent experience from unreliability about one's own attitudes and motives. Please read Stevens's narration through that apparatus.

First, classify his characteristic self-reports: his account of the evening Miss Kenton announces her engagement, which he ascribes to the strain of the day; his framing of the evening his father dies upstairs while he remains at his post during the conference, which he later weighs as evidence of his dignity; his treasured memory of the evening of silver-polishing with Miss Kenton. Which failures are experiential, which attitudinal — and are some better described as conceptual, cases where the report goes wrong not because access fails but because he lacks the categories under which to report what he feels? Second, tell me how the fact that he narrates in 1956, decades after the events, complicates the classification: how much of this is unreliable introspection at the time, and how much is distortion in retrospective reconstruction?

Third, treat the ending honestly. On the bench at Weymouth pier, Stevens tells a stranger that he gave his best to Lord Darlington and that what remains of his life is shadowed by a sense of waste, and he resolves to master the art of bantering with his new American employer. Is that accurate introspection finally arriving, one more confabulation in a new key, or something the novel deliberately leaves undecidable — and can the philosophy of introspective reliability as it stands even distinguish those? I'd also like a fair account of where the novel resists a wholesale confabulation reading, since some of his retrospective admissions do seem accurate. And I'd like a closing claim about what the novel contributes to this debate that the experimental literature cannot: something about the long-run cost of a self-narration that keeps a person functioning, and perhaps about confabulation's adaptive side, which the experiments have no way of registering. Since we will not have the text to hand in the seminar, please anchor the reading in the novel's best-known scenes and paraphrase rather than quote.
