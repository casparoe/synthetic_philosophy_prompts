I teach a political theory survey and this term I'm doing a week on Machiavelli on fortuna and virtù — the river metaphor in chapter 25 of The Prince, fortune as a flood that destroys things unless you build the dykes in calm weather, virtù as whatever it is that lets a prince do the building, plus all the trouble of keeping those concepts from collapsing into plain "luck" and "skill". I want to sneak a film night in and show the Coen brothers' No Country for Old Men, because it seems to me the film is basically a two-hour argument about exactly this pair of ideas. Anton Chigurh works as fortune personified — the coin tosses, the way he insists he simply is what arrives, the car crash at the end that not even he sees coming — while Llewelyn Moss is the resourceful man who improvises brillantly and still loses, and Carson Wells is nearly the Machiavellian expert who knows precisely what he is up against and it does not save him either. And then there is Sheriff Bell's opening monologue about the old-timers who did not even carry guns, which reads to me as the anti-Machiavellian position: fortune is not half of things, it is all of things, and the only virtù left is the humility to accept it.

Give me a reading of the film through the Machiavelli. Specifically: (1) is Chigurh better read as fortuna itself or as a virtuoso, and does the reading change if the coin tosses make him an agent of chance rather than chance itself? (2) does the film actually refute the chapter 25 picture, where fortune governs half and preparation governs the other half, or does Moss lose precisely because he lacks virtù — he keeps doing the impulsive thing, going back to the desert with water, baiting Chigurh on the phone — rather than because virtù is impotent? (3) what does Bell's decision at the end say about whether the film thinks Machiavelli's whole question, can we arm ourselves against fortune, is even the right question to ask? And be honest about where my reading strains: the film is set in a world with moral and religious overtones (the title comes from Yeats, and Bell keeps circling back to what he thinks he has failed to face) that Machiavelli's fortuna does not obviously carry.

I'll be using your answer to build discussion questions for students who have read The Prince but are sceptical that any of it survives contact with the modern world, so tell me which scenes to point them at and where a good student is likely to push back on this framing. Answer in British English please, and keep it a reading of the film rather than a lecture on Machiavelli with film examples bolted on.
