I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I've been trying to work out whether Ellsberg-style models of ambiguity actually earn their keep as descriptions of high-stakes political decisions, or whether they only ever get tested in the lab. The episode I want to use is the assessment of Iraq's weapons of mass destruction in 2002-03 and the decision to invade. Please analyse it as a case study in ambiguity aversion, and tell me where the mapping works and where it breaks.

The salient facts, so we're working from the same page: the inspectors left Iraq in 1998 and did not return until late 2002, so the 2002 US National Intelligence Estimate was built on a four-year data gap, a single high-value human source in the biological weapons programme later known to have fabricated his reporting, and a disagreement among agencies about whether imported aluminium tubes were suited to centrifuge enrichment (the State Department's own intelligence bureau dissented). The estimate nonetheless expressed high confidence in the existence of chemical and biological programmes. The Blix and ElBaradei inspection teams in early 2003 found no programme, the invasion went ahead anyway, and the Vice-President was reportedly operating on what became known as the one percent doctrine — treating even a one-in-a-hundred chance of a nuclear handoff to terrorists as requiring action.

Here is what I want from the analysis. First, a disciplined mapping: who counts as the decision-maker, what are the available acts, what are the states of the world, and crucially which parts of the problem were genuinely ambiguous rather than merely risky — the probability of WMD given the source base, the probability of finding them if inspections continued, and the outcome distribution of the war itself are candidates, and they need not fall on the same side of the Ellsberg line. Second, an honest verdict on what maxmin expected utility or a smooth-ambiguity model predicts here and whether the actors' behaviour matches: is treating an unknown probability of a threat as if it were high the paradigm of ambiguity-averse worst-case reasoning, or is choosing to invade, with its deeply ambiguous consequences, an ambiguity-seeking act that the theory can only accommodate by shifting the reference point into the loss domain? Third, something about the institutional layer: the intelligence estimate took a mess of unknowns and emitted a first-order probability judgement, high confidence, as though the unknown probability were known. What does the Ellsberg framework say about an organisation that behaves this way — is this ambiguity aversion, or a failure to register ambiguity at all, or a deliberate conversion of the ambiguous urn into a risky one for the consumer's benefit? Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory itself: whether it anticipated reference-dependent ambiguity attitudes, whether it has anything to say about the value of the inspections (which, on the theory's own terms, were an experiment that could turn an ambiguous urn into a known one, and were cut short before the draw), and where the theory is simply silent. I'm less interested in the geopolitics or the morality of the war, which I know well, than in whether the ambiguity-aversion literature gains or loses credibility from this episode, so please keep the focus on the decision-theoretic machinery and be explicit about any point where you think the mapping I've proposed is tendentious.
