OK so I'm a journalist, I usually cover physics and bio stuff for a general outlet, but I got assigned a story about a negligence case and now the two experts I interviewed are fighting over email and CCing me, and honestly I can't tell who's winning. The case: a night-shift nurse at an understaffed hospital apparently didn't administer a scheduled antibiotic, the patient deteriorated, and the question for my article is whether it's accurate to write that the missed dose *caused* the deterioration.

The philosopher says obviously yes: if she'd administered it, the patient wouldn't have deteriorated. That's causal dependence, case closed. The epidemiologist says hold on — the patient's non-deterioration also counterfactually depended on the pharmacy having stocked the drug, the manufacturer having shipped it, the schedulng software not crashing, none of which we'd call causes of the survival. So counterfactual dependence can't be the whole story, otherwise everything that had to go right is a cause.

The philosopher replied that this is why we pick out the *abnormal* links in the chain — a nurse skipping a scheduled dose is the salient abnormality, and that's how we single her out from the pharmacy and the manufacturer. The epidemiologist said "abnormal" and "salient" are features of us, the observers — what we expect, what we're on the lookout for — so on that view whether something is a cause depends on who's asking. Fine for a headline maybe, but then "caused" isn't a fact about the world, it's a fact about our expectations, and a headline asserting an objective causal fact would be quietly asserting something that doesn't exist.

The philosopher came back with: by that logic every causal statement in science is observer-relative, since every causal claim involves some contrast class and some background held fixed. She caused it *in contrast with* administering the dose, *against a background* where the drug was in the cabinet and the software worked. The epidemiologist never disputes any of that — he just keeps widening the background until everything looks flat. So the real fight is over which background counts as the default.

The epidemiologist's latest reply is that defaulting to a background where the nurse does her job is smuggling in the conclusion — it takes the world to be one where she acted properly, which is exactly what's in question, so no surprise she comes out looking like the cause. They've now both asked me separately who I think is right, which feels like a trap.

My editor wants the piece to say plainly that the missed dose caused the deterioration. Can you go through their exchange move by move, tell me who's right at each step (not just who wins overall), whether there's anything both of them are missing, and whether the sentence my editor wants is actually defensible or whether I should push back on it?
