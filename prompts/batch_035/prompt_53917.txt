I am a retired physicist — thirty-five years in condensed-matter physics, most of them on superconductivity — who has spent the last few years reading metaphysics, having concluded that I owed myself the questions I postponed for a career. I have drafted an essay that attempts to say, in philosophical vocabulary, something I believed throughout my working life without articulating it. I would like expert comments on it, and I will tell you up front what kind of comments I want: a committed verdict, not a balanced survey. Please tell me where the argument is wrong, which of its steps is weakest, and what I should do about it, rather than laying out the live options and leaving me to choose.

I'd like you to take the role of a professional metaphysician with a genuine command of physics. Please think carefully before writing; it may help to begin with a short internal sketch of the essay's logical structure — its premises, its inferences, its conclusions — so that your criticisms land on specific steps rather than on the general spirit. Bluntness is welcome. I am seventy-four; I have time to revise but not to be flattered.

The draft, condensed here (the full version runs longer, but every substantive move is included):

Title: "Dependence Without Dispensability: A Physicist's Attempt at an Ontological Alphabet"

1. Throughout my career I asserted, and believed, that superconductivity is nothing over and above the collective behavior of electrons — a sentiment continuous with the old line, attributed to Rutherford, that all of science is either physics or stamp collecting. Yet in practice I explained a Josephson junction using macroscopic variables, and would have regarded an attempted explanation that solved the many-body Schrödinger equation from first principles as a check, not as the explanation. The practice encodes an ontological thesis I never stated: the higher-level facts depend on the lower-level ones, but dependence is not dispensability. The higher-level vocabulary is not a placeholder awaiting elimination; it is where the explanations live.

2. I gather that philosophers regiment the dependence I mean by grounding: chemical facts hold in virtue of physical facts. And I gather that grounding is meant to be stricter than mere supervenience, since supervenience carries no direction — a thermodynamic macrostate necessitates a range of its micro realizations just as much as those realizations necessitate it, and necessitation alone cannot tell us which depends on which. Something must supply the direction.

3. My proposal: the direction is inherited from the asymmetry of the dynamics. Given the initial microstate and the equations of motion, the later macrostate is necessitated; never the other way around. So the temporal arrow of the dynamics supplies, for free, the direction that supervenience lacks. Grounding, in inter-theoretic cases, is law-governed necessitation running along the arrow of time.

4. A corollary I accept, somewhat to my own discomfort: grounding is transitive. If chemical facts are grounded in molecular facts and molecular facts in facts about electrons and nuclei, then chemical facts are grounded in microphysical facts directly, transitivity or not — the intermediate levels drop out of the dependence order, and their apparent autonomy is a fact about our explanatory and computational limitations, not about what depends on what. When my colleagues say you cannot get superconductivity out of particle physics alone, I now think they are making an epistemic point while seeming to make an ontological one.

The comments I would value most, in order:

(a) A definite ruling on thesis 2/3 — the dynamical account of grounding's direction. It is the part I am least sure of and the part I suspect a metaphysician will find either trivially wrong or quietly a different relation than grounding is supposed to be. If it is wrong, say so plainly and tell me what to replace it with.

(b) A definite ruling on thesis 4 — the transitivity claim and the divorce it forces between dependence and explanation. I want to know whether that divorce is coherent, not merely whether it is fashionable.

(c) An assessment, again committed, of whether the essay as it stands would be of any interest to philosophers, or whether it recapitulates, in worse prose, a position already familiar from the literature on physicalism and the special sciences.

Please do not append a reading list or bibliography; I am more interested in your judgment than in citations, and I can find the literature myself.
