I've been asked to run a ninety-minute session on intuitionistic logic and constructivism for our department's cross-disciplinary seminar — mostly philosophy grad students and software engineers with no logic beyond an intro course — and I want to walk in with a plan, not just an explanation. The occasion is telling: last weekend my closest friend, a card-carrying set theorist and mathematical platonist, and I argued for four hours about the law of excluded middle. His position, as best I can reconstruct it, was that intuitionists aren't proposing an alternative logic at all but simply conflating the truth of a mathematical statement with our ability to know it — that "not yet proven" doesn't mean "not true," and that refusing to assert P or not-P amounts to a kind of intellectual fainting fit rather than a principled stance. I think that's wrong, or at least badly aimed, but the argument convinced me I don't actually understand the position well enough to articulate *where* it goes wrong, which is exactly the gap I need to close before standing in front of the seminar. So please plan the teaching sequence for me rather than lecturing me on the content itself.

What I want from you: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — for instance, whether I can even mention the meaning of the connectives before the audience has some grip on what a proof *is* for the constructivist, or whether the "truth" talk has to be defused first so nobody spends the whole session waiting for the shoe to drop on which statements are "really" true; the one concrete example that should carry the whole session and what it must do — I'm tempted by the classic routine showing that there exist irrationals a and b with a^b rational, proved by cases on an unproved disjunction, since it makes non-constructivity sting, but I'd like you to tell me whether that example earns its keep or whether it smuggles in a side-issue; the misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or form, and where in the sequence each should be headed off — my guesses are "intuitionistic logic is just three-valued logic," "the intuitionist thinks some instances of excluded middle are outright false," "it's all really about infinity," and my friend's own line that it's psychology dressed as mathematics, but I suspect there are others I haven't met; and finally a check question I can pose at the end that would distinguish someone who has genuinely grasped the position from someone who has merely stopped resisting it.

Two more things. First, tell me explicitly what to refuse to cover in ninety minutes and say I'm refusing — Kripke semantics, Brouwer's more mystical commitments about the creative subject, the double-negation translations, realizability all strike me as over the horizon, but push back if one of them is load-bearing for a first pass. Second, my friend will almost certainly show up, and he'll raise the objection that a proof of excluded middle is "trivially available" for any decidable predicate and that the intuitionist's refusal about undecidable cases is just a confession of ignorance. Tell me where in the sequence — not at the end, I hope — I should build in something that absorbs that objection, so his intervention lands on prepared ground rather than derailing things.
