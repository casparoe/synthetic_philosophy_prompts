I'm a graduate student taking a seminar on decision theory, and a debate that keeps resurfacing in our sessions has stalled in a way I find genuinely puzzling. The case is the absent-minded driver, introduced in a paper by Piccione and Rubinstein in the American Economic Review in 1997, though nothing below depends on the details of the paper — here is the setup as we covered it in seminar. A driver heads home at night along a highway with two identical intersections, and he must exit at the second one. At each intersection he chooses CONTINUE or EXIT, but he is absent-minded: when he reaches an intersection he cannot remember whether it is the first or the second, and from his perspective each intersection is equally likely to be the one he is at. The payoffs are as follows: exiting at the first intersection gets 0 (a motel in a bad part of town); continuing past both intersections gets 1 (he ends up on the outskirts); continuing at the first and exiting at the second gets 4 (he arrives home). Ex ante, the best plan is plainly to exit at the second intersection, for a certain 4. But when the driver evaluates what to do while standing at an intersection, believing he is at the first or the second with probability one-half each, CONTINUE looks better than EXIT (expected payoff 2.5 versus 2), so that calculation tells him to continue even at the second intersection; and if one instead asks which mixed policy — continuing at each intersection with some fixed probability — maximizes the ex ante payoff, the answer comes out to 4/3, well below 4. The original paper's point was that the standard tools give inconsistent and even self-undermining recommendations in this case, and nearly three decades of proposed fixes — treating the driver as a sequence of temporally distinct selves, linking the two decisions into a single strategy choice, and so on — have not converged on anything.

When our seminar discussed what the driver should really do, the room divided into camps that, judging by what I can find in online discussions, have been holding exactly the same positions for years without anyone moving. One camp holds that the only honest answer is 4/3 or thereabouts: absent a commitment device, the driver at an intersection controls only that one choice under fifty-fifty beliefs, and recommending 4 smuggles in a fantasy of precommitment that the setup does not allow. A second camp holds that ex ante policy evaluation is the correct criterion, so the answer is 4, and the 4/3 calculations are artifacts of evaluating at the wrong point in the problem. A third camp, associated with people working on updatefulness and functional decision theory, says the whole framing is confused: the two choices are outputs of a single decision procedure, so the question what should I do now and the question what policy should I adopt are one question, and the answer is 4.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about the mathematics at all but about what an agent is taken to control at a decision point — whether the proper object of evaluation is a single action, a whole policy, or one decision function extended across indistinguishable situations — so that the camps are answering different questions under a single heading, and the 4-versus-4/3 fight is partly verbal and partly a dispute over which question deserves the name of rational choice. But I am not confident in this. There might instead be a hidden factual question about the availability and cost of commitment doing the real work, or the disagreement might bottom out in bedrock intuitions about personal identity over time — whether the driver at the two intersections is literally the same agent — that neither side can argue for without begging the question. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: tell me whether this is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two camps answering different questions under one name; support the diagnosis with what each side actually says; and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an agreed convention about when commitment may be assumed, a change of question — or whether no progress should be expected here, and why.
