Title: "Exit Costs, Not Trust Structures: Locating the Pathology of Echo Chambers"

The familiar distinction, due to C. Thi Nguyen, separates epistemic bubbles from echo chambers. A bubble excludes outside voices by omission: relevant testimony simply never reaches members, and popping the bubble — exposing members to the excluded testimony — tends to fix things. An echo chamber is worse: it actively pre-discredits outside voices, so that members hear the testimony but discount the speaker as untrustworthy, dishonest, or corrupted. Exposure doesn't help; it backfires. On this picture, the pathology of the echo chamber lies in its trust structure: outsiders are marked as sources to be ignored.

I want to argue that this locates the pathology in the wrong place. The problem is that the trust structure in question is not distinctive of pathological communities. Consider a healthy community of vaccination researchers. Its members also discount most outside testimony: they dismiss anti-vaccine blog posts, treat celebrity claims as noise, and are not disposed to revise their core views in light of lay objection. And they are right to do so. A community that gave equal initial credence to every outside voice would not be healthy; it would be incapable of cumulative inquiry. So "outsider testimony is pre-discredited" cannot itself be the mark of epistemic pathology — it is a feature of any community that has converged on anything.

What distinguishes a pathological echo chamber, I propose, is not how it treats testimony from outside the wall, but how it treats dissent from inside the wall. In a healthy epistemic community, core commitments are revisable through recognized internal procedures: an insider can challenge a core claim, be heard on the merits, and remain a member in good standing even if wrong — and certainly if right. In an echo chamber, the cost structure is different: dissent from the core triggers identity-level exclusion. The dissenter is not treated as a member making an error but as having revealed themselves as an outsider all along — recruited by the enemy, corrupted, never really one of us. Call this the internal revisability criterion. The wall against outside testimony is common to healthy and pathological communities; the wall around internal dissent is present only in the pathological ones.

The criterion does real work in three places. First, it explains why "pop the bubble" interventions fail against echo chambers without appeal to anything mysterious: since the pathology isn't the exclusion of outside voices, adding more outside voices addresses nothing. The operative intervention would instead be to lower the cost of internal dissent — and this is a testable, design-level claim about community structure. Second, it gives an account of how echo chambers arise: communities whose core commitments have fused with member identity will drift toward high exit costs for dissent even without any manipulative leader, since identity-protective cognition does the work on its own. Third — and this is the part I'm least sure of — it suggests that Kuhn's picture of normal science is healthy precisely because the paradigm functions as a core commitment with cheap internal challenge: anomalies are explained away, outsiders are discounted, but the recognized procedures for internal crisis and revolution are exactly what an echo chamber lacks. My internal reviewer voice says this is too quick, since a critic might respond that a research community stuck in a degenerating research program satisfies internal revisability in form but not in effect, and that my criterion then classifies it as healthy when it shouldn't be.
