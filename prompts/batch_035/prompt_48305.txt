I recently joined a company that builds assistive robots for elder care, and a tension I've found in our pipeline has started to feel like a philosophical question I'm not equipped to name, so I want it reconstructed properly.

Our robots learn what a given person wants by watching them live. We record how the person manages their household — when they intervene in some ongoing process, when they refrain, when they accept a small immediate cost for a long-term benefit — and the learning system infers the reward function under which the observed behavior would come closest to optimal, treating any remaining deviation from optimality as random noise: roughly, the more probable an action is given the inferred preferences, the more the model counts it as expressing those preferences rather than as a slip. The production robot then acts to maximize that inferred reward. Down the hall, however, our safety group spends its days compiling catalogs of systematic human error — the ways people in this population consistently underrate slow risks, defer to whoever is standing nearest, and let habit overrule goals they would endorse on reflection — and we build hard constraints out of those catalogs. So the same company runs two pictures of the person at once: in the learning pipeline, deviation from rationality is random scatter around a rational core, and that core is the thing the robot serves; in the safety pipeline, judgment is systematically skewed, and the skew is what the robot must guard against — sometimes against the person's own displayed behavior.

What I'd like, then, is a reconstruction of what each practice presupposes: (1) the implicit view of practical rationality in the learning pipeline — is it that a person's true preferences are those their behavior would reveal under ideal conditions, that revealed preference simply is preference, or something like a rational core with noise around it? — together with the rival views that practice quietly rules out, for instance views on which a recurring mistake reveals what someone values rather than obscuring it; (2) the implicit view in the safety practice, including what it takes the person's good to be such that behavior does not necessarily track it; (3) whether these two pictures are consistent, or whether one of them must be treated as a local fiction kept alive for engineering convenience; and (4) the question that actually brought me here: what would have to change in the learning pipeline itself — not in the safety layer bolted on afterward — if we took the safety group's picture as the true one, that is, if human deviation from optimality were mostly systematic rather than mostly random? Would it suffice to infer a bias model alongside the reward function, or does that already presuppose that biases are the sort of thing that can be subtracted off to reveal intact preferences underneath — and is that itself a substantive philosophical commitment rather than a technical assumption?

Along the way, please flag where each practice is underdetermined — where several different theories of rationality or welfare would license exactly the same engineering — so that I can tell which of our design choices actually carry philosophical weight and which merely look like they do.

Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
