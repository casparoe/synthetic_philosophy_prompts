Here's something I've been chewing on since a seminar discussion went sideways last week, and I can feel that there's a slip in it somewhere but I can't put my finger on which step. It's basically the problem of old evidence dressed up as a reductio, and I want a verdict, not a survey. The argument:

(1) A piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H for you iff learning E would raise your credence in H — formally, iff P(H|E) > P(H). This is just the probability-raising definition, and I'd rather not give it up, since it's the whole engine of Bayesian confirmation theory.

(2) If you already know E, and know it with total certainty, then P(E) = 1.

(3) But when P(E) = 1, P(H|E) = P(H&E)/P(E) = P(H), for every H. Conditionalizing on what you already know is an idle operation. So a known fact confirms nothing, for anyone who knows it.

(4) Mercury's perihelion precession was established decades before anyone wrote down general relativity. Every physicist who ever took GR seriously already knew the Mercury data. So by (3), that data confirmed GR for no one, ever. The only real confirmation was novel prediction — the 1919 light-bending results.

(5) Except the light-bending results are now a century old too, and every physicist knows them. So by the same reasoning, they now confirm nothing either.

(6) Generalize: any evidence for any theory goes evidentially inert the moment it's absorbed. What we call scientific knowledge is a stockpile of facts that, by the theory's own lights, confirm nothing, and the more you know, the less evidence you have. A fully informed agent would be sitting on a mountain of inert data and, apparently, no evidence for anything at all — not even the claim that the data was ever evidence.

(7) So either (1) is false and confirmation isn't probability-raising after all, or (2)/(3) hide a mistake about what happens when certainty meets conditionalization. The last step, from "confirms nothing now" to "confirmed nothing, period," is where my grad students think the sleight of hand is, and honestly the tense-shift there bugs me too, but I notice that if I block it I'm left saying the Mercury data only ever failed to confirm GR for people who already knew GR — and nobody knew GR before 1915, so it looks like the data still should have confirmed a theory nobody had heard of yet, which can't be how confirmation works.

And one more wrinkle, because I want you to deal with the fix and not just the fallacy. My best student insists that the standard repair — drop E from your knowledge, imagine the credences you'd have had, and condition on E in that counterfactual prior — proves too much in the opposite direction. Take H*, defined as "E, and also this coin lands heads every flip for the next year." In the counterfactual prior, E raises H*'s probability enormously (E is half of H*!), so old evidence confirms the gerrymandered conjunction more powerfully than it confirms GR. If that's right, the fix turns "old evidence confirms nothing" into "old evidence confirms anything you paste a known truth onto," and the cure looks as bad as the disease.
