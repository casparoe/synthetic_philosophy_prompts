I am the conflict-resolution officer at a mid-sized research university. Roughly twice a month I mediate disputes between academic and administrative units: two departments fighting over shared instrument time, a center and a college arguing over who controls a new hire's startup funds, units disputing precedence in naming. Most cases settle, but the ones that don't escalate into formal grievances — months of process, letters from the university counsel, and a dean's ruling that both sides treat as a loss. I have no training in game theory; my sessions are capped at ninety minutes plus a short prep call; the parties are professors and administrators who will tolerate arithmetic but not notation; and my office can propose but not compel, since agreements stick because backing out is reputationally costly, not because anything enforces them.

Some years ago I came across an idea from a corner of decision theory — the literature associated with Caspar Oesterheld and collaborators calls it "safe Pareto improvements" — and it struck me as being directly about my cases. As I understand it, the thought is this: when two parties are headed for a costly conflict, there is often a commitment they could both make, before either knows how the conflict would go, that replaces the fight with a jointly agreed lottery over the fight's possible outcomes. If each party, not knowing how the fight would have turned out, prefers the lottery to the fight, then the swap is safe in a strong sense: it makes both parties better off no matter which way the fight would actually have gone. The toy case is two parties who would litigate at great expense agreeing instead to draw lots over the contested good. I want this idea turned into something I can actually run in a mediation.

Please write the procedure in sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "The principle, in plain terms" — about half a page, written so that I could read it aloud to the parties: no notation, no citations, and it should say precisely what makes a swap safe and what it is not (it is not fairness, and it is not any Pareto improvement whatsoever).

2. "The procedure" — about a page: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the tests a dispute must pass before I even propose a lottery. These should include at least: whether the fight is genuinely costly to both sides; whether each party would prefer the lottery to the fight even conditional on the fight having gone its way; whether both parties can actually commit; whether the draw is publicly verifiable and hard to manipulate; and whether anything the parties value is non-transferable in a way that breaks the substitution. Mark where the procedure terminates with "not applicable, mediate normally" and where it terminates with "this is a values question, hand it back."

3. "Where judgment cannot be eliminated" — about half a page: the points at which the procedure requires my assessment rather than yielding an answer, marked honestly, including how to treat the parties' stated willingness to fight, which may be bluff or positioning.

4. "Worked applications" — about half a page each, running the procedure step by step on the three disputes below.

5. "What the procedure cannot do" — a quarter page of failure modes I should expect.

The three disputes, chosen so the procedure is genuinely tested:

(a) Two research groups each claim priority on a shared spectrometer's peak-season weeks. If neither yields, the grievance freezes access for the semester and both lose the data. My guess is that this is the textbook case; confirm or correct that.

(b) A weak unit and a powerful unit dispute control of a startup fund. The powerful unit's grievance costs are largely borne by the dean, and it enjoys the fight. I suspect that here there is no swap both parties prefer to fighting, and I want the procedure to say so plainly rather than manufacture a compromise. If the best available move is to decline to propose anything and route the dispute upward, the procedure should produce exactly that, even though I will not enjoy delivering it.

(c) A naming and precedence dispute: two units in a joint initiative each insist their name goes first on the grant and the resulting publications. The stakes are symbolic, but the fight is real enough to poison the collaboration. I want to see whether the procedure can handle a case in which what the parties value is not a divisible good — or whether it correctly stops and tells me that the lottery answers the wrong question.

Two further constraints. First, whenever the procedure calls for probabilities, I need a way of proposing them that a party can accept without feeling conned: the choice between equal chances and something weighted by strength of claim is itself a values question, and the procedure should say where that line falls rather than burying it in step four. Second, be blunt in section 5 about the ways my proposing lotteries could go wrong: parties accepting because a lottery feels unserious and then relitigating; repeat players learning to overstate their love of conflict to extract better splits; and my own incentive, as a mediator, to reach any agreement rather than a safe one.
