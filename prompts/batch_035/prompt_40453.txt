I am a program officer at a mid-sized philanthropic foundation making multi-year grants — typically $1–5M over three to five years — in global health, education, and policy research. My committee commits to eight to twelve such grants per quarter, and for each we have a dossier of evidence of wildly varying quality: sometimes a randomized trial, sometimes three anecdotes and a consultant's slide deck. The decision that keeps recurring is not "is the expected value positive" but two prior questions: what kind of uncertainty are we facing at all, and is it worth another six months of evidence-gathering before we commit. Our CEO has asked me to produce a written framework the whole officer team can apply, and she wants it grounded in Keynes's views on probability and uncertainty rather than in the expected-value calculations our consultants keep pushing — she read something about the distinction between a probability and the weight of the argument behind it and was struck by it.

The ideas I want operationalized, as I understand them (correct me where I have them wrong):

1. Keynes held in A Treatise on Probability that probabilities are not always numerical. Some are comparable without being measurable (this is more likely than that), some are not even comparable, and only a narrow class — roughly, those grounded in stable frequencies or symmetry — admits of numbers. A procedure that begins "estimate p" therefore fails at step one for most of what we fund.

2. Distinct from the probability itself is the weight of the argument: how much of the relevant evidence we have compared to the evidence there is to have. A conclusion can be fairly probable on the evidence so far and yet rest on very little, because key evidence is missing. This supplies a principled basis for the "should we wait and learn more" decision: gather more evidence when doing so would raise the weight substantially and costs less than the value of the resulting change in the decision — but only up to the point where the remaining evidence is unobtainable at any price we would pay, after which weight cannot be raised and waiting is pure delay.

3. Where probabilities are non-numerical or incommensurable and weight cannot be raised, Keynes thought in his economic writings that we act anyway — by relying on convention, treating the most recent outcome as representative and the future as resembling the present, and on what he called animal spirits, a spontaneous urge to action rather than a calculated expectation. A framework that pretends this never happens will be gamed by its own users; one that names it can at least discipline it.

Constraints on the deliverable:

- It must be usable by an officer with no philosophy background and no quantitative training beyond a spreadsheet: a sequence of questions asked in order, each with criteria for a sufficient answer, a point at which it returns "do not fund" or "do not fund yet," and marked handoff points where the question is one of values (risk tolerance, how a failed grant looks to our board) rather than facts, so those get escalated to the committee rather than smuggled into the procedure.

- It must handle the three kinds of case we actually see: (a) frequency-grounded risk, e.g., a health delivery program where effect sizes and their distributions are known from many trials; (b) comparative but non-numerical likelihood, e.g., teacher coaching being more likely to raise test scores than another year of curriculum purchase, with no numbers that mean anything; and (c) incommensurable and thin, e.g., seeding a new advocacy organization whose theory of change has never been tested anywhere.

- It should include worked applications to one case of each kind, at least one of which yields a recommendation the officers will dislike. I expect the thin-evidence advocacy case to come out "defer and gather cheap evidence first," and I want the procedure to be honest about when deferral is mere cowardice with a framework on it, given that some opportunities genuinely expire while we deliberate.

- It should state what the procedure cannot do. Where Keynes's own views leave genuine indeterminacy — probabilities that cannot be compared at all — the procedure must not manufacture precision, and it should say in plain terms what the officer writes in the recommendation memo when that happens.

Finally, please flag any place where you think I have misread Keynes — in particular whether the probability/weight distinction really does the work I am assigning it, and whether the material on conventional expectation and animal spirits in his later economics legitimately extends to a grantmaking context at all.
