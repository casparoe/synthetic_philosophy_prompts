I am a PhD student in philosophy of medicine, writing a thesis on the concepts of health and disease, and I serve as the graduate student member of a regional advisory panel that recommends to our statutory health insurer which treatments to cover. Our enabling statute ties eligibility to disease: a treatment can be recommended only if it addresses a disease, or a condition whose burden on the person affected is comparable to that of a disease. The case now before us is misophonia — a condition in which particular everyday sounds, most often other people's chewing, breathing, or tapping, trigger an intense, quasi-phobic reaction of rage or panic, usually followed by shame. Patients describe restructuring their entire lives around avoiding trigger sounds: eating every meal alone, wearing earplugs at family dinners, changing jobs to escape open-plan offices. A patient association has petitioned for coverage of two things: specialist psychological therapy, currently available only privately and at some cost, and audiological assessment for sound sensitivity. Because our statute forces the issue, the petition obliges the panel to take a stand on what misophonia is, and the panel is split along lines I recognize from the literature my thesis works through.

The clinicians' bloc holds that the question is whether something in the person is malfunctioning. On their view, a disease is a part or process failing to perform its biological function, as judged by how the organism is supposed to work; by that standard, they ask whether there is evidence of an auditory or auditory-emotional pathway doing something it should not do, as distinct from an ordinary strong dislike, continuous with common irritation, only more so. They note that no examination finding, imaging finding, or agreed physiological marker separates misophonia from ordinary sound annoyance, and they warn that if intensity of suffering plus life disruption suffices, then grief, homesickness, and the misery of unemployment become diseases, with the bill to follow. The public-health bloc replies that the relevant question is harm: what the condition does to lives. They observe that insomnia, likewise without a lesion, has its own diagnostic codes and is treated; that classifications have historically expanded once the impairments proved real, as with conditions long dismissed as character faults; and that a definition under which a person who cannot sit at dinner with her children for years on end counts as not ill unless a lesion turns up has its priorities inverted. A third member proposes a compromise: cover the psychological therapy only, on the model of treating distress, while declining to say whether misophonia is a disease; a fourth thinks the statute's clause about comparable burden was written for exactly such cases and that we should say so and use it.

I have been asked to draft the memorandum the panel will debate at its meeting in three weeks, the chair having said he wants a document that is more than a tally of intuitions. Please draft it. It should recommend one concrete course of action — refuse the petition, grant it in full, or grant it with conditions and a named review date — and it must give the strongest consideration on the losing side a fair hearing rather than a paragraph of dismissal. It cannot close the matter by declaring that disease is a family-resemblance concept or that the question is merely conventional: the insurer needs a line it can apply to the next petition. If the reasoning yields no general lesson, the memo should say so honestly and explain why this case nevertheless comes out the way it does.
