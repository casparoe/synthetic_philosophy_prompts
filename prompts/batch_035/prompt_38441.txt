I teach a final-year seminar in ethical theory and am setting the take-home final. Please write one essay question on the aggregation of tiny harms — the torture-versus-dust-specks family of cases — together with a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme.

The question must be unanswerable by reciting the standard dialectic. In my experience students do one of three things: they observe that the numbers are so astronomical that the utilitarian answer is obvious; they declare that no number of trivial harms can outweigh a single grave one, gesturing at contractualism or deontological constraints without argument; or they lament the limits of moral intuition. None of that should earn a pass at this level. What the question should test is whether a student can construct an actual principle — some restricted aggregation rule, threshold structure, or claim-based limit — and then discover whether it survives the cases they devise for themselves.

The question should require students to work through at least three cases beyond the canonical one, including the following two that I use in teaching. The ladder: a sequence of choices beginning with two people each tortured for forty-nine years versus one tortured for fifty, then a thousand people each for a year, a million each for a day, and so on down to an astronomical number each suffering something imperceptible — so that any principled answer must say where aggregation fails and why it fails exactly there, rather than merely that it fails. And the penny case: suppose each of the very many affected parties would knowingly and gladly accept the tiny harm in exchange for a trivial benefit, so that it is at least arguable that no one has been wronged at all; the student must decide whether individually waived harms can still add up to anything capable of being weighed against the one grave harm, or whether consent at the individual level dissolves the aggregation problem entirely. The third case should be the student's own construction, and the question should say so.

Two further constraints. First, the question should not ask students to cite literature; it must be answerable from the cases and the reasoning alone. Second, whichever side your model answer defends, it must state the strongest point against it — for the aggregator, that ordinary morality seems to forbid trading one grave harm against any number of imperceptible ones; for the anti-aggregator, that ordinary morality plainly does aggregate elsewhere, as in vaccination programs and safety regulation.

For the marking scheme, give descriptors for a first, an upper second, and a lower second that name the specific moves each band requires, and list the three most common cop-outs with the marks each forfeits.
