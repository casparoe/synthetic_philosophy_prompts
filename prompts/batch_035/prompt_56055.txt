Hi — I write a column on science and ideas for a mid-sized outlet, been doing it about ten years now. Dan Dennett died in April 2024, and I've had a piece sitting in my drafts folder since then that I keep chickening out of, because if I write it badly it reads like a hit job on a dead man. So let me hand you the argument and have you make it as well as it can be made — I want to see it at its strongest before I decide whether I have the nerve to publish.

Here's the thought. In Darwin's Dangerous Idea, Dennett gave us two images I've leaned on in my own writing for years: good explanations are cranes, built up piece by piece out of mindless processes, while bad explanations quietly assume skyhooks — lifters that come down from above, i.e. designers, minds, miracles. And he warned about greedy reductionism: explaining something by leaping straight to the bottom level in one jump, skipping the middle-level machinery, like "explaining" a car by listing its atoms. So far so good, I've used that distinction approvingly in print many times.

But then, in Breaking the Spell, he turns the crane machinery on religion — and here's my hunch, the thing I need you to actually argue: by his own lights, that book is a lot more greedy then anyone admits. Not because explaining religion naturalistically is off-limits — I don't buy that, and I don't want the argument to lean on it. Rather: the mechanism-work that a crane-building explanation of religion would require — how corporate worship persists without everyone believing, how belief-in-belief gets maintained, why people die for doctrines they can't even state — mostly gets gestured at rather than built. The book spends its energy on the case for why religion should be studied scientifically, and comparatively little on the assembly itself. It's as if the crane operator mostly describes the crane.

There's a second hunch I want built in, and I suspect this is the column's actual contribution, if it has one. The move everyone makes (including me, in print, embarrasingly) is to say the book's fault was explaining religion away — the cheap reading, the one religious readers complain about, that any naturalistic explanation of a practice automatically debunks it. I think that's wrong, and the column should say why. A good crane explanation of religion, the kind the cognitive science of religion crowd churns out — over-active agency detection, minimally counterintuitive ideas spreading better than plain or wildly strange ones, costly signalling keeping free-riders out — doesn't debunk anything; it explains how the phenomena are achieved. So the criticism of Dennett can't be the cheap one. It has to be that he promised a crane and mostly delivered a drawing of one, and that the greediness shows up as a matter of tempo, not target: the right level of explanation announced, but the mechanisms sketched at the speed of a man who found his conclusion obvious in advance. If you can make greedy reductionism-as-a-failure-of-tempo-rather-than-of-level precise, that's the spine of the piece.

So what I want from you: a real argument for the claim that Breaking the Spell is greedy by Dennett's own standard, organized so it survives the obvious defenses — that the book explicitly calls itself a sketch, a first salvo, an opening move in a decades-long research program; and that demanding full mechanisms is demanding work no single book could contain. Tell me which of those defenses actually bites and which is a dodge. And at the end, be honest with me about the strongest reply a Dennett defender could give, because I'll be running the column past friends of his and I'd much rather hear it from you than from them.

No citations or page references, please — that layer I'll do myself — and I don't want you inventing quotes. He's dead, and the last thing this piece needs is a fabricated quotation. Around 1000-1200 words is the right size for my slot.
