Next month I'm presenting a paper to a workshop on distributive ethics defending the standard prioritarian claim that prioritarianism escapes the leveling-down objection. My thesis, in two parts: the objection is fatal to telic egalitarianism because the egalitarian must concede that making the better-off worse off, with no one gaining, is in one respect an improvement, whereas the prioritarian never has to say anything of the sort, since her view concerns how well off people are and never how people's levels compare with one another; and the priority weighting is grounded in the absolute badness of certain conditions, so nothing relational enters the foundations of the view. I want the weighting to be an intrinsic fact about the moral significance of a benefit to someone whose life is going badly, not a disguised comparison with how others are doing.

The audience is mixed. Two committed utilitarians who will demand a principled stopping point for the weighting (why a 30 percent premium and not a 300 percent one) and who I suspect will press that even a strictly non-relational view favors what they will call weak leveling down: a transfer from better-off to worse-off that reduces total utility can still count as an improvement once the weighting applies, which they will say is leveling down in everything but name. A pair of relational egalitarians in the Anderson tradition who think the whole prioritarian framework is welfarist bookkeeping that treats persons as bins of wellbeing rather than participants in a relationship among equals. And several uncommitted graduate students, one of whom has been reading Scanlon on why the leveling-down objection may not be the embarrassment it is advertised to be, and will ask why, if Scanlon is right, I should be leaning so much of my case on escaping it.

Please prepare my prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way each member of that audience would phrase them; for each, a reply I can give in about two minutes, a note on where the reply is soft, and a flag for what I should concede up front rather than defend. Tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
