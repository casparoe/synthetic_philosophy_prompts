For some years I have been following the argument between critics and defenders of historical laws, and it has reached a stalemate that I would like diagnosed rather than adjudicated. On one side stand the heirs of Popper's critique of historicism: they argue that social systems are open systems, that predicting a social development changes the conditions under which it unfolds — so that forecasts can be self-fulfilling or self-defeating in a way that physical forecasts cannot — and that no proposed law of historical development, from Plato through Marx onward, has ever survived serious testing. On the other side stand the practitioners of quantitative and cliodynamic history, who model things like the recurrent interaction of population growth, elite overproduction, state fiscal stress, and political instability, and claim to find the same causal pattern recurring across agrarian states centuries apart. They reply that Popper's argument proves too much — that if reflexivity and background-condition-dependence really ruled out laws, they would rule out laws in biology, epidemiology, and ecology, which nobody wants to do — and that a law can be a recurring mechanism operating ceteris paribus rather than an unconditional prediction of a fixed sequence of stages. The two camps publish past each other; each regards the other as having missed the point entirely.

My hypothesis, which I offer only as a hypothesis, is that much of this is a verbal dispute about the word "law" — the Popperian hearing "law of history" as "unconditional prediction of the future course of history," the cliodynamicist hearing it as "robust, repeatable causal mechanism" — and that underneath the verbal layer there is one genuine substantive question, namely whether the reflexivity of human actors actually breaks macro-regularities or is instead itself a regular, modelable part of them. But I may be wrong, and the sides may also be answering different questions under one heading: perhaps the Popperian is really attacking prophecy and large-scale policy planning while the cliodynamicist is really defending retrodictive explanation, so that their claims never collide at all.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Which parts are verbal, which are methodological disagreements about standards of evidence (do fitted recurrent patterns count as findings, or are the histories too few, too selectively coded, and too dependent on the modeler's choices for that?), and which, if any, are genuinely empirical? Is the dispute about the openness of social systems substantive, or is it undermined by the fact that we accept ceteris paribus laws in other complex domains? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a class of cases, a kind of evidence, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed — with reasons, not a survey of the positions.
