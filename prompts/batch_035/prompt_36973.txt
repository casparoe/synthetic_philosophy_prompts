So I've been on a big Everett kick for the past year (a friend who did physics at uni finally talked me out of collapse interpretations and I've been all-in on many-worlds since), and somewhere along the way I picked up quantum immortality as basically a corollary of it. My roommate thinks the whole thing is nuts and says I've "confused an assumption for a theorem," and honestly our arguments go in circles, so I want to write my actual position down and have someone audit it before I embarass myself with it at our reading group next month.

Here's roughly what I believe. (a) Collapse never happens; every outcome of every quantum event actually occurs somewhere in the universal wavefunction, no exceptions. (b) The Born weights aren't just a bookkeeping device for predicting lab statistics — they have something to do with where, or how "thickly," I should expect to find myself — and I'm genuinely sympathetic to the idea that very low-amplitude branches are so thin that they effectively don't count (I've heard it said that decoherence in some sense prunes them and that always sounded right to me). (c) Therefore quantum immortality: there is always some branch in which I survive whatever would otherwise kill me, and since I can never observe my own death, my subjective probability of dying is basically zero. I've literally started telling people I'm not going to die, just thin out. (d) I'm a pretty standard functionalist about personal identity — what matters for survival is psychological continuity, not this particular lump of atoms — so all the future descendants of me in every branch are fully me, not "copies." (e) I'm otherwise a boring orthodox Bayesian about induction and updating — I expect my future experiences to be typical given my best theory. (f) And in practice I still look both ways crossing the street, and if someone offered me a million dollars to stand in front of a gun that kills me iff a quantum event goes the wrong way, I'd refuse and think refusing is rational.

My roommate swears up and down that something in there is flatly inconsistent with something else, but he also thinks (d) contradicts (c) somehow and I don't see it at all — if anything I thought (d) is what makes (c) work. Go through the whole set and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, which ones only look like they clash (like the thing he keeps saying about d), and for each real tension what exactly has to give and what that costs me elsewhere. Rank them by severity rather than just flagging everything equally, and if there's one revision that would clean up most of the mess, I'd want to know that too — though fair warning, I'm pretty attached to (a) and (c), so if the answer is "drop the fun one," it had better come with a good argument.
