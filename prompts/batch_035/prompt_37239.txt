I sit on the standing committee that reviews proposals for new and revised diagnostic categories in our national psychiatric classification — the body that decides, roughly every five years, whether a proposed condition enters the manual, is folded into an existing category, or is rejected outright. Each cycle we receive a dozen or so proposals, mostly from specialist societies and research consortia, each consisting of a one-page summary and a dossier of evidence. The stakes are the usual ones for this kind of work: whatever we accept shapes what clinicians look for, what researchers can get funded, what insurers will reimburse, and what ordinary unhappiness or difficulty gets pathologised. The problem is that we have no shared procedure for deliberation. Our debates drift between statistics, clinical lore, and half-remembered metaphysics about what a "real" disorder is, and the outcome tends to track whoever argues most persistently.

Please write us a decision procedure, grounded in whatever the philosophy of natural kinds actually has to offer — whether psychiatric categories should be expected to have essences at all, whether a category can earn its keep through cluster structure and inductive usefulness rather than an underlying mechanism, whether utility and validity come apart and which we should weight where — but translated into something a committee of clinicians and methodologists with no philosophy background can actually run. The practical constraints are these: each proposal gets a two-hour slot at a quarterly meeting; members can read the dossier beforehand but cannot commission new analyses; any recommendation needs two signatories before it goes to the plenary committee for a final vote; and the procedure must give the same answer run by different members on the same dossier, or say honestly where it cannot.

The procedure should:

1. Set out the questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each — what evidence of clustering, of stability over follow-up, of predictive leverage beyond the defining symptoms would satisfy each question, and what falls short.
2. Say where the procedure terminates early with a recommendation of no — for instance, where a proposed category is just a severity band on a continuum, an artefact of help-seeking or referral pathways, or a restatement of a treatment history rather than a candidate kind.
3. Mark explicitly which steps are empirical, which are conceptual, and which are judgement calls dressed up as procedure — the committee needs to know when it is measuring and when it is deciding.
4. Name the points where the question becomes one of values rather than facts (how much impairment is needed before a pattern becomes a disorder, whether risk without current suffering justifies a category, whose interests the labelling serves), state the value question plainly, and specify to whom it should be handed.

Then run the procedure on three proposals from our current batch:

- A proposal to recognise a distinct condition defined by non-response: failure to improve after two adequate treatment trials, with no other defining features.
- A proposal to split an existing anxiety category into two subtypes on the basis of a neurophysiological measure that correlates moderately with symptom patterns and is heritable, though no mechanism has been established.
- A proposal to add a condition capturing grief that remains impairing well beyond the period our current criteria allow.

I should disclose my own position: I am sceptical of the first proposal and invested in the second, since a colleague helped draft it. I want the procedure run honestly on both, and I want you to tell me plainly if it produces a verdict I will not like. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do — including whether it can settle anything about the deeper question our debates keep circling: whether psychiatric categories can in principle carve nature at its joints, or only divide patients into useful groups for particular purposes, and what follows for a manual built on whichever of those turns out to be true. Please write your answer in British English.
