heard the old "at least the trains ran on time" line at a family dinner last month — my cousin used it to defend strongman governance generally, and everyone nodded like that settled the argument. Since then I keep noticing versions of it everywhere: about Singapore, about the Gulf states, about a mayor here who fixed the roads and got re-elected despite having, let's say, a very flexible relationship with the rules. The more I turn it over the less I know what the sentence is even claiming. Can you break it into the distinct precise theses it could be standing for? My rough guess at the menu: (a) just a descriptive claim about punctuality or delivery, which seems trivially beside the point; (b) a causal claim that order and prosperity require suppressing dissent and pluralism, which is at least in principle testable-ish — is it?; (c) a claim about legitimacy, that delivering goods is what actually grounds a government's right to rule, regardless of how power was obtained; or (d) the genuinely radical thing, that delivering goods *justifies* rule full stop, so a coup that doubles the growth rate is fine. Lay these out from weakest to strongest, say for each whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or flat-out false, and then tell me which reading my cousin and the nodders actually need for their argument to work — because the descriptive one is obviously not enough, and the justificatory one seems to prove way to much (would they accept it about a regime they hate that also delivers?). One more thing if you can: the strongest readings seem to slide between legitimacy as in acceptable-to-outsiders and legitimacy as in owed-obedience-by-subjects, and I suspect half the table was trading on that without noticing. Flag where the slide happens, since that's probably the point I should push on next time it comes up.
