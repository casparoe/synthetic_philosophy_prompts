I advise a small funder that can support about five researchers for three years on conceptual work growing out of the split-brain literature — philosophy and theory, not new neurosurgery or imaging. The area I have in mind is the unity of consciousness as it has been contested since commissurotomy patients were first studied: when the corpus callosum is severed, does one conscious subject become two, or does one subject persist with disrupted access between its parts? The field has spawned questions I find hard to rank, and I'd like your help ordering them. Candidates include at least these, plus anything you think I'm missing: (1) the counting question — what criteria, if any, non-arbitrarily determine how many subjects of experience a given brain supports, and whether "one vs. two" is even the right granularity or should be replaced by degrees of integration; (2) the subcortical question — whether the intact brainstem and remaining subcortical connections ground a residual unity that callosal section leaves untouched, making the putative second subject a partial one rather than a full person; (3) the generalization question — whether results from these patients tell us anything about ordinary brains, e.g. whether the normal mind is better described as a persisting coalition with an interpretive press office than as a unitary self; (4) the epistemic question — how we could ever know, from behavior and report, whether a disconnected right hemisphere has experiences the patient cannot speak about, given that absence of report is ambiguous between absence of experience and absence of access to expression. Please give me a defended ordering: for each question, why it ranks where it does, which others depend on it, whether it is tractable for philosophers working with the existing case literature rather than for neuroscientists running new experiments, and what kind of development over the next few years — a conceptual distinction, a new class of patients, a better behavioral paradigm — would move it up or down. After the prose, append a brief structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "ranking" (array of the questions in your order, each a short label), "criteria" (array of strings naming the criteria you used and one line on each), "most_upstream" (the label of the question the most others depend on, plus a one-sentence reason), "deprioritized" (label of the lowest-ranked question and one sentence on why), and "neglected_by_the_field" (one line naming a question or angle the literature underweights, if any).
