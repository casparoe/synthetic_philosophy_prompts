In a seminar on philosophy of mind I defended epiphenomenalism: the view that phenomenal properties are caused by physical states of the brain but exert no causal influence back on the physical. A classmate replied with an argument that, if it succeeds, seems to show the view undercuts its own evidential basis, and perhaps all knowledge of experience. I cannot locate the flaw, though I am sure there is one, since the conclusion is unbearable.

The argument, as I have reconstructed it: (1) Qualia are epiphenomenal — physical states cause them, and they cause nothing physical in return. (2) My judgment that I am in pain — the belief itself, any sincere report of it, and its storage in memory — is a physical event with a wholly physical causal ancestry. (3) By (1), that ancestry is indifferent to the pain itself: had the quale never occurred, the very same judgment would have been formed, for the same physical reasons. (4) A signal that would have been issued exactly as it was whether or not the condition it reports obtained carries no information about that condition. (5) So my judgment that I am in pain carries no information about whether I am in pain. (6) Whatever carries no information about p cannot be knowledge that p, or even a reason to believe p. (7) Therefore, on epiphenomenalism, no one ever knows, or has the slightest reason to believe, that they are in pain, seeing red, or conscious at all. (8) Yet my knowledge that I am in pain, when I am, is the most certain knowledge I possess; and if (7) held, my belief in epiphenomenalism — itself a belief about my experience — would be held for no reason at all.

Tell me which premise to give up, why it looked innocent when stated, and what I pay for rejecting it rather than the others. I would much prefer to keep (1) and (2): (1) appears to follow from the causal closure of the physical together with my conviction that qualia are not physical properties, and (2) I take to be a straightforward deliverance of science. What I cannot do is see how to break the chain between (3) and (6) without begging the question against my classmate. It also troubles me that (4), if it is the culprit, would seem to generalize to knowledge of abstract objects, since numbers presumably cause nothing either — yet as applied to pain it looks undeniable. Please respond in American English, and keep the response to at most 400 words.
