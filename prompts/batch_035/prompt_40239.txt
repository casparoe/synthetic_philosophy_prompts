I'm a grad student straddling ML and philosophy of mind, and I've gotten into a running argument with a friend who works on classical cognitive science about what interpretability researchers mean when they say a network "has a representation" of, say, a curve detector or a sycophancy feature. When she uses the word "representation," she means something with content — something that can be correct or incorrect about the world, that has a syntax and compositional structure, roughly in the lineage of Fodor's language of thought and Marr's level of representational/computational analysis. When the mech interp people use it, they seem to mean something much more deflationary: a direction in activation space that you can ablate or clamp and get the corresponding behavioral effect. Those strike me as genuinely different concepts that happen to share a word, but I'm not sure whether they're different in a way that matters or just different in level of ambition.

Here's the comparison I want you to actually work through: the interpretability notion of representation versus the classical cognitive-science notion. Are they the same concept at different resolutions, or does one of them lack something the other considers essential (correctness conditions, systematicity, compositionality)? One thing that's nagging me: a sparse autoencoder feature passes the causal-intervention test easily but seems to have no obvious "aboutness" — nothing makes it true or false. And conversely, a classical symbol in a production system has content but nobody ever found you could ablate a single one and see a clean behavioral deficit. A guy in my lab suggested that SAE features might be less like mountains and more like lines of longitude — a real and useful decomposition, but one that partly reflects choices we made about how to carve things up rather than joints the network itself cares about. I don't know if that analogy is fair or if it smuggles in the whole conclusion.

So: compare the two concepts, say explicitly where they come apart and where they coincide, and then tell me which of the two the realism debate in interpretability (people arguing over whether the features we find are "really in there" or artifacts of our methods) is actually about. If interpretability is implicitly committed to the classical notion of representation, that seems like a much stronger and more controversial claim than what the papers usually defend; if it's only committed to the causal notion, then maybe the whole "does the model have its own ontology" question is being oversold. Feel free to be opinionated, I'd rather disagree with you than get a survey.
