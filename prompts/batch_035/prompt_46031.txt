For roughly the past two years I have been reading, entirely on my own, a classical Indian philosophical text: the verses attributed to Nāgārjuna, usually referred to as the root verses on the middle way. I have no academic background — I came to it from a general interest in the question of whether anything has an essence — and I have gradually worked my way to a statement of the doctrine that I can actually defend in conversation. I now want to stress-test that statement, and what I am asking for is specifically what would count against it, not further support for it.

Here is the statement as I have settled it:

1. Nothing possesses intrinsic nature — nothing is what it is "from its own side," independent of conditions, parts, relations, and the conventions by which we sort things out. Everything is what it is dependently.

2. This is supposed to be established by argument, not assumed. The argument form, as I understand it, runs: take anything we might credit with intrinsic nature — motion, the self, fire and fuel — and ask a question that presupposes it exists in that strong way: does the inherently existent thing arise, persist, cease, move, burn? All four possible answers fail — yes, no, both, neither. Since all four fail, the presupposition fails: there is no such thing, and the failure lies not in the world but in how the question was asked. The fire-and-fuel argument is the one I know best: the fire cannot be identical to the fuel it burns, cannot be different from it, and the remaining options collapse as well.

3. I have added a gloss of my own that I have grown attached to: emptiness is the claim that explanation never reaches a self-explaining stopping point. Nothing will turn up that grounds everything else while needing no ground itself — no first term, no final particle, no fact from which all others flow while it simply obtains.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure, in the following shape:

- Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would count against it? Distinguish what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward. Include the classic internal challenge — that if everything is empty, the doctrine of emptiness is empty too, so why assert it — and the standard reply that an empty doctrine can still be used, the way an empty raft can still carry you across. Tell me straight whether that reply works as an answer or works as an immunization.
- Which of these would a committed defender of the position concede as genuinely counting against it, and which would they wave off as begging the question, as secretly presupposing intrinsic nature? I want that split drawn from their side, not just from critics.
- The position appears to predict that no analysis of anything will ever terminate in a self-grounded term, while its rivals predict that some will. Does that give it any real exposure? Or has it been insulated — by the division into conventional and ultimate truth, by the emptiness of emptiness — into something nothing could ever count against? And give me your honest view on whether such insulation is a defect, given that the tradition itself insists the doctrine is not a theory but a raft: something to be used and abandoned, not believed.
- A worry I keep circling: the argument may only work against a very strong notion of essence — one so strong that perhaps no one has ever actually held it, in which case the position is safe but has defeated a strawman. If the notion of intrinsic nature is weakened to something people do hold, the position becomes falsifiable but exposed. Where should that dial be set, and what follows either way?

I am not looking for reading recommendations or sources; I want the reasoning. Please close with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by at most ten words naming the single most serious live threat to the position as I have stated it — or, if you judge that there is none, the words "no live threat."
