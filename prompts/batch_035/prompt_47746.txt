Analyse the continental-drift controversy — from Alfred Wegener's 1912 proposal of the supercontinent Pangaea to the consolidation of the plate-tectonic consensus in the late 1960s — as a contest between Lakatosian research programmes. I know the standard morality tale, in which Wegener the visionary meteorologist was wronged by a blinkered geological establishment that eventually had to concede everything; what I want is a disciplined Lakatosian mapping instead, and then an honest account of where that mapping strains.

Treat mobilism (the drift hypothesis) and the fixist mainstream as rival programmes. Identify the hard core of each, and the protective belts of auxiliary hypotheses each constructed: on the fixist side, the ad hoc postulation of sunken land bridges to explain identical fossils on opposite shores of the Atlantic, and the geosynclinal tradition's account of mountain-building on a static Earth; on the mobilist side, Wegener's proposed driving mechanisms — pole-flight and tidal forces, which were shown to be orders of magnitude too weak and which even sympathetic contemporaries found embarrassing. Then judge, phase by phase, which programme was progressing and which degenerating before roughly 1950, and what changed with the arrival of palaeomagnetic polar-wander data, Hess's seafloor spreading, and the 1963 Vine–Matthews interpretation of the ocean-floor magnetic stripes.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Three questions in particular. First: Lakatos allowed that a degenerating programme can stage a comeback, but by his own criteria, what should a rational scientist have done in, say, 1945, when drift had accumulated an impressive list of anomalies for the fixist programme but no mechanism and no novel predictions of its own — and does the eventual triumph show that the critics were irrational, that they were rational by Lakatos's lights, or that his lights are the wrong ones? Second: the decisive confirmation — the magnetic stripes — was arguably a novel prediction of seafloor spreading rather than of drift as Wegener had stated it; does that make plate tectonics a new programme superseding drift rather than its vindication, and if so, what survives of the martyr narrative? Third: where is the methodology silent here — on the role of new instruments such as echo sounders and towed magnetometers, on the ocean-floor mapping that wartime and Cold War naval work happened to produce, on the sociology of why American geologists were harsher critics of drift than their South African or European colleagues — and is that silence a defect in the framework or a deliberate exclusion? Conclude with a verdict on whether this episode confirms Lakatos against Popper and Kuhn, exposes a genuine gap in his methodology, or presents a case his framework never anticipated. Do not force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say.
