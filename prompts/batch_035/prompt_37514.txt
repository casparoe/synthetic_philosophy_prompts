Her verdicts so far: a ski instructor who could write the definative manual on ski technique but can't stay upright — she says he does NOT know how to ski. A self-taught natural who skis beautifully but couldn't explain the first thing about what she's doing — knows how. A guy who picked up a putter for the very first time and drained the putt — doesn't know how to putt, lucky isn't skilled. A lifelong cyclist who's totally wrong about how bikes stay up (he thinks you keep the handlebars dead straight) — knows how to ride. And a retired surgeon whose hands now shake too badly to operate but who remembers every step — still knows how to operate, she insists. When her cousin comes three cases are on the table: (1) an apprentice baker who has never once baked on her own but has watched a master for years and would turn out a perfect loaf on the first solo attempt — does she know how to bake before that first loaf? (2) a golfer who shoots great scores but only because a coach feeds her instructions through an earpiece in real time, and falls apart the second the coach goes quiet; (3) a typist doing 90 wpm who sincerely swears he has no idea how he does it. Predict her verdicts on all three, say what rule she seems to be running — I can see at least an ability-plus-reliability view and something like a Stanley-and-Williamson practical-mode-of-presentation thing both fitting the first five cases — and tell me which of the three would best pull those rules apart, plus one further case that would settle it if these don't manage to. (And is she right that my "it's all just knowledge-that under a funny description" line makes me a closet intellectualist? that part is mostly for my pride.)
