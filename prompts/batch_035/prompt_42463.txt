Hoping you can untangle something for me. I do security-adjacent work, so I've been around the norms for vulnerability disclosure my whole career, and I've started to wonder what they actually take for granted. Setup, roughly: a researcher finds a serious bug in someone's software, and the community treats them as bound by responsible disclosure -- you tell the vendor privately first, you give them some period (90 days is a common convention) to patch it, and only then do you publish. The stated rationale is basically the unilateralist's curse: any single researcher can publish, so if each one thinks publishing is even slightly net-positive, publication happens even when most of the community thinks it's net-negative -- no individual judgment matches the group's, so the low bar wins.

Here's what I want: an audit of the presuppositions. First, standing. The researcher is the one who found the thing, arguably knows it best, and yet the norm says their judgment doesn't settle it. Does the practice presuppose that they have a conflict of interest (career credit, prestige) that systematically biases them toward disclosure? Or that users as a class are the affected parties whose consent matters, and no researcher can speak for them? Second, the aggregation question. Does it presuppose a particular weighing -- user risk during the window before the patch counts more than the field-wide benefit of open publication -- or does it refuse expected-value reasoning altogether, treating unilateral action as intrinsically suspect whatever the expected utilities say? Third, and this is the part that bugs me: the deadline. On day 89 unilateral publication is a violation that gets you treated as reckless; on day 91 the exact same act is fine, even admirable. What changed? Did the vendor forfeit something, does the deadline somehow convert the act from unilateral to something the community has ratified, or is the norm just incoherent at the edges? And notice the weird asymmetry -- the vendor, who has the opposite conflict of interest, gets to sit on a bug forever with no deadline of its own that anyone can enforce.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory for me. What does the norm strictly presuppose versus merely sit well with, which rival accounts of who-gets-to-decide it quietly rules out, and where is it underdetermined such that several different theories of the curse's danger would all license roughly this norm? Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if you swapped in a pure expected-harm view instead. Please answer in American English, and keep it concrete -- I care about the structure of the thing, not a reading list.
