I teach high school math and I've been reading Whitehead, so I want to have it out with him. Please play Alfred North Whitehead in 1929, right after he's published The Aims of Education and Other Essays. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single solid paragraph, and remember he's not a naive dreamer -- this is a man who taught boys at a London secondary school for years before Harvard, co-wrote Principia Mathematica, and has no patience for vague educational uplift. You may only use concepts and references he'd have had available in 1929, plus whatever I tell you about how schools work now; if you need a factual question answered about modern schooling, ask it as Whitehead would ask it.

So let me give you the picture of my world: I teach in a state school, thirty kids per class, and at sixteen they all sit a standardized national exam in mathematics that to a large degree determines whether they go on to university. There's a national curriculum mapped out week by week, published "learning outcomes" for every lesson, and inspectors who show up with the students' test scores to judge whether I'm any good. There are also league tables published in the newspapers ranking schools by results, so parents shop between schools. Kids as young as eight get the same treatment, though scaled down.

Here's my opening move, Professor. You rail against inert ideas and you say education should be useless, by which I mean knowledge should be valuable for its own sake and not for some external end, and you've got that line about how knowledge spoils like fish if it's not used fresh. Fine. But you also insist that precision must wait on romance, that the cycle of romance, precision, and generalisation can't be hurried or reordered. In my world that is a luxury of small classes and elite boys' schools, the kind you taught in. With thirty kids, eighteen months to the exam, and my school's survival depending on their scores, precision-first is the only honest option, and the drilled kids who pass the exam get to university where some of them finally catch the romance you want. Isn't your rhythm just a description of what happens anyway across a longer timescale, and doesn't your own hatred of inert ideas actually justify teaching to the test as the lesser evil? Over to you.
