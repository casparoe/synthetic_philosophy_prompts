In a methodology seminar, my economics professor defended Friedman's line that theories should be judged by the accuracy of their predictions, not by the realism of their assumptions, with an analogy everyone in the room accepted without objection: a model is like a map. Maps leave out almost everything, they distort what they keep — a flat map of the earth bends distances and shapes out of all recognition — and no one calls a map a failure for that. In fact, the professor went on, the more useful the map, the less it resembles the territory: a perfectly faithful, one-to-one map of a city would be a life-sized replica and useless for getting anywhere. He concluded that complaining a model assumes firms are flawless profit maximizers, or that agents are perfectly informed, is like complaining that a road map omits the doorknobs — the complaint mistakes the point of the whole enterprise — and that only the predictions are up for audit.

I left convinced in spirit but uneasy, and I can't articulate why. Please audit the analogy for me. Give me the explicit correspondence first: what plays the role of the territory, the scale, the projection, the map's symbols and legend, the mapmaker, and the navigator reading it. Then lay out where the mapping breaks. In sorting the disanalogies, please separate the ones that matter for the specific use the analogy was put to — insulating the assumptions from criticism — from the ones that are merely loose fit and would not trouble the professor even if pointed out. Two candidates I'd especially like your judgment on: first, with a map we know in advance exactly which distortions were introduced and can often correct for them, since we know the projection, whereas on the professor's view we typically don't know which unrealistic assumptions are doing the predictive work and which could be dropped; second, a map's omissions do not seem to be falsehoods — a road map says nothing at all about doorknobs — while an assumption that firms maximize profit appears to positively assert something about the world, not merely decline to mention something.

Finally, tell me whether the professor's conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time; and if the map picture fails as a defense of unrealism-in-service-of-prediction, say whether there is a better analogy for what idealization in economic models is actually doing, or whether this is a case where we should give up on analogies altogether and state the methodology directly. Please answer in American English.
