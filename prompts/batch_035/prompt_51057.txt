I teach political philosophy at a university, and yesterday, in a meeting of our staff benefits committee, two colleagues conducted an argument I found I could not settle, despite it falling squarely within my own field. The context is as follows. Our university is moving pension enrolment from an opt-in system to automatic enrolment: every employee would be enrolled by default at a contribution rate the committee sets — the proposal on the table is eight per cent — and opting out or changing the rate requires a single form. The proposal's fate turns on the following exchange, and I owe the deciding opinion in writing by Thursday.

Dr. Okafor, a health psychologist on the committee, opened for the proposal. Staff consistently say they intend to enrol and to save more, she observed, and just as consistently fail to; a default flips this behaviour almost without resistance, and resistance costs one form. This is the least intrusive available remedy for a predictable error of inertia: it helps those who would sleepwalk past their own intentions at the price of one signature for anyone who deliberates. Calling it paternalism, she said, is true but uninformative — the serious question is whether it is the kind of paternalism one should object to.

Professor Lindqvist, of the law school, replied that the label is not what worries him; the mechanism is. The device works precisely on people who are not deliberating — that is what a default is for — and the committee will have designed it to do so. Knowingly aiming an intervention at the non-reflective side of a person, however benevolently and however cheaply it can be escaped, is manipulation; ease of escape caps the damages, it does not change the technique.

Okafor answered that there is no neutral arrangement for anyone to be manipulated away from. The committee must set some default, and a default of zero steers just as surely as a default of eight, only toward an outcome nearly all would reject on reflection. Since neutrality is unavailable, configuring the scheme as most members would configure it for themselves is not substituting the committee's judgment for theirs; it is implementing theirs.

Lindqvist then proposed mandated choice as the genuinely neutral alternative: no default, every employee required to return an explicit election within a fixed period. Okafor replied that this is more paternalistic, not less — it coerces deliberation on the majority who would be content with a sensible default, and it worsens the position of the least careful, who tick boxes at random or miss the deadline altogether. A good default, she said, protects the careless at no one's expense; forced choice burdens the careful and abandons the careless.

Lindqvist closed by turning to the follow-up survey Okafor had cited, in which most auto-enrolled staff report being glad the default was set for them. Retrospective endorsement, he argued, cannot alter the character an intervention had at the moment it operated; if later satisfaction could launder the method, then success would be what confers legitimacy, and every benevolent arrangement — including ones no liberal would accept — would come out clean. That people endorse a default they would have chosen anyway is evidence about their preferences, not about the propriety of the route by which the choice was produced.

I hold the casting vote and am expected to justify it in writing. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: state who is right about what, say where each overreaches, assess whether the no-neutral-default premise is as strong as Okafor takes it to be, and indicate what, if anything, both of them are missing.
