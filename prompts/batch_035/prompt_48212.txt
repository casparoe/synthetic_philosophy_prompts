I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about "who started it" questions in history, and I've realized I owe someone an account of what principle, if any, lies behind them. Here are the verdicts. Nazi Germany's invasion of Poland in 1939: Germany started the Second World War in Europe, full stop; the structural background (Versailles, the Depression, appeasement) explains why the war was possible but does nothing to dilute the blame. The Cold War: I find myself resisting the question. Neither the American containment policy nor the Soviet consolidation of Eastern Europe feels like a "start" in the Poland sense; each side was responding to what it reasonably perceived, and asking who fired first seems to misconstrue what kind of conflict it was. The First World War: I flip-flop. The July Crisis looks like a mutual sleepwalk, yet Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia feels more like a "start" than Russia's subsequent mobilization, which was itself a response — so somewhere between the ultimatum and the general war, my blame drops off, and I can't say where. The Opium War of 1839: here I blame Britain completely and assign no starting-blame to China even though the proximate trigger — Lin Zexu's destruction of British opium at Humen — was a Chinese action; the trigger was arguably justified and the war was a British choice made for trade reasons. The Cuban Missile Crisis: I don't think the Soviet placement of missiles in Cuba "started" it, because the United States had Jupiter missiles in Turkey first, so preceding provocations seem to cancel. And a case from ordinary life that I include because it keeps nagging at me: in a divorce where both partners escalated for years, I still blame the one who first had an affair far more than the other, even though by then the escalation was mutual and the affair was in some sense a response to it. I have a tentative principle I'd like you to test rather than accept: blame for starting tracks who first crossed a line that would not have been crossed without their deliberate choice, weighted by how much worse things went because of that crossing, and discounted by how reasonable the choice looked from the inside at the time. I suspect this gets the Poland and divorce cases right, but I worry it lets Britain off too easily in the Opium War (Britain's choice looked "reasonable" by its own commercial lights), and I can't tell whether the Cold War verdict reflects a genuine discount for perceived threat or just my refusal to take sides. So: is there a principle — or a small set of principles, if blame and origination come apart — that captures all or nearly all of these? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of those I should give up, and apply it to two cases I haven't listed: the slide into the American Civil War during the 1850s, and a hypothetical arms race between two states, each of which has strong, evidence-based reasons to fear the other, in which the first to deploy a destabilizing new weapon does so defensively. Finally, tell me whether my Cold War hesitation points to the conclusion that "who started it" is the wrong question for some conflicts, and if so, what question should replace it.
