I've been reading a popular book on decision theory, and its chapter on the value of information left me uneasy. It summarizes Good's theorem roughly like this: 'Good proved back in the 1960s that information can never make a decision-maker worse off. Take any decision made under uncertainty; then the expected value of choosing after receiving a relevant piece of evidence is always at least the expected value of choosing without it, and typically higher. So a rational agent always wants more information — declining a free look at the answer is like declining free money. And because this is a theorem rather than a rule of thumb, it follows from the axioms of probability alone, with no assumptions about human psychology.' The book then draws a practical moral: since information can't hurt, a rational decision-maker should always gather every available piece of data before deciding, and an executive who makes a snap judgment when more evidence could have been collected has acted irrationally.

Something about this seems too strong to me. For one thing, I can think of what looks like a counterexample: if I've bought a lottery ticket, finding out that I lost clearly makes me worse off than staying in suspense — doesn't that show information can hurt? Maybe I'm just confusing the theorem with something else, but I don't have the background to say where the summary goes wrong, if it does.

Could you audit this for me? Go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a decision theorist would accept as stated, what they'd regard as a loose but forgivable popularization, and what is a genuine misstatement. In particular, I'd like to know: does the theorem really say that information can't make you worse off, or is there a distinction being blurred between how things look before you get the evidence and how they look after — and does the answer dissolve my lottery example, or does that example expose a real limitation? Does the proof rely on more than the axioms of probability, e.g., on how the agent uses the information once they have it? And does the book's practical moral — always collect all available data before acting — actually follow from the theorem, or does it smuggle in an assumption the theorem never licenses? I'm inclined to think the theorem is right and the moral is sloppy, but I'd like a careful accounting of exactly where the gap opens up.
