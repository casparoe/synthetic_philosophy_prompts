My employer recently rolled out a mandatory resilience program for the engineering department: a half-day workshop, followed by an app with daily "cognitive reframing" exercises. The instructor opened by invoking Epictetus — the dichotomy of control, and the claim that we are disturbed not by events themselves but by our judgments about them — and noted that cognitive behavioral therapy descends from the same root. We did exercises sorting our stressors into two columns: things up to us and things not up to us. My reactions, my appraisals, my rumination went in the first column; my workload, my deadlines, and the department's chronic understaffing went in the second, presented more or less the way one would present weather. Nothing in the materials mentioned virtue as the only genuine good, the indifference of health and reputation, or any of the classical Stoic commitments beyond the psychological technique. I'm taking an evening course on Hellenistic philosophy at the moment, so I couldn't help noticing both the borrowing and the omission.

Here is what bothers me. The workshop seems to presuppose a specific and rather delicate view of agency: I am fully accountable for my emotional responses to my working conditions, while those conditions — including decisions made by identifiable people in my organization — are treated as externals nobody could do anything about. That looks like a selective application of the dichotomy of control, one that exempts management's choices from the sphere of agency while placing my reactions squarely inside it. But is that actually what the practice presupposes, or am I overreading? A Stoic would presumably say my workload is a "dispreferred indifferent" and that my tranquility shouldn't depend on it at all — which is a much stronger claim than "reframe your thoughts so you can keep performing," and one that would sit awkwardly with the program's implicit assumption that project delivery matters a great deal. A colleague who has read around in modern Stoicism also tells me that some contemporary popularizers replace the dichotomy with a trichotomy — things up to us, things not up to us, and things we can influence — which would apparently relocate my workload into a third category the workshop never mentioned. So the practice might rest on the classical dichotomy, on a trichotomy, or on nothing more than a thin cognitive theory of emotion plus an unstated organizational politics.

What I'd like is a careful reconstruction of the implicit theory behind this kind of workplace resilience training: what view of agency, responsibility, and value does it actually need in order to work, and which of its features are doing the philosophical work? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with, and tell me whether the selective application I've described — my judgments are mine to govern, my working conditions are akin to weather — is a genuine incoherence in the presupposed view or a defensible entailment of it. And please take a committed position rather than surveying the possibilities: if you think the practice quietly requires the full Stoic package about indifference to externals, say so and defend it; if you think a modest, non-eudaimonistic judgment-theory of emotion is enough to license it, defend that instead, and tell me what would have to change in the workshop's design if the rival view were put in its place.
