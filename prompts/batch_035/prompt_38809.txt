I'm a third-year undergrad in a philosophy of science course, and we've just wrapped up a unit on Duhem and the underdetermination of theory by evidence. Our professor has a habit of ending discussions with the one-liner "there are no crucial experiments," and last week she used it to deflate a classmate's claim that Eddington's 1919 eclipse observations were decisive evidence for general relativity over its Newtonian rivals. The classmate protested that the light-bending measurements changed everyone's minds, and the professor just repeated the slogan and moved on. I understand that the line is supposed to flow from underdetermination somehow, but I've noticed it seems to do different work each time she deploys it, and I can't tell which version she actually needs.

Here's my problem: the slogan seems to float between several strengths without anyone pinning it down. At its weakest it sounds like a near-tautology about logic — since any prediction depends on auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background theory, and so on, no single failed prediction can pin the blame on one hypothesis, and no single successful one can single-handedly crown a theory. Somewhere in the middle there's a historical thesis, that experiments which look decisive in retrospect never actually settle things at the time. And at its strongest it starts to sound like a sweeping epistemological claim, that no possible body of evidence could ever uniquely determine theory choice, because for any theory there's always an empirically equivalent rival we could construct.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "there are no crucial experiments" could be expressing — from the weak logical point about auxiliaries, through intermediate claims about underdetermination by total available evidence, to the strong constructivist or skeptical readings — and give a verdict on each: which are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, which are probably false, and which scientists and philosophers of science would actually accept? Then, crucially, tell me which of those readings would genuinely support using the slogan against my classmate's claim about the eclipse observations, and where that use breaks down — for instance, whether it conflates "not logically decisive" with "not rationally decisive," or overlooks how evidence can shift credence between rivals without ever conclusively eliminating any. I'd like a list I can bring to office hours, since I suspect the professor is trading on the ambiguity between the weakest and strongest versions, and I want to know exactly where the slide happens.
