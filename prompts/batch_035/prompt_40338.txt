Two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, spent most of a dinner party arguing about a thought experiment Marcus had come across online. A lamp starts out off. Someone switches it on one minute before noon, off half a minute before noon, on a quarter of a minute before noon, and so on, halving the interval forever. The question they got stuck on: at noon exactly, is the lamp on or off?

(1) Marcus said there simply is no answer. The sequence on, off, on, off never settles anywhere, and nothing in the description pins down a state at noon; insisting on an answer is like insisting on the last digit of pi.
(2) Elena said the scenario is broken for a stronger reason: no physical switch can flip infinitely many times before noon, so the setup describes an impossible object, and asking about its state at noon is like asking how much a square circle weighs.
(3) Marcus replied that this proves too much, since mathematics handles infinite processes perfectly well, and the old puzzle about the runner and the tortoise turned out to have a clean answer once you take limits; you can't dismiss something just for involving an infinity.
(4) Elena answered that the runner puzzle works precisely because the quantities involved settle down — the runner's remaining distance shrinks smoothly toward zero, so there's something to converge to. The lamp's state never settles, so the limit idea that rescues the runner does nothing here. And if you replaced the switch with a dimmer turned down by half at each step, the light would plainly end up dark, which shows this kind of infinite schedule can have an answer when there's a continuous quantity to take a limit of.
(5) Marcus conceded the dimmer point but said it just relocates the problem: the dimmer lamp is a different experiment, and for the plain on/off lamp his conclusion stands — the question has no answer.
(6) Elena pushed back that Marcus is treating the lamp as a purely abstract object when it was presented as a physical one. A real lamp put through this schedule would have its switching mechanism fail at some point, since nothing can move fast enough to keep up, and it would then sit in whatever state the broken mechanism left it — a perfectly determinate answer, just not one you can derive from the abstract on/off description alone.

They finally asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
