I write big sprawling multi-generational novels — the current one follows a factory town in upstate New York from 1912 to the 90s, and somewhere in drafting it I developed a pretty hardline position that I now can't shake: everything that happens on the page has to be traceable to particular people wanting particular things. When my editor says the decline of the town should feel like an impersonal force, like something that happens *to* people rather than something people do, I dig in, because my view is that any "force" like that is really just a compression of thousands of individual decisions — owners chasing margins, kids deciding to leave, bankers deciding who gets loans. Nothing over and above the individuals and their interactions. I gather the social science people call this methodological individualism, and that there's a long-running fight about it with people who think social wholes (classes, institutions, "the economy") do genuine explanatory work that can't be cashed out into individual doings. Someone once gave me the traffic jam example as a knockdown point — every single driver can be behaving perfectly sensibly given what the car ahead of them is doing, and yet you can't explain the jam without talking about the jam. I've never known what to say to that, so mostly I've ignored it.

Here's what I want from you. Take the individualist position seriously and tell me what would actually count *against* it. Not just the standard "but institutions!" hand-waving — I want to know which kinds of cases, historical events, or research findings would genuinely force someone like me to give ground, versus which ones a committed individualist would rightly shrug off as compressible into individual behavior with enough patience. Like, if a social scientist showed me some large-scale regularity that is stable while the individuals pass through it — high turnover, same aggregate outcome — is that a real problem for me, or just a point about what's convenient to track? What about stuff like language change, where nobody intends the outcome and it's not clear anyone's decisions add up to it in the right way? And be honest with me about the unfalsifiability worry: the view seems to always have an escape hatch ("well, *in principle* it could all be restated in terms of individuals"), and I want to know whether that makes the whole thing empty, or whether that kind of in-principle claim is a perfectly respectable thing for a view like this to rest on.

Also, selfishly — I don't just want the philosophy abstractly, I want to know what it does to my book. If the holists are right that wholes explain things, what would it even look like to write that? Is a novel, which can only ever show individual scenes with individual people doing things, structurally committed to individualism whether I like it or not? And do you think my hardline approach makes for better fiction or worse — because honestly the sections where the town "has a mind of its own" that my editor loves are also the ones readers seem to remember. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones I could concede without giving up the whole game.
