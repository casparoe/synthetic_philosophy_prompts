I'm a PhD student writing a chapter on the normative force of Dutch book arguments, and I've noticed that most textbook treatments stop at the synchronic case: a static probabilistic incoherence licenses a sure-loss book. What interests me is the diachromatic extension — the claim that an agent who is coherent at every single moment can nonetheless be Dutch-booked because of how she plans to update. I'd like you to construct a thought experiment to order.

Build me a scenario in which a diachronic Dutch book can be completed against an agent whose credences are perfectly coherent at every point in the sequence — her vulnerability arises entirely from her updating rule, not from any momentary inconsistency. Specifications:

1. The agent should be a realistic professional with ordinary stakes — an insurance underwriter or a commodity analyst, say, rather than a gambler — and the evidence that arrives mid-sequence should be the sort that plausibly comes in between contract offers.
2. Her departure from strict conditioning should be a natural, humanly recognisable one — something like overweighting vivid testimony, or an anchored adjustment away from her earlier conditional probabilities — rather than a stipulated irrationality.
3. The bookie must not have privileged access to the agent's private credences: everything the bookie knows must come from the odds the agent has publicly posted or accepted, together with any evidence the agent herself will receive. I want to pre-empt the familiar complaint that dynamic Dutch book arguments require an implausibly well-informed opponent.
4. Each individual bet must be one the agent weakly prefers, by her own lights at the time it is offered, to refusing.

Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or two. Afterwards, do the verification work: lay out the sequence of bets, show the net payoff on each evidential branch, and confirm that the loss is 'sure' in the relevant diachronic sense (loss on every branch, conditional on the evidence having arrived as it does). Then identify precisely which feature of the case does the work — I take it to be something like the bookie's ability to trade with the agent both before and after the update, on propositions whose probabilities the update moved — and explain what happens to the argument if that feature is removed: if the bookie must contract only once, or only before the evidence arrives, does the case collapse?

Finally, address two lines of response on behalf of the agent. First, the objection that susceptibility to sure loss demonstrates, at most, exploitability rather than incoherence, and that diachronic incoherence is anyway a category mistake — there are no cross-temporal 'credences' to be inconsistent with each other, only successive agents each of whom is internally coherent. Second, the reply that the agent can treat the first bet as a binding commitment and simply refuse the later one — or, more generally, that a rational agent is entitled to have a policy about when she re-opens negotiations, and that the sure loss only materialises if she hasn't adopted one. Tell me whether you find either response sufficient to blunt the case, and whether what survives is best read as a genuine requirement of rationality or merely a prudential recommendation about dealing with counterparties who move faster than you do.
