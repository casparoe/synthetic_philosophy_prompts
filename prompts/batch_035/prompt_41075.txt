My roommate and I have been arguing for weeks about when saying something actually *does* something, and I've basically been going case by case on gut feel, so I want to lay out my verdicts and see if there's an actual principle hiding in there. The wedding officiant who turns out to never have been licensed: I say the couple was never married, even though everything looked right and they lived as married for years. The celebrity "apology" drafted entirely by a PR team, posted while the celebrity feels zero remorse: I say that's still an apology -- it did the apologizing even if it didn't do the being-sorry. But a flat, sarcastic "well, sorrry" said to someone whose foot you just stepped on: not an apology at all, it's the opposite of one somehow. A promise extracted at knifepoint: doesn't bind, obviously. But a promise you only gave because the person badgered and cried and wore you down for an hour until you finally said "FINE, I promise": that one does bind, I feel like you'd be a jerk to break it. And a boss who slurs "you're firrred" drunk at the office party: not fired, no way. Yet an employee who drunk-texts their boss "I quit, effective immediately": I feel like that one kind of counts?? Which is embarassing because I can't say why the direction should matter.

Also: I bet a friend twenty bucks the bus would be on time, he just rolled his eyes and said nothing, the bus was on time, and my verdict is no debt exists -- no bet happened without him taking it up. And someone smashing a bottle against a ship while saying "I name this ship the" whatever, but they're standing at the dock next to the wrong ship entirely: I genuinely can't decide if nothing happened, or if the wrong ship got named, or if the right ship got named by mistake. There's also the case where an actor performing in a play says "I do" -- not married, clearly, no one would think that. But here's the one that bugs me most: a couple who got legally married as a prank for a youtube video, full ceremony, both fully sober, giggling the whole time, and my verdict is that they really are married and would have to actually get divorced, even though everything about their intentions looks as hollow as the actors'.

So that's the pile. Is there one principle -- maybe something about conditions for the act to come off, maybe about uptake from the other party, maybe about the role or authority of the speaker, maybe about sincerity -- that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts? Because the thing I keep noticing is that sincerity seems required in the sarcastic apology case, irrelevant in the PR apology case, and the drunken firing/quitting asymmetry seems like it can't be about sincerity at all. I'd want the principle stated carefully enough to tell me (a) which of my verdicts, if any, it just can't accommodate and why I should give those up rather than bolt on extra clauses, (b) what it predicts for the ship-naming mess, since that's the one I flip on constantly, and (c) whether the youtube-prank marriage comes out married on its own terms or only because of the legal paperwork -- i.e., whether the principle treats the ceremony and the license as doing the same job or different jobs. Also happy to hear if you think my verdicts sort into two different kinds of case that need two different principles, as long as you say where the split is and why that split isn't just me being inconsistent.
