Bit of a weird one but here goes. My best mate Dan has spent the last year and a half going deep into conspiracy stuff — doesn't hugely matter which theory exactly, that's actually part of the point — and I've been banging my head against the wall trying to work out how to talk to him about it. Then I made the mistake of reading the actual philosophy literature on conspiracy theories and realised the professionals are having basically the same argument I'm having with Dan, and it's been going on for like twenty years with nobody moving an inch, so now I can't stop thinking about it.

One side holds that conspiracy theories are epistemically defective as a class — the problem isn't what any particular theory says but how beliefs of this kind get formed and held: they insulate themselves from disconfirmation (counterevidence was planted, absence of evidence means the cover-up worked), they require you to believe that a lot of people can coordinate and keep quiet indefinitely, and so on. The other side says hang on, actual conspiracies demonstrably happen, corporations and governments have been caught covering things up, so a blanket suspicion of "conspiracy theories" as such can't be an epistemic principle — it's just a disguised appeal to trusting official sources, and each claim should be assessed on its own evidence like anything else.

And the thing is, when I watch these people argue (and when I argue with Dan), it never goes anywhere, and I want to know why. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: they're answering different questions under one name. The class-defect crowd are secretly doing descriptive work — an explanation of why people come to believe this stuff, whether or not the belief is warranted — while the each-case-on-its-evidence crowd are doing purely normative epistemology, and each side's arguments just pass the other's by. But I've also wondered whether it's verbal in a simpler sense: maybe one lot means "unwarranted theory about a conspiracy" and the other means "any theory about a conspiracy," so they're only superficially disagreeing. Or maybe the real disagreement is neither of those and it's about how much default trust institutions deserve, which is a political/normative question neither side wants to put on the table.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — substantive, verbal, cross-purposes, or two projects accidentally sharing a term? Diagnose it properly, don't just survey the positions. And tell me what would even count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding about how these beliefs actually form, a change of question, or nothing at all, and why.

The practically relevant bit, and the reason I'm asking: if the class-defect people are right, then patiently arguing evidence with Dan is pointless, because the defect isn't in the evidence — and might even make things worse. If the each-case people are right, then refusing to argue evidence is just smugness that entrenches him. I'm not asking you to tell me how to handle Dan — though honestly if your diagnosis has implications for that I'd take them — I'm asking what the disagreement between the two camps actually is, since I've started to suspect I can't even tell what Dan and I are disagreeing ABOUT, and that bothers me more than the theories themselves. Sorry for the essay, its been on my mind.
