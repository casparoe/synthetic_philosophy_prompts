"In a short essay, explain the regress problem for justified belief: how it arises, and why some philosophers think it forces a choice between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. Present each of the three responses accurately, give one standard objection to each, and say whether you think any of them escapes the regress, with reasons."

And here is what the student wrote:

"The regress problem arises because every belief must be justified by some further belief. If I believe it will rain because the forecast says so, I need a justification for trusting the forecast, and that justification needs one too, and so on. Since an infinite chain of justifications is impossible for finite creatures like us, we seem left with exactly three options: the chain of reasons goes in a circle, goes on forever, or stops somewhere. This is the Agrippan trilemma, and the three responses map onto its three horns.

Foundationalism takes the third horn: the chain stops at basic beliefs. These are beliefs that are justified but not by any other belief — they justify themselves. Descartes is the paradigm foundationalist, and on his view the basic beliefs are incorrigible beliefs about our own sensations, like 'I seem to see red.' The objection to foundationalism is that any criterion you use at the basic level either applies to itself or doesn't, and if it doesn't, the criterion is self-defeating.

Coherentism denies that the regress ever gets going. On this view justification is not linear but holistic: a belief is justified by fitting into a large, coherent web of beliefs. The objection is that coherence is no guarantee of truth — two people could each have internally coherent but mutually incompatible belief systems — so coherentism collapses into relativism, and since relativism is false, coherentism is refuted.

Infinitism, defended recently by Peter Klein, is the view that an infinite chain of reasons can justify a belief, provided each reason is available if we need it rather than actually occurrently believed. The objection is obvious: no human being can run through infinitely many reasons, so infinitism demands the impossible.

I conclude that foundationalism wins. It is the only view on which justification is genuinely transferred from the foundations upward, since coherence is just a fancy word for circular reasoning, which is always a fallacy, and infinitism is a non-starter for finite minds. So the regress is escaped by stopping it with basic beliefs, which is what foundationalism does."
