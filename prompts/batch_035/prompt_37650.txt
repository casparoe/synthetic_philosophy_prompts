I supervise a team of six care coordinators at a county adult-services agency. Every month we administer a fixed pool of publicly funded respite-care hours for family caregivers of homebound adults — roughly 400 hours against requests that typically total two or three times that. Coordinators have to decide which requests get funded, in whole or in part, and write a justification that can survive review by the state ombudsman. At present we allocate by a points formula: activities-of-daily-living deficits, caregiver burden score, hours since the family's last award. The formula is defensible in exactly the way a photograph of a stranger is defensible: it captures something true and misses nearly everything that matters.

I have been reading in care ethics — the tradition that treats care, not impartial entitlement, as the basic moral fact, and that insists morality begins in dependency rather than in the encounter between autonomous strangers — and I want to replace the formula with a decision procedure genuinely grounded in it. I understand the tradition's working commitments roughly as follows: attentiveness to the particular person rather than to their file; responsibility taken on rather than assigned by rule; competence, meaning that caring that is botched does not count as care; and the cared-for's own responsiveness as a moral source rather than mere compliance. I also understand its standing worries: that it favors the loud, the near, and the charming over the inarticulate and the abandoned, and that a rule-generating procedure is precisely the kind of impartial machinery the tradition defines itself against. That last point is a genuine puzzle for me and I want it addressed, not waved away.

Please write a procedure my coordinators could actually run in the twenty minutes they get per file. They are nurses and social workers with no philosophy background; they will read it once and then work from a laminated card. Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order:

1. **Before You Begin** (about 150 words) — the cautions a coordinator needs in hand before running anything, including a straight answer to the objection that formalizing care ethics betrays it.
2. **The Procedure** (about 450 words) — numbered steps, each stating what question it asks, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the file to a human being because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure.
3. **The Trial Run** (about 150 words on each of three cases, below) — what the procedure yields and why, including at least one outcome I will not like.
4. **Where It Fails** (about 150 words) — the situations in which this procedure predictably misfires and what my team should watch for.
5. **The Bottom Line** (about 100 words) — what I should tell the ombudsman this procedure is and is not.

The three cases, all real in structure though the details are invented:

- **Mrs. Okafor**, 82, post-stroke, cared for by her daughter Dolores, who left her job eight months ago and has become brittle and short with the home aides. Dolores is asking for two weeks of continuous respite to visit her son in another state. Her mother's clinical deficits are middling; the daughter's depletion is severe and getting worse.
- **Mr. Whitfield**, 79, early-stage dementia, lives alone. He is lucid and eloquent between nine and eleven in the morning, writes persuasive letters, and scores low on our deficit formula because he can still bathe and feed himself. His nephew, his only relative, phones once a month. Nobody is caring for him at all.
- **Harriet Boyd**, 61, sole caregiver for her husband, who has Parkinson's. She has a cardiac condition of her own, has refused help twice, and said the second time that her husband would be ashamed. Our standing policy is that a third refusal closes the file for six months.

Please write the entire response in British English. Do not soften the case whose outcome I will dislike; the point of including it is to find out whether the procedure has a spine.
