For most of my working life I was a behavioural ecologist, running Skinner boxes with pigeons and rats in the mornings and watching sticklebacks forage in the afternoons; since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind, and I have become rather captivated by Daniel Dennett's ladder of creatures — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian kinds, set out in his mid-nineties books Darwin's Dangerous Idea and Kinds of Minds. As I understand it, Darwinian creatures come hard-wired, with their behavioural repertoire settled by selection over phylogenetic time; Skinnerian creatures additionally revise themselves within their own lifetimes by trial and error, with reinforcement selecting among their actions; Popperian creatures go one better and run their trials offline, against an inner model of their world, so that candidate hypotheses can perish instead of the creature; and Gregorian creatures, named in honour of Richard Gregory, have their thinking restructured by culturally transmitted mind-tools — language, notation, methods of calculation — that they did not invent themselves. I mention all this because I want to be sure my gloss is not leading me astray, but mainly because I have written down my own position on the ladder and can no longer see whether it hangs together.

Here is what I hold. (1) I am a thoroughgoing gradualist: everything on that ladder, in phylogeny and probably in ontogeny too, is built by incremental accretion through selection processes of one sort or another, and I have no patience with talk of saltations or of anything extra being added from outside selection. (2) Nevertheless I think the four rungs mark differences in kind rather than in degree, and this is precisely what drew me to the taxonomy; a creature that can let its hypotheses die in its stead seems to me to be an agent in a genuinely different sense from one that must risk its own skin on every trial, not merely a faster or more economical version of the same thing. (3) I think the Popperian rung in particular requires genuine inner representation — a model of the environment whose errors can be detected and discarded independently of the creature's behaviour — and that this is what Skinnerian creatures, whose learning is a matter of association and nothing more, lack. (4) I am quite sympathetic to Dennett's memetic suggestion about the Gregorian rung: the intelligence of a Gregorian creature resides largely in the accumulated design history of its mind-tools rather than in the individual organism, so the cleverness is inherited rather than achieved. (5) Finally, and uncomfortably for someone who spent forty years studying learning animals, I have come to believe that morally relevant suffering requires at least the Popperian form of self-modelling — an animal that represents itself as being in pain, rather than one that merely exhibits nociception and pain behaviour — which would place a rat in a Skinner box, and certainly a fish on a hook, below the line that matters.

Please treat the five commitments as a package and audit them. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which merely look as though they are, and how badly; for each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost. I confess I do not know where the trouble lies — my suspicion is that my gradualism and my belief in differences in kind cannot both be true, but I may be wrong about that, and I am more troubled still about whether my ethical line in (5) survives whatever happens to the rest. Order the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and end by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your answer in British English.
