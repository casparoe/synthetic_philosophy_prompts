So here's the situation. I'm a philosophy professor (mostly ethics, some phil social science) and I'm on our department's hiring committee this year. Our bylaws have this quirk: every deliberation about a candidate opens with a "straw poll" taken out loud, in seniority order, most senior first. Nobody loves it, but it's been like that forever and changing the bylaws is a nightmare, so nobody's bothered.

This search cycle something happened that I think is a textbook case of what the formal people call an information cascade, though I honestly only half-remember the literature and please don't cite any of it by name in your answer, since the memo's readers can't check references and I don't want shaky attributions in a document the whole department will see. Anyway: for one of our candidates, the two most senior people both said "pass" in the straw poll. After that, literally nobody spoke in favor. Not one person. And here's the part that bugs me — two colleagues had told me privately, the week before, that they genuinely liked this candidate's work. One of the junior members later admitted to me that she'd gone quiet because, with two senior people negative on the record, speaking up for the candidate would make it look like she didn't "understand the subfield." So her private evidence got screened off by the early public signals, and the committee ended up with less information in the room than it started with, which is kind of a perverse result for a procedure that's supposed to pool information.

I also half-remember that this cascade stuff is supposed to be fragile — like, one visible dissenter can collapse the whole thing — which, if true, suggests there might be a cheaper fix than going full secret ballot. I'm not sure about that though, so treat it as a hunch, not a fact, and say so in the memo if you lean on it.

What I want from you: you're a professional philosopher specializing in social epistemology who has sat on a bunch of real hiring committees, so you know the difference between a procedure that looks good on paper and one people will actually follow. Before writing the memo, do some brainstorming — sketch two or three candidate procedures (things like written independent ratings sealed before discussion, simultaneous reveal, randomized speaking order, chair speaks last, whatever else occurs to you) and briefly say why you're rejecting the ones you reject. Show that brainstorm as a short preamble, then the memo itself.

The memo is addressed to our department chair, and it should recommend a concrete procedure for the rest of this year's searches and next year's — actual steps, and when it applies (e.g., votes on who to fly out, votes on the final ranking), not just vague "we should deliberate better" sentiments.
