Write a sestina about sensitive dependence on initial conditions — chaos theory's point that determinism and predictability come apart, that a system can be governed by fixed rules at every step yet drift arbitrarily far from where those rules seemed to point.

Here is the formal conceit, which I want you to take seriously rather than gesturing at: an ordinary sestina is a poem of perfect determinism. The six end-words are fixed in advance, and the permutation rule — each stanza's endings take the previous stanza's in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3, so ABCDEF becomes FAEBDC — mechanically determines every line's ending. Given the first stanza and the rule, the whole poem is already determined, the way Laplace's demon is supposed to read the last stanza off the first. Your sestina must break exactly this, in a controlled way: each time an end-word returns in a later stanza, alter it by exactly one letter — one substitution, deletion, insertion, or transposition of adjacent letters. The rule of alteration is fixed and deterministic; the initial six words plus the two rules jointly determine every subsequent end-word; and yet by the envoi the words should have become unrecognizable descendants of the originals, the way an error rounded off in the third decimal place determines the weather a month out. The mutations need not yield English words — coinages are fine, and if a word degrades into pure sound, that is part of the point — but choose your six starting words so the drift has somewhere to go: long, letter-rich, anagrammable words rather than short ones.

For the subject matter, pick a deterministic system observed over time — a pendulum, a family, a rumor, a small town's yearly ritual — and let the accumulating corruption of the end-words carry the theme rather than stating it. The envoi should place the two most mutated descendants in its customary two positions, so the poem closes on words the reader must trace back, line by line, to their ancestors. Someone who knows chaos theory should find the poem right about the distinction between determinism and predictability, and should be able to verify that your permutation rule really is fixed throughout; someone who doesn't should simply find it a good poem.
