You are a professional philosopher of mind, and I'd like you to serve as a careful auditor of a set of positions I've committed myself to, because a long argument with a friend has left me unsure whether they're jointly tenable. Context: we spent most of an evening arguing about whether I can trust my own reports about my own mind. She kept pressing the confabulation literature on me — split-brain patients cheerfully inventing explanations for actions they didn't choose, choice-blindness experiments where people defend decisions they never actually made, the older studies about people confidently misreporting the causes of their own choices — and I kept agreeing that the studies are solid while insisting that I nonetheless know what I'm thinking and feeling better than anyone else could. She says that combination is incoherent. Before I write this up as my position paper for a philosophy of mind seminar, I want to know whether she's right, and if so, where exactly the problem sits.

Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can: (1) My access to my own current mental states — occurrent thoughts, sensations, moods — is direct rather than inferential: I don't work out what I believe or feel from evidence the way other people have to; I just detect it. (2) The confabulation findings show that a substantial fraction of what passes for introspective report is really post hoc self-interpretation, reconstructed after the fact to make behavior intelligible. (3) Sincere first-person avowals have a kind of authority that third-person attributions lack — when I say I'm in pain, or that I believe something, the claim isn't answerable to evidence in the way claims about other minds are. (4) Introspection is a legitimate source of evidence for philosophical theorizing about consciousness — I'm sympathetic to arguments that lean on what our own experience seems to reveal, and I'd be uncomfortable with any theory that dismissed introspective reports outright. (5) Attitudes can drive my behavior while remaining introspectively inaccessible, as with implicit bias: the thought that everything influencing my conduct is open to my own view strikes me as an artifact of how attention happens to work, not a truth about the mind.

Please think carefully and brainstorm the possible tensions before answering: go through the pairs of commitments, separate the merely apparent conflicts from the genuine ones, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning — some of these may dissolve once stated carefully, and I'd want to know which. For each genuine tension, tell me what a minimal repair would look like and what it would cost me elsewhere. I honestly don't know which of the five, if any, has to go, so don't read my ordering or phrasing as a hint, and don't lecture me on each commitment in isolation — what I need is an audit of the set as a whole.
