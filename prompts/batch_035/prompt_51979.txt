So heres the situation: I write novels for a living — literary fiction, mostly close third person, the kind where the narration slowly takes on the character's rhythms. On the side I read a lot of pop philosophy of mind, and a while back I worked through The Mind's I, the Hofstadter and Dennett anthology. The chapter that's been living rent-free in my head ever since is the one where an enormous book maps Einstein's brain neuron by neuron, so that if a team of readers follows it the right way they can carry on a conversation with Einstein — and the suggestion is that the book, read properly, is Einstein in roughly the way the brain was.

This bugs me for a professional reason. My whole job is making patterns of ink that generate the experience of a mind in somebody else's head, and I suspect my craft vocabulary already has opinions about all this. Nobody's ever layed it out for me, so that's what I want.

Restate that whole cluster — the book-as-brain conceit, whether the right text could be conscious, the pattern-vs-substrate thing underneath it — in the vocabulary I actually use, which is narratology and workshop talk. I'm fluent, so don't dumb it down for me: point of view, focalization, free indirect discourse, story vs. discourse, the implied author, diegetic levels, interiority, the reader filling in gaps. To give you a sense of the level I want: does the readers-become-Einstein's-brain move have any counterpart in the implied author, or in what happens when a reader runs a character in their head? Does pattern vs. substrate map onto anything in the story/discourse split, or is that a category error on my part?

Then the residue report, and be honest with it. Which parts of the philosophers' argument translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which parts only translate if you distort them (and tell me what gets distorted), and which parts have no expression at all in how we talk about fiction. And here's the one I care about most, so don't skip it: my standing craft intuition is that a novel is a score, not a performance — the character's consciousness lives in the reader's simulation, never on the page. In narratology the reader is always external to the text; we literally have no concept of a text that constitutes its own reader. But Hofstadter's move, as I understand it, is that the brain is a book that is simultaneously its own reading. So is my score-not-performance intuition just the substrate/pattern distinction wearing a turtleneck, or is it pointing at a real disanalogy the thought experiment papers over? And if the residue is that my vocabulary simply cannot say that a text reads itself — is that because narratology is missing something, or because the self-reading book is doing rhetorical work that the argument can't actually cash?

Don't flatter either side. If part of the philosophers' debate looks less substantive once it's in my terms, say so. And same goes for my intuition.
