I've kept honeybee colonies in my backyard for six years, and this past winter I lost my stronger hive — found the cluster dead a few inches from full stores, the classic picture. Somewhere between reading up on why colonies collapse and arguing at my beekeeping association about whether our varroa treatment schedule stresses the bees, I've assembled a set of views about what a colony actually is, and I'd like an audit of whether they hang together.

Here is the set, written out as clearly as I can:

1. Adaptations ultimately exist for the benefit of genes. I take the Dawkins-style line that genes are the only things that persist across generations in the relevant way, so when I ask what a trait is for, the buck stops with whatever copies of the genes that built it. The worker's barbed sting, which disembowels her when she defends the hive against a mammal, looks to me like as clean a case as there is.

2. When I watch the colony decide things — scouts dancing over candidate nest sites when a swarm needs a new home, workers balling a queen who isn't laying well — the thing doing the deciding seems to be the colony, not any bee in it. I don't think that's a metaphor or a convenience. I believe the colony is a genuine individual, with adaptations of its own, on the same footing as a cat or a beetle, and that when I say I keep one animal rather than sixty thousand, I mean it.

3. I also believe the levels genuinely conflict sometimes. When a hive goes queenless long enough, workers start laying — and the eggs they lay are all drones, and other workers eat most of them. I take this as a real tug-of-war between what's good for an individual worker and what's good for the colony, with the colony fielding countermeasures, not as noise in an otherwise unified system.

4. I believe the individual bees in my hive have welfare that matters. I'm gentle at inspections, I feel badly when I crush a worker, and I think a bee's life can go better or worse for her. When the colony died, I grieved it in two registers at once, and I think both were appropriate.

5. I take the point from the major-transitions literature that a multicellular organism is itself a cartel of cooperators — that our cells descend from once-independent ones, kept honest by a segregated germ line and internal policing. On that view the line between a group and an individual is drawn by degree of integration rather than kind, which is a large part of why I believe 2.

What I want from you: go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it. Two things I already can't resolve and want treated as open questions rather than settled by fiat: whether 1 and 2 are rival claims about what a trait is for or just two ledgers for the same outcome, and whether 4 is a sentiment I'm entitled to given 2 and 5, or whether, if the colony is the animal, caring about a worker's welfare is like caring about the welfare of one of my skin cells. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and roughly what it would cost me to give it up. Two further requests about your own epistemic state: be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point in your response you are least sure of — I want to know whether your weakest link is one of my commitments or one of your own claims about how the levels relate.
