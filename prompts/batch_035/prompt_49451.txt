At a dinner party, two friends of mine — Maya, who grew up in a Hindu household, and Daniel, a philosophy graduate student — argued well past dessert about whether karma deserves its reputation as a morally comforting doctrine. Their exchange ran as follows. (1) Daniel opened by calling karma a theory of moral desert that no decent person should want to be true: if present suffering is always the consequence of deeds in past lives that no one can remember, the doctrine does the ideological work of implying that the beggar at the gate earned his place, letting the believer pity suffering while never quite being surprised by it. (2) Maya answered that Daniel was smuggling in a picture of karma as a courtroom with a cosmic judge; on the understanding she was raised with, karma operates more like a natural law — deeds have consequences the way thrown stones have trajectories — and no one says gravity blames the person it pulls down, so the ideological critique misses its target. (3) Daniel replied that if karma is merely a natural law, it is not a theory of moral desert at all: desert is a normative notion concerning what a person is worthy of receiving, and a blind causal mechanism cannot make anyone worthy of anything; so Maya must choose between a doctrine with the ugly ideological consequences he described and one that has given up the moral content that made it interesting. (4) Maya countered that the moral content survives at the agent's end even if not at the sufferer's end: karma tells each agent that their own actions reliably return to them, which functions as a demand for scrupulousness rather than a licence to shrug at others' misery, and karma traditions in any case pair the doctrine with an explicit duty of compassion, precisely because one cannot know what another person's karma is. (5) Daniel's last word was that this pairing is unstable: if no one can ever read off anyone's desert, the doctrine retains all the machinery of desert with none of the epistemic access, making it worse than having no desert theory at all — a theory whose central application lies forever beyond its own reach.

They eventually turned to me and asked who had won, and I honestly could not say. Please adjudicate each of the five moves in turn, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating, then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them missed.
