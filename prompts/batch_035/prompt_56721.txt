I help run a reading group attached to a modern Stoicism course, and the single most repeated picture in our discussions is the sailing one: a sailor cannot choose the wind, the weather, or the sea, but retains complete command of the rigging, the trim of the sails, and the course steered; so the good sailor is judged by seamanship, not by whether the passage was calm. The group treats it as effectively Epictetus' own teaching (some members even credit the slogan about adjusting the sails to him, though as far as I can tell it is a much later saying), and it is made to carry two conclusions at once: first, that the dichotomy of control should really be taught as a trichotomy — things within our power, things outside it, and a large middle category of things we can influence but not dictate (health, reputation, career, other people) — and second, a stronger claim, that externals are matters for technique but not for concern, so that a redundancy, a diagnosis, or a bereavement ought to be met with the same equanimity as a headwind, since neither is up to us. What troubles me is that Epictetus' own opening move, at least in the standard English renderings I have seen, seems to sort things into what is and is not our own — judgements, impulses, assent on one side; body, possessions, reputation, office on the other — with no third bucket; and Epictetus himself reaches for maritime images in the Discourses, which suggests the picture is not simply an invention of the revival.

Please audit the sailing analogy properly. Set out the correspondence table first — what in the analogy is meant to be the prohairesis or faculty of choice, what the storm is meant to be, what the rigging is, what the harbour, if anything, is supposed to be — and then say where the mapping breaks down. In particular I want you to address: (i) whether the sailor, who plainly has the safe arrival of the ship as an external goal and trims the sails precisely in order to reach port, is really an image of the Stoic agent for whom virtue alone is the good, or whether the analogy quietly imports a consequentialist structure in which externals matter for outcomes while merely failing to be the basis of evaluation; (ii) whether the middle category the analogy is used to motivate is a genuine refinement of the dichotomy or a mistake about what Epictetus means by things being up to us, given the role of judgements and assent in the original framework; (iii) whether the influence reading licenses anything beyond the obvious practical advice to act on what you can affect, or whether the revival has dressed up prudence as a revision of Stoic theory. Then give your verdict: which, if either, of the two conclusions the analogy is being used to support actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table; whether the stronger claim about indifference to bereavement or illness is carried by the analogy at all or simply smuggled in under cover of it; and, if the sailing image is doing more harm than good, what a better image would be for the point the revival actually needs — one that keeps what is right in the ancient doctrine without collapsing it into either fatalism or self-help mastery. Please write your answer in British English.
