I'm an identical twin, and a question I'd never thought to ask about that has suddenly started mattering to me. My sister and I listened to a podcast episode on modal metaphysics — a well-spoken host, clearly someone who had read the literature — and since then we have been trading messages about whether the view it attributed to Kripke is one we should accept, and what it could even mean for the two of us. The host's summary, as best I can reconstruct it, went like this: "Back in the early seventies, Kripke argued that each of us could not possibly have been conceived from a different sperm and a different egg. Had some other pair met instead, your parents would simply have had a different child — a sibling who was never born — rather than you living a slightly different life. This follows directly from his theory of names: a name is a rigid designator, referring to the same person in every possible world, and since a person just is the organism produced by that particular zygote, any world in which that zygote never forms is a world in which your name has no bearer. It works exactly like the discovery that water is H2O: the reference gets fixed, the underlying essence does the rest, and Kripke proved that for a person the essence is genetic. So there is a strict sense in which you could not have been conceived a month later, or to a different couple — those are not alternate biographies for you at all."

I'm inclined to accept the first half of this, since it does seem to me that a different meeting of gametes would have produced someone else, but the middle stretch, where the linguistic machinery is supposed to generate the biological conclusion, sounded suspiciously easy, and my sister thinks the whole thing is a semantic trick that says nothing about actual people. Neither of us is in a position to judge who is right.

Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher defending the Kripkean view would accept, which they would wince at, and which they would flatly deny — in particular whether the rigid-designation point actually delivers the conclusion or whether something further is doing the real work, whether the water analogy holds in the way the host suggests, and whether saying "the essence is genetic" is a fair statement of the thesis. Then, since you can see where this is going: if the view says I could not have come from any other sperm and egg, what is it supposed to say about my sister and me, who came from the very same zygote before it split? The podcast's phrasing that a person just is the organism produced by that particular zygote cannot be true of both of us, or we would be one person. Was one of us the original and the other a later arrival, is that a question the view answers some other way, or is it a question the view simply doesn't handle?
