I'm a second-year PhD student in comparative politics trying to find my dissertation topic, and I keep circling the debate on performance legitimacy — the idea, usually applied to China and Singapore, that authoritarian regimes can be durable because they deliver growth, order, and improving material conditions, not because citizens endorse the regime's right to rule. You're a political theorist who's spent a career on legitimacy and comparative politics — please think carefully before answering, brainstorm the open questions first, and then give me a prioritized research agenda with the ordering defended.

Here are the candidate questions I've collected so far, though add others if I'm missing something:

1. The measurement circularity problem: if the regime claiming performance legitimacy also controls the statistics that measure performance (GDP data, protest reporting), is the claim even testable? And do we have any independent handle on what citizens actually believe, given preference falsification — people telling survey researchers what's safe to say?

2. Delivering *what*, exactly? If it's raw growth that generates deference, a slowdown should erode it. But maybe what matters is predictability and the sense that things are getting better for you specifically. If so, a regime that successfully manages expectations downward during a slowdown might preserve exactly the deference that growth previously bought — which would suggest the real variable is expectation management, not performance, and the concept sort of dissolves under you.

3. Whether performance legitimacy does any work that a simpler coercion-plus-apathy story wouldn't. Maybe citizens just stay quiet because dissent is costly and politics feels personally irrelevant to them. If repression and indifference predict stability about as well, the whole concept might be redundant.

4. Singapore vs. China as a natural contrast: Singapore delivers performance through rule-of-law institutions that partly constrain the rulers themselves; China delivers it through discretionary interventions the rulers can revoke at any moment. Does the legitimation mechanism differ in kind or only in degree, and does that tell us anything about which system survives an actual crisis (a big recession, a war gone badly)?

5. Whether performance legitimacy is self-undermining: growth produces a middle class, urbanization, education — things modernization theory says should eventually create demands for participation. So is performance legitimacy a loan that comes due, or does that effect just not show up empirically?

Rank these (and anything you add) for someone with my constraints: three years, quant plus archival methods, and no Mandarin, so original survey experiments on Chinese respondents are realistically out. For each question, tell me what makes it worth the years or not — how upstream it is of the others, whether it's answerable by a grad student without impossible data access, and what kind of evidence would actually move it. And be blunt if you think one of these is overrated or rests on a conceptual confusion — I'd much rather hear that now than in year two.
