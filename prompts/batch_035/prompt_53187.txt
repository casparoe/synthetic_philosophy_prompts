I serve on the publication review board of a mid-sized AI research institute, and I've been asked to draft the memo recommending changes to our governance rules before our next quarterly meeting, so I'd like your help writing it. Some background: our current procedure is effectively a default-to-publish rule. Any of our roughly twenty research leads can green-light a paper from their own team after a lightweight review, and a paper is stopped only if someone actively intervenes — no formal vote, no quorum, nothing on the record. Over the past year, three papers that each faced substantial behind-the-scenes unease went out anyway, in one case a method that cut the compute needed for a fairly general agentic capability by a meaningful margin. In each case at least a third of the people who later said they were uncomfortable had assumed someone else would raise a formal objection. A colleague of mine keeps describing this as a straightforward instance of the unilateralist's curse: when several agents each act on their own noisy estimate of whether an action is net-positive, and any one of them can act unilaterally, the action gets taken whenever the single most optimistic estimate favors it, so error alone systematically selects for bad outcomes — no bad faith required.

Our board is now considering three reform proposals, and my memo has to recommend one (or a package) with concrete procedural detail. Proposal A formalizes a principle of conformity: no lead may publish if they have reason to believe that a majority of equally informed colleagues would judge the expected value of publication to be negative, even where their own honest estimate is positive. Proposal B replaces default-publish with a board-wide supermajority vote (twelve of twenty in favor) for any paper flagged as capability-relevant under a written screening checklist. Proposal C is the most unusual: a rotating single-veto holder, selected by lot each quarter, whose objection unilaterally blocks flagged papers, paired with mandatory pre-publication disclosure to the whole board. There is also a faction arguing for no procedural change at all — just calibration training for leads and more informal discussion — on the theory that our real problem is noise, and the way to fix noise is to reduce it at the source rather than to redesign voting thresholds.

Please draft the memo itself, roughly 800–1,200 words, addressed to the full board, with a clear and actionable bottom line. A few things I'd specifically like it to get right. First, it should be precise about what kind of problem the unilateralist's curse actually is — in particular, whether it is just a species of familiar collective-action failure or a selection effect on the maximum of noisy estimates, closer kin to the winner's curse in auctions — because that distinction bears on which reforms can help at all. Second, it should work through the mirror-image worry seriously: flipping the default from "publish unless stopped" to "stopped unless approved" may simply install the curse's pessimistic twin, in which a single noisy low estimate blocks good work, and the strongest opponents of reform in our ranks say exactly that. Third, our institute is fairly homogeneous — same subfield, same priors — so the memo should say something about whether the curse's implicit assumption of independent estimation errors does any work in our situation, and what follows if our reviewers' errors are strongly correlated. Finally, whatever you recommend, please treat the losing side's best argument honestly rather than as a box to be checked; the board will not thank me for a memo that reads like advocacy. You can give the memo any reasonable structure — recommendation, background, analysis of each proposal, likely objections — as long as a colleague could read it in ten minutes and know exactly what they are being asked to vote for and why.
