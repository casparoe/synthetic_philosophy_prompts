My daughter is due to be born in February, and since the ultrasound, questions I once treated as seminar-room abstractions — about how much the human future matters, and why — have become urgent to me in a way I did not anticipate. Before she arrives, I would like to get my own position straight, and I would be grateful for a careful audit of whether it is internally consistent. Here is the position, stated as honestly as I can:

(1) I do not believe that merely possible future people have moral claims on us. There is no one yet there to be wronged, and I am at least partly persuaded by the non-identity problem that our obligations do not properly extend to the merely possible.

(2) I am not an aggregative consequentialist. I do not think the badness of an event is a sum of well-being losses, and I do not think ethics is fundamentally about the amount of value in the universe; it is grounded, as I see it, in the lives and relationships of actual persons.

(3) I nevertheless believe that human extinction would be far worse than the billions of deaths it would involve — that there is a distinct further badness over and above those deaths, and that this further badness is not reducible to how the prospect of extinction would blight the lives of those who saw it coming.

(4) I find Samuel Scheffler's account in Death and the Afterlife persuasive in its central claim: that many of our most important present values — one's work, one's political commitments, the project of raising a child — depend on an unspoken background assumption that humanity will continue for a long time after we die, an assumption he calls the collective afterlife. I accept that if I learned humanity would end a generation after my own death, much of what now matters most to me would come to matter less.

(5) My own death, by contrast, does not seem to undermine my values in this way. I expect to die decades before my daughter does, and although that thought is painful, my concern for her life, for whatever children she may one day have, and for projects I will not live to see completed survives the contemplation of my own death intact.

(6) I think the further badness in (3) is not merely prudential: it is not just that extinction-level risks, taken seriously, would make the remaining years of people now living go worse for them.

When I try to locate this further badness, I keep running into the following sort of case. Suppose humanity were to end three hundred years from now, painlessly and without warning, with no one now alive ever learning of it. If (1) and (2) are right, the further badness of (3) must be located somewhere in presently living people; but in that scenario the last generations live well and nobody alive today is ever the wiser, so there seems to be nothing further to be bad. I am nonetheless inclined to think even that scenario would be a tragedy beyond the deaths it involves, and I cannot tell whether this inclination is a residue I should give up or a sign that one of (1), (2), or (6) has to go.

Are these commitments jointly tenable? I would like to know which of them are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and, for each genuine tension, what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me elsewhere. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. And because I may be more attached to some of these commitments than I realize, please close by saying what consideration would most plausibly move you to a different verdict about where the deepest tension lies, and where in your own audit you think the reasoning is most likely to fail.
