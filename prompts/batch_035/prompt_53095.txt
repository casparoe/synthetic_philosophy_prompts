Two friends of mine, Dee and Marcus, had a long argument about Thomson's lamp — the lamp thats switched on, then off after a minute, on again after half a minute, and so on, with infinitely many flips packed into two minutes. Dee said the scenario shows supertasks are impossible: at the end the switch must be either on or off, but the flip sequence doesn't determine which, so you've derived a contradiction, so no such lamp can exist. Marcus said that's a bad inference — the setup just underdetermines the final state, the way asking for the parity of the "last" natural number does, and an incomplete description isn't a contradiction. Dee countered that the lamp is a physical object, so at the end there IS a fact of the matter about the switch, and if the flip-by-flip story can't supply it, the scenario can't be built. Marcus said physics might supply it anyway — if the lamp's glow decays continuously after each flip and the decay time shrinks along with the intervals, everything fades to zero at the end, so continuity can fix the final state, which suggests the original puzzle is about a missing stipulation, not impossibility. Dee said fine, but he could rig the same mechanism to alternate the lamp's color red/blue, where there's no continuous quantity settling anything, so the continuity trick only rescues lucky variants. Marcus's last word was that this at most shows our physics won't permit the mechanism (flips can't stay meaningful below some scale, heat dissipation, etc.), which is an empirical limitation, not the logical contradiction Dee claimed at the start. Dee said conceding it can't be built IS conceding supertasks are impossible. They also got stuck on whether just walking across a room counts as completing a supertask (Marcus said yes, infinitely many stages in finite time; Dee said the stages there shrink in a convergent way, like Achilles chasing the tortoise, so the lamp is different in kind and lumping them together is sneaky).
