I am a novelist drafting a satire set in an unnamed country whose Ministry of Moral Optimization has concluded, after an internal productivity review, that guilt is underperforming as a national moral emotion — it operates invisibly, works without witnesses, and generates repair when the review would prefer deterrence — and has accordingly mandated a phased national transition to shame. The document I need is the ministry's five-clause directive to the citizenry, which appears in my third chapter: my protagonist, a low-ranking ministry proofreader, must correct it for grammar before publication, and her marginal notes will carry the novel's real argument. So what I am after is the directive itself, written in flawless deadpan bureaucratese, with no preamble and no closing formula — exactly five numbered clauses, each a sentence or two, the inhuman terseness being part of the joke.

The comedy has to come from the actual content of the guilt/shame distinction, so that a reader who knows the philosophy sees that the ministry has read it and misunderstood it in a characteristic way. To seed some of what I want smuggled in: guilt is famously private and audience-independent, so a clause should treat this as an efficiency problem — all those unmonitored consciences running at full cost with no witnesses on site. Shame requires an internalized other, an imagined observer whose respect one cares about, so the directive must somewhere confront the awkwardness of legislating an audience, perhaps by nationalizing it. The reparation/concealment asymmetry should surface too: guilt tends toward repair, shame toward hiding, and the ministry's language should quietly prefer the second while advertising the first. And the question the whole novel turns on — whether a culture can actually choose its moral emotions by decree, or whether the directive will simply produce citizens who feel guilty about not feeling ashamed — should be visible in the cracks of the clauses without ever being stated, ideally because at least one clause betrays that its own authors cannot comply with it.

Please keep each clause to a sentence or two, exactly five clauses, no introduction, conclusion, or ministry letterhead flourish beyond what fits inside the clauses themselves, and make sure that a specialist reader would find the satire fair — the ministry is wrong about guilt and shame in an instructive way, not in a way that would make a philosopher wince at the caricature.
