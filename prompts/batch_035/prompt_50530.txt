My wife and I had our neighbors over for dinner last weekend, and an argument broke out that I couldn't referee, mostly because I found both sides convincing at different moments. I'm a retired chemist, so take that for what it's worth. The trigger was a documentary we'd all seen about how people in different societies answer moral questions differently — whether you should report your brother to the police, whether hitting a child is discipline or abuse, how much you owe strangers versus kin, whether fairness means equal shares or proportional ones. Dave (retired geologist) said the takeaway is obvious: moral "truths" are just local customs, there's no culture-independent fact of the matter, and anyone who talks about objective morality is just mistaking their own upbringing for the universe's opinion. His wife Elena, who practiced psychiatry for 30 years, thought that was way too fast. She said people disagreed for centuries about the shape of the earth and about continental drift, and that didn't make geology relative — disagreement shows at most that the truth is hard to find, not that there isn't one. Dave came back with what I thought was his best move: the geography analogy fails because you can settle geographic disputes by observation and triangulation from a neutral standpoint, and there's no such standpoint for morals — every "observation" you make about whether, say, an honor killing is wrong is made from inside some moral framework, so the disagreement isn't like a disagreement about mountains, it's more like a disagreement about which coordinate system is "really" north.

Elena didn't accept that. She said frameworks themselves can be evaluated — by internal consistency, by whether they can account for the full range of cases without ad hoc patches, by whether they survive reflection and learning about other people — and that this is the same kind of objectivity we settle for in plenty of respectable domains. Dave answered that this just begs the question: the cultures whose intuitions differ from ours would say exactly the same about their own frameworks — they're consistent, they hang together, they've survived centuries of reflection among their own people — so what Elena calls "surviving reflection" is just her own culture's standards of reflection wearing a lab coat. Apparently among the researchers who do this cross-cultural work there's even an acronym for the kind of societies that supply most of the psychology data — western, educated, rich ones — and Dave said the whole framework-evaluating picture of morality is exactly what you'd expect those societies to come up with. Elena said something about that being a genetic fallacy, and then dessert arrived and we changed the subject to the neighbor's fence.

Since I couldn't referee it, maybe you can. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall. A few things I'd especially like your read on: does the empirical fact of variation actually support Dave's conclusion, or could our moral intuitions be unreliable guides to moral truth everywhere without that meaning there's no truth to guide us to? Is the "no neutral standpoint" premise enough to get you to relativism, or are there objective domains (mathematics? logic?) that also lack a neutral observational standpoint? And is Elena's move — evaluating frameworks by consistency and reflection — a genuine answer or just smuggling her own starting point in as the referee, as Dave claimed? Also curious what, if anything, you think both of them were missing entirely.
