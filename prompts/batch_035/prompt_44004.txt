Before I say something embarrassing in my seminar, I want to check an argument I've assembled about global workspace theory. My understanding is that Ned Block drew a distinction between access consciousness, meaning content that is available for reasoning, report, and the control of action, and phenomenal consciousness, meaning what it is like to undergo the state; and that workspace theory, in Baars's and Dehaene's versions, is meant as an account of access, on which content becomes conscious by being broadcast to a wide range of cognitive processors. It seems to me, though, that the empirical literature has already moved past that division. In the Sperling paradigm, subjects who view a brief array of letters say afterward that they were aware of all or nearly all of them, even though they can report only a few once the display is gone; since the total extent of their experience is simply what they tell us it was, this looks like a direct measurement of phenomenal consciousness. Meanwhile, neuroimaging shows that when a stimulus becomes reportable, the frontoparietal workspace network ignites, which would give us a second, neural measurement of the very same property. If phenomenal consciousness can be measured in these ways, then I take it the hard problem was never anything more than the claim that consciousness cannot be measured; once the measurements are in, the explanatory gap should be closed, or at worst reduced to the ordinary business of finding neural correlates. So my question is this: given that the measurements are on the table, why do philosophers such as Chalmers keep insisting the gap is still open? Is it just a matter of defending the discipline's territory, or have I misdrawn the line somewhere?
