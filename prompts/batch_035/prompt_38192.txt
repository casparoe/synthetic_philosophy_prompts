Our university has introduced a general-education requirement that every student complete one module on Western values, and I have spent the academic year on the committee that rules on which proposed teaching modules satisfy it. Our record so far: we approved a module on Locke's case for religious toleration and the separation of church and state; we approved a module on fifth-century Athenian democracy and citizenship; we approved, on condition that it be taught alongside a unit on the conquest of the Americas, a module on the Salamanca school's defense of the natural rights of indigenous peoples; we approved a module on Justinian's compilation of Roman law, compiled in Constantinople but sold to us by its proposer as the root of the continental civil-law tradition; we rejected a module on the Byzantine doctrine of symphonia, the harmony of imperial and priestly authority; we rejected a module on the falasifa, built around Averroes on the harmony of philosophy and religious law; and we rejected a module on Christian charity and the corporal works of mercy, with one member minuted as observing that Christianity is now a global religion and that charity is not distinctively anything.

Three proposals are on next month's agenda. The first would teach religious toleration through the Ottoman millet system and the convivencia of medieval Islamic Spain: toleration before Locke, in institutions that predate him by centuries. The second would teach the twelfth-century translation movement centered on Toledo, by which Muslim and Jewish scholars made Aristotle, together with the Arabic commentary on him, newly available to Latin Christendom. The third would teach the Meiji-era Japanese reception of Western political thought: natural rights and constitutionalism as argued for by writers such as Fukuzawa Yukichi, under the slogan of Japanese spirit and Western technique.

I find I can predict some of my colleagues' votes but not their reasoning, because at least two tests seem to fit our rulings so far, at least roughly: one on which the requirement concerns a fixed list of political and legal values, wherever and by whomever articulated, and one on which it concerns the inheritance of Greece and Rome as taken up by the Latin church — though I suspect there are others, and I am not certain either of these two fits every ruling we have made. Please tell me which rules genuinely fit our precedents; what each rule predicts about the three proposals, and which earlier ruling is the closest analog for each; your best guess at how we shall in fact rule on each, with your reasons; and, wherever our record genuinely underdetermines the decision, what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Finally, describe the further module proposal — an unrealistic degree of specificity is welcome — whose approval or rejection would best reveal which rule the committee is actually running.
