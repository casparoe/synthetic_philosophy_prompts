I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and a recurring comparison in our seminar on the badness of pain has begun to bother me. Whenever pain asymbolia comes up — the condition, described classically in patients with certain limbic and cingulate damage, in which people report that a stimulus hurts but seem not to mind: they do not pull away, they do not guard the limb, some even appear amused by the pinch — the same analogy is produced: pain is a burglar alarm. The nerve signal is the sensor; the unpleasantness is the siren; suffering is what the household does when the siren goes off; and asymbolia is a house in which the sensor trips and the siren sounds, but nobody inside treats it as urgent. The conclusion drawn is that the badness of pain lies not in the sensation but in the downstream response: asymbolics have the alarm without the emergency, and they are fine, so what is bad about pain must be the caring, not the feeling. This is then extended in two directions: to fetal and animal pain, where it is argued that without the alarm-to-response wiring there is no pain-badness worth the name, and to drug design, where the suggestion is that we should aim to disconnect the response rather than dull the sensation.

I would like a proper audit of the alarm analogy rather than another round of gesturing at it. Specifically, please provide: (1) an explicit correspondence table — what in the analogy plays the role of the sensor, the siren, the monitoring service, the intruder, and the household, and what each of these is supposed to be in the actual case; (2) the points where the mapping breaks, separated into those that matter for the conclusions being drawn and those that do not; (3) what the analogy quietly imports — an alarm is an artifact whose function is fixed by a designer, its badness for the household is exhausted by the costs of responding to it and of ignoring it, and it can be responsibly disconnected once the risk is known — and I suspect some of this is doing work it should not; and (4) a verdict on whether the conclusion survives: does asymbolia actually establish that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the feeling? In answering, I would especially like you to confront an ambiguity the seminar keeps sliding past. On one reading, the asymbolic does not feel the badness, because the affective component is absent from the experience itself; on another, the asymbolic feels it fully and it simply fails to motivate — and the alarm picture, in which the siren and the response are separate stages, cannot seem to distinguish these two readings, even though the ethical upshots (for instance, about what a fetus, or a patient under certain forms of sedation, is undergoing) differ sharply between them. If the analogy cannot carry this weight, please say so and propose a better one, indicating what it would predict about the two readings.
