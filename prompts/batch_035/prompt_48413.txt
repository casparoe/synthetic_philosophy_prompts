I keep running into a certain move in free will arguments and I can't figure out if it's a recognized thing with a name, or just something people do. The pattern: someone asks why you made some choice, you give a reason, and they just push back one level. Okay, you chose this job because you value security — but why do you value security? Because of how you were raised. Did you pick your upbringing? Eventually you land on genes and parents and culture, none of which you chose, and then comes the punchline: it's not just that this particular choice wasn't free, it's that nothing ever could be, because to be truly responsible for what you do you'd somehow have to be responsible for how you are in the first place, and how you are was fixed before any choosing got done. What strikes me is that the argument doesn't seem to need determinism at all — if some quantum coinflip landed in my childhood and gave me my temperament, that's not "me" controlling anything either, randomness is just as alien to the self as heredity is. So it feels more like a regress than a scientific claim: control over actions requires control over the sources of control, and the sources run out no matter what physics says.

I know the surrounding landscape a bit — hard determinism, the consequence argument, the general idea that free will and determinism clash — but this specific move of never letting you stop at "I just am the kind of person who deliberates like this" seems different, since it's supposed to work in every possible world, not just deterministic ones. So: does this line of argument have an established name, and who actually defends it? Is it taken seriously or regarded as a trick? I'd also want to know how it relates to the nearby stuff — the luck objection, those thought experiments where a neuroscientist manipulates someone's decisions, and whatever the difference is between the two Strawsons people always mix up in this debate. And to be clear, don't just hand me a label, I want to know where the joints are: what exactly is the argument claiming, where do philosophers think it goes wrong (or whether the standard "you're equivocating on 'responsible'" reply actually lands), and what the strongest response to it is. I've argued this with a friend for months and we mostly talk past eachother, so I need the actual carve-up, not a name.
