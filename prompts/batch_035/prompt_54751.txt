I teach an intermediate course on decision theory and game theory to a mixed group of eighteen philosophy and economics majors, and we have one 75-minute session coming up on the absent-minded driver problem — the one Piccione and Rubinstein made famous. My students are comfortable with expected values and extensive-form games, and last term they handled a staged centipede game perfectly well, so they can be trusted with dice, sealed envelopes, and mild deception by the facilitator. Here is the setup I want the activity built around. A driver is on a dark highway at night with two exits that look identical; he cannot recall whether he has already passed one, so at any exit he reaches, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. At each exit he can only choose "exit" or "continue," and because the two choice situations are informationally identical, whatever policy he commits to is the same policy applied at both exits. The payoffs I want to use: exiting at the first exit is worth 1, exiting at the second is worth 4, and driving past both exits is worth 0. So the plan he would obviously adopt if he could tell the exits apart — continue at the first, exit at the second — is precisely the plan he cannot implement.

What I want them to feel, in order: first, the shock of discovering that their favorite plan is unexecutable and that the best single instruction they can actually give themselves, with no randomization at all, is worth at most 1; second, the discovery that letting themselves randomize *at the wheel* does better, though still a pretty dispiriting fraction of the 4 they thought they'd secured — and I'd like the debrief to include the actual arithmetic, computed after the game and not before; and third, the seductive pull of interim reasoning, the thought that strikes you standing at an unknown exit — "if this is the second one, exiting is worth 4" — and how deviating from your own committed policy on that basis is both completely natural and self-undermining from the planning perspective. That third one is the heart of the session and I suspect the hardest to stage, because the whole problem is that the driver can't count his own intersections, while my students can and will, unless the design takes that away from them deliberately — perhaps by having the facilitator run trials in secret, with the student-driver receiving no feedback about which exit they're at or even whether they've been past one already.

So please design the full session: staging, rules, materials, a points-based payoff scheme (no real money, but the points should sting a little), the sequence of rounds and roles, and a timeline that fits 75 minutes including a 15-minute debrief at the end. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the formal distinctions — the difference between what's optimal from the planning standpoint and what's optimal from inside an indistinguishable node, the difference between randomizing once over plans and randomizing at each exit, the sense in which the driver is almost two agents who can't communicate — and it should connect back to whatever actually happened in the room rather than to the textbook. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect students to peek, to collude with the facilitator, or to insist on counting intersections) and how I should handle each, and be frank about which feature of the absent-minded driver's situation no classroom staging can genuinely reproduce — where the facilitator has to supply the absent-mindedness by fiat and the analogy visibly becomes a convention the group accepts rather than something they experience.
