I teach an upper-level seminar called Learning and Induction with a mix of philosophy and ML students, and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on the no-free-lunch theorems and their relationship to Hume's problem of induction, but im tired of answers that just recite "any two algorithms perform equally when averaged over all possible objective functions" and consider the job done. The crux I want the question to force is whether the NFL theorems amount to a genuine formal vindication of Humean skepticism about generalization, or whether they're just a restatement of Hume in fancier notation -- and either way, what they show about where the real work of induction gets done, i.e. in substantive assumptions about the world baked into the learner, and whether those assumptions can themselves be justified empirically without the whole thing going circular. I also want the question to make them notice the obvious disanalogy a clever student will spot anyway: actual learning problems aren't drawn uniformly from the space of all possible target functions, so whether the uniformity assumption is doing skeptical work or just smuggling the skepticism into the theorem's setup is exactly the thing a good answer has to confront rather than paper over.
