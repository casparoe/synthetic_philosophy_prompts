I have read rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, which means I have had the sunk cost fallacy drilled into me for well over a decade, and no exhibit gets cited more than Martin Shubik's dollar auction of 1971: a dollar is auctioned to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder pays their bid too and gets nothing, and the two leaders keep outbidding each other straight past a dollar because at every step quitting costs more than a nickel. The standard lesson — one this community repeats almost ritually — is that escalating is what bias looks like from the inside, and that the rational move is to cut your losses before the trap closes.

Lately I suspect that lesson is stated too fast, and I want the suspicion turned into a precise object. Construct a thought experiment for me: a dollar-auction-flavored scenario, set somewhere ordinary (a bidding war over a supplier contract, a rivalry between departments, a startup burning runway to close a round — your choice), in which the participant who visibly escalates — who keeps paying in after a bystander would scream sunk cost — is behaving rationally, while the advisor who tells them to cut their losses and walk away is the one giving bad advice. That is the reverse of the usual moral, and I want it earned.

Constraints: the scenario itself should be one paragraph and realistic, no clever demons or perfect predictors. The escalating agent must have no private information — the advisor can see everything the agent sees — and no exotic stubbornness; the rationality must fall out of the payoff structure and the interaction, the way the trap in the original auction falls out of the fee rule, rather than from anything stipulated about the agent's character. After the scenario, walk me through the reasoning that makes escalation correct here (compare it against the backward-induction story told about the original game, or against whatever replaces that story when the payoff structure changes), identify precisely which structural feature is doing the work — I half-suspect it is the asymmetry between the current leader and the current runner-up, or some future-interaction term, but I want to be told rather than agreed with — and show what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, steelman the sunk-cost purist: what would they say to deny that your case involves escalation of commitment at all, and is that denial substantive or merely a relabeling of the same behavior? If it is merely verbal, say what observation would separate their reading of the case from yours.
