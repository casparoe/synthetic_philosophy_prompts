At our hospital, the standing defense of withdrawing ventilator support runs on an analogy: the ventilator is a rescuer who has been holding the patient's head above water. Turning it off is the rescuer letting go and swimming to shore: the patient is returned to the threat that was already there — the water — so the underlying disease, not anyone's action, does the killing, and the clinicians merely declined, at the end, to go on preventing it. The ethics committee uses this analogy to support two conclusions: that withdrawal is letting die rather than killing, and that it is permissible whenever never starting the ventilator would have been. One attending remains uneasy and puts the worry like this: we don't simply stop — someone walks to the machine and dials the settings down over two days, titrating sedation around each step, and the patient dies within minutes of the last adjustment; that looks like a series of acts with a foreseeable death at the end, not a rescuer quietly slipping away.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a lecture on the acts/omissions doctrine. Set out what maps onto what — the water, the weight pulling the patient down, the rescuer's arms, the moment of release, the bystander on the shore who never entered, the rescuer's own strength running out — and then say where the mapping breaks, sorting the disanalogies into those that matter for the committee's two conclusions and those that don't.

Two issues I want addressed specifically. First, does the analogy get its force from taking the natural course of the disease as the baseline, so that "the disease kills" comes out true by stipulation rather than argument — and if so, is that a fatal circularity, or a legitimate way of fixing the description of the case? Second, is the attending's point about gradual weaning a genuine disanalogy, or only the same situation made vivid?

Close with a verdict: does the conclusion that withdrawal is letting die, and permissible whenever non-initiation would be, survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if it does not, say whether some repaired or replacement analogy could still support it, or whether the conclusion would have to be defended on an entirely different footing.
