For about twelve years I've served on the board of a small family charitable fund — no philosophy training on my part; I studied accounting — and somewhere along the way I developed what I later found out is called a view about distributive justice, though I developed it entirely from committee meetings. The view is roughly: a benefit matters more the worse off the person receiving it is. So a $5,000 grant that keeps someone housed counts for more than a $5,000 grant that marginally improves an already comfortable person's prospects, even if the second is more "cost-effective" in the ordinary sense. I've defended this to my fellow board members with some success.

But working through the implications on my own, I've run into trouble, and I want to understand which of the problems I've noticed are the ones worth actually thinking hard about, versus ones I should just make a decision about and move on. Here is my situation, as I understand it.

First, I notice that my view, pushed to its limit, seems to say that a world where the well-off are knocked down a peg and nobody is helped is in one respect better — inequality went down. I find that hard to accept, and yet when I try to build the weighting more carefully — a curve that gives each person's wellbeing a weight depending on their position — I think I can avoid saying that, since lowering someone always lowers the weighted total. But I'm not sure whether this fix is a genuine escape or whether I've just defined my way out of an intuition I can't shake. Second, I can't decide whether my worry about "knocking people down a peg" is about the resulting state of the world or about someone doing the knocking: if inequality shrinks through a hurricane rather than a policy, my reaction is different, and I don't know what to do with that. Third, I genuinely can't settle what the curve should look like — whether the extra weight for the worse off should depend on their absolute position, on how far they are below some "enough" line, or on how bad their situation is relative to others in the same pool. These give different answers for our actual grant decisions, which is the part that keeps me up at night: next cycle we must choose between one large grant to a program serving people in real destitution and several smaller grants to programs that do measurable good for people who are merely struggling, and which way I lean seems to depend on which of the three curve-shapes I privately endorse.

So here is my request. Assume I can spend perhaps a year of serious reading and thinking, in evenings, with no intention of entering academia — the practical beneficiary is our fund's allocation policy and my own peace of mind. Rank the open questions in this territory, explain your ordering, and be explicit about your criteria: which questions actually change what our board should do, which ones other questions silently depend on, and which ones are interesting to specialists but wouldn't move our decisions at all. Include anything important I haven't listed — I coined my own vocabulary for all of this, so there are surely standard questions I've never happened upon.

One request about how you present it: since I can't easily tell on my own, please keep a clear running distinction between what is broadly settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested. Where serious people disagree, say that they do and give each side its due rather than picking a winner and presenting it as the received answer — I'd rather know the shape of the dispute than receive a verdict that pretends to more than the field has. And if any part of my framing above smuggles in a controversial assumption, please point that out too, since I may have built the whole puzzle crooked without noticing.
