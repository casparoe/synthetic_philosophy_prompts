I teach just war theory, and for some years I have defended, in my graduate seminar, the moral equality of combatants — the thesis that combatants on both sides of a war are legitimate targets and may kill one another provided they fight within the rules, so that the justice of their side's cause is irrelevant to the permissibility of what they do on the battlefield. I have now assembled my defense into numbered steps for a departmental colloquium, and a colleague in ethics tells me the argument proves too much. Before I stand up in front of my colleagues, I want a careful diagnosis of where, if anywhere, it goes wrong.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) What transforms battlefield killing from murder into permissible killing is not the justice of either side's cause but the combatants' mutual undertaking of the role of fighter: by donning the uniform and taking up arms, soldiers on both sides knowingly and voluntarily enter a practice whose participants are permitted to kill one another.
(2) A voluntary undertaking of a role can change both what its holder is permitted to do and what may permissibly be done to him — as when a patient's consent turns what would otherwise be battery into surgery.
(3) Since combatants on each side have undertaken the role and their opponents have undertaken it as well, combatants on both sides are permitted to kill one another; since civilians have not undertaken it, they remain immune.
(4) Whether a combatant acts permissibly therefore depends only on his conformity to the rules governing the role — proportionality, discrimination, no treachery, humane treatment of prisoners — and not on the ad bellum justice of his side.
(5) Hence combatants are moral equals: the unjust conscript who kills a just defender while scrupulously following the rules does him no wrong, and the just defender stands no higher, morally, than his attacker.

My colleague's extension: nothing in the argument restricts it to war. If voluntary entry into a practice of mutual killing licenses the killing, then dueling associations, gladiatorial games stocked with volunteers, and paid death matches between consenting fighters are permissible too, provided their internal rules are followed and no bystanders are harmed. And she adds a second blade: if I retreat to the claim that the war convention is special because it is authorized by states, then moral equality rests on state license, which would make even a war of aggression permissible by decree — a conclusion nobody in the room would accept.

There is a further wrinkle I cannot dodge: conscripts. Many soldiers in history volunteered for nothing. If (1) requires voluntary undertaking, then conscripts have not changed their moral status, and killing them is murder — so the argument proves not only too much, in my colleague's direction, but also too little, since it cannot cover the conscript armies whose equality it was meant to secure. I gather the standard repair is some appeal to tacit or hypothetical consent, but I cannot see how to make that repair without either collapsing back into state license or thinning consent to the point where the gladiators qualify for it as well.

Tell me, then, which premise or step I should give up, and what each rejection costs. Specifically: (a) is the dueling extension a fair application of the argument or a disanalogy I can articulate in advance? (b) is the conscript problem fatal to the consent rationale, or can the tacit-consent repair be made to work at a price worth paying? (c) if I abandon consent altogether, is there any remaining route to moral equality that does not run through state authorization? For what it is worth, if forced to choose, I would sooner retain a robust distinction between combatants and civilians than retain the moral equality of combatants — but I want to know precisely what each sacrifice buys.
