So, I'm a psych PhD student (emotion regulation lab, so adjacent to this stuff), and last weekend two friends from my cohort and I watched a really bleak, devastatingly sad film together, and afterwards they got into a full argument about why anyone seeks out art that makes them sad, and now they want me to referee it and I genuinely can't call it. Help me out.

Setup: all three of us cried. None of us regrets watching it. That's the puzzle.

Rosa's line was basically Hume's, though she didn't know it at first (someone told her afterwards the view is in Hume's short essay on tragedy). Real grief is aversive, but when the sorrow is wrapped in enough artistry — the images, the pacing, the music — the same representations that would hurt in real life give pleasure instead. The artistry doesn't just sugarcoat the pain, it converts it; the eloquence moves the passion over onto the pleasing side. That's why we pay for the well-made version, while a clumsy melodrama about the same events just feels gross.

Jules said that gets the phenomenology backwards. When she listens to devastating music after a breakup, she's not enjoying the craft while the sadness gets quietly transformed into pleasure — she's enjoying the sadness itself. She wants to feel it, she'd be dissapointed if the song somehow didn't hurt. Pain and pleasure coexist in it; there's no conversion happening. What's pleasant is the whole being-moved state, which is partly made of the sorrow.

Rosa: if the sadness itself were the pleasure, why the asymmetry? Real grief is avoided like the plague; art-grief is sought out and even paid for. Same emotion, opposite valence — something has to explain the flip, and well-made-ness plus conversion is the natural story.

Jules: no conversion needed, the difference is belief. She knows it's a film; there's no actual loss, no action tendency, nothing to cope with. Distanced sadness is pleasant the way a rollercoaster is — the arousal without the demand. So the flip is explained by safety, not by the artistry doing alchemy on the emotion.

Rosa came back: then the artistry is doing no work at all in your story, and that can't be right either — a shot-for-shot remake with flat direction wouldn't do the same thing to you. So either the craft converts the emotion, or you owe me a story where the craft matters but doesn't convert.

Jules: the craft matters because it intensifies the feeling, and intensity is what being-moved is made of. More hurt, more of the good state. And if anything your conversion story predicts the wrong thing — the better made the scene, the less it should hurt, since more and more of the sorrow got converted into pleasure. But the best scenes are the ones that hurt the most.

Then they both looked at me, and I said I'd think about it. That was a week ago.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And tell me if there's anything both of them are missing that would dissolve part of this. (My suspicion, which I'm honestly not sure is fair, is that they're both assuming pleasure names one single thing, and that the fight partly dissolves once you pull that apart — but you tell me.)
