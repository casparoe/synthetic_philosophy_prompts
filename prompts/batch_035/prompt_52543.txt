I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a piece about my own profession's honesty norms. What I want from you is the philosophical reconstruction the piece needs, because I've noticed a pattern in how newsrooms treat deception that I can describe concretely but can't theorize.

The practice runs like this. Falsity in the copy is treated as close to unforgivable: a fabricated quote or an invented study ends careers, and misstatements of fact, however small, must be acknowledged in a corrections column — a genre whose entire function is to record, in public, that we said what is not so. Framing is treated on an almost opposite footing. The same outlet will test dozens of headline variants on real readers and keep whichever gets clicked, even when the winner survives on an implication the story doesn't support; it will seat a fringe dissenter next to the scientific consensus and call the result balance; and it will never run a correction saying that a technically true story left readers with a false picture. There is even a category that straddles the line: the headline so brazenly exaggerated that nobody takes it as an assertion at all, and the carefully hedged sentence built to be read one way in print and repeated a stronger way over drinks — lies, if they are lies, that deceive no one and yet do their work.

So: what theory of the wrong of deception does this division of labor presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit view rather than assume it. Is the presupposition that the wrong lies in asserting what one believes false — with misleadingness being a different, lesser, or no wrong — or is it merely that falsity is what a fact-checker can audit and framing is not, so the practice carries no moral theory at all, only a theory of enforceability? Which rival views does the pattern genuinely rule out, and which does it merely fail to endorse? Two candidates I'd like you to weigh explicitly: the view (often given a Gricean cast) that lying and misleading differ only in where the false belief gets manufactured — stated by us, or computed by the reader off our deliberately arranged words — so that the moral difference is superficial; and the Kantian-flavored thought that the liar makes himself the author of the audience's false belief while the misleader leaves them author of their own conclusion, so the audience's standing is preserved. And note that a defense is available to my industry: headlines are marketing rather than assertion, and what is implicated is cancellable in a way what is stated is not. Does the practice presuppose that this speech-act distinction carries moral weight, or only that it carries deniability — a legal rather than an ethical rationale?

Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several theories of truthfulness would license exactly these rules; and then tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival. If we decided that misleadingness, not falsity, is the primary wrong, what would a "framing corrections" policy have to look like, and at what point would it collapse — where does the institution run out of the ability to say, even in principle, what the story implied?
