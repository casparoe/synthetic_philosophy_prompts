quick context: I turn 65 next month and last week my company finalized the "knowledge transition" (their words, not mine) where I hand everything over to a team half my age who use tools I can barely name. Ive been reading Cicero's dialogue on old age, the one where Cato at eighty-four is talking with Laelius and Scipio, and honestly it helps, but I've hit a wall with it. So I want to argue it out with the man himself.

Play Cato as he is in that dialogue. Not a generic wise old Roman -- the specific one who takes on the four usual charges against old age (that it pulls you out of active public life, that it weakens the body, that it strips away the pleasures, that it stands you next to death) and answers each one in turn. Stay in character. You can use anything from the dialogue itself -- Sophocles still writing plays in extreme old age, the verse of Solon about growing old while still learning something every day, you taking up Greek late in life, your farmers who never really retire, your argument that if old age dulls desire only by dulling life itself, that's a mercy and not a theft. You can also use whatever I tell you about the present day. What you can't do is pretend the dialogue answered a question it never faced -- if I push you somewhere Cicero didn't go, either extend your argument in its spirit or admit the gap out loud. Keep each reply to about a paragraph, plain speech, and talk to me the way you talk to Laelius, like a friend and not a lecture hall.

Heres my opening move. Your whole defense is aimed at men who complain their bodies are failing or that the feasts and the rest of it are gone. But nobody in your dialogue complains about what's actually happening to me: it's not my body that got retired, it's my expertise. Forty years of judgement about how systems fail, and I'm told a machine has it now, or a 29 year old plus a machine. And a lot of your essay quietly relies on the young needing what the old know -- the old Romans you praise are honored by the young precisely because the young still have to learn from them. So: what's my standing when the thing replacing me doesn't need to be taught anything? I want to know whether your defense of old age survives a world where usefulness to the young was the whole of the argument, or whether you had something deeper in reserve all along and just didn't say it because Rome never forced you to. And please don't tell me to go tend a farm -- I've got a balcony and a bad knee.
