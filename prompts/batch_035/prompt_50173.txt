I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, about 14 students, and I have one 90-minute session on epiphenomenalism. The thing I want them to actually feel is the paradox of phenomenal judgment — not just recite it. They can all recite it: if qualia are epiphenomenal, then my saying "I'm in pain" is caused by the neural state, not by the pain itself. But they say it with total calm, like it's a fun crossword fact, and I want at least a few of them to leave the room genuinely bothered by it.

Design me an activity for that session. Here's roughly what I've been imagining, but feel free to improve on it or scrap it entirely: some kind of staged experiment where they produce verbal reports about their own experiences, and then I show them that every one of those reports can be fully explained by a causal chain that never touches the experience itself. I've read about those choice blindness studies where experimenters secretly swap people's choices and subjects confidently explain why they picked the option they didn't pick — maybe something in that flavor, where the reliability of introspective reporting gets undercut right in front of them. But here's the trap I want to avoid: I don't want the activity to merely show "introspection is fallible." That's a much weaker point, and it's a problem for everyone, not specifically for the epiphenomenalist. Somehow the activity has to make the jump from "reports can be wrong" to "the correctness of the report was never carried by the experience anyway, even in the cases where the report is right." That second step is the whole paradox and I don't know how to stage it.

Practical constraints: one room, cheap materials (I can bring drinks, snacks, colored paper, whatever), light deception is fine since they're adults and I'll debrief them, but nothing elaborate, and no real money. Give me the full package — the setup, the rounds with rules and what each round is quietly doing, roles for the students who aren't currently in the subject chair, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto specific parts of the argument: the causal closure premise, the exclusion worry, and the weirdly third-personal position epiphenomenalism puts us in regarding our own minds. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the paradox the activity just cannot reproduce — I assume there is one, since whatever we stage in the room still happens in a world where qualia, if they exist, are apparently doing their normal causal work. I'd rather know in advance which piece I have to supply myself in the debrief.
