So here's my situation. I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas, and for the last year or so my beat has drifted onto AI policy. There are two researchers I keep going back to for quotes, and they've been arguing past eachother for basically forever — in my inbox, in the comments under each other's op-eds, at panels. I want to try to get them onto a single page, and I figured the right artifact is the joint statement they could both actually sign if they sat down and did one of those good-faith adversarial collaboration exercises. Here's the shape of the fight, as best I can reconstruct it.

Researcher A's line is that the only historically honest anchor for transformative AI is the printing press. Not the speed-of-printing part — she says the real disruption was legitimation: who got to produce knowledge, certify it, and act on it, and what happened to the gatekeepers (the scribes, the censors) whose whole position rested on copying being expensive. She's honest that the transition was awful — Europe got its century of religious war out of it, and she'll say so — but her claim is that nobody had to invent a wholly new kind of institution from scratch; the old ones bent and eventually held, and the long-run balance sheet came out clearly positive.

Researcher B says that's a category error and the real analogue is nuclear weapons. His claim: what changed in 1945 wasn't that some capability got cheaper, it's that a single consequence became possible that could not be walked back, and the response wasn't old institutions bending — it was constructing new ones with no precedent at all, the inspections-and-treaties apparatis that had to be built because nothing that existed could hold that class of risk. His counter to A: the press never handed any one actor the ability to end the story, and a precedent only teaches you about institutional failure modes when the failure is survivable for the civilization.

And here's the thing — when I push each of them one on one, they agree on a lot. AI is unprecedented, both analogies break somewhere, neither thinks history repeats itself. So I suspect a decent chunk of this is verbal, and I have a specific suspicion: they're both doing "pick the feature of the precedent that does the argumentative work," they just disagree about which feature, and maybe also about what an analogy is even for — B talks like his is evidence, A talks like hers is a hypothesis generator, and I don't think either has noticed they're doing different things with the same move.

So draft me the document. I want the claims they could both sign, numbered. Then the residual disagreements, each stated twice — once in wording A would accept as fair to her side, once in wording B would accept as fair to his — because they will both read this and neither will sign anything that smells slanted. Then the cruxes: my guesses are (1) whether a bad-but-survivable outcome tells you anything about a non-survivable one, and (2) whether "the balance sheet came out positive over 500 years" is even a coherent way to talk, given the people who bore the worst of it don't get compensated by later gains — but you tell me if those are the real cruxes or if I'm off. For each crux, give me some concrete case or consideration they'd both agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, so I can check later who called it. And if you conclude part of the dispute really is just about words, tell me which part and why, since if that's true I'd rather say so in my piece than keep booking them on panels together.
