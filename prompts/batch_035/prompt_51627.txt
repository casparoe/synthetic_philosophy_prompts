The hook is something a researcher told me: best estimates put something like 10 quintillion insects alive at any moment, roughly a billion per person on Earth. Even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is really low — some people who study this put it well below 1% — multiplying a tiny probability by that absurd number still gives you more potentially-sentient creatures than there are humans, by many orders of magnitude. The "scale argument," I gather: huge numbers can outweigh a low probability.

My editor thinks this is a killer framing, but before I build a whole piece on it I want to understand what it actually implies, if anything. So: what are the most important implications of the scale argument for everyday decisions and for public-facing science writing? Specifically I'd love help with:

1. Does the reasoning survive the standard worry that expected-value thinking with tiny probabilities and astronomical payoffs is exactly where our intuitions fail us? Where's the line between this and stuff like Pascal's mugging?
2. Where does "moral weight per creature" come in? Even granting some probability of sentience, it seems like a fair question whether an insect's interests could weigh as much as a chicken's or a dog's, and whether big numbers can compensate for small weight. Is that a coherent question or a category error?
3. The "proves too much" objection: if scale beats low probability, doesn't the same logic sweep in soil mites, nematodes, maybe even future ai systems or weird edge cases people have thought of? Where do people who take the argument seriously actually draw the line, and on what grounds?
4. Practical upshots for a general reader: swatting mosquitos, backyard bug spray, industrial agriculture and pesticides, dietary choices. Is there anything the argument actually recommends, or is it more of a "fund more research" conclusion? I've heard some people in the effective-altruism-adjacent world take insect welfare surprisingly seriously, and I want to represent that accurately without making it sound like settled science.

One big request for my sake: please seperate what's settled among people who actually study this stuff (population estimates, the bare logic of the argument, maybe what we know about insect nervous systems and behavior) from what's genuinely contested (probability of sentience, how to aggregate low-probability moral stakes, whether expected value is even the right frame).
