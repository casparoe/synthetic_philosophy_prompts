My oldest friend chairs the ethics consult service at a big teaching hospital, and for years we've argued about how much authority a person's earlier self should have over their later self. I've basically kept a scorecard of her verdicts from the cases she talks about (simplified a bit, and no identifying details obviously). Case 1: man brought in unconscious after a bike crash, with a decades-old, well-documented refusal of blood products on religious grounds — she said don't transfuse. Case 2: directive refusing life-sustaining treatment in the event of permanent unconsciousness, patient now in a permanent vegetative state — she said withdraw everything. Case 3: woman who wrote, while clearly competent, that she wanted no further cancer treatment if she were ever in severe pain that couldn't be controlled — now her pain is mostly controlled, she's in good spirits and says she wants to keep fighting, and my friend said keep treating. Case 4: man's directive refusing hospitalization once he had advanced dementia and was suffering — now he's demented, mostly unresponsive, clearly distressed whenever anyone touches him, and she said no hospital, keep him comfortable at home.

Next month they've got a case that I think will be interesting: a woman whose directive says no antibiotics once she no longer recognizes her children. That's exactly where she is now — hasn't recognized the kids in months — but she's calm, likes her music, still enjoys her food, and even comes out with the occasional fully lucid sentence. She has pneumonia and the question on the table is whether to treat it. I've been trying to work out what my friend will say before the meeting, since we've got a fancy dinner riding on it and I intend to win. Her record seems to fit more than one rule: something like "the directive binds whenever its stated trigger is literally met" fits all four of her past cases, but so does "directives are really about the feared condition, so they only bind when the patient's current life is actually as bad as anticipated, not just when the wording technically matches," and maybe also "current preferences govern whenever the patient can still express them, with prior wishes only filling the gaps." Do you see other rules that fit her record just as well? What does each rule predict for the pneumonia case, what's your best guess for how she'll actually rule and why?

One more thing — I have a candidate for a case that would split these rules apart, tell me if it works: a patient with moderate dementia who's openly miserable about his decline, whose old directive refuses dialysis at exactly this stage of dementia, but who now says clearly and repeatedly that he wants the dialysis. Does that case seperate the rules cleanly, or do some of them collapse back together on it? And if it doesn't fully do the job, what case would?
