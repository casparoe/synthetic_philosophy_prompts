My dad is a retired insurance adjuster who got deep into epistemology podcasts during lockdown, and now at dinner he hands down rulings on whether people "really knew" stuff, like a little court. His track record so far: a stopped clock that happened to show the right time — no knowledge. A coworker who straight-up guessed when the fire drill would be and got it right, and he believed her — no knowledge. Ten minutes after the lottery draw, before checking his ticket, being completely sure he lost — no knowledge, and he got weirdly stubborn about that one. Seeing the neighbor's dog in the yard — knowledge. The rickety footbridge he crosses every day, last inspected years ago, which he admits he'd trust even if it were actually unsound — knowledge. The old lady at the hatchery who can't explain how she sexes the chicks but is essentially never wrong — knowledge.

So last night he tells us this story: he needed to know if our pharmacy was open till 9, called what he thought was our branch, asked their hours, was told open till 9, and went at 8:30 — it was open. Except he'd actually dialed the branch across town, which happens to keep exactly the same hours, and our branch was open till 9 anyway on its own. And in basically every nearby version of that evening, our branch is open till 9 regardless of which number he dials. He wouldn't give a ruling, he said he needed to sleep on it.

Predict his verdict. What rules actually fit his whole track record — I can see at least two that survive, and one popular one (the one about: if it had been false, would he still have believed it) that his footbridge ruling seems to kill. Say which surviving rule fits his rulings best and why, give me your best guess with your reasons, and tell me what case I should spring on him next to really tell the surviving candidates apart.
