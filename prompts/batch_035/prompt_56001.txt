I'm an econometrician who has wandered into economic history, and I keep encountering the Great Divergence literature — Pomeranz's contention that Britain's escape from the Malthusian trap turned on coal and New World resources rather than on superior institutions, and the older Needham question of why industrialization happened in Europe and not in China, given China's long head start in technology. My instinct is that everything the historians are arguing about is, or ought to be, a fight about identification. Restate the Great Divergence debate in my working vocabulary — potential outcomes, treatment effects, the fundamental problem of causal inference, SUTVA, selection on observables versus unobservables, omitted variable bias, bad controls, overidentification, external validity, and the difference between estimating an average treatment effect and explaining a single realized draw. Translate faithfully: institutions, factor endowments, coal, colonial resources, market integration, and the like should come out as variables, units, and assumptions wherever they can, and you should say explicitly where the translation is doing violence. Then give me the residue report.

I especially want your view on three places where I suspect the translation breaks. First, the contrastive question itself: asking why Britain and not China presupposes that Britain and China are two draws from some common process differing in a specifiable treatment, but a world with a rich Britain may be a world with a poor China — comparative advantage and trade mean the outcomes are not independent draws, and I do not see what SUTVA even means at the level of civilizations. Second, the sample: this is n = 1, or at best one draw per civilization, and the outcome was selected precisely for its rarity; I know the difference between estimating a treatment effect and constructing a post hoc narrative for a single observation, but I want to know whether the historians have any resources for saying more than that. Third, the choice of contrast class: the question could equally have been why Britain and not Holland, why Europe and not the Yangzi delta, why the eighteenth century and not the eleventh, and the different framings apparently yield different answers — is there anything in my vocabulary for the claim that the choice of control is itself the explanandum, or is that a genuine residue?

Close by telling me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary — something causal inference cannot say but history can — or a sign that the historical debate is less well posed than it looks, with the econometric reframe being the cleaner version of what the historians have been groping toward all along.
