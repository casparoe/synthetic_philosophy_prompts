You are a philosopher of economics with a reputation for diagnosing stalled debates: distinguishing verbal from substantive disagreement, locating hidden empirical questions, and saying honestly what would count as progress. Before committing to a verdict, please think carefully through the candidate diagnoses and weigh them against what each side actually says; a neutral survey of positions is of no use to me, and neither is a defense of Keynes.

The debate. My reading group has spent more than a year on Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren," and we have hardened into two camps that no longer move. The essay, written as the Depression was setting in, predicts that within a hundred years the economic problem would be solved, living standards several times higher, and the working week reduced to about fifteen hours, in three-hour shifts. Keynes distinguishes absolute needs, which are independent of our fellows, from relative needs, which are satisfied only by superiority over others, and he concedes that the second sort may be insatiable — a concession that now does a lot of work in our arguments. He treats the love of money as a possession, as opposed to money as a means to living well, as a kind of morbidity, half criminal and half pathological, that he expects to die out once abundance arrives, while admitting that this expectation is a guess: as of 1930, he says, there is no evidence about how people behave once their wants are comfortably supplied. His closing image is that of people who will value the art of living itself above its means.

The facts we all accept: output per worker grew roughly in the range he projected; hours of work fell steeply through the mid-twentieth century and then flattened, in some countries rising slightly, from around the 1980s; his target date, 2030, is nearly upon us and the average workweek is nowhere near fifteen hours.

The two camps. The "wrong economics" camp holds that Keynes's psychological forecast was sound — the disposition to accumulate for its own sake really would fade in genuine abundance — and that what failed was the institutional side: productivity gains were diverted into positional consumption and the claims of the rich, so the satiation point was pushed permanently out of reach. The prophecy failed for distributive, not anthropological, reasons. The "wrong psychology" camp holds that the economics roughly came true and the anthropology failed: the accumulation disposition is not a disorder curable by prosperity; the relative needs Keynes himself flagged are the permanent engine, and the essay's most striking claim, the prediction of a moral transformation, was simply false. A further layer: one member contends the essay should not be read as prophecy at all — Keynes was writing against a mood of pessimism, and the piece is closer to a polemical performance than a forecast — which the rest of us treat as an evasion.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two camps share all the facts and even the same hero; what they really differ over is an assumption neither can argue for — whether human wants quiet down at some level of objective satisfaction, with one camp treating this as true and institutionally adjustable, the other as false and fixed. Because nothing in the observed data would look different under a psychology that transformed but was institutionally thwarted than under one that never does, the disagreement as posed may be unfalsifiable.

What I want from you:

1. A diagnosis. Is this a substantive empirical disagreement in disguise, a dispute about which facts count as data (note that both camps cite the same plateau in hours as decisive — one camp reads the pre-plateau fall as the psychology working, the other reads the plateau as the psychology expiring), a verbal dispute about what it means for an economic problem to be solved, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about human malleability? Support the diagnosis with what each camp actually says.

2. A verdict on the interpretation question: is the "it was a performance" reading a separate disagreement, a way of changing the subject, or the same disagreement one layer down?

3. What would count as progress. Given your diagnosis, name the specific kind of development that should move the debate — a conceptual distinction, a class of evidence, a reframing of the question — or argue that none should be expected, and say why.
