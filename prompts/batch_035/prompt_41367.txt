Two colleagues in my decision-theory reading group, Giles and Nadia, spent an entire session arguing about the absent-minded driver problem, and I would like a proper verdict on their exchange. The setup, as we framed it: a driver on a motorway at night will pass two identical exits. He cannot remember whether he has already passed one — the intersections look alike and he is equally absent-minded at both — so from either intersection he cannot tell which one he is at. Exiting at the first gets him a motel for the night (payoff 1); exiting at the second gets him home quickly (payoff 4); continuing past both leaves him looping forever (payoff 0). At each intersection he chooses between exiting and continuing, and he may randomise.

Giles opened by saying the problem is settled at the planning stage: choose the mixed strategy that maximises expected payoff, which is to continue with probability 3/8 at each intersection, for an expected payoff of 25/16. Since the intersections are indistinguishable, the driver at an intersection learns nothing he did not already know when planning, so there is nothing to update on, and the only rational course is to execute the plan.

Nadia objected that the driver's own strategy partly determines which intersection he is at, so the action stage cannot be a rubber stamp. Consider the pure plan of exiting at every intersection: a driver following it who finds himself at an intersection must be at the first one, so continuing would yield 4 rather than 1. That plan is self-undermining the moment it is adopted, which shows action-stage reasoning has real bite.

Giles conceded that pure plans may be unstable but said the 3/8 plan survives the test. Under it, the probability that a driver at an intersection is at the first one is 8/11, and both exiting and continuing then carry an expected value of 20/11, so the driver at the intersection is precisely indifferent and has no incentive to deviate. He added that even the cynical model on which the driver is really two selves, one at each intersection, each taking the other's behaviour as given while being forced to act identically, lands on continue-with-probability-3/8 as the stable strategy. Everything converges.

Nadia replied that the indifference calculation treats the current choice and the choice at the other intersection as independent: the driver re-optimises here while holding the plan fixed there. But he is the same person, with the same information, at both; if his reasoning now concludes that continuing is acceptable, he should expect the very same conclusion at the other intersection. Treating the two choices as linked, continuing now becomes evidence that he continues past both exits and gets 0, while exiting now becomes evidence that he exits at the first and gets 1 — so linked reasoning at the action stage recommends exiting outright, not indifference. She further noted that even the valuation of a plan is perspectival: valued from inside an intersection, the position the driver actually occupies, the expected value of continuing with probability p works out to (1 + 7p − 8p²)/(1 + p), which is maximised near p = 0.32 rather than at 3/8.

Giles answered that the linked-decision argument proves too much: it recommends exiting always, a policy worth 1 against the 3/8 policy's 25/16, so it must be imposing a constraint the driver does not actually face — deciding at a single intersection — when his real object of choice is a policy carried out at both. And the inside-an-intersection valuation double-counts the path on which the driver reaches both intersections: his decision is counted twice there although the payoff arrives only once. That is the right way to price bets offered at each intersection, he said, but not the right way to choose a plan.

They ran out of time before resolving anything. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of Giles's and Nadia's contributions, say whether it is correct, and about what. In particular, I want to know whether the 3/8 plan really is stable in the sense Giles claims, whether Nadia's linked-decision reasoning is a genuine rival to it or a confusion, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
