I spent thirty-five years as an epidemiologist before retiring, and one habit from the bench I cannot shake is asking what a claim has to deliver before it counts as established. In my old field we had Koch's postulates: explicit criteria for when a microbe could be said to cause a disease. I have lately been reading discussions of artificial intelligence and human prospects, and they keep leaning on something people call the strategy-stealing assumption: roughly, that any strategy humans could use to secure an advantage over sufficiently capable AI systems is a strategy those systems could use just as well, so that if humans are to retain an edge, it must come from strategies that are in some way unavailable to machines. The assumption is doing a lot of quiet argumentative work, yet I have not found anyone state what a careful version of it would have to get right. So, before I read further, I want the specification settled. Write the desiderata: what any adequate precisification of the assumption must deliver. I would expect the list to cover, at minimum, which cases it must classify correctly (mutual-destruction standoffs versus ordinary economic competition; strategies that depend on embodiment, on being recognized and trusted as human, or on building coalitions among humans); which distinctions it must respect (between copying a strategy and having the means to execute it; between there being no human-favoring strategy and the best such strategy being only marginally better; between what holds at parity of capability and what holds under large asymmetries); and which empirical facts about how strategies actually spread and are countered it cannot contradict. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and flag any subset of the requirements you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable -- that possibility is what I most want to see, since a spec that cannot be met is itself a result. Then, and only briefly, score two readings I have encountered against your list: the narrow reading on which "strategy" covers only informational and computational moves, and the broad reading on which it covers anything an agent intentionally does to gain advantage. I am less interested in the scorecard than in whether either reading can meet the non-negotiables.
