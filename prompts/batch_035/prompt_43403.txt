During a family argument about whether a government official can be blamed for carrying out a policy he privately believes is deeply wrong, my brother ended the discussion by saying "an unjust law is no law at all," as though that settled both whether the official was bound to obey and whether "I was following the law" could excuse what he did. I have no background in philosophy or law — I'm an accountant who reads on the side — and I have been trying on my own to figure out what that sentence actually means. The more I sit with it, the less sure I am whether it is a profound claim, a harmless bit of moral rhetoric, or something in between.

Here is what I think I understand, from my own reading: the line comes out of a natural law tradition, and it is precisely the sort of saying that divides two camps — those who hold that whether something is law is one question and whether it is just is a separate one, and those who hold that serious enough injustice can bear on whether a directive counts as law at all. I've also read about the situation facing German courts after World War II: people who had denounced their spouses and neighbors to the Nazi authorities were being prosecuted, and the courts had to say something about the Nazi-era statutes those informers had acted under — whether those statutes were law that had to be reckoned with, or something less than law, and what followed either way for the informers' guilt. As I understand it, the slogan, or something near it, was doing real argumentative work there and not just decorating a verdict. If I've gotten any of this wrong, say so plainly.

What I want is a careful unpacking of the one-liner itself. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "an unjust law is no law at all" could be making. I can think of some candidates, and I suspect there are others I'm missing: that it's a mere moral condemnation dressed up as a statement about law, committing itself to nothing; that being aimed at justice or the common good is part of what makes something a law in the first place, so that an unjust "law" is a counterfeit, like a forged check; that unjust laws create no obligation to obey, though they may still be backed by force and worth complying with for other reasons; that judges and officials are entitled, or required, to refuse to apply such directives as if they were law; that "following the law" is never even a partial excuse when the law in question is unjust; or that habitually calling unjust things law corrodes the practice of law over time.

For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or just confused — and, crucially, whether it would actually have supported what my brother used it for, namely settling the question of the official's obligation and blame. Don't pick the reading my brother probably meant and stop there; I want the full range with verdicts. Then test the strongest readings against the postwar informer cases: which reading, if any, would a court have needed to accept to justify what it did, and does that reading survive the test or show its flaws there?

Finally, address the reverse worry that has been nagging me: on which of these readings does the slogan amount to saying that each person gets to decide for himself which laws are real laws, and therefore which ones bind him — and is that a fatal problem for those readings, or is there a way for them to keep the point without collapsing into private judgment? I would rather have an honest answer that the sentence is mostly rhetoric than a flattering one that rescues it, but I'd like to know exactly which parts, if any, are worth rescuing.
