I've been defending deontological constraints in our department's ethics workshop, and a consequentialist friend produced an argument that concludes not merely that constraints are mistaken but that they are incoherent. I can't locate the flaw, though I refuse to accept the conclusion.

(1) What distinguishes a deontological constraint from a policy of minimizing violations is that it forbids an act (say, killing an innocent) even when committing that act once would prevent more such acts overall.
(2) If a constraint forbids killing the innocent, what grounds it must be a fact about the wrongness of killing the innocent; otherwise the constraint is arbitrary.
(3) A constraint grounded in the wrongness of killing the innocent is justified by the moral significance of killings of the innocent.
(4) In the standard case — a villain will kill five innocents unless I kill one innocent myself — the constraint requires that I allow five killings rather than bring about one.
(5) A prohibition justified by the moral significance of killings of the innocent cannot, without irrationality, require allowing five such killings rather than one.
(6) So the constraint is not justified by the moral significance of killings of the innocent.
(7) By (2), the constraint has no justification at all.
(8) Therefore deontological constraints are arbitrary, and deontology is not a rival moral theory but a confusion.

Each step looks tolerable to me individually. My vague suspicion is that "moral significance" is doing two different jobs somewhere around (3) and (5), but I can't pin it down, and I worry that even if I'm right, rejecting the relevant step will force me into something ugly — for instance, if the constraint isn't answerable to how many killings occur, don't I owe an account of why the five I allow aren't simply outweighed by the one I commit, and can any such account be given without either smuggling agent-neutral arithmetic back in or resting on the bare fact that those deaths were not caused by me? Tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — including the costs of the runner-up options. If you think the argument is valid, say so and tell me which premise is least costly to deny. I'd most like to keep (1), which seems definitional.
