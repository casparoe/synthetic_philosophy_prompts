I've been reading about Peter Suber's game Nomic and I keep thinking it's basically the cleanest laboratory example of a problem real constitutions have: what happens when the rule for changing the rules is itself changeable. Suber's starting setup splits the rules into immutable and mutable ones precisely because if everything is mutable, players can vote to make themselves dictators on turn three, and if everything is immutable the game can never grow. The whole design is built to force the paradox of self-amendment on you.

So here's the real-world episode I want you to run through that lens: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, India, 1973. Quick facts so we're on the same page. The Indian constitution lets parliament amend it through Article 368, by adding, varying or repealing provisions — no stated exceptions. In 1967 the supreme court in Golak Nath held that amendments count as law and so can't touch fundamental rights. Parliament hit back with the 24th Amendment (saying amendments aren't that kind of law) and the 25th (gutting the property right). In Kesavananda, a 13-judge bench decided 7-6 that parliament can amend anything, but cannot damage or destroy the constitution's basic structure — an unamendable core that appears nowhere in the constitutional text itself. The court basically wrote an immutable tier into a document whose own amendment clause said nothing was off limits. Compare Germany, where the eternity clause is actually written down, and you see India's version is judicially invented. Later courts even used it to strike down constitutional amendments, and after the Emergency there was a big fight about whether that was genius or usurpation.
