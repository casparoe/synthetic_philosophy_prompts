The argument runs as follows. (1) On the Bayesian picture, a rational agent holds a coherent probability distribution over some language and updates it by conditionalization when evidence arrives. (2) The classical justification for this whole package is the Dutch book argument: an agent whose prices for bets can be exploited by a combination of bets that guarantees the bookmaker a profit is irrational, and coherence plus conditionalization is exactly what immunity to such exploitation requires. (3) Now take two sentences, A and B, that are logically equivalent, but where the equivalence is hard to see — it holds, but only via a proof longer than the agent could ever run before the heat death of the universe. If the agent prices A at 0.7 and B at 0.5, a bettor who spots the equivalence buys and sells accordingly and locks in a guaranteed profit, since A and B have the same truth value in every possible world. (4) So immunity to Dutch books requires never pricing logically equivalent sentences differently, which is just logical omniscience. (5) No physically realizable agent is logically omniscient: in anything as expressive as first-order logic, recognizing equivalence is undecidable, and any bounded agent will miss some equivalences no matter how well it reasons within its budget. (6) So the Dutch book justification of Bayesian rationality applies only to agents that cannot exist. (7) Therefore either every bounded agent — every agent that has ever actually reasoned, including every human who ever updated on evidence — is irrational, or the central justification of Bayesian rationality justifies nothing about any real reasoner. My friend takes the second horn and concludes we should stop treating conditionalization as the norm of rational updating at all.

The conclusion doesn't merely prove too much about Bayesians; it seems like it would prove too much about any possible successor norm, since whatever replaces conditionalization will also be something bounded agents sometimes violate.
