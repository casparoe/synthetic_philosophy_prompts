I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and I've been leaning on the fairy tale of the emperor's new clothes as my stock illustration of the difference between mutual knowledge and common knowledge: the courtiers all see that the emperor is naked, each knows that the others see it too, and yet the pretense holds — until the child cries out, the observation becomes public, and the coordinated laughter collapses the whole performance in a moment. I've started to worry that the story does its work by sleight of hand, and I'd like a genuine audit of the analogy rather than a defense or an attack.

Specifically: (1) Give the correspondence table — what plays the role of the public announcement, the common prior (if any), the coordinated action, and the equilibrium being selected — and flag what has no counterpart in the story at all. (2) In the story as usually told, everyone in the crowd can see everyone else's face, so even before the child speaks there may already be several layers of higher-order knowledge in place; if so, what exactly does the cry add, and is the story perhaps really about something other than completing the hierarchy of everyone-knows-that-everyone-knows — say, about eliminating the risk of being the lone dissenter? (3) The moral I want my students to draw is that a single public signal suffices to switch a population from one self-enforcing convention to another. Stress-test that moral against two considerations: first, that common knowledge of rationality and payoffs still leaves multiple equilibria whenever interests conflict, so publicity alone may not select among them; second, the game-theoretic example in which two parties exchange automated confirmation messages back and forth — so that they share any finite number of levels of mutual knowledge, however high — yet the outcome of the protocol differs radically from what genuine common knowledge would deliver. Where does the child's cry survive that stress test, and where does it break? (4) Finally, the story quietly assumes the cry is costless and audible to everyone at once. In settings where the first deviator is punished and communication is noisy or purely local, does the conclusion — that a pretense is always one public truth away from collapse — survive at all, and if not, what would a more faithful analogy for the role of common knowledge in collective action look like?
