I'm a PhD student in nineteenth-century American history, currently preparing for my historiography field exam, and my advisor has asked each of us to draft a short statement of methodological commitments to be workshopped by the group. I've written mine and would like a consistency audit before I circulate it, because I have a nagging sense that something in it doesn't hang together, but I can't locate the problem myself. The commitments, roughly as I'll state them: (1) I'm a moral realist about at least the big things — slavery was wrong, full stop, not merely wrong by our lights — and I take moral progress to be a real phenomenon rather than a change in fashion. (2) I hold, nonetheless, that historians should aim to explain and understand past actors in their own terms rather than condemn them by the standards of the present, and that retrospective moral judgment is mostly bad history. (3) I allow one narrow exception: a historical actor is fairly condemned when they violated the standards of their own time — the slaveholder whose own world contained enslaved people and abolitionists telling him plainly that slavery was an abomination has no excuse to plead. (4) I take those "standards of their own time" to be contested and plural rather than identical with what the era's elites accepted; the moral resources for condemning the slaveholder were available within his own world and did not need to be imported from ours. (5) I believe some anachronism in description is unavoidable — our categories for race, identity, even religion are ours, and the aspiration to purge them entirely is incoherent. (6) I despise Whig history, yet I see nothing wrong with a narrative organized around the expansion of moral concern, provided the historian is careful.

What I would like is a proper audit rather than a lecture: go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and for each genuine tension how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be — what I would have to give up or re-motivate, and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please rank the tensions by severity and identify the single worst offender rather than treating the set as uniformly unstable. Two places I already half-suspect trouble, though I don't trust my own diagnosis: whether (2) can survive (1), since my usual reason for keeping moral judgment out of historical writing seems to quietly rely on the thought that our standards are merely later, not better, which is exactly what (1) denies; and whether (6) is as bad as my seminar instincts tell me it is, or whether Whig history and a progress-organized narrative really are different things. I'm also unsure whether (3) and (4) together quietly smuggle present standards back in through the question of whose contemporaries count as the actor's moral environment. Where I'm wrong about a tension being genuine, tell me how it dissolves.
