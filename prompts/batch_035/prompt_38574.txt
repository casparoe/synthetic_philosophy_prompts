I'm a retired physicist (semiconductors, mostly, thirty-odd years of it) and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy, and I finally worked through Wigner's essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics and now it's living in my head rent free. So I sat down and made a list of my actual gut reactions to cases of math "working" on the world, and I want to know if theres a principle behind my reactions, because I suspect there isn't and I'd rather know.

The list, verdicts included:

- Riemann developed his geometry as pure math, decades before Einstein, and then general relativity turns out to be basically written in it. Verdict: genuinely unreasonable, this is the real thing.
- Group theory predicts the omega-minus baryon before anyone's seen it, and then the bubble chamber people find it right where the math said it would be. Verdict: even MORE unreasonable than the Riemann case, this one keeps me up at night.
- Fibonacci ratios in sunflower seed spirals. Verdict: not unreasonable at all — growth processes and selection pressure, the plant is just packing seeds, no mystery.
- You literally cannot write quantum mechanics down without complex numbers. Verdict: deeply unreasonable, same league as Riemann.
- The same diffusion equation shows up for heat in a rod, ink in water, rumors spreading through a town, and option prices on Wall Street. Verdict: NOT unreasonable — if anything this shows math is unreasonably poor, we've got like five equations and we reach for one of them every single time, like a guy with one hammer.
- Least-squares fitting, which I did my whole career. Verdict: not effective at all, we're just drawing the smoothest line we can through points we half trust and calling the residual "noise."

And two things I can't square. First, I keep reading people say the whole mystery is a selection effect — we only notice the math that worked and forget the piles of math that never modeled anything — and honestly that story sounds right to me for the diffusion and least-squares cases... but if it's right in general it should also deflate my verdict on the omega-minus, and it doesn't. Second, the case I keep going back and forth on: prime numbers and RSA encryption. Is that math being unreasonably effective, or did we go looking for exactly the kind of problem math could solve, in which case its more like the least-squares case than the Riemann case?

So: is there one principle that gets all these verdicts, or close? What does it say about a case I haven't listed — say, deep learning, where the math of gradient descent on these huge networks demonstrably works and nobody can really say why? And be honest with me about which of my verdicts has to go, because I can already tell the omega-minus one and my sympathy for the selection-effect story can't both survive.
