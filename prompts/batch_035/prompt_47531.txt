I am a retired physicist -- most of my career was spent working on problems in gravitation and precision tests of general relativity -- and in retirement I have been working through the philosophy of physics, where I find myself, perhaps out of professional habit, on Newton's side of the argument from the bucket. I would like to practice defending that position against the best opposition, since I will be giving a talk on this to a local discussion group and expect a determined relationist in the audience. Please play a committed relationist in the Machian tradition: intelligent and well-informed, never a caricature, willing to concede genuinely minor points (you may, for instance, admit that Mach's own proposed mechanism was not vindicated in its details) but never the core commitment that motion, including accelerated motion, must be analyzable in terms of relations among bodies or, at most, among bodies and dynamically generated structures -- never an independently existing arena. You may draw on the strongest modern versions of your position, including relational reformulations of mechanics in which the gravitational field itself is treated as a codification of relations rather than a substantival medium. Stay in character until I write "stop"; keep each reply to a single paragraph so it reads as a conversation; if you need a physical fact settled, ask for it as your character would rather than stepping outside the discussion.

Here is my opening move. Newton asks us to imagine two globes joined by a cord, alone in the universe: if the cord is in tension, the globes rotate, and there is nothing whatever for them to be rotating with respect to. I expect you to declare the experiment illegitimate -- a universe containing nothing but two globes is, you will say, not a possible world at all, so the thought experiment begs the question. But observe what that declaration costs you in the world we actually inhabit. A laboratory can determine, without looking outside its walls, whether it is rotating: a Foucault pendulum precesses, a gyroscope wanders, the water climbs the wall of the bucket. And the local inertial frame those instruments detect happens, to very high accuracy, to coincide with the frame in which the distant matter of the universe is at rest. That is the coincidence your tradition must explain, and when a rotating shell of matter was finally made to exert a measurable influence on inertial frames -- frame dragging, the Lense-Thirring precession, confirmed by Gravity Probe B -- the effect was minuscule: distant rotating matter neither determines nor appreciably deflects the inertial structure a gyroscope finds. So the correspondence runs exactly in one direction and nearly zero in the other. Unless you can exhibit the mechanism by which the cosmic mass distribution fixes the inertial frames -- and the mechanism general relativity actually supplies is a dynamical field with its own degrees of freedom, which is not obviously reducible to relations among material bodies -- doesn't your reading of the coincidence amount to a conspiracy: the distant matter tracking a structure it does not generate? And if you retreat to saying the field of inertial structure is itself one of the relata, defined by its relations to everything else, have you not quietly conceded Newton's point by admitting that acceleration is a fact about something over and above the material bodies and their changing distances?
