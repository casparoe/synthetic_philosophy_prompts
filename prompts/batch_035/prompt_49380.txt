I edit a newsletter on allocating money to do good, and I want to run an interactive feature: a quiz that tells readers which *variant* of the importance/neglectedness/tractability framework they actually operate. The catch is that my readers all know the vocabulary — they will happily say "neglectedness matters" — so the quiz must sort them by how they resolve concrete allocation dilemmas, on which the variants genuinely diverge, rather than by which words they identify with.

Please write the quiz as follows.

**Questions.** Eight questions. Every question is a concrete funding decision with a fixed budget — what the reader would actually do — and never an invitation to endorse a principle. No question may ask, in effect, "do you care about scale/tractability/neglectedness?" or use those words in the options. Between them, the eight cases must force a verdict on each of the following fault lines, at least once each:

1. *Aggregation form:* whether the three factors combine into something like a product, or whether one factor acts as a pass/fail threshold that the others never override.
2. *What neglectedness is:* total resources already committed, number of independent actors, how long the cause has been fashionable, or room for more funding before diminishing returns bite.
3. *Tractability under uncertainty:* a cause of astronomical scale where nobody can say whether progress is possible at all — does unestimable tractability count against the cause, leave it untouched, or push it up under expected-value reasoning?
4. *Self-undermining dynamics:* what to do once a framework of this kind becomes widely followed and the previously neglected causes are crowded.
5. *Level of analysis:* whether the reader allocates at the level of causes (picking a problem area first) or jumps straight to comparing the best marginal intervention in each area, letting cause-level categories fall out of the comparison.
6. *Breadth vs. depth of benefit:* whether scale counts the number of beneficiaries, the severity of what they suffer, or some trade-off between them — and the reader must choose under a case where these come apart, not endorse a formula.

You may add two further questions of your own design, but state what fault line each additional question is meant to probe.

**Leaves.** The scoring key must map answer patterns onto at least these five positions, each stated with (i) a short name you coin for the quiz, (ii) the answer signature that lands a reader there, (iii) one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, i.e. the implication its holder will least enjoy when the quiz result spells it out: the strict multiplier (all three factors weigh numerically against each other); the lexical threshold view (one factor, typically scale, gates everything else); the arbitrage view (neglectedness is nearly the whole game, and cause selection is the hunting of inefficiencies others will soon spot); the marginal fungibility view (framework talk is idle; only the best available marginal intervention in each area is comparable, so cause categories do no work); and the deflationary view (the whole three-factor apparatus rationalizes decisions made on other grounds and should be abandoned for direct estimation).

**Gaps and limits.** Include any answer combinations your key cannot place, with a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody holds them because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And flag explicitly which pairs of leaves your questions cannot reliably separate, rather than papering over it.

**Commitment.** I don't want a menu. Where you could have designed the split two defensible ways, choose one, and say in the design_choices field which you took and why. In particular, commit to a single answer to this: which one fault line, out of the six above, does the real sorting work, and which are nearly decorative? Defend that ranking rather than presenting the fault lines as co-equal.

**Format.** Deliver everything as a single JSON object, no surrounding commentary, with exactly this schema:

- "title": string
- "questions": array of 8 objects, each with "id" (integer), "case" (string, ~100 words, including the budget and the dilemma), "probes" (the fault line or lines it targets, by number or short name), and "options": array of 2–3 objects, each with "key" (a short slug) and "text" (the decision, phrased as what the reader does)
- "scoring_key": array of objects, each with "position_name", "signature" (the pattern of option keys that lands here), "description" (one sentence), "awkward_commitment" (one sentence)
- "unoccupied": array of objects with "pattern" and "diagnosis"
- "separation_limits": array of strings, one per indistinguishable pair of leaves
- "design_choices": array of objects with "choice", "rejected_alternative", "reason" — including the mandatory entry about which fault line does the sorting work

Make the cases varied in domain (global health, animal welfare, far-future risks, local charity, policy research) and invent the specifics rather than leaning on real organizations; where a case needs numbers, use round ones. The quiz is for readers who already know the framework's name, so do not explain it anywhere — the cases must carry everything on their own.
