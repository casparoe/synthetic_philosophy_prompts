The exam question was:

Explain the multiple realizability argument against type-identity theory. Does the argument show that functionalism is true, or only that type-identity theory is false? In the course of your answer, discuss at least one objection to functionalism that draws either on multiple realizability itself or on the charge that functionalism is too liberal or too chauvinistic.

The student wrote:

The type-identity theory, associated with Smart and Place, holds that mental state types are identical to types of brain states: pain just is the firing of C-fibers, or whatever human neural state turns out to be the relevant one. Putnam's multiple realizability argument against it starts from the observation that pain is plausibly found in creatures whose physiology differs enormously from ours — octopuses, hypothetical silicon-based aliens, and perhaps future machines. If pain were identical to a specific neural state type, then either these creatures do not feel pain or they must realize that very neural type, and neither option is acceptable. The identity theory thus looked human-chauvinistic: it built our neurophysiology into the essence of mentality. Functionalism escapes this by identifying a mental state by its causal role — its typical sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and relations to other mental states — so that one role can be implemented in many different substrates.

But the argument establishes more than functionalism. Once we see that a given headache could have been realized in very different physical states, we have to conclude that not even token identities hold: the claim that this particular headache of mine is identical with this particular neural event is also refuted, since in some possible world the same headache occurs non-neurally. So Putnam's argument rules out physicalism about the mental altogether, not merely the type-identity version of it.

There is also a liberal side to the worry. Searle's China-brain thought experiment asks us to imagine the population of China organized by radio so as to implement the functional organization of a mind, connected to a body. Functionalism seems committed to that collective having a mind, which many find incredible; this is the "absent qualia" objection. Kim adds a different problem: if mental states are multiply realizable, they cannot be strictly reduced to physical states, but then their physical realizers seem to exclude them from genuine causal efficacy, leaving them epiphenomenal. Functionalism can answer the chauvinism charge, but between absent qualia and causal exclusion it faces pressure from both directions.
