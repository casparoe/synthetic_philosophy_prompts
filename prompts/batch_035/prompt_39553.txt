I've become convinced of a view about assertion — the act of telling someone something, as opposed to guessing or joking or wondering out loud. My view is that there's a rule specific to assertion itself: you may assert that P only if you actually know that P. It's not just that knowledge is a nice bonus or a pragmatic afterthought; it's the constitutive norm, in the way that I've heard it said the rules of chess don't merely reward but define legal moves. So when someone asserts what they merely believe on good evidence — a confident weather forecast, a doctor passing on a test result she's 99% sure of — I want to say they've violated the rule of the practice, even if nothing bad happens and nobody is misled. What started me on this was noticing that "How do you know?" seems like a perfectly fitting challenge to almost any assertion, while "How do you believe?" just sounds strange — as if the practice itself already anticipates being asked for knowledge specifically.

But I'm doing this on my own, from reading Wikipedia and arguing with my brother-in-law, so I want to stress-test the position rather than defend it. Tell me what would count against it. I know the standard moves vaguely — lottery cases where you can't know you'll lose but seem entitled to say you'll lose; "selfless" assertions where someone asserts what they don't know for a patient's benefit and seems to do nothing wrong; cases of proper practical stakes. But I want more than a list: which of the possible defeaters should genuinely worry me, and which would a committed defender of the knowledge rule concede as counting against them versus explain away? For instance, does it hurt the view that we happily reprimand children for asserting what they merely truly believe ("you don't know that, you just think it") — or does that actually support it? What if it turned out that in ordinary speech we grade assertions by confidence rather than by knowledge — is that even a discovery a view like mine could conflict with, or does it change the subject from what the rule of the practice is to what people happen to enforce?

Also, I've noticed a temptation in myself that I want you to police: whenever a counterexample comes up, I retreat to saying the rule only governs some special, idealized kind of assertion, or that "know" can be read loosely so the counterexample wasn't really an assertion of a non-known proposition. I'd like an honest assessment of whether the view, once I've protected it this way, risks anything at all — and whether a norm governing a practice could legitimately be insulated from counterexamples in that manner, or whether a rule that nothing observable could break isn't a rule anymore. Rank the defeaters from what should actually make me give the view up to what's merely awkward, and tell me which rival rule (truth? justified belief? reasonable confidence?) would inherit the field if the knowledge rule fell, and what its own weak points would be.
