I'm a sixth-form student with a small home lab: three old machines running a cluster with a distributed key-value store, where I have to think about quorums, leader election, and what happens when a node gets partitioned off and comes back with stale data. In that world I first met the phrase "split-brain": the failure mode where a network partition leaves two nodes each convinced it is the primary, and they start accepting conflicting writes until something with a fencing token knocks one of them down. Some weeks later, reading about the history of neuroscience for an extended project, I discovered that surgeons and philosophers use exactly the same word for something in people — patients whose corpus callosum was severed, leaving the two cerebral hemispheres unable to exchange messages. I've read enough to know roughly what the experiments showed, but not enough to navigate the philosophy, and I keep involuntarily translating everything into the vocabulary I actually use.

So that is what I'd like you to do, deliberately and carefully. Take the main philosophical positions on the split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness — the interpretation that each hemisphere supports its own stream of experience, so that one human being contains two minds; the view that there is a single subject whose unity becomes partial or fading rather than simply broken; the functionalist thought that unity of mind is a matter of informational integration rather than a single anatomical location; the idea of a left-hemisphere "interpreter" that confabulates a coherent story about behaviour it did not initiate; and the deflationary suggestion that asking how many minds there are is like asking how many things are in a room, a question with no fact of the matter until we fix a way of counting. Restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in my terms: nodes, processes, message passing, network partitions, quorums, consensus, fencing, cache coherence, distributed locks, split-brain failure modes. Then give me the residue report I gather this kind of translation is supposed to end with: which of the philosophers' claims survive the translation cleanly, which only survive after being distorted or stretched to fit a systems metaphor, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. For the residue, I want your genuine assessment of the direction of the defect — whether it shows that systems vocabulary is missing something (and what), or whether it shows that parts of the philosophical debate were less clear, or less substantive, than they looked.

One request about how you present it: please keep visibly separate the things that are settled among researchers who study these patients, the things that are genuinely contested interpretations of the same data, and anything that is your own synthetic judgement — I don't want to come away with a contested view mistaken for the consensus, and I suspect the temptation is strong here because the results are so vivid. And please write in British English throughout.
