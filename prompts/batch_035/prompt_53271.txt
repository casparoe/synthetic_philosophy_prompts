I'm a journalist who has spent most of her career covering science and risk — pandemics, climate assessments, the occasional bank failure — and I'm now at work on a long feature about a habit of mind I keep bumping into. Institutions want one number: a probability, a likelihood grade, a headline figure for the front page. But some of the scientists I interview refuse to give one, and not, I've come to think, out of evasiveness. During the pandemic, a modeller told me that her uncertainty about the reproduction number was too lumpy to be averaged into a single figure — she would give me an interval and a rough shape, but she would not pick a point inside it. My editors read that as hedging. I am no longer sure it is.

Since then I have discovered that philosophers and decision theorists have a name for what she was doing: representing credences imprecisely, as a set of probability functions rather than a single one. So I would like a probability estimate from you, properly decomposed, on the central philosophical question: how confident are you that an ideally rational agent can sometimes correctly have imprecise credences — that this is a genuine feature of rational belief, not merely unrecognised ordinary ignorance plus stubbornness?

Please don't tell me it depends on one's framework. I want a number and the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term. Terms you might include: whether the probability axioms, including additivity, are non-negotiable norms on rational credence; whether completeness — the requirement that a rational agent have some determinate credence in every proposition — can fail without irrationality; whether the Ellsberg urn and kindred cases are better modelled by a set of probabilities than by a single sharp probability whose value one merely happens not to know; and whether the distinction between my credence is imprecise and I don't know my own credence survives scrutiny or collapses under reflection. Then combine the terms into your final credence, tell me which term that number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or evidence would move it most.

For calibration: my statistician friend puts the imprecise view at about 0.15 — he thinks it is all prior uncertainty people have failed to articulate — while the philosopher whose seminar I sat in on puts it at 0.85. Where, in your decomposition, does their disagreement most likely live? What is it plausible that each of them is really disagreeing about, beneath the headline number?

And a final question for the piece itself, to be answered after the numbers: if the imprecise view is roughly right, does it change how a journalist should report expert disagreement? When three well-informed modellers give me 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8, I currently report the spread and let readers draw what they will. Should I also refuse to combine their views into a single consensus figure, and is treating their disagreement as if it were one wide credence a category error that the imprecise view precisely diagnoses? I want to get this right for readers who will never read the philosophy.

Please write your answer in British English throughout.
