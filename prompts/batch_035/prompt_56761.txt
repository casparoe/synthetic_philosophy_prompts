I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book whose heroine, Maya, runs a food bank in a fading rust-belt town, and a big subplot is the deals she keeps getting offered by people whose values she can't stand — the town's casino company, the family that owns half the farmland and treats its workers terribly, and so on. I don't want Maya to come off as either a pushover or a prig, and I've realized I can't actually write her choices until I know what the real principle is, so I've been road-testing my own gut on real cases and writing down the verdicts. Here they are. A wildlife charity co-running a peatland restoration with a grouse-shooting estate that still burns heather — the charity never says a word about the shooting, they just share the work: fine, even good, that's just gains from trade and I'd think more of them for it. Same charity, but now the estate's owner offers to be headline sponsor of their fundraising gala, logo on everything: not fine, and I feel quite firm about that, which is odd because its the same money from the same man. A vegetarian school cook buying from the town butcher because he's the only supplier who'll deliver before 7am, even though he gets to put "proud supplier to the school" on his van: fine, mostly. A city councillor who campaigned against gambling voting for a budget that includes lottery money for the parks, after her amendment to strip it out fails: I think fine, though I wobble. A climate campaigner signing a joint statement with an oil-workers' union, including some "we stand with workers in the industry" language she'd never say off duty: fine. And a friend of mine who writes fiercely anti-consumerist essays but pays her rent ghostwriting ad copy: not fine, though I want to like her, and Maya happens to have a cousin in exactly that spot.

So what principle, if any, captures all that? I can half see one — something about not lending your endorsement, as opposed to just trading with people — but it seems to fall appart between the gala (not fine) and the butcher's van (fine), since in both the other party gets to use a bit of warm glow from association with the good guys for their own image. And the case I keep going back and forth on, which Maya faces in chapter nine, so I genuinely need an answer: the casino offers the food bank a large no-strings donation through a donor-advised fund, no banner, no logo, nothing public at all — the money is every bit as usefull as the sponsored kind, it just doesn't cost them any reputation. If you can give me a principle, or two candidates, tell me which of my gut verdicts each one has to overturn, and what it predicts for the anonymous case and for one or two situations I haven't thought of — say, Maya accepting a seat on the casino's "community foundation" board, where she'd have real say over how the grants get spent. It's fine to tell me some of my verdicts are just mood; I'd much rather find out now than in the reviews.
