In four weeks I defend my master's thesis before a three-member committee: a historian of technology who is a committed social constructionist, an STS scholar who works on standards and infrastructure and leans the same way, and an external examiner, a control-engineering professor who regards the entire "politics of artifacts" literature as unfalsifiable rhetoric. I need a prep sheet for the question period.

The thesis I am defending is a qualified version of the claim that artifacts have politics. My criterion, which I call counterfactual parity, runs as follows: an artifact is political in a strong, non-metaphorical sense when, holding the technical problem fixed, a society differently arranged with respect to power and interest would plausibly have settled on a materially different design, and the difference tracks whom the artifact makes things easy for. Where the constraint set leaves only one reasonable design, I concede the artifact itself is not political, only its use is; where the constraints underdetermine the design, the residue of choice is where the politics lives. On this view the famous example — the much-disputed story that a mid-century planner had parkway overpasses on Long Island built with low clearances so that buses, and the people who depended on them, could not reach the public beaches — matters less as established fact than as a test case. If the archival details of that story dissolved, I want to say my framework survives, because the criterion is counterfactual rather than archival; but I am not confident I am entitled to that concession, since if every vivid example dissolves on inspection, underdetermination itself starts to look like something the literature manufactured for its own convenience.

Please draft the objections in roughly the order the committee will raise them, phrased the way each member would phrase them. I anticipate at least these, and want you to develop whatever else follows from the committee's commitments: the historian's archival challenge to the overpass story and the demand to know what my criterion says about cases where the political interpretation was applied retrospectively by later scholars; the infrastructure scholar's likely claim that counterfactual parity just is the social-constructionist position under a new name, so that I am defending something my own committee has held all along, and that whatever I add on top is either trivial or wrong; and the engineer's demand that I state one prediction the framework makes that ordinary engineering tradeoff analysis does not — a case where we could tell, prospectively, whether my "political residue" is real or just ignorance of constraints we would later discover were binding after all.

For each objection, give me what to concede up front, the two-minute reply, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. I especially want your judgment on the objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether a test whose verdicts are verdicts about counterfactual history simply relocates the politics from the artifact into whoever gets to run the counterfactual — the historian, in practice — so that my thesis, which was meant to give artifacts a politics of their own, ends up handing it back to the very interpretive community whose work I was trying to constrain. Tell me whether I should pre-empt that one or whether pre-empting it concedes more than silence would.
