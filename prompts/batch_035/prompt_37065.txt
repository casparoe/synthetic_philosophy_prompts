I'm taking a course on the ethics of war and we just did the week on the moral equality of combatants (Walzer vs. McMahan and all that), and it sent me down a rabbit hole about the actual legal architecture, because something feels off but I can't quite name it. Here's the practice as I understand it: under the Geneva Conventions, a captured soldier recieves POW protections no matter which side they fought on and no matter how unjust their side's cause — a Wehrmacht soldier captured in 1943 is entitled to the same treatment as an American one, at least on paper. And a rank-and-file soldier who fights faultlessly in a war of aggression commits no crime under international law; at Nuremberg it was the leadership that was prosecuted for planning aggressive war, not the soldiers who carried it out. But at the same time "I was just following orders" is explicitly not a defense for war crimes — a soldier is supposed to know that some orders (shoot the prisoners, target the village) must be refused — and states have historically court-martialed, even executed, their own soldiers for desertion or refusing to fight in wars those soldiers thought unjust, with those refuseniks sometimes getting pardoned decades later. So the same system seems to be saying: soldiers are NOT responsible for whether the war is just, but they ARE responsible for how they fight it. Oh and there's also the weird bit where POW status is conditioned on fighting "lawfully" in the technical sense — uniform, open carry of arms — so the system does condition protections on conduct, just never on cause.

My question is basically: what theory of moral responsibility does this whole arrangement actually presuppose? Does giving POW status to soldiers of an unjust aggressor presuppose the moral equality of combatants in the philosophers' strong sense — the view that a soldier fighting for an unjust cause does no wrong at all by fighting, provided they follow the rules — or is it just reciprocity and prudence, you-treat-ours-well-and-we'll-treat-yours-well, which doesn't need any moral claim about the soldiers at all, just mutual interest? And is the "following orders" doctrine for war crimes actually consistent with the pass soldiers get on aggression? Is the thought that a conscript can reasonably be expected to know shooting prisoners is wrong but can't reasonably be expected to know whether their war is just — an epistemic line — or is it something about duress, or about the difference between what a state may demand and what it may excuse? I'm honestly not sure whether the pieces fit or whether they're just a historical accretion from different eras that nobody ever tried to make coherent.

So what I'd like is the implicit theory reconstructed: what does this practice strictly need in order to make sense, which rival theories would it also fit (I'm guessing pure reciprocity, or maybe institutional skepticism about letting anyone judge which side is just, would also license it), and where is it genuinely underdetermined — i.e., which parts of the philosophy could change without the law having to change? And then flip it: if the revisionists are right that unjust combatants do something wrong by fighting at all, what concretely would have to change in the practice — do you get trials for mere participation, or reparations from conscripts of the losing side, or does the revisionist view collapse back into something like the current law once you account for excuse and duress? My professor keeps saying the law "presupposes" Walzer but I have a suspicion it presupposes much less than that, or possibly several incompatible things at once, and I want someone to actually work it through.
