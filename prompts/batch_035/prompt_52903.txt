I'm a retired epidemiologist — thirty-some years at a state public health department — and since retiring I've gotten heavy into philosophy, mostly asethetics lately. My book club has a member who studied Russian literature, and she ends every disagreement with Tolstoy's line that art is infection. Last month it was used to wave off a whole discussion of an abstract painting one of us had seen at a museum: if it doesn't infect you with a feeling the artist actually lived through, she said, it's not art at all, it's decoration or a puzzle. And that pretty much ended the conversation, because none of us could say what the claim even amounts to. The funny thing is I spent my whole career on transmission — who catches what, from whom, at what rate — so you'd think I'd love the metaphor, but that's exactly why I don't trust it here. When I hear 'infection' I immediately want to ask about host range: infect whom, everybody, or just people already trained to be susceptible? And I suspect my friend is quietly sliding between a causal claim (the work transmits a feeling), a sincerity claim (the artist genuinely felt it), a universality claim (it would infect anyone, peasant included), and an evaluative claim (the more infection, the better the art) — and she probably needs all four at once to get her verdict, though I can't yet prove that.

So here's what I want: lay out as a list I can bring to the next meeting the distinct precise readings packed into 'art is infection' — from the most trivial on up to the genuinely radical ones. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, whether it's actually something Tolstoy held, and — this is the part that matters — whether it supports what my friend used it for, i.e. ruling the painting out of art altogether rather than just out of her taste. My own hunch is that the causal reading is nearly empty as a definition (almost anything that evokes anything would count) and that only the evaluative reading gets her the conclusion, but that reading then owes us an account of why degree of infection tracks artistic value — which seems to make the schlockiest tear-jerker that ever infected millions with a cheap feeling into the supreme art. If Tolstoy has resources against that, fold them in — I've read he had conditions on the infection, something about sincerety and individuality and clarity, but I only half remember it. And throughout, please flag which of these readings only sound scientific without being scientific; I've earned the right to be annoyed by borrowed lab coats.
