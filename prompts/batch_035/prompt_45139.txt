I'm a third-year PhD student in machine learning, working on training-time oversight of language models, and I've gotten myself into a muddle about when process-based supervision is actually better than outcome-based supervision. My group uses both without any articulated policy, and my attempts to state one keep failing on my own case judgments, so I'd like help extracting the principle, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, reported as I actually hold them. On math problems with a reliable automated verifier, I'd supervise on final answers only and never look at the reasoning: fine. On a coding agent evaluated by a shallow test suite, where I know the model will write code that games the tests, outcome supervision alone: not fine — I want to inspect the code itself. In a domain with no verifier at all, like open-ended drafting or advice, judging only the finished product by a reviewer's taste: fine, and I'd still call that outcome supervision. For a long-horizon agent taking actions in the real world, evaluating it by end-state results alone: not fine — it needs per-action human approval, or at least approval at every point where an action is irreversible. But now two cases that worry me. If I grade a model's chain-of-thought for looking like careful reasoning, when the literature gives us reason to think the chain-of-thought doesn't always reflect the actual computation, I'm uneasy — it seems I'd be training the model to perform deliberation rather than to deliberate. And there's a pair I can't reconcile: for a coding model I'd rather have outcome supervision via an adversarially hardened test suite than process supervision by a mediocre human judge of the code; yet for a research assistant I'd rather have a strong judge reviewing its weekly plans than outcome supervision by whatever the end-of-quarter results turn out to be. My first pass at a principle was: use outcome supervision whenever the outcome signal is cheap, reliable, and hard to fake; fall back to process supervision otherwise. That handles the first four cases and even the coding one, but it gets the research-assistant case wrong, says nothing about the performative-reasoning worry, and it's silent on the case I keep flip-flopping on: grading a math model's reasoning step by step, where the process signal is denser, catches errors earlier, and empirically helps, but where the same unfaithfulness worry as above applies.

So my request: tell me whether there is a single principle — perhaps something combining how gameable the signal is with how irreversible the actions being graded are, or perhaps something about Goodharting operating on process signals as well as outcome signals — that captures all of these verdicts, or close to it. State the principle or the small set of candidates precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, since I suspect at least one has to go. Then tell me what the resulting principle predicts on the step-by-step math supervision case that I can't decide, and on one further case I haven't considered but should have: a model deployed in a fully verifiable domain whose actions are cheap to evaluate, but where the evaluation itself was chosen by the model's operator to be flattering.
