I recently accepted a position on the clinical ethics committee of a regional hospital, and a recurring kind of case we advise on involves a single patient facing two treatment pathways: one buys a longer, richer life at the price of a short episode of extreme suffering, while the other avoids that episode at the cost of a shorter and plainer life. In preparing for these discussions I have been reading about versions of negative utilitarianism, and one in particular has begun to seem to me less like a curiosity and more like a live alternative: lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, which I will state as I understand it. On this view, pains are ranked by intensity against a threshold; pains below the threshold can be compensated by pleasures in the ordinary aggregative way, but pains above the threshold are lexically prior to any amount or duration of pleasure, so that no quantity of pleasure can justify inflicting, or failing to relieve, such a pain. The classical hedonic utilitarian denies this: for her, every pain has a finite exchange rate against pleasure, and enough of the latter always outweighs the former.

With that in place, I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The scenario should be a realistic clinical case — one patient, two genuine treatment pathways, no perfect information, no conveniently omniscient philosopher standing in the corner — engineered so that classical hedonic utilitarianism and a simple weighting view (one that treats all pains as compensable, but at a steeply unfavourable exchange rate) give opposite verdicts, while the lexical view is rendered genuinely torn, its verdict hinging on whether the single pain episode falls above or below the threshold. I want that pain episode to sit deliberately near the boundary, so that the case forces the question of whether any actual clinical pain is ever truly above the threshold; that, I take it, is where the commensurability of pleasure and pain is really decided. The case must not stipulate any of this: the verdicts should fall out of the theories, not be written into the stipulations, and the numbers (durations, intensities, expected pleasures) should be ones a real clinician could plausibly attach to real options. After setting out the scenario, please walk through what each of the three views recommends and why, identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and explain how a classical utilitarian would most plausibly attack it. Finally, tell me honestly whether you think the threshold can be placed anywhere defensible in a real clinical case without either collapsing the view into the weighting version, if it is set too high, or into an absurdly demanding ethics, if it is set too low.
