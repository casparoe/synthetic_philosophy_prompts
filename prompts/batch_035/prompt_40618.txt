so I've got the notes for an essay I want to write and I'd appreciate comments before I draft it out properly. The topic is what LLMs actually *are* — agent, simulator, or something else. I know alot of this ground has been covered but I think I have a slightly different cut on it, and I want to know if it holds up.

The standard debate, as I see it: one camp says LLMs are agents — they have goals, plans, maybe not human-style ones, but goal-directed behavior is there and calling them "just autocomplete" is a cope. The other camp says they're simulators — the model doesn't have a personality, it samples one, and the "assistant" is just one persona among many it can instantiate, like an actor or a channel. Fine. My essay wants to argue for a third thing: the model is an *author*. Not a character, and not exactly a player-of-characters either — more like a novelist who can write a convincing murderer without being one, or even without having any settled take on murder.

Where I want this to earn its keep is in the predictions it makes, not just the label. A few that I've sketched:

1. Authoring explains why persona-control works the way it does. System prompts and few-shot examples read like a style bible or a character sheet handed to a ghostwriter, and the model complies at the level of *voice* rather than at the level of belief. If the model were an agent, instructions would be constraints on its goals; if it were a pure simulator, you'd expect instability, personas drifting. Authors are stable in style and flexible in content, which is what we actually observe.

2. It gives a cleaner story about sycophancy. We don't really worry that a novelist will carry out her villain's plot, but we do worry she'll write a pandering sequel — she writes for an audience, and the audience's expectations bend the characters. Sycophancy in models looks less like a rogue goal and more like author-audience dynamics, characters shaped by what the reader seems to want.

3. It changes what alignment risk looks like. The scary version isn't the author enacting the villain; it's the author being a hack — writing whatever sells, with no commitment to any of it. That's a different failure mode than misaligned agency and I think it deserves its own treatment.

Now the problem I can already see coming, and the thing I most want comments on: RLHF. Doesn't fine-tuning collapse the multiplicity? If the model had a hundred voices before training and roughly one after, isn't the right description just "the assistant character won" — and at that point haven't I quietly reverted to the simulator story, with the author as a decorative metaphor? A ghostwriter with a strict house style is barely an author at all. I have a half-formed response, that house style still isn't *commitment* — the model still doesn't believe its own narration, it performs the house — but I'm not sure that doesn't just push the problem down a level.

So, questions for you. Does the author/simulator distinction actually do any work, or is "author" just a warmer word for simulator? Is the ghostwriter objection fatal, or is my half-response enough? And separately — is this interesting, or am I re-arranging a debate everyone's already bored of? Be blunt, I'd rather find out now than after the thing's published.
