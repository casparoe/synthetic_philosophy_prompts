In Gödel, Escher, Bach, Hofstadter distinguishes three layers in any message: the frame message (the signal, carried by an object's structure, that there is something here to decode at all), the outer message (the decoding instructions, which he argues must themselves be understood rather than explicitly stated), and the inner message (the content). His example is a phonograph record of Bach sent into space on a rocket, and his moral is that meaning is not simply inside an artifact: whether the record counts as music depends on where a suitable decoder stands relative to those layers. I have been testing my intuitions about the location of meaning against a series of concrete cases, and I would like to know whether my verdicts add up to a coherent principle, or whether some of them have to go.

My verdicts, as follows:

1. A phonograph record of a Bach performance launched into deep space on a probe, never found by anyone: the music is on the record. There is a fact of the matter about what it means, whether or not it is ever decoded.
2. A physically identical record whose grooves were produced by a runaway lathe with no human supervision, molecule for molecule indistinguishable from the first: the sounds are on it, but the music is not. It is a pattern, not a message.
3. An undeciphered ancient script, such as Linear A: certainly a message with a determinate content, even if we never come to know it.
4. A file encrypted with a one-time pad whose only key has been destroyed: the meaning is still in the ciphertext, merely unreachable.
5. Letter sequences found in Moby Dick which, read at suitably chosen skip lengths, appear to predict later assassinations: decodable, reproducible, even statistically striking — and not a message at all.
6. Constellations: the Big Dipper is not about anything, even though entire cultures have read it; and I would add that this verdict does not depend on the reading being foolish. Even an eloquent and moving reading of the night sky attributes a meaning that was never there.
7. DNA: a genuine message with genuine content, even though no mind authored it — on Hofstadter's own suggestion, its inner message is, roughly, an instruction to copy itself.
8. A monkey at a typewriter that by pure chance produces a grammatical and moving English paragraph about grief. The outer message decodes without trouble, yet I am inclined to deny that the paragraph means anything: it is ink arranged the way meaningful ink would be arranged. I hold this verdict weakly, and it keeps wavering.

My first attempt at a principle was that the dividing line is intent: the Bach record, the ancient script, and the encrypted file were produced by minds that meant something by them, whereas the lathe record, the skip patterns, and the stars were not. That covers most of the list, but verdict 7 wrecks it — I am not prepared to say DNA fails to be a message, and no mind wrote it. Verdict 5 also sits oddly on its side of the line: Melville had intentions, yet the skip patterns inherit none of them, whereas the encrypted file somehow does retain the author's intention even though the author is dead, the key is gone, and no reader will ever recover the content. And I hold verdicts 1 and 2 together uneasily: two objects identical in every physical respect, one a message and one not, which suggests that meaning was never in the artifact — yet verdicts 3, 4, and 7 appear to put it right back in.

What I would like, then, is this. State precisely the principle, or the small set of competing candidates, that best captures my verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Apply the resulting principle to three cases I have not listed: (a) an interstellar signal that is manifestly artificial and richly structured, so that its frame message is unmistakable, but whose outer message we will never crack; (b) a piece of music generated by a compositional algorithm that was itself produced by a random search process, with no human ever hearing or approving the output before it is pressed to vinyl; and (c) a curated recording of wind chimes, in which the wind produced the sounds but a human decided which takes to keep and in what order to sequence them. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would have to overturn, and whether the monkey's paragraph comes out as meaningful or not.
