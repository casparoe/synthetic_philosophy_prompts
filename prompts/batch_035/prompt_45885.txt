Sen's 1970 result is often compressed into the slogan that the Pareto principle and liberal values are inconsistent.

(a) State the theorem precisely. Define the framework of a social decision function, the minimal liberalism condition, the Pareto condition, and the consistency requirement on social preference at which the impossibility is aimed. One student's answer, reproduced in full:

---

Sen's theorem states that no social decision function can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain (U), the Pareto condition (P), and minimal liberalism (L).

A social decision function assigns to every profile of individual orderings a social preference relation, and the relation must be a complete and transitive ordering of the alternatives; otherwise it could not generate a social choice from every finite set, since a cyclic ranking would leave some sets with no best element. Since no complete ordering can be cyclic, producing a cycle is enough for the impossibility, and the result is in this respect Arrow's theorem in libertarian costume: the same clash between U, P, and a decentralization condition, with decisiveness over a pair standing in for IIA.

Minimal liberalism is the liberal condition: every individual in society is decisive over at least one pair of distinct social states, meaning that whenever she strictly prefers one to the other, society strictly prefers it too. The "minimal" refers to how small the protected spheres are, not to how few people have them; everyone gets one.

The Pareto condition says that if every individual strictly prefers x to y, society strictly prefers x to y. Only strict, unanimous preference is needed — the weak version suffices.

For the proof, take Sen's two readers and one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. The states are NR (nobody reads it), P (Prude reads it), and L (Lewd reads it). Prude's ranking is NR > P > L: the book is indecent and should be destroyed unread, but if someone must read it, better Prude than Lewd, since it is worse to enjoy filth than to endure it. Lewd's ranking is L > P > NR: he would rather read it himself, though he would not object to Prude's being made to read it either. Each person's protected pair is {NR, own reading}: Prude is decisive over {NR, P}, Lewd over {NR, L}.

Suppose the function satisfies U, P, and L. By Prude's right, NR > P. By Lewd's right, L > NR. And both would rather Prude read it than Lewd — Prude to spare Lewd the pleasure, Lewd because the spectacle of Prude forced through it would be even better than reading it himself — so by Pareto, P > L. The social relation is cyclic (L > NR > P > L), contradicting transitivity, so no such function exists. Note that the construction needs at least three states, since two states cannot form a cycle.

The slogan overstates things. The conflict arises only because each agent has preferences over the other's sphere: if Lewd had simply ranked L > NR > P, no cycle would arise. So what the theorem really shows is a conflict between rights and Pareto only for profiles involving meddlesome preferences. Sen himself later showed, in a 1974 follow-up, that if the domain is restricted to profiles in which each person's ranking of her protected pair is unaffected by what happens in others' spheres, both P and L can be satisfied; this resolution keeps both conditions exactly as originally stated, at no cost to the liberal. Meddlesome preferences deserve no weight anyway, since a liberal society should not let one person's taste about another's private conduct determine that conduct. A second escape is Nozick's: rights are side constraints on admissible outcomes rather than inputs to a social ranking, and Sen's formalization of rights as decisiveness within a preference aggregation bakes in a contested conception of what rights are. If rights work as side constraints, there is no single social preference relation for the Pareto principle to operate on, and the theorem does not apply.

Overall the slogan should be read as: for the liberal, something has to give — at least on Sen's way of formalizing rights.
