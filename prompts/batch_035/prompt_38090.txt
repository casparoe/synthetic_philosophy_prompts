I teach a graduate seminar on the naturalization of intentionality, and every year the same thing happens: students arrive equipped with labels — teleosemantics, informational semantics, causal theories of content — and the labels turn out to predict nothing about what they will say when an actual case is put to them. So what I want is not a syllabus taxonomy but a sorting instrument: a decision tree that locates a student at a named position by her answers to concrete cases, built from the logical space first, with the positions placed into it afterwards. The tree should have no more than eight leaves and no more than seven internal nodes, and every node must be a question she could answer without any idea which theory her answer is feeding; nothing of the form "do you accept teleosemantics," since the whole point is to sort by what she says about cases, not by what she calls herself.

The cases I want the nodes to turn on are these. A frog's snap-response, plausibly selected for catching flies, fires at a BB pellet rolled past it; in a variant, a laboratory frog has snapped at pellets so often that they now trigger the response as readily as flies do, and I want a node that asks whether the state in that animal means fly, or pellet, or fly-or-pellet, or small dark moving thing. A chick's crouching response, evolved for hawk silhouettes, is triggered by a passing airliner. A bird's alarm call was selected for warning kin, but in a population where kin are absent the call now functions chiefly to attract mates, so that the two consumers of the signal want different things from it, and the tree should ask which consumer, if either, fixes what the call is about. A pigeon trained to peck a key for grain later pecks under a red light that never delivers grain. And a lightning strike assembles, molecule for molecule, a duplicate frog with no evolutionary history behind any of its mechanisms; I want the tree to ask whether its internal states mean what the original's mean, and whether the answer changes if the duplicate is instead the sole member of a whole species with no selection behind it. Beyond these, the natural questions are whether there is any determinate fact as to what an animal takes an object to be when it errs; whether, given such a fact, it is fixed by the state's present causal and informational relations or by why the mechanism exists at all; and whether, inside the historical answers, the relevant history is selection in general or something narrower like the uses consumers have made of the representation. You may reorganize the nodes if the space demands it, but the leaves must include etiological teleosemantics of the Millikan or Papineau sort, Dretske's learning-based historical account, Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory, a pure indicator or covariance semantics, a deflationary position on which there is no inner fact for a naturalizer to fix, and an indeterminacy position on which many such states simply lack content. Where you find a leaf that nobody in the literature actually occupies, say so and tell me whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate — my own suspicion is that it will not cleanly divide the learning-based historical views from the non-historical causal ones, and I would much rather know that than have it papered over.

One constraint on form: I want this as continuous prose only, the way one writes a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Present the tree by walking the reader through it in flowing paragraphs, node by node in the order she would meet them, saying where each answer sends her, and put the notes on unsplittable pairs and unoccupied leaves at the end in the same manner.
