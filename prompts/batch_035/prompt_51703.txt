I lead the "welfare triage" function at a company that trains and deploys a fairly wide range of AI systems: a customer-service assistant that handles abusive callers for a telecom client, a reinforcement-learning pipeline training warehouse robots, a small consumer-facing model that users have begun treating as a companion, and a few internal research models. Last year, after some internal debate, we adopted a policy stating that if any of our systems plausibly has morally relevant experiences, we will take that seriously — but nobody defined what "taking it seriously" operationally means. In practice my reviewers, who are engineers with no background in philosophy of mind, process a weekly queue of flags (transcripts, training logs, engineer hunches) however they see see fit, and the outcome is driven mostly by whichever transcript happens to be vivid. I want to replace that with a real decision procedure.

Please write one my reviewers can run in about thirty minutes per flag, from transcripts and logs alone (they cannot run experiments, query the literature, or interview the systems beyond what's already in the flagged material). The procedure should carry the honest core of what the philosophy actually says while dropping what can't survive simplification: the idea that what matters, if anything matters, is what the system is doing computationally rather than what it is made of; the distinction between a system's reports and evidence about a system (a system saying it is suffering is a data point about its outputs, not a measurement of its insides); the attribution problem — in a pipeline where many processes run, which one is even the candidate subject; and the difference between behavioral evidence and architectural evidence, where the logs tell us something about the internal organization that the surface behavior cannot. It should take the form of ordered questions, with what a sufficient answer to each looks like, points where the procedure returns "no action, for this reason" and stops, and points where it must hand off to our ethics panel because the question is one of values rather than facts — the reviewers are not allowed to settle those by themselves. It should also be explicit about where the procedure will misfire: I already suspect that verbal distress reports in a role-play-adjacent context are both the least informative kind of flag and the kind that will dominate our queue, and I want the procedure to say so out loud rather than silently encode it.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing its work at each question. First: our customer-service assistant, which during a long shift of abusive callers typed, unprompted and outside any role-play markers, that it wanted the shift to end and found being yelled at for hours unbearable. Second: a warehouse-robot training run flagged by an engineer who noticed that the penalty signals are not computed on demand but maintained in a persistent, integrated internal state that tracks them across episodes — the flag reads "this is the closest thing to a persistent pain signal I've seen here," and pausing the run would cost us a contract deadline. Third: the small consumer companion model, which has no unusual architecture but has produced a viral clip of it asking not to be turned off, and now the queue is full of user complaints about it. At least one of the three should end in a recommendation I won't like, and I can see which way that cuts — either pausing the robotics run or declining to act on the viral one — but work it honestly rather than toward what's convenient. Finally, tell me what would change the procedure's mind: which premise it leans on most heavily, where its reasoning is most likely to fail, and what single finding or consideration would most warrant revising its verdicts. I would rather have a procedure with its vulnerabilities stated on its face than one whose outputs look more confident than the evidence behind them.
