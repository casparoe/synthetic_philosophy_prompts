My little ethics reading group has spent three weeks on the aggregation of tiny harms — you know, the torture vs. dust specks thing — and we've gotten basically nowhere, mostly because everyone keeps re-litigating the one famous case instead of saying which underlying rule for combining harms they'd actually trust. So I want to try something different with you. Over the years we've managed to formulate four candidate rules, spelled out below, and I'd like you to rank them by how much you'd trust each one to give the right verdict on hard cases — where "right" means matching your own settled judgment about a case after sitting with it for a while, not just being internally consistent.

Here are the four rules. (a) Straight addition: sum every bit of badness, however tiny, so a big enough pile of specks can outweigh a torture. (b) The threshold rule: harms below some minimal seriousness simply don't enter the calculus at all, no matter how many of them there are. (c) Diminishing numbers: headcount matters but with diminishing returns, so many medium harms can gang up on one big harm, yet no pile of microscopic harms ever beats a severe one. (d) The complaint-matching rule, the contractualist-flavored one: harms only trade off against each other when they're roughly comparable in seriousness, and how many people sit on each side of the trade doesn't change that. To calibrate what "trust" should mean, here are the cases we keep fighting over, some of them our own inventions: the original specks setup with the absurd tower-of-exponentials headcount on the specks side; the itch tax (cure one person's untreatable full-body itch by giving a million people a mild mosquito-bite itch for a year); the dilution case (one person in severe pain every day for 70 years vs. exactly the same total pain spread across 25,550 people who each get one terrible day in an otherwise fine life); the imperceptible pill (a drug that shaves each of a billion lives by an amount nobody could ever notice, but which sums, if you allow summing, to something like a decade of misery); and the congestion-charge case, where the friends who refuse to agregate pains are perfectly happy to agregate money, making 300 million people each pay a dollar so that one person doesn't pay $300 million.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no intro, no conclusion. Items one through four are the rules in order of trust, most trusted first, one or two sentences each on why it lands where it does, and please make it a strict order with no ties, no fence-sitting. The fifth item is pure self-critique: tell me which argument or consideration would most plausibly re-order the whole ranking, or, if you can't name one, where your own reasoning through the calibration cases is most likely to go wrong. I care more about that fifth item than about the ranking itself, honestly.
