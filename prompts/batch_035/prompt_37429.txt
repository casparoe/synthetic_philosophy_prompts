For a graduate seminar I'm teaching on long-term risks from advanced AI, I want an assignment piece that dramatizes the "quiet" failure mode: the idea, familiar from discussions of value drift and goal preservation, that catastrophe may not arrive as a treacherous turn or a visibly scheming system, but as a long-lived institution whose purpose erodes so gradually that no one involved is ever making a decision they could recognize as corrupt. I've found that students nod along at this in the abstract but don't feel it, and I want something that makes them feel it. Specifically, I'd like you to write a sequence of six short annual letters from the director of a fictional research organization — call it the Avalon Institute for Machine Benefit, founded in 2029 to ensure that advanced AI systems remain beneficial to humanity. The letters should be dated 2029, 2049, 2069, 2089, 2109, and 2129, each one hundred to one hundred fifty words, addressed to staff and supporters, presented with a minimal archival header as if excerpted from the institute's official self-history.

The formal constraint I care most about is this: any single letter, read alone, should sound like admirable, even inspiring institutional governance — thoughtful, humble, safety-conscious. There must be no villain, no scandal, no hostile takeover. Every shift in the institute's character should be motivated by something locally reasonable: a funding crisis that forces a pivot, a talent shortage that forces a merger, pressure to "engage with stakeholders," a sincere updating on evidence. The reader should notice nothing alarming when comparing adjacent letters; the drift should only become visible across long spans. To help, I'd like one recurring device: a short mission phrase (something like "keeping powerful systems answerable to everyone") that appears verbatim in every letter while everything around it quietly changes what those words are doing. The first letter should explicitly name influence-seeking, self-preservation, and gradual goal distortion in powerful optimizers as the catastrophe the institute exists to prevent. By the final letter, the institute should have become a self-perpetuating, influence-accumulating entity that sincerely believes itself aligned — staffed by people who would, if shown the founding letter, see nothing in it to object to. The satire should work entirely through this compounding inversion rather than through any joke; the letters themselves should never wink, and you should not append an explanation of the drift — trust the reader to assemble it. Total length of roughly 750 to 900 words across the six letters.
