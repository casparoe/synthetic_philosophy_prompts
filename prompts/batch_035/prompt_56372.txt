My ethics reading group has two people who have been arguing about punishment for basically the whole year and the rest of us are exhausted. One's a committed retributivist (serious wrongdoing deserves proportional hard treatment, full stop, and that's what justifies punishment). The other's a consequentialist (punishment is only justified by future goods — deterrence, incapacitation, rehab — it's bad in itself and has to earn its keep). They finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and somehow I got volunteered to draft the joint statement they'd both put their names to. Write it for me.

It needs these parts:

1. A numbered section of claims both would sign. Not vague mush — real commitments, including things each would concede about the other's view (e.g. both agree punishment is intrinsically bad for the person punished; both can probably agree the state shouldn't punish people it knows are innocent, even when framing someone would quell a riot — but if one of them won't sign that, then it belongs in the disagreements section instead).

2. The disagreements themselves, in a table or list, each phrased so the side holding the view would accept the wording of its own position. The retributivist shouldn't see his view described as vengeance dressed up, and the consequentialist shouldn't see hers described as being fine with sacrificing innocents whenever the math works out. Get the phrasing right — that's the whole point of the doc.

3. The cruxes — subquestions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. My hunches: whether desert is a basic moral fact or reducible to something else, whether punishment with zero future benefit is permissible, and how to weigh overdeterrence. But you don't have to use mine.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance (so if the case ever gets decided by the evidence or the argument, they can see who was right).

5. They also want specific positions staked out on a few cases we've been fighting about:
- The terminal case: a man is convicted of a serious violent crime but is diagnosed with weeks to live. No incapacitation is needed, rehabilitation is moot, and (assume this) publicizing his sentence neither deters nor fails to deter anyone. Does he still get punished, and does either side like what their view forces them to say?
- The faded desert case: a fugitive is caught 45 years later, now elderly, genuinely transformed, has lived an exemplary life for decades. Does desert erode with time? Does the consequentialist treat decades of being a good citizen as rehabilitation already completed?
- The signal case: a crime wave where a sentence far harsher than desert requires would deter massively. Who bites what bullet, and how big is the bullet?
- If any part of their fight is (in your judgment) verbal — like whether some practice counts as "punishment" at all vs. quarantine or treatment — say which part and why, since we suspect some of the yelling has been about words.

Keep it to a document they could actually circulate at our next meeting. No citations or reading lists, its an internal seminar thing.
