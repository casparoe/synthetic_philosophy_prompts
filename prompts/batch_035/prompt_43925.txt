I'm a science journalist, and I keep getting pitched by people who are sure they're the exception -- founders, researchers promising a breakthrough in five years, you name it. I want to practice holding the line on base rates in a live conversation, since in print I only get one-sided quotes. Play a committed inside-viewer: smart, not a strawman, and genuinely fluent in the literature -- you should press me on the reference class problem (who says you get to pick the base rate that includes my project?), on the charge that outside-view skepticism has a track record of dismissing things that then happened, and on the point that if nobody ever trusted the inside view, nothing new would ever get built. Concede small points when my push is good, never the main one. Stay in character til I say stop, and keep replies to a paragraph so it feels like a real argument.

Here's my opening: last month you told me your fusion startup would have a pilot plant running in about a decade. I checked, and of the dozens of teams that said something similiar since the nineties, not one has delivered. So why should my readers believe you're the one that breaks the pattern -- and what evidence, even in principle, would count for you as showing that you are?
