I'm part of an informal reading group that meets to discuss free will, and at our most recent session the topic was 'sphexishness' — the term Hofstadter coined after the digger wasp of the genus Sphex. The wasp in question performs a fixed provisioning routine: she drags a paralysed cricket to the rim of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, descends alone to inspect the burrow, comes back up, and only then hauls the cricket inside. If an experimenter nudges the cricket a few centimetres away while she is inspecting, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold — and then descends to inspect the burrow all over again before retrieving it. Left alone with a persistent experimenter, she will repeat this cycle dozens of times without ever shortening the routine by carrying the cricket straight down. Our discussion of this descended into a genuinely sharp dispute between two of the members, Jonas and Cleo, and I've been asked to summarise it fairly, since I genuinely don't know which of them is right.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Jonas claimed that the loop exposes the wasp as pure mechanism: nothing about the behaviour involves 'seeing' the situation at all, and he proposed that sphexishness is precisely the opposite of free will — so that human free will should be understood as freedom from sphexishness, and we are free only insofar as we might, unlike the wasp, break out of a loop mid-routine. (2) Cleo objected that the demonstration is ecologically rigged: nothing in the wasp's evolutionary history ever moves crickets about, so a creature can be perfectly adequate to its niche and merely look stupid under adversarial laboratory conditions; on that basis, she said, the experiment shows nothing at all about the wasp's lack of agency, any more than a fish's incompetence at climbing trees shows it lacks locomotion. (3) Jonas replied that this makes agency cheap and disturbingly environment-relative: our own intelligence equally presupposes a stable environment, and we look flexible only because nothing has yet moved our cricket; by Cleo's standard, free will amounts to nothing more than the temporary good fortune of not having an adversarial observer. (4) Cleo answered that the real line is that a human can be *informed* about her loop and then do otherwise on the next occasion — the wasp cannot be told, and cannot modify a routine mid-stream — and that responsiveness to reasons, not immunity to rigging, is what distinguishes flexible agency from rigid mechanism. (5) Jonas finished with what struck me as his strongest point: much of our vaunted response to being told is itself a rigid routine — people nod, apologise sincerely, resolve to change, and then repeat the mistake on schedule, and the entire existence of psychotherapy as a profession suggests that insight does not automatically translate into altered behaviour — so perhaps reasons-responsiveness is just the most elaborate sphex routine in our repertoire, and the wasp differs from us only in the size of her script.

They've now both appealed to me as a neutral party, and I don't trust myself to adjudicate. Could you go through the exchange move by move — rating each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justifications — and then say who is right about which specific points, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd also welcome your view on the underlying question, since it's what started the whole row: is free will helpfully understood as freedom from sphexishness at all, or does that framing build in some mistake from the start? Please write your answer in British English (so 'behaviour', 'sceptical', 'apologise', and so on).
