I am a novelist some three hundred pages into a book that follows a single family across three generations, and the spine of the novel has turned out to be the changing meaning of an insult. The grandfather, a magistrate's son from a herding region, lives in a world where worth is entirely social — conferred by others and instantly at stake in every slight, so that a slur demands an answer and turning the other cheek reads as consent to be small. His daughter, a corporate lawyer in a large northern city, carries the opposite article of faith: that worth is intrinsic, cannot be damaged by words, and that the truly secure person lets insults pass because they cannot touch what she is. The granddaughter, a college student, has in my drafts a third relationship to the same slight: she documents it, contextualizes it, and takes it before a third party — an ombudsperson, a panel, a public — on the theory that the injury is real, that words do wound, but that the remedy is appeal rather than retaliation. I have been reading the sociologists who call these honor, dignity, and victimhood moral cultures, and I want the novel not to sneer at any of them: the grandfather's world produces real courage and real corpses, the daughter's produces real tolerance and real indifference, and I honestly do not yet know what the granddaughter's will produce.

For the epigraph to Part Three, I want a villanelle, and I would like the form to carry the argument rather than merely illustrate it. The two refrains should be the honor culture's claim — that worth lives in the eyes of others and must be defended with the body — and the dignity culture's counterclaim — that worth is intrinsic and beyond the reach of words. The nineteen lines will make these claims circle each other without ever engaging: each stanza a small dramatization of whichever culture then holds the floor, until the final couplet forces the two refrains into the same breath. That is where I want the third sensibility to surface, not as a synthesis or a resolution but as the granddaughter's realization that both her ancestors were answering the same question — where does my worth live, and what must I do when it is attacked? — with opposite and equally partial answers. Someone who knows the honor/dignity/victimhood typology should be able to identify which line belongs to which refrain and where the third culture enters; someone who does not should simply find it a good poem about an insult traveling down three generations. Please resist the temptation to let either refrain win.
