I'm a junior taking an elective called Minds and Society, and we've spent the last two weeks on collective intentionality and joint action. My teacher set up the unit with two opposing pictures, and I want to make sure I understand where they actually come apart rather than just being able to recite the labels. On the first picture, associated with John Searle, there is a genuinely collective form of intentionality: when we sing a duet, there is a single primitive we-intend that isn't built out of my intention, your intention, and our beliefs about each other, and it is the same mental ingredient that supposedly lets a group own property or a committee reach a decision. On the second picture, associated with Michael Bratman, there is no group mind hovering over the individuals: a shared intention is just a structure of individual attitudes — I intend to do my part, I intend that we do the thing together, I believe you intend likewise, and these attitudes interlock in the right way — which is supposed to be enough for cooperation without any spooky collective subject.

For the assignment we each have to bring one worked example to seminar: a real or realistic case of people doing something together, used to show that the two pictures assign it to different categories or disagree about something substantive. The stock examples our teacher used, like two people painting a house together, are cases where both pictures agree on the verdict, so they don't do the work. I've half-formed my own candidate — the standing ovation that starts with one clapper, so that by the time the applause has gone round the hall, people at the back are doing something together with strangers they will never meet, and the whole event has the feel of a deliberate tribute even though no individual ever intended the applause as a whole; each person only ever intended to clap for a bit, given that others were. But I suspect this example is less clean than I think it is, and I'd like a fresh one — or a repaired version of mine.

Please give me one well-developed example of a case where the reductive and the primitivist analyses of joint action genuinely diverge, ideally something from ordinary modern life (a flash mob, an internet pile-on, a senior prank organized in a group chat where nobody ever explicitly agrees, a crowd weaving through a train station at rush hour) rather than the textbook cases. Explain precisely where the two analyses come apart over your example: whether the group can be said to intend something that no individual member intends; whether an individual can count as defecting, freeriding, or breaking faith with the others; whether the group as such can be praised or blamed; whether the action has a goal that is only visible from the group's point of view. And please flag the place where my standing-ovation idea goes wrong, if it does, since I might still want to defend it in seminar.
