"Kripke argued that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus,' if true, is necessarily true, yet can only be known a posteriori. (a) Explain what a rigid designator is. (b) Explain how the rigidity of names is supposed to generate the necessity of the identity claim. (c) Explain why the identity is nonetheless a posteriori rather than a priori. (d) Say what this does to the traditional Kantian pairing of necessity with analyticity and apriority."

Here is one student's answer, typed out as submitted:

"A rigid designator is a term that refers to the same object in every possible world. Ordinary proper names like 'Aristotle' and natural kind terms like 'gold' are rigid on Kripke's view, while definite descriptions like 'the teacher of Alexander' are not, since in some possible worlds someone else taught Alexander. So if 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are both rigid and both pick out Venus, then 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true in every possible world, that is, neccessary. And this couldn't have failed: no object can fail to be identical with itself, so once both names pick out the same planet, the identity holds everywhere. Of course the astronomers could have discovered otherwise — the morning star and the evening star might have turned out to be two different bodies — but in that scenario the statement would have been about different things, so this doesn't threaten the necessity.

The statement is still a posteriori. Babylonian astronomers had to work out empirically that the star seen at dusk and the star seen at dawn are one and the same planet. It's worth remembering that this puzzle is originally Frege's, and Frege essentially had the solution already: on his view names are rigid designators whose reference gets transmitted through a causal-historical chain of uses going back to an initial baptism. Kripke's contribution was mainly to formalize that and extend it to natural kinds.

Finally, since necessary truths just are analytic truths, Kripke's example shows that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is analytic after all — the necessity established above forces that conclusion. So the Kantian picture survives more or less intact: necessity, analyticity, and apriority still line up, we just have to allow that some analytic truths are discovered empirically rather than from the armchair. Either way Quine's attack on the analytic/synthetic distinction gets blunted."
