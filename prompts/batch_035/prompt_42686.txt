I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working in bioethics, and I'm about to draft a dissertation prospectus on duties of rescue. Before I build anything on it, I want an audit of the package of views I've drifted into over the last two years, because I suspect at least two of them quietly undercut each other, but I can't tell which. Here is the package, stated as plainly as I can:

(1) The pond argument is sound in its strong form: if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we are obligated to do it — and the usual demurrals (the victim is far away, I am one among millions equally positioned to help, my contribution would be a drop in the bucket) do not dilute the obligation.

(2) There is a genuine doing/allowing asymmetry: putting someone in danger is one thing, and a morally different and lesser thing; rights constrain what we may do in a way they do not symmetrically constrain what we omit to do.

(3) Partiality to intimates is a real moral datum and not a bias awaiting an evolutionary explanation: my obligations to my own child, partner, and close friends are grounded in relationships, and they can sometimes override what I owe strangers.

(4) The rule of rescue tracks something real: there is a distinctive, strict duty to an identified person in immediate danger — the child in the pond, the trapped miner — that does not attach in the same way to improving the survival odds of statistical beneficiaries, and this difference is not mere sentimentality about vividness.

(5) In the allocation of scarce resources, numbers dominate: when an institution must choose between rescuing one identified victim and saving several unidentified ones, it should save the several, and cost-effectiveness analysis is the right decision procedure for such choices without exception.

(6) There is a demandingness cap: I am not required to give until further sacrifice would leave me nearly as badly off as the people I would be helping; protecting my central life projects is permitted even when doing so means that strangers die whom I could have saved at real but non-ruinous cost.

What I want is an audit, not a defense of Singer and not a sermon against him. Please rank the tensions among these by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each pair that looks to be in tension, say whether the appearance dissolves on inspection and why. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the package. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I don't yet know where the problem is, so please don't assume I have a favorite among these six, and don't soft-pedal the diagnosis to spare any of them: if the package as stated can't be held together, tell me exactly which commitment has to go. No reading list or bibliography, please — just the analysis of my own set.
