I teach a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and in our next unit we take up the familiar claim that the leveling-down objection tells decisively against telic egalitarianism while leaving prioritarianism untouched: on the one hand, a view that counts a state of affairs in which the better-off are reduced, with no one gaining, as in any respect an improvement looks untenable; on the other, prioritarianism — on which benefits to the worse-off matter more, but inequality itself has no intrinsic value — seems to walk away unbruised, since strict leveling-down always comes out worse on the weighted sum. I am, however, unconvinced that the asymmetry is as clean as the textbooks present it, and I want to make my students do the hard part rather than recite the folklore. So I would like your considered credence, with the reasoning fully decomposed, for the following proposition:

The leveling-down objection, properly stated, refutes telic egalitarianism while leaving prioritarianism essentially unscathed.

Please do not give me a survey of positions; give me a number and its derivation. Specifically, I want your probability estimates for at least the following sub-claims, each with a one- or two-line justification:

1. That the leveling-down objection has genuine dialectical force at all — that is, that the egalitarian's standard reply (that equality is realized and hence in one respect good even when its realization makes everything worse) is a failure, rather than a legitimate appeal to moral pluralism about how goods are to be weighed against one another.

2. That prioritarianism truly escapes the objection, rather than merely escaping its crudest formulation. Here consider the structural parallels: a concave moral weighting function can prefer a world with strictly less aggregate welfare to one with more, whenever the smaller total is sufficiently concentrated among the worse-off. If the egalitarian is faulted for counting a worse-for-everyone world as better in one respect, is the prioritarian not similarly faulted for counting a poorer world as better in virtue of where its losses fall? Does it matter that the prioritarian's pro-attitude is person-regarding rather than relational, or is that distinction doing less work than its defenders suppose?

3. That prioritarianism remains a stable, distinct position at all — that it does not quietly collapse into telic egalitarianism (or into a disguised variant thereof) once its commitments are spelled out precisely, in which case the alleged asymmetry of exposure to the objection would be illusory.

In combining these terms, say explicitly whether you are treating them as independent, and if not, how the dependencies run; a naive multiplication may be exactly what is wrong here. Then tell me which of your sub-claims the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of case would move that term the most.

For context: my own credence in the proposition as stated is roughly 0.55, while my co-teacher, who has spent more time with the ex ante literature, insists it is below 0.2. Her sharpest point is this: once outcomes are uncertain, the priority weighting can be applied to each person's prospects before the gamble resolves, and on that ex ante reading prioritarianism can endorse a lottery that is worse in expectation for every single person than the sure alternative, provided the risk is concentrated on those whose prospects are already dim. That, she argues, is a leveling-down verdict in all but name, available to a view that never asserts anything about equality. Where, most likely, does our disagreement actually live — in sub-claim 1, 2, or 3, or in some further question about the ex ante/ex post tension that I have not listed? I would find a diagnosis of the disagreement at least as valuable as your number.
