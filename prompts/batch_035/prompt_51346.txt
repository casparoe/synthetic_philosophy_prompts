"Strawson's famous move was to sidestep the ancient debate about determinism by pointing out that our responsibility practices are grounded in feelings rather than theories. His argument runs: (1) we cannot help but feel resentment, gratitude, and indignation when others wrong or benefit us; (2) since these reactive attitudes are psychologically inescapable, the question of whether anyone ever really deserves them cannot arise; (3) therefore determinism, even if true, is simply irrelevant to moral responsibility, and the incompatibilist's worry rests on a confusion. Strawson adds that when we do set responsibility aside — for children, the mentally ill, or the severely manipulated — we drop the reactive attitudes in favour of an objective stance that treats the person as a thing to be managed rather than a participant in human relationships, and he argues that adopting this stance universally, as the incompatibilist's complaint implicitly demands, would be impossible anyway. The upshot is that compatibilism is vindicated: responsibility survives determinism because the sentiments that constitute it could never be abandoned."
