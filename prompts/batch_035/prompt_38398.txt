My department runs a voluntary reading group where each member eventually circulates a one-page position statement for the others to attack, and in three weeks it's my turn. This term's theme is the architecture of mind, and I've been trying to write down where I actually stand. The trouble is that once I list my commitments, I can't tell which of them pull against each other and which are compatible, and I don't trust my own diagnosis, since I presumably got into this state somehow. Here is the set. (1) I endorse the parity idea behind the extended mind thesis: if a process outside my skull is doing, in the ongoing guidance of my behavior, the same job an in-the-head process would do, there is no principled reason to count one as part of my cognition while refusing the other. (2) By that light, I think my phone really is part of my mind in a fairly literal sense — the notes application I have written in daily for six years, the calendar, the maps. I consult them the way the familiar textbook case of Otto and his notebook works: automatically, trustingly, without deliberation, and I would be cognitively impaired without them. So I believe my memory and my sense of direction extend into my pocket. (3) Yet I hold on to first-person authority: I know what I believe and intend not by observing evidence and inferring, but directly, in a way no one else can; that, I assume, is just what it is to have a mind of one's own. (4) I also maintain an asymmetry in how failures get owned: when I misremember something and pass on a wrong figure, that is my mind malfunctioning and it reflects on me; when the app contains a wrong entry and I act on it, that is a tool failing — a product defect. I do not want to say that I remembered wrongly, or that it shows anything about me. (5) Finally, though I have signed on to the extended mind, I cannot shake the conviction that my thinking happens in my head: when I deliberate, I would swear the work is being done behind my eyes rather than out on the desk, and I am not sure whether that is a datum I must accommodate or an illusion I should dismiss.

So: which of these five are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and what is the cheapest consistent package? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each one tell me exactly what has to give — which commitment, or which reading of it. I would rather not be told to keep an open mind; I want specific verdicts, each with the strongest case made for the losing side. And since I will have to defend all of this under attack in the reading group, please also tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong: what consideration, or what kind of worked example, would change your ranking or flip one of your verdicts, and where your reasoning is on its shakiest ground.
