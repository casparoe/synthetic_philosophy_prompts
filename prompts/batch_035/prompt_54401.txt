I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been given something unusual: a year to report a long-form project on the units-of-selection debate — the old fight over whether natural selection acts on genes, individuals, or groups. My working framing is this: the mid-century group-selection proposals of the Wynne-Edwards era were widely thought to have been routed by the gene-centric counterrevolution, and then a further wave of formal work suggested that kin selection and multilevel-selection accounting are, in many settings, mathematically equivalent ways of bookkeeping the same underlying process. That second result is read two opposite ways in the literature I've been reading — either as dissolving the whole dispute into a matter of taste, or as a provocation showing that the real questions are empirical ones the formalism can't settle on its own. Meanwhile a sprawling popular discourse treats "the selfish gene" as licensing claims about human nature that, as far as I can tell, no serious participant in the technical debate would endorse. I can't do all of this in one project, and I'd rather not produce another retelling of the 1970s polemics.

So what I need from you is a prioritization, not a survey. Which two or three open questions in this area are most worth a year of a journalist's attention — and, more importantly, defended in terms of a journalist's criteria rather than a researcher's? By that I mean: I care about which questions, if resolved or even sharpened, would do most to correct how an educated lay audience actually misunderstands evolution (the folk "for the good of the species" intuition, the slide from gene-level selection to psychological egoism, the assumption that group selection is pseudoscience), but also which ones have genuine live stakes inside the field itself — for instance, whether selection among units during evolutionary transitions, somatic selection in cancer and aging, or cultural group selection in humans are cases where the choice of framework makes different predictions rather than just different narratives. Tell me what you would deprioritize as merely terminological or as downstream of an answer that wouldn't change anything a reader would notice, and what developments — a field result, a fossil or genomic finding, a widely circulated argument — would move a question up or down your list.

One request on rigor, since this is for publication: throughout, please mark clearly what is settled among people who actually work on this, what is genuinely contested, and what is contested only at the level of rhetoric and book titles rather than substance. I've found that the settled/contested line gets blurred constantly in this debate, and I don't want to hand my editors a piece that presents one school's victory as a consensus where practicing biologists are still split. If a question is open precisely because the two sides disagree about what would count as evidence, say that explicitly — that disagreement is itself something I'd want to report.
