I've been comparing my gut verdicts about the functions of biological traits, and I'd like to know whether a single principled account of function can capture them — one that looks back to why a trait exists, or at what it currently contributes, or something else — or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

The verdicts. The function of the heart is to pump blood, and this holds even for a failing heart that can barely pump anything. The appendix: even if it houses useful gut bacteria, I resist saying that is its function; it seems like a leftover doing something helpful, not something that is for that. Feathers: on the usual story they arose for insulation or display, with flight coming later, yet I have no trouble saying the function of a modern bird's feathers includes flight. An implanted artificial heart: I would say without hesitation that its function is to pump blood, though nothing about it was shaped by natural selection. The nose: it is true that the nose supports eyeglasses, but it would be absurd to call supporting eyeglasses a function of the nose. And a lab-grown lump of cardiac tissue that happens to contract and circulate fluid, with no evolutionary history and no designer behind it: I lean toward saying it has no function at all, though I am unsure.

The trouble is that the appendix and the artificial heart seem to share a shape — a thing doing something useful without having been selected for it — yet my verdicts about them point in opposite directions. Is there one account that fits all six verdicts? If not, which verdict would you overturn, and at what cost in the rest? Please also tell me what the best resulting account says about a case I have not mentioned: a bird population that has just started using its wings in a novel courtship display, before any selection for displaying has had time to act — is the display already a function of their wings, or does it become one only once selection begins favoring it?
