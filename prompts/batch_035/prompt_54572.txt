I'm a retired chemist who's been auditing an intro philosophy of science course at the community college since I left the lab, and this week the instructor asked me to help mark the short essays (she knows natural kinds are my new obsession). I said yes before it occured to me that I have no idea how to grade philosophy — in the lab a number was right or it wasn't, and here its all judgment calls. So for the essay below, give me a letter grade plus the specific comments that justify it, each one tied to a particular sentence, the kind of comment I could point to if the student came to office hours to argue.

The assignment was: Are there natural kinds? Defend an answer using at least one example from chemistry and one from biology, and say what your view implies about whether jade counts as a natural kind.

The essay, exactly as submitted:

"Natural kinds are categories that exist independently of our classifying them, and the sciences have found them wherever they have looked hard enough. Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment shows why meaning can't be in the head: on Twin Earth the watery stuff is XYZ rather than H2O, so when our twins say 'water' they are talking about something different from what we are. This is really just Kripke's insight that natural kind terms are rigid designators, picking out the same stuff in every possible world. Chemistry is the paradigm case. Every sample of lithium behaves the same, and the periodic table predicted the properties of elements before anyone isolated them, which would be a miracle if the joints weren't out there in nature. Jade fits the same pattern: it is a natural kind because, like water, it has a hidden essence that chemists later discovered underneath the everyday term. Biology is where the picture gets fuzzy. Ring species and asexual lineages show that living things do not sort into kinds with essences, and on Boyd's homeostatic property cluster view, which John Dupré also defended, a species is held together by nothing more than overall similarity. So elements are natural kinds but species are not: since life only branches and never crystallizes into a table, what counts as a species is really up to us."
