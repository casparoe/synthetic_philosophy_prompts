So a bit about me for context: I spent 35 years as a soil chemist at an agricultural research station, retired two years ago, and have been going deep on philosophy since -- mostly ethics, since that's the part my whole career never touched. Kant was my entry point and I loved it, clean axioms like a good protocol. Then I stumbled into the care ethics people and honestly it annoyed me at first, but it's growing on me. Joan Tronto's breakdown of care into phases (caring about, taking care of, care-giving, care-receiving) and elements like attentiveness and responsiveness really got under my skin, and Noddings' idea that the one-caring has to actually receive the cared-for somehow, via what she calls engrossment.

Here's my situation: I run a little discussion group at the community center, 12 people, mostly fellow retirees, some with backgrounds in nursing and teaching so they've DONE care work, which I never really have. One 90-minute session. I don't want a lecture -- I could give a lecture myself and it would be bad. What I want is an activity where they run into the core tension themselves before I name it: that caring seems to demand being partial and responsive to this particular person in front of you, while our intuitions about fairness (and frankly everything I liked about Kant) demand treating people according to the same rules regardless of who they are.

My half-formed idea: some kind of dispatch or triage game where small groups have to allocate a limited pool of help (visits, rides, check-ins) among a set of "clients" described on cards. One group gets a strict impartial allocation rulebook they MUST follow, another group gets no rules but can request more information about any client they want, maybe a third hybrid. Then partway through, inject complications -- a client who's gaming the system, a client who's quietly worse off than their file says, two clients who need the same slot and one is the group member's old friend. I want them to feel it when the rulebook gives the "wrong" answer and when unstructured caring also gives a wrong answer (the squeaky wheel gets the visit and the quietly deteriorating person gets nothing).

Can you design this properly? Roles, the client cards (give me the actual vignette text, or at least the first few), how many rounds, how the limited help-pool should work without real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the Tronto and Noddings vocabulary. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I already suspect one table will just get moralistic about the freeloader client and run out of time -- and which part of care ethics no 90-minute simulation can actually reproduce, so I know what I have to just assert in the debrief myself.
