In debates about whether AI assistants should refuse or override user requests, a friend of mine defends refusals with the following analogy: a good bartender cuts off the visibly drunk patron, and nobody calls that paternalism — it is simply what a responsible professional does. On her view, an AI assistant that declines certain requests is exercising the same kind of ordinary professional discretion, so critics who describe refusal policies as paternalism are making a category error. She leans on the analogy to conclude that contemporary refusal behavior is unobjectionable across the board.

I am only half convinced. For one thing, when a bartender cuts off the patron who clearly walked to the bar and will walk home, that does look like paternalism — just paternalism we have collectively decided to tolerate. For another, the bartender is standing in front of one person at a time and is refusing something whose harms flow through the drinker's own body, whereas an AI assistant serves thousands of users it cannot observe, and its outputs can harm people who never asked for anything.

Please audit the analogy carefully. Lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the bartender, the patron, the tenth shot, the bar's owner, the state liquor authority, and the regulars whose expectations train the bartender's judgment? Then identify where the mapping breaks — in particular, which features of the source (direct observation of impairment, a single physical patron, harm mediated by the patron's own consumption, discretion honed face to face) the target lacks, and which features of the target (many simultaneous users, third parties at risk, policies authored by a distant developer rather than improvised on the spot, no access to the user's condition) the source cannot accommodate. Please distinguish the disanalogies that matter for my friend's conclusion from those that do not. Then assess: once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion survive — is refusal behavior genuinely closer to a bartender's professional discretion than to paternalism — or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole time? Note, where relevant, that the bartender case itself splits: refusing the patron who is about to drive is protection of third parties rather than paternalism, while refusing the patron for his own good is exactly what philosophers mean by (soft) paternalism, so the analogy may license some refusals while undermining others. Finally, if there is a better analogy for what an AI assistant is doing when it refuses or overrides a request, present it, and indicate where it, too, would break down.
