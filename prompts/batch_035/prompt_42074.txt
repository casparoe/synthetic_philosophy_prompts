I'm a clinical ethicist at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and in three weeks I'm presenting to our ethics committee a position paper I've been circling for two years. The thesis: advance directives concerning life-sustaining treatment have what I call conditional authority — stronger than mere evidence of what a patient once wanted, weaker than the binding force we give a contemporaneous competent refusal — because a directive functions as testimony about values rather than as a command issued by an owner to her future body. On my view, a directive to withhold, say, antibiotics from a patient with advanced dementia binds only insofar as the values it expresses (a lifelong horror of dependence, say) are values the patient can still be understood as bearing, in however vestigial a form; where the demented self has plainly and stably become someone with different, contented interests, the directive's authority lapses rather than being overridden by anyone. I arrived at this the hard way: my own mother, four years into Alzheimer's, wrote a directive in her early fifties refusing nearly everything short of comfort care, and last spring she was humming happily through her antibiotic course while I, of all people, recused myself from the committee's deliberation over whether to honor the paper. So the view is not academic to me, which is part of the problem.

The committee is a genuinely mixed audience, and I want to be ready for what they specifically will throw at me, not for the generic objections I'd get at a philosophy conference. The chair is a palliative care physician who privately thinks directives mostly serve as instruments of family closure and treats them as revisable by default. The most philosophically serious member is an internist with broadly Kantian instincts who holds that the directive is an exercise of autonomy that later incapacity cannot cancel — roughly Dworkin's line about precedent autonomy, which he knows well and defends fluently. There's hospital counsel, who worries about liability and the federal framework requiring us to honor documented wishes; a chaplain whose concern is that we never treat the patient in the bed as if she has already left; a patient advocate who has watched families weaponize the phrase "she's not herself anymore" to justify indefinite treatment; and a rotating family member seat currently held by a widower who twice tearfully asked us to keep treating a wife whose directive said to let her go. The chair and the internist have both read the standard literature — the autonomy defense and the critics in the tradition of Dresser and Buchanan who argue that the actually existing patient's interests should count for more than the remembered ones — so I cannot present my middle position as unmapped territory.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this specific committee will actually raise, in roughly the order they're likely to arrive and phrased in each member's own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each could do to my position, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. To help you target them, here is my sketch. (1) Contemporaneous competent refusals bind because they express the values of the person whose life it is. (2) The demented patient is not a different person, but neither is she a blank: there can be thin but real evaluative continuity between the author of the directive and the patient in the bed. (3) The proper function of a directive is to testify to the values held at the time of writing, not to command a future self. (4) Testimony has authority only where the witness remains competent to testify about that subject matter, and the competence that matters here is not cognitive but evaluative: does the patient, in behavior and partial utterance, still recognize the good she was pursuing when she wrote it? (5) Therefore directives lapse not when the patient forgets them but when the patient has demonstrably ceased to bear the relevant values. I already know the soft spots I'm aware of — humming through antibiotics is not a value, and my evidentiary standard for "vestigial values" in a nonverbal patient is embarrassingly thin — so please be candid about those and anything else I'm missing. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it's the dangerous one.
