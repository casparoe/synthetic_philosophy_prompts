I'm in a reading group working through Popper on demarcation, and next month we're doing a session on how his criterion plays out in evolutionary biology. Since he wrote most of the demarcation stuff before the interesting 70s fights, I want to try predicting how his standards would rule on later developments rather than rehashing the textbook examples. We've assembled his track record first, and honestly it's bugging me.

His verdicts so far, as best we can tell: Einstein's relativity is science at its best, because the light-bending prediction put real risk on the table and could have come out wrong at the 1919 eclipse. Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's psychology are pseudoscience because they explain everything after the fact -- whatever a patient does confirms the theory, so no observation could ever hurt them. Marxism started out as science but degenerated into pseudoscience once its followers kept reinterpreting it to dodge refutation. Astrology explains everything and predicts nothing. And then the awkward one: he said natural selection, at least in some formulations, isn't a testable scientific theory -- that survival of the fittest reads as near-tautological, fitness just being defined by who survives -- but that it's nonetheless an enormously valuable metaphysical research programme that guides real testable science. (I've also seen claims he softened on this later in life, in some letter or afterword, but I've never tracked down the source, so treat that as hearsay and don't hold me to it.)

Here's the thing that gets me. Freud and Adler got "pseudoscience," Darwin got "valuable metaphysics." Both have unfalsifiable-looking cores that can apparently accommodate any observation. So there seem to be at least two rules that fit his track record: one where demarcation is about whether the theory as a whole sticks its neck out with risky predictions, and one where what matters is whether an untestable core is doing productive work generating testable satellites. I can't tell which rule he's actually running from the verdicts alone, and I suspect they diverge on the stuff that came later.

So predict what he'd say about these three, under each rule, and tell me which rule fits him better and why:

(1) Punctuated equilibrium (Gould and Eldredge, early 70s). Stasis in the fossil record is real; change happens fast in small isolated populations. Is that a risky new prediction that could have failed, or an immunizing move -- a way of explaining away the fact that we mostly DONT see the smooth transitions gradualism had promised? I can see both rules going either way here and I want to see exactly which does what.

(2) Kimura's neutral theory (late 60s). Most substitutions at the molecular level are neutral drift rather than selection, which predicts roughly clocklike accumulation of differences between lineages. That seems risky and quantitative, the kind of thing Popper liked. But the core claim -- most of this is drift -- can always be softened when selection stories work better. Does a theory earn scientific status through risky satellites when its core has an escape hatch, or does the escape hatch poison the satellites?

(3) A pop evolutionary psychology paper giving an adaptive explanation of music, where the same authors could have run basically the same machinery in reverse if the data had come out otherwise -- music as group bonding vs. music as courtship display, either way the adaptationist story absorbs it. Each individual explanation is dressed up as testable, but the programme as a whole looks like it can absorb any finding.

Which of the three would best tell the two rules apart, and can you describe a further case -- something I could bring to the group -- where the two rules give opposite verdicts with no wiggle room? One more thing I keep going back and forth on: is the tautology complaint even right? If fitness is defined independently of actual survival, say as expected reproductive output given heritable traits, then survival of the fittest looks testable after all, just probabilistic. Does fixing that matter for which rule he's running, or is it a separate question from the demarcation one?

To be clear, we're not asking what's actually true about the biology. I want his own standards applied consistently to him, whatever they end up committing him to.
