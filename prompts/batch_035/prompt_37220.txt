I'm taking a philosophy of science course on scientific realism, and this week's dispute turned on a pair of analogies that our seminar keeps leaning on as if they did the same work. The professor, arguing for structural realism, reaches first for Eddington's old parable of the ichthyologist: a researcher who casts a two-inch mesh net into the sea, surveys the catch, and announces the grand law that no sea creature is shorter than two inches. The professor says this is our epistemic situation exactly — the mesh is our mathematical and conceptual apparatus, the catch is physical science, and the sea is whatever there is in itself — so we should conclude that science can deliver structure, structure all the way down, and nothing about the intrinsic character of things. But she also leans on the historical-retention example in the same breath: the Fresnel-to-Maxwell transition, where the ether died but the equations carried over, which she says shows that what survives theory change is precisely the structure, and that this is what "knowing the structure" amounts to.

A classmate (and, reading between the lines, half the seminar) thinks the two analogies are being run together. As she tells it, the net parable is about the limits of any inquiry given our apparatus — it's a story about selection, and it would seem to support a kind of pessimism or selectivism about science rather than any positive claim about structure. The retention case, by contrast, is a historical observation about what scientists actually keep, and it's not obvious that "the equations survived" licenses "we know the structure" rather than just "we keep the equations." So the classmate thinks the professor is using a limitation argument and a preservation argument as if each bolstered the other, when at most one of them does any real work, and it isn't clear which.

I'd like you to adjudicate this by auditing the analogies rather than just surveying the debate. Please give me: (1) a correspondence table for the net parable — what plays the role of the mesh, the sea, the catch, the two-inch law, and the surveying — and then a table for the retention story, since it too is functioning as an analogy (theory change as something like a change of notation that preserves content); (2) where each mapping breaks, flagging which disanalogies actually matter for the structuralist conclusion and which are just noise — for instance, whether the fact that real nets are physical objects with structure of their own, or that fish are themselves things rather than relations, does any damage; and (3) your verdict on whether the classmate's challenge succeeds: does the professor's case for structural realism survive once the two arguments are separated, and if the net parable can't carry it, is the retention example enough on its own, or does structural realism need something neither analogy provides? If you think one or both analogies should be replaced, say what a better one would be and what it would get right. I'd be grateful if you kept the discussion self-contained — I can look up the literature later, but I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
