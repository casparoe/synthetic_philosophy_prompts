I teach a small honors seminar called "Formal Systems and Their Limits" — 14 students, mostly philosophy and math majors, one 80-minute session, movable desks and a whiteboard. Next week we reach the part of the course on formal derivability and what Hofstadter calls "jumping out of the system." I want the students to encounter this experientially before they read about it, and I have a specific vehicle in mind: a tiny formal system and a target string that is, unbeknownst to them, underivable.

The system (call it the MIU system) works like this, so you need nothing beyond this paragraph: strings are built from the letters M, I, and U, and always begin with M. The one axiom is MI. Four rules of inference: (1) if a string ends in I, you may append U; (2) if a string is Mx, where x is any string, you may extend it to Mxx; (3) if III occurs anywhere in a string, you may replace that occurrence with U; (4) if UU occurs anywhere, you may delete it. The target is MU. The fact I will not tell them, and you should design around: MU is not derivable. The number of I's in a string, taken modulo 3, is invariant under all four rules — MI has one I, MU has zero, and zero is divisible by three while one is not. Within the system no amount of clever derivation will ever certify this; you have to step outside, treat the whole system as an object of study, and find the invariant.

I'd like you to design the in-class activity. Before writing the final design, please brainstorm two or three candidate structures and briefly say which you'd pick and why — I want to see your reasoning, not just the output. Then develop the winner fully, with concrete rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff scheme that uses no real money (points, bounties, and bragging rights are fine).

A few requirements on the design itself:

1. The students must hit the wall themselves. No lecture up front, and the phrases "MU puzzle," "invariant," and "jumping out of the system" must not appear until the debrief. The racing phase should feel like a game they're trying to win, not a setup for a lesson.
2. At some point the payoff structure must change so that proving MU underivable becomes worth as much as, or more than, deriving it — but the change should be staged so that going for the proof feels like giving up, or like exploiting a loophole in my bounty rules rather than doing the "real" work. I want them to feel the pull of staying inside the system.
3. Consider assigning one or two students a special role — for example, a "rules clerk" who mechanically validates every proposed move and rejects anything not licensed by the four rules, so the formality of the system is embodied in a person rather than explained on a handout.
4. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical content: what their derivations were (theorem-proving within the system), what the rules clerk was (the difference between syntax and meaning), what whoever found the invariant did (the jump out of the system), and how this connects to Gödel-style limitative results — a system that cannot, from inside, certify certain truths about its own derivations.
5. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: students who Google the answer on their phones, a group that derives MUU and insists they've "basically got it," someone who guesses the impossibility in thirty seconds from the vibes, the timing collapsing, whatever you think likely — and how I should handle each.
6. Finally, be frank about which feature of the target idea this activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief. In particular, I suspect the real Gödelian phenomenon — a system reasoning about itself, the inside/outside boundary being crossed by the system's own resources — is not something the MU puzzle actually instantiates, and I'd like your honest assessment of how far the analogy really goes.

Please take the philosophical stakes seriously: the pedagogical goal is that students come away having felt, at least once, the difference between grinding away inside a set of rules and stepping back to see the rules as an object — before anyone tells them that this distinction has a history.
