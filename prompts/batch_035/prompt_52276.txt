I'm TA-ing a 200-level intro ethics course (22 students), and I get one 75-minute section to run on hedonism before they read the classic Bentham/Mill passages the following week. My section leader is pushing us to make sections activity-first instead of me talking at them, and honestly these students are checked out enough that if I just lecture about higher and lower pleasures they'll be on their phones the whole tiem. So: design an in-class activity that makes them actually run into the heterogeneity problem themselves — the thing where experiences we'd all call pleasures (the taste of a perfectly ripe peach, finishing a hard proof after hours stuck on it, schadenfreude when a rival's startup implodes, an orgasm, the wash of relief when a biopsy comes back clear) look so different from each other that it's hard to say what they even have in common, and where the obvious answer, that they all feel good, starts to look either so thin it does no work or else circular.

Rough shape I'm imagining but am open to better ideas on: they get slips of paper describing experiences in the second person, they have to rank or price them somehow — maybe bid on them with a fixed budget of points as if they were allocating a life? — and then the trap springs: each group has to write down the ONE thing all the pleasant experiences share that the unpleasant ones lack, and other groups get points for finding counterexamples. Then a debrief where I map what actually happened in the room onto the real positions without it turning into a lecture: Bentham treating pleasure as one commensurable thing you can do arithmetic on, Mill's higher/lower pleasures move, Moore's complaint that there isn't any one thing there, and the more modern question of whether the only survivor is a super-thin notion like a common felt tone. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I have literally never run an activity like this), and be honest about which part of the problem no classroom exercise can reproduce and I'll just have to supply myself.

Format stuff: start directly with the activity itself — no opening paragraph about why hedonism matters, save all that for the debrief section. In the debrief, keep what's settled (e.g. what Mill actually claimed, what the standard objections are) clearly separate from what philosophers genuinely still fight about — I don't want to hand my students, say, the felt-tone idea as if it were consensus when it's contested, or sneak in a contested reading of Bentham without flagging it. And if the activity as you design it leans on some particular interpretation of Mill or on a particular way of individuating pleasures, say so explicitly rather than burying it.
