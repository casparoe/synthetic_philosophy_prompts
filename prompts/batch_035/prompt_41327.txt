I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy; I've simply been turning a question over in my mind for some years and would like help testing my ideas against a made-up example. The question is who is entitled to vote on a collective decision. In my own notes I've settled on three tests, and I've given them my private names. The footprint test: anyone whose life the decision touches at all gets a vote. The rule test: anyone the decision will be enforced against, or anyone inside the body that makes the decision, gets a vote. And the cornered test, which I believe is my own invention: you get a vote only if you could not realistically have gotten out of the decision's way, meaning that avoiding its effects would have cost you more than an ordinary person can be expected to pay. My suspicion, which I would like to see challenged, is that in real life these tests almost always agree, and that people who defend the footprint test never notice this because they only ever compare it against obviously bad alternatives, such as letting only property owners vote. So here is what I want you to build for me. Construct a single, realistic, one-paragraph scenario of an ordinary collective decision, the sort a town council, school board, or hospital trust would make, in which the three tests each enfranchise a genuinely different group of people. Specifically, there must be at least one kind of person who passes the footprint test but fails the other two, at least one kind who passes the rule test but fails the other two, and at least one kind who passes the cornered test but fails the rule test. The people in these marginal groups must be ordinary and concrete, commuters, employees, homeowners, and the divergence must not depend on the decision affecting enormous numbers of people, on future generations, or on anyone behaving badly. The disagreement must fall out of the facts of the scenario rather than being stipulated; please do not build the case by simply declaring that one test misfires. After the scenario, still in ordinary prose, walk me through it. For each of the three tests, tell me exactly who gets the vote and who is left out, naming the kinds of people rather than speaking abstractly. Then tell me which single feature of the scenario is doing the work of pulling the three tests apart, and what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, in particular whether the case collapses back into a boring one where all three tests coincide. Finally, give me your honest verdict on whether my cornered test emerges from your case looking sensible, or whether it produces a result that would embarrass me in front of a defender of the footprint test, and if it would, explain what such a defender would say the cornered test gets wrong. Please write everything as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just paragraphs, as though you were explaining all of this to me in a letter.
