You are an epistemologist with deep expertise in the theory of rational belief and disagreement, advising someone on a concrete situation. Please think carefully before answering; you might start by privately brainstorming the distinct questions buried in the situation below, then give your considered advice.

Here is the situation. My colleague Dana and I are the two reviewers on a team that audits machine-learning systems for safety failures before release. Three weeks ago we completed independent audits of the same system. I found a serious failure mode; she judged the system sound. Since then we have shared every scrap of evidence and walked each other through our reasoning, so we are working from exactly the same material, and each of us can find at most minor quibbles with the other's analysis. Historically, our track records on this kind of audit are, as far as anyone can tell, equal. On past disputes like this one, my instinct has always been to hold my position and let events decide who was right; Dana's habit has been to move halfway toward the other side.

Two complications are eating at me. First, we cannot even agree on whether we are equals. I think our track records on disputed audits are roughly even; Dana keeps her own tally and says she has been right substantially more often than I have. But her tally and my tally disagree, and neither of us can check the other's bookkeeping without trusting the other's bookkeeping. So our disagreement about the audit has generated a disagreement about whether we are the sort of peers whose disagreements should move us toward each other or leave us both standing where we were — and that second disagreement has exactly the same structure as the first. Second, a year ago we had a standoff much like this one: Dana met me halfway, I did not move an inch, and it eventually came out that she was right. I have been quietly ashamed of my conduct since, and I cannot tell whether that episode is now a reason to defer (I owe her one), a reason to distrust my current confidence (I was the stubborn one, and I was wrong), or no reason at all (it is one data point).

So here is what I want from you. Is holding my ground defensible? Is meeting her halfway defensible? Or is the honest answer that defensibility is the wrong frame here? Whatever you recommend, please also tell me what to say when Dana asks why — I want reasons she cannot dismiss as a description of my own psychology. Be willing to say something definite; I already have plenty of ways to remain undecided.
