I'm the patient-safety officer for a mid-sized regional hospital network. My office is responsible for the root cause analyses (RCAs) that follow every serious adverse event — roughly three to five a month. Each RCA is done by a multidisciplinary team (nurses, pharmacists, a physician or two, sometimes a risk manager), over about two weeks, none of them with any philosophy training, and the final report goes to the medical executive committee, which uses it to decide what gets retrained, what protocol changes, and occasionally what gets disclosed to a patient's family or reported to a regulator. One recurring step turns out to be surprisingly contentious: writing the causal chain. Nearly every interesting candidate cause in these cases is an omission — the nurse who didn't notice the order, the pharmacist who didn't flag the interaction, the covering physician who didn't escalate, the software that didn't alert. Our template distinguishes causes, contributing factors, and background conditions, and the choice between these buckets has real consequences: items in the causal chain drive corrective action and legal exposure; items demoted to background conditions effectively vanish. Right now the choice is made by intuition, and different teams make it differently.

Last weekend I had a long argument about this with my friend, who is finishing a philosophy PhD and takes a dim view of how our reports use the word cause. Her position: an omission is not an event; nothing happens when someone fails to act; and only things that happen can cause things that happen. So our causal chains should contain only positive acts — the wrong drug given, the wrong button pressed — while the failures to act belong in a separate register, as mere background. My position was the counterfactual one: the patient's harm counterfactually depends on the failure to give the antidote — had the omission not occurred, no harm — so it belongs in the causal chain as much as anything does. Then we each got stuck with absurd consequences. Her view implies that if a malfunctioning pump actively administers the wrong drug, that caused the injury, but if a nurse fails to give the right drug to a patient who then dies, that caused nothing at all — which our committees, our lawyers, and presumably our patients' families would find bizarre. My view, applied consistently, implies profligacy: the harm also counterfactually depends on the absence of any passerby who could have intervened, on the hospital not having staffed a second pharmacist, on nobody having designed a better alerting system decades ago — every non-event in the universe lines up for a spot in the causal chain. She then argued that the distinction I actually care about is normative, not descriptive: the nurse's omission and the absent passerby play the same counterfactual role, and only the nurse's violation of a duty makes us call hers a cause — so the concept our reports need is already duty-laden, and pretending otherwise is what makes our analyses inconsistent. She cited a couple of technical proposals along the way — I remember her describing Phil Dowe's quasi-causation, where an absence isn't a genuine cause but plays an equivalent counterfactual role, and something of David Lewis's about absences standing in for the concrete event that would have filled the gap — but I can't claim to have absorbed them. We ended with her conceding that some causal talk about omissions might be legitimate, and me conceding that our reports probably need a norm-loaded concept of cause regardless — and with five RCAs on my desk and no method for any of them.

What I want from you is this: take whatever you judge to be the strongest available position on causation by omission — the counterfactual view with its profligacy problem, norm-dependent solutions that make violation of a duty part of what counts as a cause, the quasi-causation line, the substitution of the hypothetical event that would have filled the gap, or some combination — and translate it into a procedure my RCA teams can actually run in a Tuesday-afternoon meeting. Please write it as a professional philosopher of causation who has also sat on hospital safety committees and knows that theory dies in that room unless it is ruthless about simplification. I'd like you to think carefully before committing: start with some private brainstorming on which underlying position can survive translation, and settle that before writing the procedure, because the procedure should be the consequence of that choice, not a compromise that preserves nothing from any of them. The deliverable, specifically:

1. An ordered set of questions the team asks about a candidate omission, in sequence, with concrete criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each — enough that two different teams would reach the same bucket.
2. Explicit outcomes at each step: causal chain, contributing factor, background condition, or out of scope, with the stop rules for each.
3. The points at which the procedure cannot settle the question and must hand it to a person — because it turns on a value judgment, such as how demanding the relevant duty was, or on luck in how the counterfactual came out — marked as such, not buried. I want the procedure honest about where judgment cannot be eliminated.
4. A statement of which distinctions from the philosophy you deliberately dropped in the simplification, and why the ones you kept are the ones that matter for our purposes.

Then run the procedure on three cases drawn from our recent caseload (details anonymized):

Case A. A patient died of progressive sepsis. A covering physician viewed a critically abnormal lab result in the chart but did not escalate, assuming the primary team owned follow-up; the primary team assumed coverage had passed to the covering physician.

Case B. A patient with a documented allergy received the allergenic drug. The pharmacy verification step that would have caught it was skipped by a pharmacist managing a staffing shortage, and the ordering physician had not checked the allergy list, as policy required.

Case C. An elderly patient fell out of bed overnight on a unit where the scheduled hourly rounding did not happen because the unit was running at 30% below normal staffing, a level the executive committee had accepted as a standing operating condition for that quarter.

Include at least one case where the procedure yields a result I will not like — I have a strong suspicion Case C is that one, since the consistent application of my own counterfactual reasoning puts a committee decision in the causal chain — and argue for why the procedure should stand anyway.

Finally, two requests about honesty. First, tell me what this procedure will systematically get wrong: where it will misfire, including the ways that writing an omitted act into the causal chain can cause teams to stop searching for systemic causes once they have found a cause. Second, throughout the response and in a closing note, indicate how confident you are in each major component of the procedure, and single out the single point you are least sure of.
