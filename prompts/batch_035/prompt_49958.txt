"Explain Jackson's knowledge argument against physicalism: state exactly what is supposed to follow from the case of Mary, the colour scientist raised in a black-and-white room, and why. Then discuss two prominent replies to the argument, saying for each whether it concedes that Mary learns anything at all when she leaves the room."

And here is the student's answer (transcribed as written):

"Jackson's argument, first presented in his 1982 paper, goes as follows. Mary is a brilliant scientist who has been confined since birth to a black-and-white room and studies the world through a black-and-white monitor. She learns every physical fact there is to know about colour vision: the physics of wavelengths, the neurophysiology of the visual cortex, everything. When she is finally released and sees a red tomato, she learns something new — what it is like to see red. If physicalism were true, she would already have known this, since she knew all the physical facts. So physicalism is false. Note that the argument also shows that qualia are epiphenomenal: since Mary's complete physical knowledge left out what it's like to see red, conscious experience must have no causal effects on the physical world, otherwise she'd have known about it.

The first reply is the ability hypothesis, associated with Daniel Dennett. On this view, Mary doesn't gain any new propositional knowledge when she leaves the room; she only gains new abilities — the ability to recognise, imagine, and remember red experiences. So the premise that she 'learns something new' is ambiguous, and once the ambiguity is cleared up the argument fails. The ability hypothesis is basically the same as the acquaintance hypothesis, which is the second reply: what Mary gains is direct acquaintance with an experience she already knew about in a descriptive, third-person way. Both replies concede she gains something, just not new facts.

Jackson himself later abandoned the argument and became a physicalist, which shows the argument is unsound — even its own author couldn't defend it against the replies above."
