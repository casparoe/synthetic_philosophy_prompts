I have a friend whose reactions to sad art we have been arguing about for years, and I have begun to suspect she is running a consistent rule that none of us has managed to state. Her verdicts so far: she weeps openly at tragic opera — we took her to one last spring and she had bought the recording within the week and was angling for a second visit; she insists a devastating novel is better on the reread, when she knows exactly what is coming and dread has been replaced by something she calls "savouring"; she thoroughly enjoyed a lavish historical weepie about a shipwreck from over a century ago, in which the disaster was real but the grieving family at the centre was invented; she refuses point-blank to watch documentaries about recent real tragedies, calling the very idea ghoulish; she walked out of a glossy award-season tearjerker last autumn in a fury, saying she had been the victim of "emotional pickpocketing" — even though she cried just as hard at the opera and loved every second of it; and she has no interest whatsoever in being frightened, avoiding horror films entirely, so any rule you propose has to cover that verdict too. Then there is the strangest entry in the record: the month her father died, she played one harrowing album on repeat for most of a fortnight, and now describes that fortnight as one of the most valuable things she did that year — and when we pressed her on whether she had actually enjoyed it, she said yes, plainly, and couldn't explain why.

The new case is as follows. A small opera company is staging a chamber opera about a colliery disaster from fourteen years ago — real event, real dead, a few of the bereaved still alive and listed in the programme as consultants. It is staged promenade-style: the audience walks through the workings while the drama unfolds around them, a soprano playing a widow takes hold of audience members' hands during her aria, and at the close the names of the real dead are read aloud. The notices have been rapturous, including from two critics whose judgement she trusts and quotes at us; she has read the reviews and describes herself as intrigued rather than repelled. A group of us are going in a fortnight, and she has been invited.

I want less a prediction than the reasoning around it. First, tell me whether she will enjoy it — on the night, and once she is home. Then tell me the rules she seems to be running: set out at least two that fit her record so far equally well, say what each predicts about this case, and be explicit about where they agree and diverge. Give your best guess with your reasons for preferring one rule to the others, and then name the further case — one we could actually arrange or wait for — that would best tell the rival rules apart, explaining why that case and not some other. Please answer in British English throughout.
