I'm the deputy manager of a domiciliary care service in a mid-sized town. Twice a year, our allocation panel — me, a nurse assessor, and a finance officer from the funding authority — reviews the visiting-support hours for each of our roughly 200 clients and adjusts them against a fixed budget. We get about fifteen minutes per client. The funding authority audits our decisions and expects a documented, consistent rationale for every one, which in practice means we score people against a needs-assessment form and let the numbers decide. I've read enough care ethics to think this is exactly backwards: the form measures what's countable, the countable becomes what counts, and the clients whose needs are least legible on a form — the isolated ones who mostly need someone to notice them — silently lose out to the ones with the most checkable deficits. But I can't just tell the panel to "be attentive." I need a procedure.

Please write one. Specifically, a decision procedure the panel can actually run in those fifteen minutes, grounded in the actual commitments of care ethics rather than in generic utilitarian triage. It should build in, as explicit steps or questions, the things the scoring form leaves out: attention to needs as the client and the people around them actually present them (not just as the assessment recorded them last time), some check on whether the care as currently delivered is being received as care at all — the client's own verdict that the visits are worthless carries information the deficit score doesn't — and some safeguard for the client least able to articulate anything at the meeting. It should say which steps produce a documented rationale the auditors will accept, and where it instead has to record, honestly, that the decision turned on a judgment call the panel is not going to be able to justify by numbers. Mark the points where the procedure must hand the decision back to a person because what's at stake is a relationship or a question of values, not a fact. Then run the procedure on three clients: (1) a widower who is physically independent but visited mainly for conversation, whose family wants his hours cut; (2) a woman with heavy physical care needs whose daughter does most of the work and is burning out, and who insists she needs nothing new; and (3) a client with early dementia whose stated preferences contradict what the neighbours report — include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will find hard to accept, and show it doing so. Finally, tell me plainly where this procedure will misfire: care ethicists have long argued that codified care stops being care, and I want to know whether my procedure refutes that worry or merely instantiates it, and which parts I should treat as permanently provisional.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
