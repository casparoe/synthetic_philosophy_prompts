In the UK and several other European jurisdictions, a woman who carries a pregnancy for others may be a surrogate only on an unpaid basis: she may be reimbursed for expenses and, at most, some lost earnings, the contract is unenforceable, and any outright payment beyond that risks the refusal of a parental order. Yet the same jurisdictions routinely allow fertility clinics to pay egg donors a fixed per-cycle 'compensation' — a sum that, whatever the label, functions as a price for time, discomfort, and medical risk, and that these systems regulate rather than prohibit. I have two questions about what this pattern presupposes. First, what implicit theory of the body, of commodification, and of exploitation must be in place for the two rules to sit side by side — is it something like the Titmuss thought that money degrades gift relationships, applied selectively to gestation but not to gametes; or a view that a surrogate would be selling a child whereas an egg donor is only selling tissue; or a worry that payment exploits the surrogate's economic vulnerability, which would underwrite the ban on payment but not the permission of the unpaid, medically risky arrangement? Second, a colleague of mine insists the whole asymmetry can be saved by distinguishing indemnification from remuneration: the law tolerates making a surrogate whole for her ordeal but never paying her for a service, and the egg donor's compensation is just the same indemnification. I suspect this moves the problem rather than solving it, but I want the objection pressed properly. Please reconstruct the implicit theory the expenses-only regime actually rests on, say whether that theory is internally consistent once egg-donor payments are taken into account or whether the practice is instead philosophically underdetermined — compatible with several theories none of which it requires — and tell me concretely what would have to change in either the surrogacy rule or the egg-donation rule if the presupposed theory were made consistent. Close your reply with a line beginning 'Verdict:', in one sentence, stating whether the asymmetry can be defended without abandoning one of the two rules.
