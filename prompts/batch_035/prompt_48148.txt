I should be grateful for an audit of an analogy that keeps cropping up in my discussions about hard choices.

A friend of mine maintains that comparing options that excel along different values is like comparing displacements in different directions on a map: travelling a mile due north and travelling a mile due east are both journeys of a mile, but there is simply no answer to the question of which journey is greater, since length along one axis and length along another do not add up to a single quantity. In the same way, she says, the prestige and income of a legal career and the creative fulfilment of a life spent writing are measured along different axes of value; since nothing in reason dictates a conversion rate between them, there is no fact of the matter about which life is better, the two options are exactly on a level, and it is no less rational to settle the matter by coin toss than by deliberation — indeed, she thinks, extended deliberation in such cases is a kind of theatre.

I have two further notions to fold in, which I have picked up from philosophers but do not fully understand. First, I am told that philosophers distinguish incommensurability from mere incomparability, and that two options can be related in a fourth way: neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal in value, but on a par, so that a comparison is possible even though no ranking results. Second, I have heard it said that in hard choices we do not merely discover reasons but can create them, by committing ourselves to one option rather than the other — in effect making reasons rather than finding them. I do not know what to make of either suggestion, or whether either of them undermines my friend's picture.

Could you begin with the correspondence table for her analogy — what in the choice situation plays the role of the axes, the displacements, the unit of length, and the absence of a common measure — and then set out where the mapping breaks down. I am particularly interested in whether the geometric analogy, once unpacked, actually supports her conclusion: after all, displacements in different directions do admit of comparison once a norm is fixed (a traveller who cares about distance as the crow flies can rank them perfectly well), and I suspect the analogy quietly imports the claim that there exists no supervalue under which the options could be ranked, rather than demonstrating it. I should also like to know which of the disanalogies matter for her intended use of the analogy and which are idle, whether the coin-toss conclusion survives once the genuine disanalogies are on the table, and whether either of the two philosophical notions I mentioned — parity, and the creation of reasons through commitment — gives a better account of what is going on in hard choices, or whether they are themselves just further metaphors dressed as doctrine. If one of them does improve the picture, please say what follows in practice for how a deliberator facing such a choice ought to proceed.

Please write your answer in British English.
