I write a column on science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm working on a piece about longtermism — the view that securing humanity's potential future matters more than our other priorities because that future could be astronomically large. A decision-theorist friend sent me an argument meant to show that the simulation argument takes the air out of this, and I can't tell whether it works or whether it proves too much.

(1) Fanaticism about the far future rests on expected-value arithmetic: a small probability of an astronomical payoff can outweigh any near-term good. (2) The simulation argument's trilemma says that either civilizations like ours almost never reach technological maturity, or almost none of those that do run ancestor simulations, or we are almost certainly inside one; a longtermist of all people should resist the first two options, which leaves her with non-negligible credence in the third. (3) If we are simulated, the far future that longtermist projects aim to secure is not the base world's future — it exists at the simulators' pleasure and could be switched off at any moment, and nothing we do touches the real world's trajectory at all. (4) So the expected value of any far-future project must be scaled down by the probability that we are simulated, and once scaled down, the astronomical payoffs no longer swamp near-term goods: fanaticism is dampened.

The problem is that the same scaling seems to apply to every long-horizon project, not just fanatical ones — climate policy, institution-building, basic research whose payoff is centuries out. My friend says he accepts this and finds it a feature rather than a bug: a general discount on long-horizon reasoning, a new kind of humility. I think something is wrong earlier — either in step (3)'s slide from our not affecting the simulators' world to our not affecting anything that matters, or in step (4)'s arithmetic — but I can't name it, and I want to be fair to him before I file. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs; and if he is right that the argument is sound all the way through, tell me what the defender of long-horizon projects should then say. Please keep your answer to 400 words or fewer — my editor counts.
