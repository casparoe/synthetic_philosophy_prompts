I'm a fourth-year physics undergraduate taking an elective in philosophy of science, and the unit on scientific realism and theory choice has put string theory — and, with it, the string landscape and anthropic multiverse reasoning — on the table. Set the multiverse aside for my purposes; my question is about the confirmation structure, not the cosmology. On the philosophy side we have been discussing Richard Dawid's program of non-empirical confirmation: the claim that a theory with no distinctive novel predictions, string theory being the paradigm, can nonetheless gain credence from observations that are not observations of the theory's own predictions. The case rests on three arguments: the no-alternatives argument, that the community's prolonged failure to construct a viable alternative raises the probability that the theory is at least on the right track; the argument from unexpected explanatory interconnections, that the theory turned out to be far more internally coherent, and to connect to far more of mathematics and physics, than anyone had a right to expect; and the meta-inductive argument, that research programs previously assessed in this way have historically panned out.

My problem is that this is being taught to me in prose, and the prose keeps sliding between claims that, in the vocabulary I actually compute in, are different claims. I want you to restate the case in that vocabulary. Concretely: I know Bayesian inference at the level of doing it — model comparison via marginal likelihoods, the Occam factor as the ratio of posterior to prior volume, hierarchical models in which the reliability of a process is itself a parameter estimated from past performance, simulation-based and likelihood-free inference, the look-elsewhere effect, overfitting and effective model dimension. Assume I can follow anything at that level of sophistication and do not simplify on my behalf.

Translate each of the three arguments into this vocabulary, one by one, as faithfully as you can. My expectation is that the no-alternatives argument will come out as something like: the community's search is a stochastic process over hypothesis space, its failure to turn up alternatives is data about where prior mass actually sits, and that data shifts the posterior over "on the right track." And that the interconnections argument will come out as something like the ordinary Bayesian value of one parameter set fitting many datasets, and the meta-inductive argument as a hierarchical prior over the community's track record. But I do not want you to accept my framings — restate each argument as it actually goes, and tell me where my gloss is wrong.

Then give me the residue report, argument by argument: which parts translate cleanly, which parts translate only by distortion — where, to make it fit, you had to import a statistical assumption that is quietly doing the philosophical work — and which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all. In the no-alternatives case, attend especially to whether "the search would have found an alternative if one existed" can be made into anything like a likelihood statement, given that the space of possible theories is not enumerable and the search procedure was neither random nor well-characterized. In the interconnections case, attend to whether "more coherent than expected" can be made precise without an independent model of what should have been expected. And then say what you take the residue to mean: a defect of the statistical vocabulary, such that there is something real about theory confirmation my toolkit cannot see, or a sign that the original claims were less precise than they looked — in particular, whether "confirmation without empirical support" collapses, once translated, into ordinary Bayesian confirmation by evidence that simply isn't experimental, in which case the controversy is smaller than it appears.

Do not leave me with a menu of readings of the residue. I would rather have a definite position I can argue with in seminar than a balanced survey.
