At my school's philosophy club we've been arguing about procrastination, of all things, and someone quoted the line people attribute to Socrates, that no one does wrong willingly — meaning, in this context, that anyone who genuinely knows what the good thing to do is will do it, so that all bad action comes down to ignorance. She used it against me when I admitted that I know perfectly well, while spending three hours on my phone the night before a French exam, that studying was better for me; she said that if I really knew it, I would have acted on it, so my "knowledge" was somehow not the real thing. I have also been reading, for a psychology elective, about hyperbolic discounting — the finding that we tend to prefer a smaller reward that is available immediately over a larger reward that is further off, in a way that flips depending on how far away both options are — and I suspect that this somehow complicates her use of the slogan, but I can't articulate how. I'd like you to lay out, as a list I can bring back to club, the different precise claims the sentence could be expressing: for instance, a strict psychological claim that genuine knowledge of the good necessitates acting on it, a definitional claim that whatever leads to bad action thereby fails to count as knowledge (which would make the slogan impossible to test), a weaker claim about the moment of action versus standing knowledge, and anything else you can think of, including readings that turn on what "willingly" is doing. For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, whether it would actually support her move of denying that I knew studying was better, and what it would say about the case of someone who discount hyperbolically — someone whose preference flips as the smaller, sooner reward gets close, even though their judgment about which option is better stays put. Finally, say which reading you think people who repeat the slogan usually intend, and whether my procrastination case refutes that reading or merely refutes the stronger ones.
