I'm a journalist who has spent roughly two decades covering science and ideas, first on daily news and now in long-form narrative nonfiction. I've absorbed Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity — an abstract object, real in roughly the way a center of gravity is real, a fictional character the brain writes and keeps on writing — mostly through interviews and conference coverage, not through careful study of the primary literature. So I hold the position loosely and want to understand it better through the only technical vocabulary I genuinely command, which is the vocabulary of my trade.

Restate Dennett's position in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. I mean the working concepts of narrative journalism: the throughline of a serial feature, the nut graf, house style, editorial voice, sourcing and the difference between what a source says and what the story asserts, composite characters and the ethics of compressing several people into one, the way a beat reporter builds up a figure across years of coverage, the fact that no single article contains the character but the corpus does, and the fact that readers experience that character as a real person even while the desk knows how much curation went into it. I'd especially like to see whether the "center of gravity" side of the metaphor — the claim that the self is an abstractum with genuine explanatory power rather than a mere fiction we can discard — has an analogue in how we talk about a long-running character in a publication's pages, or whether that analogue distorts the claim.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Dennett's position translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which parts survive only because you quietly bent them, and which parts have no expression at all in the newsroom's terms? Don't accept my framing of the residue if you think it's off; correct it.
