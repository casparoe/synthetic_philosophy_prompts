Quick background on me: I'm a retired geophysicist, spent most of my career bouncing seismic waves around sedimentary basins, and the last few years I've gotten really into philosophy of mind. I help out as an unpaid grader for a second-year course at the local college (the instructor knows I'm not a philosopher but wanted extra hands). Casual level course, mostly non-philosophy majors, but the instructor likes to push them a bit.

The homework question the students got was this:

"Explain Dennett's idea that the self is a 'center of narrative gravity.' What is the analogy with physics supposed to show about what kind of thing the self is, and does the analogy hold up?"

One student submitted the following answer, and I'd like a second opinion before I put a grade on it. Please give me (a) a grade out of 20, and (b) point-by-point comments saying what in the answer deserves credit and what should cost marks, so I can write honest feedback instead of just a number.

The student's answer, verbatim:

"Dennett says the self is a posit, like a center of gravity in physics. Nobody thinks there's a little dot inside a boulder where the gravity lives — the center of gravity is something we calculate because it makes the physics tractable. Similarly, when we interpret a person, we posit a single point to which we attribute beliefs, desires, and a perspective, because that makes their behavior intelligible to us. This goes with his Multiple Drafts picture of the brain: there's no one place where everything comes together, so the self can't be an actual thing located somewhere in there. Since the self is only a posit, Dennett's real position is that the self does not exist at all — it's an illusion, the same way the center of gravity is basically a fiction physicists tell themselves. The brain spins a narrative and casts the self as the main character of that story, so the self is a character in the narrative rather than its author, a picture Dennett takes over from MacIntyre's account of the narrative self. A possible objection is that people obviously do have beliefs and feel pain, but Dennett can just reply that those are attributions too, made from the same interpretive stance. Overall the analogy works: it lets us keep talking about persons in everyday life while denying we have any inner essence."

To be honest, some of this reads as solid to me and some of it I can't quite place — I'm not sure whether every move in there is really Dennett or whether a couple of things are subtly off, and I don't trust myself to tell which is which. So I want the point-by-point breakdown, not a general impression. Theres no rubric, the instructor just said to grade out of 20 with justification.
