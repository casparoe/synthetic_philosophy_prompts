Been down another LessWrong rabbithole about heap-ness and it got me thinking about vagueness again. You have the usual lineup: epistemicism (there's a sharp cutoff in the number of grains, we just can't know it), supervaluationism (the world has no cutoff but 'definitely a heap' is still meaningful), fuzzy degrees of heap-ness, etc. My housemate keeps pushing this view she calls "cognitive blur": there IS a determinate grain count where heap-hood starts, but our concept of a heap is implemented by a noisy neural classifier whose decision boundary jitters, so all the vagueness lives in our heads as measurement noise, not in the world or in language itself. To me it smells like epistemicism with extra steps, but she insists it's closer to supervaluationism since nothing in the world is indeterminate, only our access to it. Compare her view seriously to both standard theories: is cognitive blur a genuinely distinct third option, or does it collapse into one of them once you push on it? Also say what would actually settle this — e.g., would a hypothetical perfect grain-counting machine that tells you exactly where your own concept draws the line count as eliminating vagueness or just exposing it?
