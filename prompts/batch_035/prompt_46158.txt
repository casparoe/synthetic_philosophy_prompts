You are acting as a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of causation, with particular expertise in cases of causation by omission — situations in which an absence or a failure to act appears to bring about an outcome. Please think carefully and systematically: begin by brainstorming the candidate general principles that could underlie the verdicts below, test each one against every verdict, and only then commit to predictions. Do not consult or recommend outside sources; everything you need is contained in the cases themselves.

My friend and I have a long-running argument about what counts as a cause when nothing happens. Over the years she has issued verdicts on a series of cases, and I have kept a record:

1. The contracted gardener. A gardener, hired and paid to water a client's roses while the client is on holiday, simply does not water them; the roses die. Her verdict: the gardener caused the death of the roses.

2. The tourist. A tourist who strolls past those same roses every day of the holiday never waters them, never gives them a thought, and has no idea they are being neglected; the roses die. Her verdict: the tourist did not cause the death of the roses.

3. The doctor. A doctor on duty fails to administer the standard antidote to a poisoned patient, who dies. Her verdict: the doctor caused the death of the patient.

4. The wildflower. A wildflower on an untended hillside in an arid region, where summers are typically rainless and this one was no exception, dies for lack of water. Her verdict: the absence of rain did not cause the death of the flower, and neither did anything anyone failed to do.

5. The defective sprinkler. A building's automatic sprinkler system, correctly installed and maintained, fails to activate one afternoon because of a manufacturing defect that no one could have detected; the lobby plants die. Her verdict: the sprinkler's failure to activate caused the death of the plants.

At our next session, two new cases will come up:

Case A — the neighbor. A next-door neighbor, herself an avid gardener, notices the client's roses wilting during the holiday. She assumes the client is simply neglecting her own roses, as the client often does. Watering them would cost her nothing; she is home all week; she has no agreement, duty, or expectation to do so. She does not water them, and the roses die.

Case B — the abnormal season. In a different region, one where summer rains have arrived reliably every year for as long as anyone remembers, a single summer passes without rain. The rain is a matter of regularity only — no one owes it, no system produces it, no one is responsible for it. The crops die.

Your tasks, in order:

First, articulate the candidate rules or general principles under which my friend's five recorded verdicts are all correct. There should be more than one; do not stop at the first principle that fits. For each candidate, verify it against all five verdicts and note where it is strained.

Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about Case A and Case B. I expect the candidates to diverge on at least one of them; if two candidates agree on both, say so explicitly rather than manufacturing disagreement.

Third, give your best guess as to her actual verdicts on Case A and Case B, with your reasons for preferring that prediction over the alternatives. If her track record genuinely underdetermines a verdict, say so, and explain what each possible answer would reveal about the rule she is implicitly running.

Fourth, design the further case — a single hypothetical, described as concretely as the ones above — that would best discriminate between the candidate rules you have identified, and explain exactly which pair of rules it separates and why.

Throughout, keep in mind the classic pressure on omission cases: for any outcome, there are countless omissions (strangers, distant mechanisms, absent events) on which the outcome counterfactually depends, yet she denies causation for most of them. Your account of her rule should explain the pattern of inclusion and exclusion, not merely restate it.
