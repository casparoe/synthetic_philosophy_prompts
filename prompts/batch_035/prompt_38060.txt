I'm a software engineer — distributed systems, about twelve years in industry, currently at a company building developer tools on top of large ML models — with no formal philosophy training. My exposure to AI safety is reading papers' abstracts, blog debates, and long arguments with colleagues, not the academic literature. I've been asked to give an internal tech talk on how my team should think about safety targets for ML-based features, and before I write it up I want someone to audit the bundle of positions I've drifted into, because I suspect it doesn't hang together but I can't diagnose where.

Here are the positions, as I'd state them in the talk:

1. The right definition of "safe" for a capable ML system is worst-case over inputs, not average-case over the deployment distribution: a system is safe only if no input in a specified class triggers catastrophic behavior, no matter how improbable that input is under normal operation. Safety arguments that lean on "the deployment distribution resembles the training distribution" don't count, because distribution shift is the norm and adversaries get to choose their inputs. The adversarial-examples literature convinced me of this years before I thought about AI risk per se.

2. At the same time, I'm a realist about what's achievable: nobody is going to formally verify a hundred-billion-parameter network, so the operative safety bar in practice will be empirical adversarial evaluation — red teams plus automated input search — and we ship when strong attackers with a realistic budget can't find catastrophic failures after extensive, well-funded effort.

3. Within any safety budget, resources should be allocated by expected harm reduction: estimate the probability of each failure mode, multiply by severity, fund mitigations in descending order of cost-effectiveness. Under scarcity, this seems like the only sane allocation rule, and I'd defend it against anyone proposing a non-probabilistic rule.

4. However, I reject Pascal's-mugging-style reasoning: when a scenario's probability is small enough, or its probability estimate is essentially made up rather than grounded in anything, the size of the claimed downside stops mattering. There is some point at which "but the stakes are astronomical" is no longer a reason to divert budget, and I think it's rational to act on that cutoff even though I can't articulate a clean threshold or justify why a tiny probability of infinite harm shouldn't dominate everything.

5. I believe a sufficiently capable optimizer will find any vulnerability that actually exists — not merely the ones a red team happens to find — and will act on it if adversarially incentivized. Security is therefore a property of not having the hole at all, not of the attacker failing to search for it.

I have a general unease that these five don't sit together — when I rehearse the talk in my head, some slide transitions feel like I'm quietly changing the subject — but I genuinely don't know which pair is the problem. For what it's worth, one colleague claims position 4 is flatly inconsistent with caring about AI risk at all, and I'd like to know whether he's right rather than assume either way.

Please audit the set. Specifically: identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad; distinguish the real tensions from ones that only look like tensions and explain why those dissolve; and for each real tension, say what has to give and what it costs me to give it up (in terms of what else in the set or in my practice it takes with it). Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the resulting package is something I could honestly present in one talk or whether I need to openly present it as a two-tier view with the seam showing.
