hey, so I teach an upper-level seminar — "Minds at the Margins," animal ethics meets formal epistemology, mostly philosophy seniors and a few cog sci grad students — and I need you to write the final exam essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme for it. You're a professional philosopher who works on both animal cognition and decision theory, so please think carefully about the technical bits before writing — maybe start with a quick brainstorm of what the cruxes actually are, then draft. Don't just pattern-match to a stock "expected value = probability times stakes" explanation, that's exactly what I'm trying to avoid.

The topic is the scale argument for insect welfare: roughly, even if each individual insect has only a tiny probability of being sentient, there are so many of them (something like 10^18 alive at any moment, more if you count annual births across short-lived invertebrates) that the expected amount of morally relevant experience at stake swamps everything else in our decision-making about what to care about. A student who has merely memorized the standard pitch can write two fluent paragraphs on that. I want a question that can't be answered that way.

Here's what I want the question to force out. The scale argument usually gets run as one move, but it actually smuggles in at least three separate commitments, and students should have to take them apart: (i) risk neutrality about aggregate welfare — why maximize expected total rather than being risk-averse or treating numbers as having diminishing moral returns; (ii) what the per-insect probability even is — does one confident "insects aren't sentient" view beat a spread of credences across species, and note that expected value is a product of two uncertain numbers (probability of sentience times how intense the experience is if there), so the uncertainties compound rather than one cleanly offsetting the other; and (iii) the ladder problem — the same logic that says "care about insects because there are so many" says your priorities should flip again whenever you step down to even more numerous creatures, so you either find a principled stopping point or accept a world where nematodes dominate ethics. Students who just nod along to (i)–(iii) without tension aren't really doing the work.

The twist I've been using in seminar — I want it baked into the question, and it's mine, so they can't look it up — I call it the dimmer switch vs. trapdoor problem. Either sentience is a threshold property (there's a fact of the matter whether the light is on: an insect is in or out, and moral status follows across the threshold like falling through a trapdoor), or it's graded/marginal (any nonzero degree matters and the only question is how much). If it's a threshold, the per-insect probability does real work and expected value is arguably the right frame. If it's graded, probability talk quietly slides into "expected degree" talk — you're multiplying a probability by an intensity you've already conceded is nonzero — and some of my colleagues think that makes the "low probability" framing of the scale argument rhetorically inflated. The question should make students say whether the scale argument comes out stronger, weaker, or just *different* under each reading, and defend it.

One more thing for them to chew on: the asymmetry move. Even granting low probability, false negatives (ignoring suffering that turns out to be real) plausibly matter more than false positives (spending resources on little automata). So maybe the scale argument doesn't need expected value at all — it needs a precautionary structure. Students should have to say whether that's a genuine rescue of the argument or a covert reintroduction of the same numbers under another name. That's honestly my favorite part of the question so please make the model answer actually take a stance there instead of fence-sitting.

Constraints: question answerable in 90 minutes; model answer around 700 words; marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (total 20), each with a one-line note on how students typically blow that particular point. Important: no citations, no name-dropping, no reading list — students may bring in their own examples but the marking scheme must not require any particular author or paper, and neither should the model answer. Also add a short "what not to do" paragraph I can print under the question itself, aimed at the recitation trap. Thanks!
