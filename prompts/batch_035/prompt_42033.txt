You are a formal epistemologist with a working command of Bayesian decision theory, and I am asking you for a referee's verdict rather than a survey. Think the argument below through step by step before committing; then take a definite position and defend it. Do not close by laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.

At a workshop dinner, an economist walked me through the following argument, and I cannot find the seam in it.

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents begin with the same prior, change their minds only by conditioning on what they observe, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition become common knowledge between them, then those posteriors are equal. Bayesian agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree.

(2) When two people argue a question through to the end — each stating not just a conclusion but how confident they are, each taking in the other's answer, both aware that the discussion is over — their final credences are common knowledge between them. My friend added that the exchange itself is nothing but iterated announcements of credence, and that under these assumptions such an exchange must converge.

(3) Careful, honest people thinking about a factual question are Bayesian in the relevant sense: their confidence is a conditional probability, and it moves only by conditioning on evidence.

(4) Careful, honest people with a shared culture and education have a common prior in the relevant sense: where they differ, it is because they have seen different evidence, not because they started from different places.

(5) Take two such people — careful, honest, similarly raised — who fully discuss a factual question and find they still disagree. By (2) their final credences are common knowledge; by (3) and (4) the remaining conditions of the theorem are met; so by (1) their credences are equal. The disagreement they report is therefore impossible, and since the discussion did happen, the fault must lie in the people: one of them was not, after all, being careful or honest.

(6) So wherever disagreement survives full discussion, someone is being sloppy or insincere. The colleague who has considered my research program doomed for fifteen years; the physicists who have gone on disagreeing about the interpretation of quantum mechanics across a century of journals; the two statisticians who never did converge on how the data should be modeled — in each case, my friend concluded, the polite formula that we can agree to differ is a fiction covering the fact that one of us is defective.

I cannot accept that upshot. But every step looked fine to me, and (1) is a theorem, so I am reluctant to touch it. Tell me which premise to give up. Explain why it looked fine — in particular, spell out what the relevant sense in (3) and (4) would have to amount to for the theorem to bite, and whether the way it looked fine is the way it needs to be true. Then price the alternatives: what the argument loses without the premise you reject, and what becomes of my friend's conclusion once it goes. If you think the argument is sound as it stands and the conclusion must simply be swallowed, say so and defend that. Otherwise commit to one step and price the rest. I want a diagnosis with a verdict, not a menu.
