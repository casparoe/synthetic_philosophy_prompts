I am a graduate teaching assistant designing a review-session handout for an introductory ethics course that has just finished a unit on utilitarianism and its classic objections. Please write a decision tree, formatted as indented branch nodes and leaves, that sorts a student's response to the utility monster problem into a named position. The entry point of the tree should be the following stipulation rather than anything from the literature, so that students are answering questions about a case rather than about labels: suppose we discover a being for which diverting essentially all of society's resources toward it produces more total welfare than any alternative distribution — welfare in the utilitarian's broad sense, whatever the student takes that to be.

To keep the tree from being answerable by reciting a slogan, build it from three variant monsters, each of which must appear at some node because positions that coexist peacefully on the ordinary monster come apart on them: (1) the intensity monster, which gains enormous welfare per unit of resource where humans gain little — huge experiential magnitudes concentrated in a single mind; (2) the duration monster, which has ordinary human intensity of pleasure but a lifespan trillions of times ours, so that its lifetime total swamps ours; and (3) the swarm monster, a colony of astronomically many very simple minds, each with barely positive welfare, whose aggregate exceeds ours.

Branch nodes should be questions a student could answer without knowing which position they lead to — for example, whether the objection disappears if the welfare gains are spread across many minds rather than one; whether there is a ceiling on how good a single life can be; whether the culprit is aggregation across persons as such or only aggregation on this scale; whether the right response is to say more about what is being aggregated or to say something about how aggregation interacts with distribution. The tree should end in at least the following leaves: the bullet-biting total utilitarian; the average-utilitarian response; the objection that diminishing marginal utility, taken seriously, already dissolves the case; the idealization objection, on which the case is underspecified or unphysical and shows nothing about any utilitarianism worth holding; the lexical-threshold or limit-based view; and a distributive-constraints leaf covering separateness-of-persons and sacrifice-limiting principles. Where two of these leaves cannot genuinely be separated by any question you can ask about the three monsters, say so rather than inventing a question that only pretends to split them.

Finally, for each combination of answers that your questions permit but that no well-known position occupies, note it in one sentence and briefly diagnose whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

At the end, add a short epistemic note: say how confident you are in the tree's construction overall, and single out the one leaf assignment or branch question you are least sure of, explaining why.
