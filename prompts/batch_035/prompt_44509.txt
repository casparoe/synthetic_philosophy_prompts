I'm in a reading group on experimental philosophy, and after the last session two members, Priya and Tom, spent a good hour arguing about the Knobe effect, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Maybe you can referee. (Please answer in British English — I get distracted by American spellings.)

The case, in case you need it: a chairman is told a new programme will increase profits and also harm the environment, says he doesn't care at all about the environment and just wants profits, starts the programme anyway, and most people say he harmed the environment intentionally. Swap "help" for "harm" and most people say he did NOT help intentionally. That asymmetry was the subject of the fight.

(1) Priya's opening move: the effect shows that the ordinary concept of intentional action is normative at its core — part of what it is for an act to be intentional is that it reflects badly on the agent. So philosphers who give purely descriptive analyses of intention (knowledge, plus aim or control) are analysing something other than the concept people actually use.

(2) Tom said that's way too fast. All the studies show is that blame contaminates people's answers. Asked "did he do it intentionally" about a bad outcome, respondents hear a question about culpability and answer that instead. On his story the concept stays clean and the folk are just answering the wrong question, which is noise, not a discovery about the concept.

(3) Priya came back with the follow-up study about office pens, where whether the agent was permitted to take the pens flips whether people say the side effect (a receptionist left with no pens) was brought about intentionally. A rule about pens isn't exactly a moral outrage, so what's driving the judgements can't just be badness leaking in — it looks like norm violation as such is doing the work, which fits a normative concept far better than a contamination story.

(4) Tom had a fallback: the "deep self" sort of explanation. People judge an outcome intentional when it flows from the agent's stable attitudes and values. The harm-case chairman's indifference to the enviroment is part of who he is, so the harm expresses him and gets called intentional; the help doesn't express him, so it doesn't. That predicts the asymmetry with a purely descriptive mechanism — you don't need the concept of intention itself to be normative.

(5) Priya said that smuggles the normativity in anyway, because deciding which attitudes count as someone's "real self" is itself partly an evaluative judgement — we decide which attitudes are the genuine ones partly in light of whether they're contemptible or admirable — so the descriptive story collapses back into a normative one.

(6) Tom's last word: even if the folk concept is normative, that's bad news for Priya, not good news. If whether an act counts as intentional depends on how blameworthy the agent is, there's no independent fact for the concept to track, and appeals to what the folk say can't settle anything. So the effect ends up being an argument against the whole method of appealing to intuitions, not for the normativity of intention.
