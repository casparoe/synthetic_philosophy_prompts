I am a program officer at a private health foundation, and I need a reusable decision procedure for a recurring problem that our current practice handles badly: allocating a fixed grant budget among proposals when our decision-makers disagree not merely about facts but about the fundamental standards by which grants should be ranked.

The setting is as follows. Each quarter, the foundation's five trustees and I rank eight to twelve proposals and allocate roughly ten million dollars. The trustees' first-order moral views diverge sharply. One is a committed consequentialist who cares only about aggregate health benefit; one is a strict deontologist who regards certain means as absolutely forbidden regardless of payoff; one holds a contractualist view on which a policy is wrong if any affected individual could reasonably reject the principle licensing it; one is a prioritarian who weights benefits to the worse-off; and one holds a religious view with absolute prohibitions that the rest of us consider mistaken but that we have no basis for excluding from the deliberation. Our current method is a weighted vote, which everyone privately suspects is arbitrary: it treats the disagreement as if it were about shared values with different intensity, when it is really about the correct standard itself. I have encountered the philosophical literature on moral uncertainty — in particular the proposal, associated with William MacAskill and Toby Ord, that a rational agent facing several moral theories should maximize expected choice-worthiness, weighting each theory by the agent's credence in it; and the alternative, suggested by Nick Bostrom, of modeling the theories as parties in a parliament that bargains over the budget. But I have also gathered that the expected-choiceworthiness approach runs into a serious technical difficulty: it requires comparing how much better one option is than another *according to each theory*, on a common scale, and different ways of putting theories' evaluations on a common scale — for instance, normalizing by the variance of each theory's ratings — can flip the final ranking depending on choices of scale that look arbitrary. That difficulty, plus the fact that some of our trustees hold views with lexical structure (an absolute constraint that no amount of benefit can override), is why I am asking for a procedure rather than a formula.

What I need from you, concretely:

1. A step-by-step procedure the committee can run in a two-hour quarterly meeting, using no mathematics beyond arithmetic, and producing a ranked allocation. It should specify, in order: how each trustee's credences in the moral theories are elicited and recorded; how each proposal is scored under each trustee's operative view; how those scores are made comparable across views; and how absolute prohibitions are handled at the scoring stage rather than smuggled in later.

2. An honest treatment of the comparability problem. Where the choice of common scale changes the outcome, the procedure should say so explicitly and fall back on a bargaining rule of the parliamentary type, with the bargaining weights and the fallback rule fixed in advance. The procedure should not pretend to have derived from philosophy an answer that actually depends on an arbitrary normalization choice.

3. A statement of which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification — at minimum, the difference between disagreement about facts (which evidence can resolve) and disagreement about standards (which it cannot), and the difference between a view held with lexical priority and one held with ordinal rankings — and which distinctions can safely be dropped for our purposes.

4. Worked applications to three realistic proposals, at least one of which must yield a conclusion I or the committee will dislike: (a) a mass deworming program producing small benefits to very many children, strongly favored by the consequentialist and prioritarian rankings but generating large cross-theory differences in *magnitude*; (b) funding a lobbying campaign that would plausibly improve factory-farm animal welfare but works through political channels that the deontological and religious trustees consider corrupting, and whose benefits flow to beings the contractualist trustee is not sure count at all; and (c) a very expensive treatment for a rare fatal childhood disease, which every theory favors in direction but which ranks very differently across theories in magnitude, so that the comparability choice plausibly decides whether it outranks the deworming program.

5. A clear marking of the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the decision to human judgment, and a candid statement of what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it genuinely resolves our disagreement or merely launders it, and whether a committee in our position should prefer a transparent laundering of disagreement to the opaque kind our weighted vote currently performs. Please do not soften the conclusion that the procedure may, in the end, be mostly a device for making our disagreement visible and livable rather than resolving it.
