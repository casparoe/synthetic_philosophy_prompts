In a seminar on campaign finance, a classmate shut down my argument for capping individual contributions to candidates with the one-liner "money is speech" — as though that settled that the cap is a form of censorship. I'm confident the sentence expresses several distinct claims in different mouths, only some of which could support his conclusion, but I can't disentangle them myself. Please lay out, as a structured menu I can bring to the next seminar, the different precise theses "money is speech" could be making. Distinguish at least the following candidates: that spending money is a necessary means to effective speech (so limits on spending burden speech only instrumentally); that expenditures are themselves expressive acts, so that the act of donating communicates endorsement or association; that restrictions on spending are ipso facto restrictions on speech, whatever the channel; and that money conveys content in its own right, the way a black armband does. For each reading, state what kind of "is" it involves (identity, constitution, necessary means, mere enabling condition), whose speech is at issue (the donor's, the candidate's, or the public's access to an undominated debate), whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or plainly false, and how it fares against a natural test case: if limits on spending were not in any sense limits on speech, the state could cap a newspaper's printing budget — a consequence no one on either side of the debate accepts. Then identify which reading my classmate actually needed in order to infer that the contribution cap is censorship, whether that reading is the defensible one or a weaker one gerrymandered to his conclusion, and how the strongest version of his argument would go — I want to answer the position at its best, not the slogan. Please do not quote case law or supply citations; describe the lines of reasoning in your own words, and I will add references myself.
