I run a small building-supply company and I've never studied philosophy, but I read a fair amount of popular economics — I'm comfortable with things like transaction costs, the double coincidence of wants problem in barter, money's classic three functions (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value), the idea of a numeraire good, velocity, and the difference between lending for production and lending for consumption. A while back someone told me that Aristotle had a theory of money and fair exchange, and after poking around I gathered the outlines: he seems to have thought money emerged naturally out of barter once people started importing what they lacked and exporting their surplus, with a conventionally agreed commodity stepping in as a measure; in the Ethics he apparently tries to explain when an exchange between, say, a builder and a shoemaker is fair or proportionate, and says something about demand being what makes totally different goods commensurable; and in the Politics he draws a line between managing wealth for the sake of living well versus accumulating it as an end in itself, treating the second as unnatural, with money-lending at interest singled out as the worst case because it makes money breed money.

What I'd like you to do is restate his whole position in the vocabulary I actually use. So: translate his account of money's origin into the story I know about barter frictions and the double coincidence of wants — does his version even have that problem in view? Translate the fair-exchange discussion into terms of relative prices and a numeraire — when he says demand makes a house and shoes commensurable, is that an early version of subjective value theory, a just-price idea, or something that doesn't fit either? Translate the natural/unnatural wealth-getting distinction into something like the difference between finance serving real exchange and finance as an end in itself — or into nothing, if nothing fits. And translate his objection to interest into whatever modern vocabulary you can, say, the distinction between real and nominal returns, or the time-value of money, or none of those.

Then I want the honest residue report, because that's the part I actually care about. Which parts of Aristotle map cleanly onto modern economics, which parts only fit if you bend them or smuggle in assumptions, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? In particular: his exchange-fairness discussion seems to be making a normative claim — that justice demands a certain proportion between the goods — and I can't see where modern price theory has room for that; is that because my vocabulary is a value-free description where he was making a claim about how people ought to deal, or because his claim was muddled even on its own terms? Same question for the natural/unnatural line and the interest objection: is there something real there that a purely functional account of money can't express, or is it just a prejudice of his time dressed up as analysis? I'd rather have an answer that tells me where the translation breaks than one that makes him sound like a proto-economist.
