I'm reading a popular book on great paradoxes of logic and science, and its chapter on Hempel's ravens has left me unsure what to believe. It summarizes the paradox roughly as follows: by Nicod's criterion, a universal generalization like "all ravens are black" is confirmed by each of its positive instances, i.e., by each observed raven that turns out to be black. But that hypothesis is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens," and by the equivalence condition, logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same evidence. Hence a white shoe, being a non-black non-raven, confirms that all ravens are black. The book then says Hempel bit the bullet and insisted that a white shoe confirms the raven hypothesis just as strongly as a black raven does, and that he eventually conceded this result was fatal to his whole framework of confirmation by instances. It closes by presenting "the Bayesian solution," attributed to I. J. Good, according to which non-black things so vastly outnumber ravens that a shoe confirms the hypothesis only by a negligible amount, in effect not at all — which is meant to explain why nobody has regarded the paradox as a serious threat to induction for decades.

I'm taking an introductory philosophy of science course, and I have two worries about fairness to Hempel. First, the "just as strongly" claim: I can see that the equivalence condition pushes you toward saying a shoe confirms the hypothesis at all, but it's not obvious to me that equivalence of hypotheses forces any particular comparison of degrees of confirmation, especially once we're allowed to know things like how many non-black things there are versus how many ravens. Second, the chapter's framing of Hempel's own attitude as a grudging capitulation doesn't match my vague memory that Hempel treated the result as one his theory should simply accept and explain, with the appearance of absurdity coming from somewhere else. And I may be misremembering Good's point too — I half recall that the interesting thing in his treatment was that whether a non-raven confirms the raven hypothesis depends on background knowledge, not merely on the sheer count of non-black objects, and that some backgrounds even make the shoe undermine the hypothesis.

So please audit the book's paragraph for me. Go claim by claim: which parts would a confirmation theorist accept as stated, which are subtly off (and in what direction — overstatement, conflation, technical term stretched to its everyday sense), and which are outright wrong about Hempel's or Good's positions? In particular, tell me whether the "just as strongly" step is something Hempel was committed to or a conflation of two distinct claims his theory keeps apart, and whether the book's use of the phrase "the Bayesian solution" papers over real disagreement among Bayesians about what is being solved. Finally, the book draws a moral I haven't mentioned yet: that the ravens case shows any observation, with enough logical ingenuity, can be made to confirm any hypothesis, so that the whole idea of evidence supporting a theory is in trouble. Does that conclusion follow from the parts of the summary that survive your corrections, or is it trading on the very missteps you've identified?
