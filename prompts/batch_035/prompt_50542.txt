I am a novelist, and I am asking you to act as a professional philosopher who works at the boundary of ethics, economics, and public policy. What I want is not a survey or a bibliography — no references, no reading lists — but a sustained reconstruction of the theory that a particular institutional practice quietly presupposes. Please think carefully and in stages, and begin with some brainstorming before committing to a view.

Here is how the practice came to my attention. For a novel I am writing, whose central character is an economist who works on regulatory impact analysis, I spent months reading the actual documents by which governments decide how much to spend on climate policy. The machinery runs roughly as follows. A proposed regulation's benefits are estimated as the "present value" of all of its future effects, and future effects are converted into present value by dividing them by a number that grows with every year of distance. At a discount rate of three per cent a year, a harm befalling someone eighty years from now — a drowned town, a heat death, a ruined harvest — enters the ledger at roughly a tenth of the weight of the same harm today; at five per cent, roughly a fiftieth. Past a certain horizon, whole categories of damage become smaller than a rounding error. The number that governs all of this is the social discount rate, and it is not a technicality. When the Stern Review of 2006 appraised climate policy using a near-zero rate of pure time preference, chosen on explicitly ethical grounds, the recommended level of spending came out an order of magnitude higher than what standard American appraisals, built on market-based rates of several per cent, would license. That argument over the rate has now run for twenty years without either camp conceding.

Three things about the practice puzzle me.

First, the formula that generates the rate — the one descending from Frank Ramsey's work in the 1920s — blends at least three rationales that answer three different questions. There is the term for pure time preference, under which a later person's suffering counts less simply because it is later; there is the term that reflects the opportunity cost of capital, under which money spent now compounds and can do good elsewhere; and there is the consideration that there may be nobody there in 2120 at all. In the documents I read, officials slide among these without announcement. Ramsey himself thought discounting future well-being just because it is future was ethically indefensible, and Sidgwick held that the timing of a pleasure could make no difference to its ethical weight — and yet the apparatus that officialdom actually uses contains the discounted term anyway. Which of the rationales does the practice genuinely rest on, and can the three be held together?

Second, the same governments that run these appraisals do the opposite elsewhere. They subscribe to constitutional preambles and international declarations asserting duties to posterity; the American Constitution was framed, its own preamble says, partly on behalf of generations yet unborn. And bequest law never discounts: a trust that pays out to my granddaughter in 2065 is held at face value, and no court reduces a promise because it matures later. So we have one institution that arithmetically shrinks the interests of the future and another, in the same polity, that treats them at par. Is that a contradiction, or are the two practices tracking different things?

Third — and this is the novelist's observation, so take it for what it is worth — a novel's prologue, set three generations before the action, is granted by every reader exactly the same reality as its final chapter. The discount, whatever it is, is not a fact about the human imagination. I have come to think of the appraisal apparatus as containing a prologue discount that no novelist could impose and keep an audience. This suggests the rate is not discovered but added: something the framework imports from somewhere, and could in principle have imported differently.

So here is what I want from you. Begin by brainstorming the candidate reconstructions before endorsing any of them: (a) the practice presupposes that future people have lesser moral status, and the rate expresses that; (b) the practice presupposes nothing at all about future people, and merely borrows the market rate of return as an administrative device, with the ethical content smuggled in somewhere else entirely; (c) the practice presupposes some agent-relative structure — obligations held by us toward people we can never meet, of a different kind from the obligations that hold within a generation — such that the discount expresses the shape of the duty rather than the worth of its object; (d) the practice presupposes consequentialist aggregation as such, and the rate is just a free parameter inside it, carrying no independent philosophical weight. Then tell me, of these, which the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely sits comfortably with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, which genuinely rival theories of our relation to the unborn future would generate the same numbers and could therefore coexist with the practice as it stands. Then assess the coherence question: can one office, without self-contradiction, apply a several-per-cent rate while its government maintains preambles about posterity and an undiscounted law of bequests? And finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced — say, by the Sidgwickian view that the time at which a harm occurs is ethically irrelevant: what happens to the analyses, what happens to the policy conclusions, and what in the surrounding legal and administrative institutions would have to be rebuilt to match?

Structure your answer under headings, flag the points where you are unsure, and keep the whole thing grounded in the practice as I have described it rather than in what any particular economist happened to write about it. And if you have the inclination, close with one paragraph addressed to me as a novelist: what would a reader have to be shown on the page for a person alive in 2120 to carry the same weight as a person alive today — is there anything in the implicit theory you have reconstructed that explains why the appraisal apparatus can do with arithmetic what narrative apparently does without effort?
