I'm a novelist, and for the past few years, in the gaps between drafts, I've been reading Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — slowly, the way one reads a book one intends to be changed by. The chapter that has stayed with me is the one about the location of meaning, where a message is divided into three parts: the frame message, which is carried by the medium itself and says, in effect, that this is a message and that decoding machinery should be brought to bear; the outer message, the layer of cues that must be recognized before any interpretation can begin, telling you which decoding method to use; and the inner message, the meaning that was intended all along. Hofstadter's own treatment of decipherment is brief and suggestive, and as someone whose livelihood depends on meaning surviving transit from one mind to another — often through a translator I've never met — I want to see the schema put to work on a real case rather than admired in the abstract.

The case I have in mind is the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs, from the Rosetta Stone's discovery in 1799 to Champollion's announcement in 1822. The familiar facts, as I understand them: a decree of 196 BC, issued at Memphis, survives on a slab in three scripts — hieroglyphic and Demotic Egyptian above, Greek below; the Greek could be read immediately and announced that the three texts said the same thing; royal names in cartouches, Ptolemy among them, gave the first toehold, with Thomas Young making early progress there; and Champollion, drawing on his knowledge of Coptic, the descendant of ancient Egyptian still alive in liturgical use, cracked the system and could soon read the hieroglyphs as a language. Please analyze this episode through the three-part schema, and be disciplined about the mapping. Which physical features of the stone carried the frame message — and why, for the many centuries before 1799, did viewers who plainly saw writing on Egyptian monuments fail to have their decoding machinery triggered at all? What exactly constituted the outer message here: the Greek text functioning as a kind of built-in instruction manual, the cartouche convention, Champollion's Coptic? And where does the inner message begin — is the Greek text already inner content, or is it part of the apparatus that gets you to the inner content, and does the schema have a clean answer to that?

Then run the analysis in both directions. I want to know where the schema genuinely illuminates the episode — I suspect it explains something real about why the failure lasted so long, namely that the obstacle sat at the outer level, not the inner, and that the inner message was in a sense sitting there fully formed while remaining perfectly inaccessible. But I also want to know where it strains or goes silent: the schema presents the outer message as something the message itself carries, yet in this case the key to the outer message was outside the message entirely — in a living scholar's head, in the historical accident of a trilingual slab, in a colonial war's loot. Does that make the outer message a property of the message or of the world it lands in? And, since the schema is offered as an account of where meaning is located: was the decree's meaning in the stone during the centuries when it was unreadable, or does meaning only come into existence when the outer message succeeds and a reader arrives? I ask because the answer bears on my own trade. When my novel is translated into a language I cannot read, I am in the position of the Greeks on the slab: I'm told the three texts say the same thing, but I cannot check. I'd like an honest verdict on whether the Rosetta episode confirms the schema, forces a refinement of it, or reveals something it simply never anticipated — not an application in which it conveniently succeeds at every step.
