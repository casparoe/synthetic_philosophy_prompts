I spent thirty-eight years as an analytical chemist, the last twenty of them responsible for quality assurance in a water-company laboratory, where standard operating procedures were very nearly my working religion. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy I never made time for, and I have just finished Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations alongside Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language. The rule-following passages landed on me with uncomfortable force, because I recognise the problem from the other side of the bench: no SOP we ever wrote contained the rules for its own application, and we never noticed, because we had all been trained by the same people and simply agreed about what counted as following it.

I now volunteer as quality lead for a citizen-science river-monitoring network: about a hundred and forty volunteers sampling their local streams monthly, results published in an open database and occasionally relied on in pollution cases. The written sampling protocol runs to eight pages. Disputed readings reach me several times a quarter, and the question is always formally the same — did the volunteer follow the protocol? Since reading Kripke I no longer believe the protocol itself settles any of them: each dispute is really about whether the volunteer's performance accords with what we, the community of practitioners, count as the same thing we did before. But I cannot put that to the committee, because they will hear 'there is no fact of the matter' as 'anything goes', and we have data relied upon by a statutory body.

What I would like, therefore, is a decision procedure that our part-time, non-philosophical quality officer can actually run in an afternoon when a disputed reading arrives: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates in a verdict — 'not a deviation', 'deviation, result stands with an annotation', 'deviation, result quarantined' — and where it stops and hands the matter to the human committee, because what remains is a question of what the network ought to be rather than of fact. Ground the procedure in what the rule-following literature actually supports: the difference between a disposition and a norm; why agreement in practice is the only arbiter we have; and — this is the part I most want done properly — why agreement is still not the same as correctness, so that the procedure must contain some way of registering when the community has converged on a wrong practice, our own quus. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire: where two sub-communities have been trained differently and are internally consistent; where deferring to the old hands entrenches an error; and where the procedure, like the protocol it adjudicates, cannot contain the rules for its own application.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a volunteer, instructed to take a midstream sample, wades out to collect from the true middle of the river rather than reaching from the bank as everyone else does, and her results run persistently against the rest. Second: a founder-member whose technique has drifted over fifteen years away from the written protocol but whose record anchors the entire dataset — no one wants to quarantine the network's longest series. Third, one the committee will not enjoy: the calibration instruction says weekly; a diligent volunteer found her meter drifting on the Thursday, recalibrated it, and her Friday result — already cited in a prosecution file — is challenged as taken with the instrument in a state the protocol nowhere describes. For each case, state the verdict the procedure yields and whether the committee should overrule it.

Finally, tell me what all this implies for drafting the next edition of the sampling protocol: whether the lesson of the rule-following paradox is that protocols should be written differently — more worked examples, more training by shared practice, fewer attempts to close every gap with words — or whether that lesson is one we cannot act on, and why. Please write your answer in British English.
