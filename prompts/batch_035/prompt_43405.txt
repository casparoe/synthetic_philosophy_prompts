I'm a graduate TA for an intermediate decision theory course, and next week I run my first section of the semester on expected utility and its empirical failures. The lecturer has just covered the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the representation theorem in two lectures; my job, in fifty minutes with a whiteboard, is the Allais paradox and what it's supposed to show about the independence axiom. The students are second-year economics majors who have seen expected utility for money exactly once and mostly treat the axioms as algebra they will be tested on, not as substantive claims about rational preference. I'm genuinely torn about sequencing: the natural order is to restate independence formally and then exhibit the violations, but my instinct is that the axiom only becomes interesting after students have made the Allais choices themselves and felt the tension from the inside — that until then "independence" is just the name of one more line on the formula sheet. I'd like a defended view on which order actually works, not just both options listed.

So plan the section for me rather than explaining the paradox to me. What should the carrying example be — the original large-payoff setup with a certain prize versus the two risky pairs, or a reformulation with stakes the students would actually face, and what must the example have and not have in order to carry the point (I worry that scaling the payoffs down changes the psychology, but I also worry the original amounts are abstract enough that students go numb)? Which misconceptions should I expect, in the order they'll actually arrive — my guesses are "this is just risk aversion, which we already knew," "certainty has a utility the theorem forgot to include, so the fix is easy," and "so expected utility is refuted and the axioms are arbitrary dogma" — and where exactly in the sequence each should be headed off? Is having them vote on the two choice pairs before any theory is on the board a help or a hazard, given that some will defend their choices and I don't want the section to become a debate about whether the 'irrational' answer is really irrational? And is it worth showing them a presentation of the same underlying choice where the shared outcome is made salient, so they can watch their own answer flip — or does that give away the punchline and reduce the paradox to a lesson about paying attention?

Finally, give me one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would tell me whether they understand independence as a claim about how one pair of lotteries may be compared to another, rather than as a memorized axiom label — and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level (generalized utility theories, the follow-up literature on whether the violations survive with experience, the framing experiments) and how to say so in a way that doesn't sound like an invitation to dismiss the whole framework as optional decoration.
