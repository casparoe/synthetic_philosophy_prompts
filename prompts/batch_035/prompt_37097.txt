I'm a senior data scientist at a firm that builds probabilistic geopolitical and macroeconomic forecasts, and my entire working vocabulary is statistical: calibration curves, proper scoring rules, log loss, base rates, overfitting and regularization, train/test discipline, data leakage, backtesting. I'm fluent in that vocabulary and only an amateur in philosophy, so I'd like the translation to run from philosophy into it, not the other way around.

The reason I'm asking: last quarter I ran a postmortem of our own forecasts from 2019 through 2021, and the experience unsettled me. Our pre-event probabilities were mostly middling, yet every retrospective writeup we produced made the realized path look overdetermined — once the outcome was on the page, we "had seen it coming" in a way the archived numbers flatly contradict. I recognize this as hindsight bias in the psychological sense (I believe the classic experiments are Fischhoff's), and while reading around on it I kept hitting a second, apparently distinct complaint aimed at historians: the narrative fallacy, the accusation that historical explanation is a seamless causal story fitted onto a known endpoint, and that this is not merely an error but arguably the historian's stock in trade. Around the same time I was taking an evening course on historiography, where I learned that many historians and philosophers of history don't regard this as a vice at all: they defend narrative explanation as a genuine form of understanding, distinct in kind from the covering-law or generalizing ambitions the positivists wanted, and some go further and insist that narrative captures something — contingency, meaning, the standpoint of participants — that no law-like model could. I can't tell how much of that debate is substantive and how much is people talking past each other with ill-defined words, and my instinct is to resolve the question by forcing it into terms I can actually evaluate.

So here is the request. Restate the debate over hindsight bias and narrative explanation in historical writing in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: hindsight bias as a form of outcome leakage — training on the test set; the narrative fallacy as fitting a high-variance, high-capacity model to a single realization without any complexity penalty or holdout; the covering-law aspiration as parametric generalization; the historians' preference for the particular as something like skepticism about the existence of a stable data-generating process for human affairs. Then give me the residue report I actually care about. Which of the historians' claims translate cleanly into things my field already has names for (and what are the names)? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly gets distorted? And which have no counterpart at all — for instance, the claim that narrative confers a kind of understanding that no scoring rule measures, or the observation that historical stories are partly constitutive of later events in a way forecasts only sometimes are? For that last category, I want your view on whether the residue reflects a genuine blind spot in the forecasting vocabulary or whether it's the historians' position that dissolves once stated precisely — whether, say, "narrative understanding" is just a folk name for good prior elicitation, or whether it's something no amount of calibration can capture. Please be ruthless about not smuggling my framework's own assumptions (replicability, stationarity, the idea that "the past" is many draws from a process rather than one unrepeatable event) into the translation and presenting them as findings about the history side.
