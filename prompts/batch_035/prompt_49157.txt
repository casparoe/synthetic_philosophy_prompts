Quick context: I'm in an epistemology seminar and my term paper is on self-deception, specifically whether it's even possible. My supervisor's advice was basically "stop arguing for a theory before you know what a theory here is supposed to do," so that's the ask: write the desiderata for an adequate theory of self-deception. Which cases does it have to get right (the classic one: someone whose partner is obviously cheating, who sees all the evidence and somehow doesn't see it; but also the milder everyday stuff like the smoker who just "hasn't gotten around to" reading up on the risks, or the student who's certain the exam went great); which distinctions does it have to respect (self-deception vs plain wishful thinking, vs motivated reasoning, vs just being a sloppy reasoner with no desire involved, vs lying to yourself in the full-blooded deliberate sense); and what it has to do about the paradox that makes people doubt the phenomenon in the first place -- that deceiving yourself seems to require both holding and not holding the belief at once, being the con artist and the mark simultaneously. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, then tell me which requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- that's the part I actually care about, since if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that's basically an argument that "self-deception" is a label covering several different things pretending to be one phenomenon.

Two more things. First, I want a view on which single requirement does the most to exclude the main candidate pictures on the table (the ones where the mind is somehow divided into a deceiver part and a deceived part, versus the ones where it's all just biased-but-sincere information processing, no internal con artist needed) -- but keep that part short, my paper is about the spec, not a survey. Second, and this matters: no reading lists, no citations, no name-dropping of specific papers, because I'll be working somewhere with no internet access and I don't want to have to guess at references. Just the conceptual work in plain terms. Also fine if you flag where you're unsure whether something is a genuine requirement or just a prejudice imported from one camp -- like whether the deception has to serve a desire, or whether it must be in some sense intentional, or whether success matters (can you count as self-deceived about p if you end up believing not-p only by dumb luck?). Those boundary questions are where my paper is heading, so the more of them you can turn into explicit requirements, the better.
