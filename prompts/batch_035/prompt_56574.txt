I teach a bioethics seminar, and every year the same fight breaks out: half the room holds that there is a morally fundamental difference between killing and letting die — the reason we may withdraw a ventilator but not administer a lethal injection — and the other half thinks the distinction is a superstition. The philosophical literature seems to have stalled in the same place, and I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a verdict.

Here is the shape of the debate as we run it. Rachels argued, with the two bathtub cases, that since Smith drowning his cousin and Jones letting his cousin drown are identical in every respect except the active/omissive one, and we judge them equally, the distinction cannot carry intrinsic moral weight. The other side responds with the Doctrine of Double Effect, or Foot's original trolley case, or the thought that omissions need a background of positive duty to be morally charged at all — so withdrawal of care is permissible not because letting die is special but because we never took on the extra duty to kill. Then come the harder cases: Kamm's trolley variations where you redirect a threat by interposing something in its path rather than by turning a switch; cases where the victim would be crushed by your own body unless you actively move; withholding versus withdrawing treatment; and Kagan's challenge that defenders of the asymmetry owe us an account of which morally relevant feature killing has and letting die lacks — not just an appeal to the fact that most people feel the difference. At some point the seminar always collapses into competing thought-experiment intuitions, and nobody has moved in fifty years.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about cases at all, but about what a moral distinction is for: one side is doing victim-focused morality — what happened to the patient, who is dead either way — and the other is doing agent-focused morality — what kind of will the action expresses, what the doctor's hands are doing. If that's right, the dispute may be two questions under one heading. But I'm not confident, because the Double Effect camp at least purports to be answering a victim-relevant question too. So: is this a substantive disagreement about the moral facts, a verbal disagreement about the word "action," a clash of bedrock intuitions that function as data, or two projects divided by a shared vocabulary? Diagnose it using what the sides actually say, and tell me what would count as progress — a new kind of case, a formal distinction, a change of question — or whether we should expect the debate to stall forever and why.
