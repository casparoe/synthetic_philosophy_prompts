I'm a third-year PhD student working on the metaphysics of time, and for the past two semesters our department's reading group has been stuck on the grandfather paradox — not on whether time travel is a fun thought experiment, but on whether backward time travel is even coherent. The group has split into camps that have not moved an inch. The impossibilists argue that autoinfanticide generates a straightforward contradiction: if Tim goes back and shoots his grandfather before Grandfather meets Grandmother, then Tim is never born and never shoots anyone; so Tim cannot do it; so the time travel he would need in order to do it is impossible. The compatibilists, following David Lewis's classic line, reply that Tim can kill Grandfather in the ordinary sense — he has the rifle, the skill, the clear shot, and nothing in his vicinity stops him — and simply doesn't, because Grandfather's dying in his bed in 1957 is fixed in a way Tim's local capacities cannot touch. The stalemate between those two is familiar enough, but a third strand has started to worry me: a probabilist in the group keeps pressing a Horwich-style point that even if no contradiction follows from a single trip, a world with backward travel and many would-be assassins would be infested with unexplained coincidences — jammed rifles, slips on banana peels — at rates that beggar belief, unless consistency itself is promoted to something like a law.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis rather than gospel, is that the standoff is at bottom partly verbal: the impossibilists and compatibilists are using the word "can" in different senses — one indexed to what is compatible with the broad history of the actual world, one to Tim's local abilities — so their arguments pass each other by, while the probabilist's objection is really a different kind of complaint altogether, about standards of explanation rather than possibility. But I can also see it the other way around: maybe the appeal to ambiguity is itself the dodge, since the genuinely interesting question seems to be whether consistency constraints could be laws rather than mere coincidences, and that looks substantive, not verbal.

So diagnose the disagreement for me. Is it verbal, a clash of background views about laws and explanation, two questions being pursued under one name, or a real substantive dispute that is simply hard? Ground the diagnosis in what each camp actually says, not in labels for positions. And tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress — a sharper semantics for ability claims, a worked-out account of consistency as law, a principled verdict on how much unexplained coincidence is too much — or whether the field should expect to be reconvening on this same question in five years. Lead with your diagnosis, defend it in at most 600 words, and skip the survey of the time travel literature; I want the structure of the disagreement, not a tour of it.
