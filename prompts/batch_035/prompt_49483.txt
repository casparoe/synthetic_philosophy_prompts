My grandma is in memory care with advanced dementia, and every time I visit she asks when grandpa is coming. Grandpa died two years ago, and the staff straight up told us to just say he's coming tomorrow, because reminding her he's dead means she grieves it fresh, every single time. When I mentioned this to my pastor he got real quiet and then said 'every lie is a sin', and afterwards emailed me some Augustine to read -- apparently the guy wrote whole treatises on lying and held you can't lie even to save someone's life. I'm not trying to dunk on my pastor, but that one sentence seemed to be doing alot of work while being super vague, and now it actually matters for how I talk to grandma every week. So what are the different precise claims 'every lie is a sin' could actually be making? Off the top of my head: maybe it's near-tautological if 'lie' already means 'wrongful speech', in which case the whole fight is over whether what I'd say to grandma even counts as a lie; maybe it turns on the definition of lying itself (is a lie any false statement, or any statement meant to deceive, so that clever but literally true answers that mislead her are just as bad?); maybe 'sin' ranges anywhere from a tiny pro tanto wrong to mortal-sin territory no matter the stakes; maybe it's a claim about who you owe truth to and whether grandma in her current state can even be 'deceived' in the morally relevant sense. Lay the readings out as a menu, from trivial to radical, and for each one tell me whether it's plausible and whether it actually delivers the conclusion that I can't tell grandma grandpa is on his way. Also, if you can, say where Augustine himself landed -- like whether silence and evasive-but-true answers were his escape hatch, and how he justified saying no even in life-and-death cases. I want something I can actually think with at the dinner table, not a sermon.
