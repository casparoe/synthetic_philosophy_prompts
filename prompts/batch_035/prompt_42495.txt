For three years my closest friend has worked as a probation officer, writing the pre-sentence reports that tell judges how much of the blame an offender's history should absorb, and over that time we have argued about her cases until I know her moves by heart. Her verdicts so far: a man whose assaults began after a brain tumor — no responsibility at all, not a shred; a man who took a drug he knew could induce violence and then beat someone — fully responsible, on the ground that the violence wasn't chosen but taking the drug was; a woman from a savagely violent home who went on to hurt people — partly responsible, less than someone with a decent start, but not off the hook, because in her words she is still the one who did it; and a prosperous man, loving parents, every advantage, who ruined people out of vanity and greed — fully responsible, on the ground that nobody chooses their character anyway and he is fair game like everyone else.

The reason this has stopped being a parlor game is my brother. By every account, including his own, he spent the twenty-five years since he was fifteen deliberately rebuilding himself from a sentimental, guilt-prone boy into, as he puts it, someone immune to sentiment: notebooks, a fixed practice of refusing the comfortable option, an apprenticeship under a notoriously ruthless employer. Last year, holding our father's power of attorney through his dementia, he moved the house and the savings into his own name; our father died in March. At the funeral my brother told me, without heat, that the boy he was at fifteen was made by other people, but that the man who did this made himself, on purpose, over twenty-five years, and would do it again. This weekend the family meets to decide what he is to us now, and I am expected to speak.

Before I do, I want to know what my friend will say, and I find I cannot predict her. If she runs the rule her files mostly suggest — that blame is discounted by how bad and how unchosen the inputs to your character were — then my brother, who was dealt a good hand and then dealt himself a colder one on purpose, comes out more responsible than anyone she has ever reported on. But if she runs the other thing she sometimes says after a second glass of wine — that in the end nobody's character is anyone's doing, so what we call responsibility is a feeling we cannot help — then his self-making was itself the act of a fifteen-year-old he did not make, and the transformation is only a slow-motion version of the tumor case: something that happened in him, not something he did. Those rules agree on every verdict she has given; on my brother they split. So: which rules actually fit her track record, what does each predict for him, what is your best guess and why, and what further case would settle it? And if the deep rule is the one she is really running, I would like to understand why, in her files, it has shown up only as a discount — the violent home counting for something — rather than as the total exoneration that rule, applied consistently, seems to require.
