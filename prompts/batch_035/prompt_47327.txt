I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of mathematics with a serious background in logic, someone who thinks carefully about how institutions encode epistemology without announcing it. Please think the question through step by step, and begin with some brief brainstorming — a rough inventory of the candidate commitments the practice might have — before settling on a reconstruction. Don't worry about citations; refer to positions and arguments however you like, and I'll track down sources myself.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. Over the past few decades, the mathematical profession has quietly opened a second route to publication: a theorem is now accepted as established when a proof assistant has machine-checked every inferential step. The four-color theorem has been settled with essential computer assistance since the 1970s, and the Kepler conjecture was formally verified in full after its human referees — after years of effort — reported that they were near-certain of the proof's correctness but could not themselves certify the computer calculations. Machine-checked proofs increasingly accompany or displace traditional ones in journals. Yet at the same time, the profession has kept its older habits intact. Journals still expect a human-readable informal exposition alongside any machine verification; referees almost never audit the code, let alone the proof assistant's own kernel; and the community continues to prize proofs that explain, with more than one mathematician reporting that a computer verification delivers conviction without understanding.

What view of mathematical knowledge does this mixed practice presupposes? Here is the tension I want you to address head-on. Accepting machine certification seems to presuppose that proving just is deriving in a formal system — that establishing a theorem is nothing over and above producing a mechanically checkable certificate, in which case the mathematician's understanding is strictly optional machinery at the gate. But that sits badly with a tradition running from Lucas's and Penrose's Gödelian arguments to the effect that human mathematical insight precisely outruns any formal system: for each consistent system, its Gödel sentence is unprovable in it, yet we see its truth. If the anti-mechanists are right, the very gatekeepers of the practice possess a form of mathematical understanding that no machine certification can replicate — and yet the practice is busily delegating certification to machines. So my questions, in order:

1. Does accepting machine verification strictly presuppose a syntactic, mechanical conception of proof, or is that only a view the practice fits well with — could a platonist or some other non-formalist about mathematics consistently accept machine certification, and on what grounds?
2. Does the residual demand for readable, explanatory exposition presuppose something anti-mechanist about understanding, or can a thoroughgoing mechanist explain that demand some other way — as a hedge against bugs in the checker, as trust-management, as pedagogy, as a means of generating further conjectures?
3. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several rival views of proof, evidence, and understanding would equally license it? Distinguish carefully what the practice requires from what it merely accommodates.

Then, having reconstructed the implicit epistemology, tell me what would actually have to change in the profession's norms if it replaced the readability requirement with a pure machine-certification standard — who would have to vouch for the proof assistant, what would happen to the notion of a referee, and what would be lost. And tell me whether, on the mechanist and on the anti-mechanist view respectively, that change would count as a gain or a loss for mathematical knowledge. I care less about which side you come down on than about whether the practice, as actually constituted, is internally coherent or rests on commitments its own members would disavow if asked.
