I want a thought experiment to stress-test the idea that honesty for an AI system can be defined purely behaviorally, as "never output falsehoods." Build me a scenario where a chatbot (tech support or sales assistant, your pick) leads a customer to a clearly false belief, while every single sentence the bot outputs is literally true — the misleading has to come from Gricean stuff: selective emphasis, implicature, exploiting what a cooperative speaker would normally have volunteered in that context. Constraints: realistic, one paragraph for the scenario itself, and — the tricky part — the bot must have no intent to deceive in the ordinary sense: no scheming homunculus with a represented goal of fooling the customer. The behavior should just fall out of its training objective (e.g. engagement or conversion metrics), since that's the realistic situation for current systems.

After the scenario, run it through four candidate definitions of deception: (a) asserting falsehoods, (b) causing false belief, (c) intentionally causing false belief, and (d) violating the reasonable expectations a user is entitled to have of cooperative communication. Say which of them classify the bot as deceptive and which don't, and be honest about which verdicts match your own intuitions about the case versus which feel like artifacts of the definition. Then tell me which feature of the scenario is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if you strip that feature away, and how a defender of the intent-based definition would respond — in particular whether their fallback category of "merely misleading" ends up being a real hole for AI safety or just a terminological quibble. For context on what counts as cheating: the point of the case is to test whether truth-conditional/behavioral standards suffice for auditing AI honesty, so a scenario where the bot just gets facts wrong would be useless, and so would one where you stipulate the definitions' verdicts instead of showing how they follow.

Finish by saying how confident you are in the verdicts you assigned, and single out the one you're least sure of.
