A guy in my ethics reading group (huge Benatar fan, wears the t-shirt, so to speak) thinks he's found a knock-down argument that the procreation asymmetry can't be true, and I can't find the weak step. For context, the asymmetry I mean is the intuitive view that it's wrong to create someone whose life will be worse than nothing for them, but that it's not wrong -- and not a duty either -- to refrain from creating someone whose life would be great. He grants that intuition completely; his claim is that it can't be grounded in anything coherent. Here's his argument. (1) If creating the miserable person is wrong, the wrongness has to be grounded in one of two places: either someone is wronged (made worse off, treated badly) or the world is impersonally worse. (2) If it's the wronged-someone route, notice the miserable person you create isn't worse off than they would have been, because absent your choice there's no them to be better or worse off -- you can't be deprived of, or burdened by comparison with, a life you never had. So nobody is wronged. (3) If it's the impersonal route, then a world with the miserable person is impersonally worse than an empty world, and by symmetry a world with a happy person is impersonally better than an empty one. But then declining to create a happy person when you easily could makes the world worse, and surely if making the world worse one way grounds a wrongness claim, making it worse the other way does too -- which hands you a duty to procreate, the exact thing the asymmetry exists to deny. (4) So both grounding routes fail, and the asymmetry is a free-floating intuition with nothing underneath it.

I actually want to keep (the analogue of) the asymmetry -- it seems really obvious to me that it's monstrous to create a life of agony and merely permissible to skip creating a happy one, and I don't want a theory that can't say that. But I also can't immediately see which of his steps is the cheat. My tutor muttered something about the move in (3) from how the world is to what we're required to do, and a friend says the real trick is buried in (2), where "worse off than they would have been" quietly assumes all harms are comparative. And I can see that if you take the wronged-someone route seriously enough to block him, you end up saying that a person whose whole life is unremitting agony wasn't harmed by being brought into existence, which sounds like a reductio of me rather than him. So: which step should I reject, why did it look so innocent, and what do I have to pay for rejecting it? Also tell me if there's some way to ground the badness of miserable creations that doesn't fall into either of his two buckets, or whether his dichotomy in (1) is itself doing sneaky work.
