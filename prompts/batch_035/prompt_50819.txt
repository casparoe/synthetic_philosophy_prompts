I have just bought a house, and I did it in a way that has unsettled me: I looked at eleven, set a vague threshold of "has natural light, isn't a renovation trap, and isn't more than a forty-minute commute," and made an offer on the first one that cleared it — even though the seventh was plausibly better and I knew it. Defending my own conduct has pulled me into the satisficing debate, and I have been stuck there since.

As I understand it, the debate has two layers, and both have stalled. The first is the old one begun by Herbert Simon: since search is costly and cognition is limited, a rational agent stops at the first option that exceeds an aspiration level rather than maximizing. Almost everyone accepts this in some form, but it raises the question of whether satisficing is genuinely an alternative to maximizing or just maximizing with the search costs written into the payoff — in which case the satisficer was never doing anything different from the optimizer, only doing it with realistic constraints. The second layer is the later, stranger claim: that even with no search costs, no cognitive limits, and a known better option on the table, choosing the good-enough option can be rational in its own right — perhaps as an expression of moderation, perhaps because standing in a relation of "good enough" to an option can itself have value. On this view my skipping the seventh house wasn't an admission of limitation but possibly fine, full stop. Critics respond that this is either covert maximizing (the value of not agonizing over houses has been folded into the utility function), or else it's not rationality at all but something else — a lifestyle or a virtue — trading on the prestige of the word.

Here is what I want from you: not a verdict on whether I should have bought the seventh house, but a diagnosis of why this debate has gone nowhere. Is it a verbal dispute about 'rational' — one side legislating for the ideal chooser, the other prescribing for actual agents like me, with the two sides never contradicting each other? Is the disagreement really about whether search costs, deliberation costs, and the value of decisiveness are part of what's being chosen or constraints around the choosing — a boundary-drawing question that could be settled with a sharper formalism, or one that no formalism can settle because the boundary is itself the issue? Or is it, at bottom, a clash of starting intuitions — the thought that rationally you should always take the best available versus the thought that there is nothing left to ask once something is good enough — such that neither side can produce an argument that doesn't presuppose its own conclusion? I'd like you to take a position on which diagnosis fits, defend it with what the disputants actually claim, and then tell me what progress would look like: a distinction that dissolves the verbal core, a decision-theoretic result, a well-constructed case that isolates the residual disagreement, or an argument that the residual disagreement is real, permanent, and simply what you'd expect from a concept straddling the ideal and the practical. If it helps, you may use my house as a test case, but I care more about the structure of the impasse than about my commute.
