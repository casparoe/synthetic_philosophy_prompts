I run a little film club at my local community centre and we're screening Amadeus (the Miloš Forman one) next month. I'm also auditing an evening course on nineteenth-century philosophy, and we just did Schopenhauer's aesthetics, and something clicked. As I understand him, Schopenhauer thinks music is categorically different from the other arts: painting and sculpture copy the world of appearances at one or two removes, via the Ideas, but music somehow gets behind appearances altogether and is a direct image of the Will itself — which is why it's the highest of the arts and why a melody can be profound without representing anything at all. That seems almost tailor-made for Amadeus. Salieri's whole tragedy is that he can analyse Mozart's manuscripts and find them flawless, note for note, while being utterly unable to produce the thing himself — and he experiences Mozart's music as something that just arrives, complete, as if Mozart were taking dictation. That feels very Schopenhauerian: the genius as a medium rather than a craftsman.

But I can also see the reading breaking down, and this is what I want the essay to dig into. Salieri hears the music as proof of a personal God who has chosen to speak through one vulgar little man — and a message from a divine designer sounds much more like music as representation, or as someone's intentional communication, than like Schopenhauer's blind, goalless Will. Schopenhauer also has that famous contrast between talent, which hits a target others can't reach, and genius, which hits a target others can't even see — which fits Salieri and Mozart perfectly — yet Schopenhauer seems to have no room at all for Salieri's actual problem, which is moral and personal: being able to *recognise* greatness you can't achieve, and being destroyed by that recognition. Does the film dramatise Schopenhauer's theory, quietly invert it, or end up somewhere he couldn't account for? And tell me what you think the film adds to Schopenhauer that the philosophy itself doesn't give us — is it an argument for taking the *listener's* torment seriously, which Schopenhauer, so focused on the genius, arguably doesn't?
