A colleague of mine in decision theory has an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose individual steps I have trouble condemning. She calls it an argument for decision-theoretic nihilism, and it runs as follows.

(1) Rational comparison of risky acts requires an aggregation rule: something that takes an act's possible outcomes together with their probabilities and returns a value that can be compared with the values of other acts.
(2) The probability-weighted sum — expected value — is the only aggregation rule that meets three minimal conditions: it weights every outcome in proportion to its probability, it is insensitive to how we label, group, or enumerate the outcomes, and it coincides with plain valuation of the outcome when risk is absent.
(3) The Pasadena game shows that expected value is not always defined. In it, a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads on toss n, and the payoff is an amount that alternates in sign and grows like 2^n/n, while its probability shrinks like 2^-n. The probability-weighted payoff series therefore shrinks like 1/n with alternating signs: it converges, but only conditionally. Summed in the natural order it converges to ln 2; reordered, it can be made to converge to any value you like, or to diverge outright. The game's expected value is thus not large or small but undefined — an artifact, she says, of there being no fact about which outcome comes first in a sum.
(4) What drives the Pasadena pathology is available to any act whose payoff distribution is unbounded both above and below: the positive and negative parts of the series each diverge, so the total is order-dependent and the expectation fails to exist.
(5) Every real decision under uncertainty is like this in principle. The set of possible consequences of a career choice, a military intervention, or a climate policy is open-ended; nothing in the world imposes a cap on how good or bad the outcome can be, and our uncertainty extends indefinitely into the future without a terminal point at which the book of consequences is closed.
(6) So for every real decision, expected value is undefined.
(7) By (2), no alternative aggregation rule is even a candidate.
(8) Therefore there is no rational basis for preferring one risky act to another. Decision theory has no subject matter.

She insists that (8) simply follows and that we should accept that our ordinary comparative judgments between gambles are habits rather than judgments. I am willing to grant that (3) is a correct description of the game. But something feels wrong between (3) and (6) — perhaps in the word available in (4), or in the slide within (5) from there being no known cap to there being divergent tails — and I suspect (2) is stated more strongly than she is entitled to. I also cannot tell whether the deepest mistake is the assumption, built into the whole setup, that expectation is an ordered summation over a list at all.

Tell me which step or steps to reject. For each, explain why it looked fine and what rejecting it costs — what we must give up, which anomalies we accept, and what the standard responses (bounded utility, capped or truncated expectations, risk-weighted rules, dominance reasoning without aggregation, imprecise probabilities) each have to pay to reject the same step. Also tell me whether the argument is valid as it stands or whether some step is valid only under a reading its premises do not supply. I would most like to keep (1) and (3); I am far less attached to (2), though I want to know exactly which of its three conditions fails and whether a weakened version of it survives.
