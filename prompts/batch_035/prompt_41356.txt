My friend Dara, who is a chemist and relishes being right, and I spent most of Friday night arguing about induction, and I walked away with a puzzle I can't find a name for. She was defending the plain inductive move: every sample of gold she has ever assayed has been dense and chemically inert, so the next sample will be too. I thought I agreed with her until I noticed that you can describe her notebook entries with a word I invented on the spot. Call a metal "remgold" if it behaves like gold in every assay performed before June 2030 and behaves like molten lead afterward. Every single one of Dara's observations is consistent with the claim that her samples are remgold, exactly as they are consistent with the claim that they are gold — the two descriptions agree on everything tested so far and diverge only about the future. Her own rule, "expect the next instance to resemble the pattern of the examined instances," endorses both projections equally. But the second one, plus a calendar, predicts that the 2031 sample will pour rather than tarnish. Same evidence, same rule, two predictions that contradict each other, and the only difference between the two descriptions I can point to is that "gold" was lying around in the language before she started testing and "remgold" was coined after, in my kitchen. It's deeply uncomfortable that this might be what separates legitimate from illegitimate projection, and it's equally uncomfortable that I can't find anything else that does the separating. We tried it on other cases: "every raven we've seen is black" felt projectible, while "every raven we've seen is crowdark, i.e., black until next solstice and white thereafter" felt like a cheat, even though our combined observations support the two hypotheses in lockstep.

I want to be clear that I'm not asking the old Hume question, or at least I don't think I am. I've read enough to know Hume asks why the future should resemble the past at all, and my question is downstream of that: granting that we are going to project from past to future, which of the many descriptions of the past — all of them equally faithful to the data — are we entitled to project? I also don't think this is ordinary overfitting, the way my ML friends use the word, because overfitting is normally diagnosed by holding out data and checking, and Dara and I are asking what to believe before any future test can arbitrate; by construction the two descriptions will not come apart until 2030. And it isn't quite the "ad hoc rescue" pattern either, since nobody's prediction has failed and nobody is patching anything — the gerrymandered description fits the completed record just as snugly as the homely one does, from day one. The nearest thing I can think of is choosing among curves through a scatter of points, where infinitely many lines fit the points but only one feels like the "real" law; but with curves you can at least say something about simplicity or parameter counts, and my remgold predicate doesn't seem to be losing on any count of syntactic complexity I know how to take — it's a fairly short word.

So: is there an established concept, or an actual literature, for this gap between descriptions that fit the same evidence but differ wildly in how entitled we feel to extrapolate them? If there is, tell me what it's called and what the standard treatment says, but more importantly tell me whether the standard notion covers my case exactly or only approximately — I have variants (predicates defined by the date of observation, predicates defined by the sampling procedure itself rather than any date, disjunctive monsters that piggyback on two ordinary predicates) where I genuinely can't tell whether they're supposed to count as the same phenomenon or as near misses, and I'd like to know where the boundary of the concept is supposed to run and why. If nothing in the literature quite carves this at the joints, coin a term for it, define it precisely enough to exclude the near misses I mentioned, and say something about how a principled definition could avoid collapsing into mere "predicates coined late" — since surely the timing of the coinage can't be doing the real work. Please end your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing just the established term or your coined term.
