I've been reading about epistemic akrasia and I'm stuck on what seems to me a knockdown point: akrasia just is believing that p while also believing that your evidence doesn't support p, so it is irrational by definition — as little an open question as whether it's irrational to accept a conjunction while disbelieving one of its conjuncts. Yet the literature treats akrasia as though it might be rational, with cases of roughly this shape: I have checked my proof of a surprising mathematical claim a dozen times and it looks sound; a logician whose judgment I trust has read the proof and tells me it fails, though she cannot locate the error; so my total evidence, it seems, favors not believing the claim, and yet I go on believing it on the strength of my own checking. My question is what exactly is supposed to block the definitional argument. Suppose the defender of akrasia grants that my higher-order belief — that my evidence doesn't support the theorem — is something I ought to respond to. Then isn't that belief itself just further evidence, no different in kind from her testimony about the flaw, so that its force transmits directly to the first-order level and leaves my continued belief incoherent in the same flat way as disbelieving a conjunct of a conjunction I accept? Or does the argument only work because it quietly treats higher-order evidence as just another first-order input — and, if so, what is the alternative picture on which responding to it is anything other than updating on it?
