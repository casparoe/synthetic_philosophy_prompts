I'm a senior ML engineer at a company that runs large-scale online experiments. My working vocabulary is the one I actually use day to day: training distributions and covariate shift, propensity scores, stratified evaluation, A/B tests and their observational knock-offs, confounders, colliders, the do-operator, and DAGs sketched on whiteboards to argue about what to condition on. I'm fluent in all of that; I have no philosophy background. A colleague who reads philosophy of science walked me into a dispute about Simpson's paradox that I can't stop thinking about, and I want it restated in my vocabulary, plus an honest accounting of what doesn't survive the translation.

The setup, as it came to me: in the classic kidney-stone data, treatment A beats treatment B within both strata — small stones and large stones — yet B beats A in the aggregate. My first reaction was that there is no paradox: the aggregate is a mixture, the mixing proportions differ across arms, the unadjusted comparison is a biased estimate of the treatment effect, and the whole thing dissolves once you condition on stone size, which is a confounder. End of story. My colleague says the philosophical dispute is precisely about whether that reaction is a discovery about the data or an imposition on it. Apparently there are at least three positions in the vicinity:

1. The evidentialist reading: both tables are perfectly good descriptions of the data. Association is not a property of the world but of a joint distribution together with a conditioning set, and there is nothing incoherent about the association flipping when the conditioning set changes. On this view the aggregate table is not wrong; it is a correct answer to a different question, and nothing in the data itself privileges one question over the other.

2. The causalist reading: the reversal shows that the question "what is the association, really?" is underdetermined by the data. Any answer requires assumptions that the data alone cannot supply, and the natural ones are causal — that the partitioning variable is a confounder rather than a collider or a mediator, that stratum membership is not itself affected by treatment, and so on. The data cannot tell you which table to trust; only the causal story can.

3. A third position my colleague half-remembers from the philosophers and defends on his own account: the right conditioning set is fixed neither by the data nor by causal structure alone, but by evidential relevance to the hypothesis actually under test — something in the neighborhood of a total-evidence requirement. On this view the causalist smuggles in a decision-theoretic, action-oriented standard for choosing what to condition on and presents it as the only standard there is.

Restate each of the three positions in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can — in terms of estimands, interventions versus conditioning, what can and cannot be read off a covariance matrix, and the situations we actually hit in production, where a metric reverses sign when we slice by platform or by account age. Then give me the residue report: which parts of each position translate cleanly into things I already say; which parts have to be distorted to fit; and whether anything in the dispute has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. In particular, I want to know whether picking an estimand before looking at the data answers the philosophers' question or merely relocates it — and if there is a genuine residue, whether it is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the dispute is less substantive than it looks. I'd also like your verdict on position 3 as I've garbled it, since it is the one I cannot map onto anything we do.
