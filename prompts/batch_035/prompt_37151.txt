I'm helping design a small research program — roughly eight philosophers and decision theorists, funded for four years — on interpersonal utility comparisons: the question of whether and how one can say that a given welfare gain for one person is larger or smaller than a given welfare gain for another. This question sits underneath prioritarian weighting in policy analysis, distributional weights in cost-benefit analysis, and any attempt to aggregate well-being across beings with very different psychologies.

Some background, to fix ideas. Within a single person, von Neumann–Morgenstern utility theory yields a cardinal scale, but nothing about that scale is shared between persons: each individual's utility function is pinned down only up to a positive affine transformation, so the size of utility differences is meaningful within a life but, on the standard view, meaningless across lives. The familiar replies each carry a known difficulty. Harsanyi-style extended preferences seem to require, at the final step, just the sort of interpersonal judgment they were supposed to ground — a regress. Approaches based on empathetic projection or simulation inherit the simulator's standpoint and biases. And purely ordinal or majoritarian approaches discard the distributional information that motivated wanting comparisons in the first place. Assume two application domains in the background of the program: (a) global health and income policy, where analysts must already attach weights to benefits received by distant strangers; and (b) comparisons across beings with very different psychologies — non-human animals now, and conceivably systems with reward structures quite unlike ours later — where there may be no shared behavioral currency even in principle.

Which open questions in this area should the program prioritize, in what order, and why? I care more about the reasoning than the list. For each question you rank highly, please say: what makes it upstream of the others (how many further questions presuppose its answer); whether it is tractable with philosophical methods as opposed to psychology or economics; and what a convincing partial answer would look like concretely enough that we could recognize progress. I am especially interested in how you handle one structural trade-off. The questions about the ultimate justifiability of interpersonal comparisons may be the most important, but if they are likely to stall in the same impasse that has persisted since the ordinalist revolution in economics, perhaps the program's effort is better spent on second-best questions — for instance, how to make policy robust to uncertainty about the correct interpersonal scale, or how much of the aggregation we care about survives if comparisons are only a partial order rather than a full ranking of gains and losses. So I also want you to address where the marginal effort should sit between foundational and second-best work, and what developments during the program's lifetime — conceptual or empirical — would cause you to reshuffle the priorities.
