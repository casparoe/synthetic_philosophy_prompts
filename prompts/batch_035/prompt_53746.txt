I run a weekly philosophy discussion group at a community college, about 14 students, 75 minutes. We've been on the epistemology of testimony for a bit. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between reductionism (the Hume-ish line: you're only justified trusting testimony insofar as it can be backed by your own observations of how often that kind of source gets things right) and anti-reductionism (the Reid/Coady line: testimony is a basic source of justification, on par with perception — trust is the default, not something you earn back).

Here's the shape I'm imagining, but feel free to improve on it: some students witness an "event" firsthand (I'm thinking staged cards or images they have 10 seconds to look at), and they pass written reports down a chain to people who never saw anything, and the receivers have to act on the reports — sort items, place points-bets on answers, something with stakes but no real money. Crucially, run it under two rule regimes: one where you may only trust a report if you personally have a track record with that exact reporter from earlier rounds, and one where trust is the default and defeaters are what costs you. Then compare how the two regimes actually perform and where each one breaks (chains too long, no time to build track records, etc).

Debrief should map what happened onto the real arguments, and be honest about the part of the dispute the game can't settle — my worry is that if the default-trust regime just wins, students will think that settles it, when a reductionist can reply that the accumulated track records in the game ARE the reduction, just compressed. Don't lecture up front, and they prolly shouldn't hear the terms "reductionism"/"anti-reductionism" until the debrief. Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
