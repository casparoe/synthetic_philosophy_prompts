I'm a PhD student in conservation biology, and somewhere along the way — partly through fieldwork, partly through reading the animal-ethics and wild-animal-welfare literature — I've arrived at a view most of my colleagues would find repugnant: that in many cases, destroying or degrading wild habitat reduces the total amount of suffering in the world, because the average welfare of wild animals is negative. The reasoning runs roughly like this: the overwhelming majority of individual animals on Earth are short-lived, r-selected invertebrates and fish, most of which die before maturity, often painfully; given that baseline, a hectare of intact habitat plausibly contains more aggregate suffering than a hectare of pasture, cropland, or parking lot; so habitat loss, however bad for biodiversity, ecosystem functioning, and human interests, may be positive along the dimension of suffering. Philosophers like Oscar Horta have defended versions of the underlying picture, and I've sketched the conclusion in seminars, where reactions ranged from "this is ecology's trolley problem" to "you're smuggling in utilitarianism plus a welfare-sign claim nobody can verify." I want to stress-test it properly, so tell me what would count against the view — not just that it's counterintuitive.

First, the empirical defeaters. What findings would undermine it, and which are live rather than merely logical possibilities? For instance: evidence that most wild animals live mostly-decent lives with brief bad endings, or that the density and welfare of animals on developed or agricultural land (soil fauna under turf grass, arthropods in crop fields, fish in aquaculture replacing wild stocks) rival or exceed what the intact habitat supported. Where could actual data come in and change my mind, and where is the relevant question probably intractable — cross-species welfare comparison, say, or measuring anything like average affect in a population of a billion soil nematodes? Be honest about which parts of my empirical base are firm (population-dynamics facts like high juvenile mortality in r-selected species) and which are extrapolation.

Second, the theoretical defeaters: which commitments, once made explicit, do the view's own defenders concede are load-bearing? Population ethics is the obvious one — on a person-affecting view, where nonexistence isn't bad for anyone, the framing of habitat destruction as preventing suffering wobbles badly; does the view survive on person-affecting grounds at all, or only on totalist ones? Then the aggregation problem: averaging welfare across individuals with wildly different lifespans, nervous systems, and forms of valenced experience. Is that a principled commitment or a placeholder for a decision we haven't yet made? And is there a version of the view that doesn't collapse into absurdity about which habitats to prefer — old-growth forest versus deep ocean versus Antarctic?

Third, give me the defeaters ranked: fatal (would force abandonment), serious (would force a major retreat — say, to a restricted class of habitats or taxa), and merely awkward (embarrassing implications that don't dent the core). For each, say whether a committed defender of the view would concede it counts against them or would wave it off, and whether waving it off is legitimate. Relatedly, assess the unfalsifiability charge: critics say the sign of average welfare is unmeasurable, so the view can absorb any evidence — is that fair, and if it is, is it a defect for a view of this kind, or just the standard situation of normative views with uncertain empirical premises?

A final epistemic request: throughout, keep separate what is settled among people who actually know the area, what is genuinely contested among them, and what is my own idiosyncratic framing. Don't present contested claims — the sign of wild-animal welfare, the aggregation function, the population ethics — as if they were consensus among ecologists or philosophers, and don't launder the contested parts into the settled parts. And if you think my colleagues' default, that habitat destruction is straightforwardly bad for animals, is the better-anchored position, say so plainly and show me which of my premises it beats me on.
