At a family gathering over the holidays, my uncle (a retired engineer who reads a lot of history) and my cousin's wife (finishing a doctorate in economics) got into a long argument about whether history is, as my uncle put it, "locked in." He opened by pointing out how much of the present is explained not by anything efficient but by early accidents that got frozen in place: entire countries drive on one side of the road because of decisions going back centuries; in the early 1900s car buyers happened to converge on gasoline engines over electric ones for reasons that could easily have gone the other way; and once a century of refueling infrastructure, tooling, and habits piled up on top, the switch costs became enormous. His thesis: when enough layers build on an accident, the accident might as well have been a law — history is path-dependent "all the way up," small causes have huge consequences, and the world we have is one of many that could easily have been otherwise.

She pushed back with what she called convergence: nearly everything important was invented or discovered more than once, at nearly the same time, by people working independently — calculus, the telephone, natural selection, and plenty of others. When the background knowledge is ready, she said, the result arrives no matter who happens to deliver it. So replay history with small changes and you'd still get roughly the same technologies, roughly the same industrialization, roughly the same kinds of states — different names on the patents, but the same world at the level that matters. Path dependence governs the trivia (which spelling wins, which side of the road), while the big picture is overdetermined. He answered that this confuses the invention with the outcome: even granting multiple discovery, what a discovery *does* depends on the institutions and the sequence it lands in — had industrialization taken root under different political arrangements, the resulting distribution of power, wealth, and languages would have been different, and there's no reason to think those macro outcomes are overdetermined as well. Besides, she was counting only the multiple discoveries that occurred in our world, which was already shaped by the accidents she was dismissing; in the branch of history where the first discoverer dies young, the "inevitable" rediscovery may arrive fifty years late, into a world that has already moved on. She replied that his position was unfalsifiable in the opposite direction — every counterfactual is exactly as dramatic as the person conjuring it wants it to be — and that the reasonable view is simply micro-level contingency plus macro-level convergence, which he was inflating into global contingency for rhetorical effect. They were still at it when I went to bed, and I honestly couldn't tell which of their steps were sound.
