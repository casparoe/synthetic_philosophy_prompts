I teach an evening course in introductory epistemology at a community college — eighteen adult students, most of them with no prior philosophy, and one 75-minute session to spend on a single question I want them to actually feel rather than take notes on: why should knowledge be worth more than a true belief that gets you exactly the same result? Before we read anything, I want them to run into the gap between having a correct answer and having a justified, stable, re-derivable one, and to discover for themselves that the two come apart precisely when something goes wrong. My rough idea is a navigation or wayfinding scenario — the ancient version of this puzzle involves asking whether a guide who genuinely knows the road and a guide who merely has a correct opinion about it both get you to your destination equally well — but I'm open to any concrete setup that does the job. What matters is that the value of knowledge shows up as something participants experience in their choices and their outcomes, not something I assert in a wrap-up lecture.

Please design the full activity: the setting (a staged map-based scenario, a building-based task, or something else you can justify), the exact rules, the materials (index cards, handouts, whatever), a timeline for the 75 minutes including any setup I can do beforehand, and a payoff or point scheme that creates real but small stakes without money. Crucially, build in a perturbation round — some way of breaking the environment after participants have committed to a route or method, so that those who merely memorized a correct instruction and those who understood why the instruction works get separated by events rather than by my grading. Do not have the facilitator name the historical problem or its source at any point during the activity; the name should only surface in the debrief, along with the connection to the original example of travelers and a destination.

For the debrief, map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical distinction: which features of the participants' situations correspond to justification, reliability, and the ability to re-derive; and say plainly what the strongest objection is — namely, that the activity only shows knowledge being instrumentally more useful, which doesn't yet settle whether knowledge is intrinsically more valuable than true belief. Also include an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (participants conferring when they shouldn't, someone recognizing the puzzle from elsewhere, the perturbation being too easy to reason around) with concrete handling suggestions, and a frank note on which part of the philosophical problem no in-class activity can reproduce, so I know what I'll have to supply by direct argument afterwards.
