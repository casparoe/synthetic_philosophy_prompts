my brother and I keep fighting about our cousin and I want an outside view. Cousin is in his 40s and has burned through basically all the family goodwill — chronic lying, money borrowed and never repaid, and last year he committed actual fraud at his job and only barely stayed out of jail over it. Up until recently my brother was the angriest one of us about all this. Now he's flipped completely and says we should stop holding any of it against the guy, ever. His reasoning goes like this: the cousin never chose to be the way he is. He didn't pick his temperament, didn't pick how he was raised, didn't pick the brain he was born with, and since everything he does flows from causes he never chose, it makes no sense to treat him as blameworthy. Getting angry at him is like getting angry at weather.

I have two problems and I can't untangle them myself. First, something feels off about the argument even though every step sounds okay when I hear it — but the conclusion seems to prove way too much. If this reasoning works, then nobody can ever be blamed for anything, including people who defraud their employers deliberately and in cold blood, and my brother doesn't seem ready to bite that bullet for strangers, only for family. So either the argument is unsound somewhere, or it's valid but proves less than it looks like it proves, and I honestly can't tell which. Where would you say it goes wrong, if it does? Which premise would you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost you?

Second thing. My brother's position doesn't even seem stable in his own behavior. He still thanks the neighbor who shovels his sidewalk, and he was furious for weeks when some guy dinged his parked car and drove off without a note. I told him that looks inconsistent given what he now claims to believe, and he said thankfulness and that kind of anger are just feelings, they don't commit him to anything about desert. Is that right? Is holding people responsible for good things somehow a different case from holding them responsible for bad ones, or is he just running the argument selectively when it's convenient and comfortable, and dropping it the rest of the time? And if his "just feelings" reply works for him, does it also work for me — could I go on blaming the cousin while agreeing with the letter of my brother's argument?

For what it's worth I'm not looking for advice on how to handle the cousin practically (whether to lend him money etc.) — I specifically want to know if my brother's argument is any good as an argument, because it keeps coming up between us and I suspect it's being used as a philosophical cover for just wanting to stop feeling bad about the situation. Maybe lay out for me what the strongest version of his view even is, and what the best responses to that version look like.
