Hi — I sit on the executive board of a regional transit workers union (around 9,000 members), and we've got a governor's race in about seven weeks that will matter enormously for our next contract fight. Here's the situation, bluntly: exactly two candidates can win. Candidate A is with us on maybe 60 percent of things but crossed us on the big one two years ago — she signed the bill that bars us from striking during declared transit emergencies, and our members still fume about it. Candidate B is against us on basically everything and has promised to reopen our current contract if he's elected. At last week's meeting the board split three ways and I got tasked with circulating a recomendation before the next one, so I need a memo.

Camp one wants a full-throated endorsement of A: she's the lesser evil by a mile, and pretending otherwise to keep our hands clean is self-indulgent while members' jobs are on the line. Camp two wants us to stay neutral and just run a big member turnout operation — endorsements, they say, are how unions burn credibilty with their own people; our job is getting members to the polls, not telling them what to do once they're there, and A doesn't deserve our name next to hers given the strike ban. Camp three wants a conditional endorsement: we back A only if she commits, in writing and publicly, to amending the strike-ban law in her first legislative session, and we withdraw the endorsement loudly if she doesn't.

Draft the short memo (under ~700 words, half the board won't read past that anyway) with:

1. A clear recomendation — pick one of the three camps or propose something better if you genuinely see it, but it has to be actionable before the election, not a "both sides have merit" shrug.
2. Real engagement with the strongest versions of the losing arguments, not strawmen. The neutral camp in particular has a serious point about what an endorsement is *for* — is it a prediction about which candidate will be better for us, or a statement about who we are, and does it cheapen the members' own judgment? Take that seriously even if you reject it.
3. Something on the turnout question, since it's tangled up with the endorsement one — does telling members to vote for A do anything a pure turnout push wouldn't?

And this last part matters to me: end the memo with an honest note on where the reasoning is most likely to fail and what would have to be true for the recomendation to flip (e.g. new polling showing a third candidate viable, a written commitment from A, evidence members would punish us for endorsing). I'd rather circulate something that admits its weak spots than something that sounds airtight and isn't. Thanks.
