I'm a retired geochemist -- thirty-odd years doing water quality work for a government lab, mostly on arsenic in groundwater -- and after retiring I got sucked into philosophy. Long story, but a used copy of Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics at a church sale two years ago did it. I've now worked through the Cambridge utilitarians and gotten weirdly attached to Sidgwick, and the thing that fascinates me is his side career: the psychical research. I've drafted an essay about it for the little reading group I'm in (all retirees, one ex-philosophy professor who is merciless), and I want comments before I bring it to the group. Not line edits -- what I need is (a) a check on whether I've got the history right, especially the spots I flag at the very end where my memory is getting shaky, and (b) whether the central argument actually holds up, because the ex-professor already told me it's too clever by half, and I want to know if he's right before he says it in front of everyone.

Here's the essay draft:

---

The Utilitarian at the Séance: Why Sidgwick's Ghost-Hunting Was Ethics By Other Means

Every field has its embarrassing chapter, and for the SPR-era Victorians the embarrassing chapter is that the most rigorous moral philosopher in England spent his evenings investigating telepathy. I want to argue this was not a lapse but the logical continuation of his ethics.

In the Methods of Ethics (1874) Sidgwick builds utilitarianism so carefully that he ends up demonstrating it can't be completed from inside. Reason endorses both maximizing your own happiness and maximizing happiness generally, and there is no argument from within practical reason that settles the conflict -- he called it the dualism of practical reason. Rational ethical life therefore needs a postulate: that the universe is somehow morally governed, so that the conflict dissolves at the level of the whole system, even if we can't see it dissolving within a lifetime.

Now, a Victorian churchman would just postulate God and be done. What makes Sidgwick interesting to me as a scientist is that he refused to. For him the postulate became an empirical question, and the most direct piece of evidence you could ask for is survival of personality after death -- which means you investigate it the way you'd investigate anything: data, controls, statistics.

Hence the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, with Sidgwick as its first president. Hence also the work I really want to defend: the Census of Hallucinations, published in 1894. The idea was to interview a huge sample of ordinary people with a standardized question -- have you ever had a vivid hallucination of a person? -- and then check how often those hallucinations coincided with the death of the person seen within about a day, against what you'd expect from chance overlap. And the coincidences came in way above chance. My claim is that this is a case-control study in embryo. They did in 1889-1893, with interviewers and tally sheets, what epidemiologists formalized a generation later. Compare that to the physicists of the day dismissing the whole business without collecting a single data point, and tell me who was behaving scientifically.

So my verdict: the ghost-hunting wasn't the rationalist going soft. It was the rationalist following his own argument off a cliff, in the most disciplined way possible.

---

That's the draft. Now the specific things I want from you:

1. Fact checks, since my library is three books and a filing cabinet. I have Sidgwick personally running the Brighton trance experiments -- but now I half remember it was mostly Edmund Gurney doing the hypnosis work, and that Gurney died before the census even came out. Which is it? Similarly I give the death-coincidence rate as roughly one in forty against a chance expectation somewhere in the tens of thousands -- if you know the actual figures, tell me whether I'm in the right ballpark, and if not, don't guess, just say so. And am I right that the term dualism of practical reason is Sidgwick's own label from the Methods, and not something later commentators cooked up?

2. The too-clever-by-half objection. The professor's version is: you're giving Sidgwick a post-hoc rationale. The man wanted there to be an afterlife, like most of us, and the philosophy was scaffolding built after the wanting. Suppose he'd never hit on the dualism -- does anyone believe he wouldn't have joined the ghost society anyway? Do you think my inference (from the ethical theory to the empirical program) actually has textual legs, or have I constructed a tidy Sidgwick who exists only in my draft?

3. The case-control analogy is the part I'm proudest of and therefore the part I most suspect. Is it doing real work, or is it a flattering anachronism? If the census was in embryo what I say it was, why did it persuade essentially no one outside the SPR?

Be as blunt as you like. I'd rather take the beating from you than in front of the reading group on thursday. Also apologies for any typos, I typed this up fast after dinner and my proofreading is not what it was.
