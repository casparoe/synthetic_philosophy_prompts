I'm a PhD student in bioethics, writing a dissertation chapter on what makes consent valid, and my advisor keeps telling me my argument proves too much, and now I can't unsee it. It started from the standard thought that deception invalidates consent: if you agree to something under a materially false belief that the other party deliberately induced, your consent isn't a genuine exercise of your will — the deceiver steered it, and the consent came out of their plans rather than your judgment. Fine. But the more I sat with it, the more the same structure seemed to show up everywhere without anyone calling it deception, and when I run the chain out it lands somewhere absurd. Here it is: (1) Consent is valid only if it expresses the consenter's own evaluative judgment, not somebody else's script. (2) Consent fails this test when the other party deliberately arranges what you believe so that you'll consent — that's why lies about contraception, or about being divorced, or about the side effects of a drug, invalidate the consent they produce. (3) Arranging what someone believes doesn't require lying: selective disclosure, curating which truths are salient, timing the revelation of inconvenient facts — these do the same job by another route, and they're done on purpose. (4) Ordinary romantic, medical, and commercial life runs on exactly this — nobody recites their debts on a first date, real-estate agents stage the open house with fresh bread smells, employers sell a "culture" during recruitment — and it's all aimed at getting a yes. (5) If deliberately arranging someone's beliefs to obtain consent invalidates that consent when it's done by lying, it invalidates it when it's done by curation, since the validity of consent can't turn on the packaging of the influence rather than it's content. (6) So most everyday consent — marriages, hires, purchases, leases, most enrollment forms — is invalid. Which is nuts.

My guess is something slips between (3) and (5), probably in the phrase "the same job," and I keep half-drawing the line with the old legal contrast between fraud in the factum and fraud in the inducement: deception substitutes false premises for your deliberation, curation just changes which true ones are salient. But every time I try to state that line it either looks circular (the bad influence is the one that invalidates consent) or it gets hammered at the edges — a staged open house is all true facts, arranged precisely so the basement never becomes salient, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's closer to the lie or to the curation. So: which step should I give up on, and what does giving it up cost me? For what it's worth I'd much rather keep (1) and jettison (5) than the reverse, but I want to know the price either way, and whether the distinction I'd need for (5)-is-false can be stated non-circularly. And if you think the argument is actually valid and the conclusion just has to be swallowed — that everyday consent really is mostly compromised and the doctrine of validity is mostly a polite fiction — I'd take that answer too, but then you have to tell me what a world structured around mostly-invalid consent is supposed to look like, because I don't think anyone involved in it behaves like they believe that.
