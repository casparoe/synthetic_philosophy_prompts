I work as a country analyst for a food-security early-warning service. Our bulletins currently tag each emerging crisis as either a natural disaster (drought, flood, pest) or a political emergency, and the tag drives our recommendation: food aid shipments versus pressure on the government over procurement, pricing, and export policy. I've been reading about Sen's entitlement approach and want it turned into something I can actually use. Please write a decision procedure I can run in the first weeks of a suspected crisis, using only data that arrives quickly (market prices, wage series, distress sales of livestock and land, migration, grain movements) rather than mortality figures, which come too late to matter. The procedure should start, as Sen insists, from who has lost the ability to command food and why, rather than from aggregate food availability — but it must also say when availability really is the binding constraint, so we don't deny aid to a genuine harvest collapse. Specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at what point the classification shifts from natural disaster to political failure, and where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to human judgment. Then run it on three scenarios: (1) a 20% harvest shortfall in a functioning market economy where wages hold; (2) a normal-harvest year in which inflation and lost purchasing power price grain out of reach of rural laborers, on the Bengal 1943 pattern; (3) a blight-driven harvest failure in a country whose government keeps exporting grain to service contracts, as in the Irish and late-Soviet cases. Include where the procedure gives an answer our donors won't like — such as laying the crisis at an ally's policy — and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.
