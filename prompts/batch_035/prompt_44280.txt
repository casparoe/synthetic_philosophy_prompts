Just got back from a three-hour argument with my flatmate about where the mind actually stops (the pub closed around us, it got heated). She's deep into the extended mind stuff from Clark and Chalmers, the Otto and Inga thing. Anyway, we ended up going through a stack of cases and I wrote down her verdicts as we went, and now I want to know whether her position hangs together or whether she's just vibing. Her record:

- Otto and his notebook: part of his mind, full stop.
- Inga's ordinary biological memory: mind, obviously.
- A healthy person who keeps notes in a phone app, checks it constantly but also double-checks it against other stuff and sometimes thinks, hang on, is that right?: NOT part of her mind.
- Someone playing Tetris who rotates the piece by hitting a button rather than doing it in their head: the button-pressing isn't cognition, it's just an action.
- A blind person's cane: yes, part of their perceptual apparatus.
- A navigation team on a ship running everything through a shared paper checklist: the checklist is nobody's mind. It belongs to the team or the system or whatever, but it's not part of any individual person's mind.

Then the two cases we never settled:

(a) A woman with face blindness who keeps a spreadsheet of her colleagues' distinguishing features (the mole, the haircut, the terrible laugh), updates it religously, and completely trusts it — but she reviews it the night before at home, because she can't exactly check her phone mid-conversation without being rude. Is the spreadsheet part of her mind?

(b) A songwriter who swears the songs live in her hands and that one specific piano together, and genuinely can't compose anywhere else. The piano is dedicated to the job, she trusts it utterly, but the actual composing through it is slow and effortful. Is the piano part of her mind?

So: what will my flatmate say on (a) and (b), and what should she say if she wants to stay consistent with her own record? What rule is she actually running — something like trust-plus-accessibility, or a fluency criterion where it only counts if it's seamlessly integrated, or something about dedicatedness to a single job? Are there other rules that fit her track record just as well but come apart on (a) and (b)? And what further case should I spring on her next time to actually separate the candidate rules — I was thinking something internal but effortful, like a mnemonic she has to consciously grind through in her own head, since that would tell us whether it's fluency doing the work or just location. I'd like to be armed this time.
