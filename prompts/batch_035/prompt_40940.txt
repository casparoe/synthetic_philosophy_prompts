Until about six months ago I was a complacent Copenhagen-style instrumentalist, but a graduate seminar on the measurement problem has gradually persuaded me to take the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation seriously, and I have now committed to writing my term paper on what is widely regarded as its most embarrassing issue: why a rational agent facing a quantum measurement should care about the future branches in proportion to their squared amplitudes rather than simply counting branches and caring about each equally. As I understand the state of the debate, there are two principal strategies for answering this, and I want my paper to be organized as a head-to-head comparison of them rather than as a survey. The first is the decision-theoretic program pioneered by David Deutsch and elaborated most fully by David Wallace: start with an agent who has preferences over future experiences, impose axioms of rationality on those preferences, and show that the squared amplitudes end up playing precisely the role that probabilities play in classical decision theory, so that weighting branches by their amplitudes is simply what it is rational to do. The second is the self-locating uncertainty program associated with Sean Carroll and, if I have the attribution right, Charles Sebens: shortly after a measurement but before looking at the result, the agent is uncertain which branch she is in, and a suitable principle of indifference over indistinguishable copies of herself is supposed to deliver credences matching the Born weights rather than a branch count.

Here is what I would like from you. First, lay out the two approaches as precisely as you can while staying non-formal: for each, state exactly what is taken as primitive (preferences over outcomes, or credences about self-location?), what is treated as derived, and the point at which the squared amplitude first does any work. Second, evaluate the two against each other on the criteria that should matter to an Everettian: circularity (does either approach smuggle probability in through the back door, for instance by assuming an indifference principle that is itself already probabilistic?), robustness (what happens when the two branches are unequal in weight, or when it is disputed how many distinct observers a branch contains?), and the strength of the conclusion (does the approach show only that an Everettian agent should bet as if the Born rule held, or something more about what probability actually is in a fundamentally deterministic theory?). Third, be honest about where each is most vulnerable: I have gathered that critics accuse the decision-theoretic axioms of quietly encoding Everettian commitments rather than pure rationality, and that the self-locating approach has to say something delicate about unequal branch weights and about what counts as one copy — I would like to know whether these charges stick, and whether either program could coherently borrow the other's strengths. Finally, since my paper needs a defensible thesis: in your judgment, does either approach explain why the Born rule has the particular form it does — this function of the amplitudes, rather than some other weighting — or do both merely justify our using it? Please argue for a verdict rather than assuring me that the matter is contested.
