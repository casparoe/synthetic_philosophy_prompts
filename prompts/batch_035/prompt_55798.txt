Two members of my forecasting team have been arguing past each other for two years, and I've asked them to run an adversarial collaboration. I need the joint statement they should produce. The first is an inside-view partisan: she trusts her object-level reasoning, treats her own calibration record as one more piece of evidence to weigh from the inside, and holds that wholesale deference to others' opinions is self-undermining — if every reasoner moderated toward the aggregate of the others, the aggregate would eventually contain no independent information at all. The second is an epistemic modesty partisan: he holds that learning of an informed person's disagreement is itself substantial evidence, that you should weight your own credence roughly as one member of a reference class of similarly situated reasoners, and that inside views are least reliable precisely where they feel most compelling.

Draft the document both could sign. It should contain the following parts:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. These should be neither vacuous nor slanted, and I'd expect candidates to include: that Aumann's agreement theorem is valid as mathematics and that its practical import turns entirely on whether its common-prior and common-knowledge assumptions are ever satisfied among real reasoners; that the inside view and the outside view are not rival authorities but different kinds of input, so that the question is how they compose; and that the force of any outside view depends on a choice of reference class that is itself a judgment someone has to make. Refine, add, or strike as needed so both sides would actually sign.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement in wording the relevant side would accept for its own view. At minimum, address these candidates (sharpen or split them if the parties would):
   - The doppelgänger case: you learn that a duplicate of you, sharing your memories and reasoning habits, who differs only in how a single coin landed and in what each of you observed of it, assigns a credence noticeably different from yours on some question. Is the rational response to split the difference, to hold firm, or something else — and what does each answer commit its side to?
   - Whether your own track record — calibration data on similar past calls — counts as inside-view or outside-view evidence, and which adjudicates when the two conflict.
   - Whether deference weights can be bootstrapped from the very judgment being deferred to: if your weights on other reasoners are themselves set by your own assessment of them, does modesty collapse into a fixed point that is either ill-defined or just your original opinion relabeled?
   - Whether the empirical record of forecasting tournaments, prediction markets, and aggregation algorithms settles anything, and if so for which class of questions — well-specified short-horizon forecasts versus long-horizon one-off judgments where the reference class is contested.

3. A cruxes section: for each genuine crux, identify a test, argument, or concrete case both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, stated specifically enough that it could come out wrong.

4. A short section stating which, if any, parts of their dispute are verbal rather than substantive — for instance, whether some of it turns on what deserves to be called "evidence" rather than on what to actually do with one's credences.

One epistemic requirement: throughout the document, keep what is settled separate from what is genuinely contested among people who know this terrain. The mathematics is settled; the existence of robust overconfidence findings in some forecasting settings is largely settled; but whether the self-undermining argument against modesty actually works, and whether disagreement with an apparent peer is better modeled as evidence about the proposition or as evidence about which party is confused, are live questions — do not present either side's framing of these as the consensus. Also flag, rather than paper over, any agreed claim whose wording has been chosen to conceal a disagreement the parties were unable to resolve.
