I'm a graduate student who has spent this term working through the paradox of deontology, and I've come to hold a fairly specific version of the view: there are genuine agent-relative constraints — it is wrong for me to kill one innocent person even to prevent five comparable killings by others — but these constraints are not absolute. They have thresholds, so that when the numbers on the other side become catastrophic enough (thousands, millions), the constraint gives way and I may, indeed must, minimize violations. I arrived at this position through a seminar paper and a long-running argument with a consequentialist friend who keeps pressing the paradox itself: that a deontologist like me must sometimes be prepared to let more total violations of the very constraint I cherish occur rather than violate it once myself, and that this is close to incoherent. He also claims that the threshold concession simply shows my view collapsing into consequentialism under pressure, and that once I've accepted thresholds I have no principled place to stop.

I want to know what would count against my position, and I want the full map of its exposure, not just the familiar points. Include, where you think they belong on the list: the paradox itself taken to extremes (letting enormous numbers die rather than dirtying my own hands once); experimental work suggesting that deontological judgments in nearby cases are driven by unreliable or morally irrelevant processes, with the personal/impersonal trolley contrast as the classic stimulus; cases where the doing/allowing or intending/foreseeing distinctions seem to make no difference to my own judgments once I introspect carefully; the charge that a threshold has no principled location and is therefore arbitrary, wherever I draw it; and anything else you think deserves a place. Include developments that have arguably already occurred, and be honest about whether my qualifications — above all the threshold — have insulated the view into unfalsifiability, and whether that would even be a problem for a view of this kind, as opposed to an empirical theory.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, ranked from most to least decisive. Each item should be one or two sentences: state the defeater, what would have to be true for it to be a genuine one, and whether a committed threshold deontologist could concede it while keeping the view intact. No introduction or conclusion — just the five items.
