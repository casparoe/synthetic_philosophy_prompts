My partner and I have just set up lasting powers of attorney under the Mental Capacity Act here in England, and the experience has left me stuck on a problem I can't solve, so I'm hoping you can help. I'm a software engineer by trade -- no philosophy background at all -- but I spend my working life reasoning about state and persistence, and I suspect that's why this argument feels to me like a question about whether a process is still the same process after its memory has been overwritten.

A colleague of mine argues that advance directives (living wills, to the older among us) should never bind once dementia is advanced, and I can't find the flaw. His argument: (1) We honour an advance directive because it is the patient's own decision -- the person, whilst competent, chose how their later self should be treated, and self-determination extends across time. (2) A decision about someone is only authoritative if that someone is the decider himself; I cannot dictate your treatment, and the signer can only dictate his own. (3) What makes a later person the same as an earlier one is psychological continuity: memory, values, character and plans carrying forward. (4) Advanced dementia destroys precisely that; the patient in the care home retains little or nothing of the signer's memories, values and plans. (5) So in advanced dementia, the patient is not the person who signed -- that person no longer exists, in the way a person ceases to exist at death. (6) Therefore the directive has no owner; there is no longer anyone whose own future treatment was decided, and it should be set aside in favour of the patient's current welfare, assessed day by day.

What makes me suspect the argument proves too much is that I can run the same reasoning against commitments nobody questions. My partner and I have been together for two decades; on my colleague's view, the person I made certain promises to has been progressively replaced by whoever she is today, so the promises should carry only whatever residual force the remaining psychological overlap supports. The mortgage I signed at twenty-eight binds a man who has since replaced most of his opinions, half his memories and all of his knee cartilage. A pension, a will, even a decision recorded yesterday is void to the degree its maker has drifted. Every long-horizon obligation becomes a probability rather than a fact, and my colleague cheerfully accepts that: he says continuity comes in degrees, the directive's authority comes in degrees with it, and wherever you set the threshold, a directive is fully binding the day before the patient crosses it and fully void the day after -- which he regards as no more embarrassing than any other threshold. I'd very much like to keep (1), because the practice of honouring directives strikes me as sound, and I'd like to reject (6). But which premise or step should I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me? In particular, if I reject (3) and say the patient in the care home simply is the signer -- some bodily or animal continuity doing the work -- does that not make her current refusals count as self-determination too, in which case the directive seems to lose the very authority I was trying to secure? I want a diagnosis of where the argument goes wrong (if it does), not just a verdict on its conclusion.
