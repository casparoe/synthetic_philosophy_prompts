Retired geophysicist here — spent about thirty years in paleoclimate work, ice cores and the like, and since retiring I've been binging popular and semi-popular history, especially the kind where my old subject matter shows up. Tambora and the year without a summer, the Little Ice Age and the witch trials, plague and the end of feudalism, germs doing the heavy lifting in the conquest of the Americas. And I keep noticing something about the prose that I don't have a word for. When the humans are on stage the verbs are ordinary: people decided, sold grain, raised taxes, fled. But the moment my old freinds enter the story, the grammar shifts. The eruption "reshapes" the century, the climate "pushes" Europe into turmoil, the bacterium "writes" the history. Sometimes a whole chapter gets structured so the volcano is effectively a character with an entrance and a role, and the humans get demoted to reacting to it, like weather in someone else's story.

Two more observations before I ask my actual question. First, when an interviewer pushes these writers on it, they almost always retreat to something like "obviously that's shorthand, no one thinks a volcano has intentions" — but then their actual explanations keep leaning on the nonhuman cause as a member of the cast rather than a part of the mechanism, and the passages where humans deliberated end up doing less explanatory work than the passages where the climate "did something." So I genuinely can't tell whether the framing is doing real causal work in their accounts or is just decoration nobody is willing to either defend or drop. Second, the genre can also swing to the opposite failure, which I'd call over-correcting: the volcano is reduced to backdrop, all the agency goes back to kings and markets, and — having actually looked at eruption chronologies for a living — that seems equally distorted to me. The eruption really was a huge exogenous input. So these writers seem caught between crediting the volcano too much and too little, and the "it's just rhetoric" defense lets them never say which they're doing.

So: is there an established name or literature for this move — treating a nonhuman cause as an actor or character in historical explanation rather than as a mechanism, in a way the author won't fully own but the explanations depend on anyway? I know "environmental determinism" as the standard accusation against this whole genre, but that label is about the content of the claim (climate fixes outcomes), not about the thing I'm pointing at, which is the attribution of something like agency and the odd rhetorical state around it. I also half-remember the term "actant" from skimming some sociology of science, but I can't tell whether it's the thing itself or just adjacent to it. If there's a real term, tell me what it is and how exactly it differs from plain determinism; if there isn't, coin one, and tell me where the edges are — e.g., is a flood that breaks a levee an actor while the levee isn't, or does the levee count too once you're on this road, and does a one-off eruption differ from a slowly shifting climate baseline that has no "event" at all? I don't want just a name, I want the carving. Please end your answer with "Verdict:" followed by the single best term (existing or your coinage) for the thing I described.
