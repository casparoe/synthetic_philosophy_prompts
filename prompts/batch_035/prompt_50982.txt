My grandmother died last month at 88. She taught philosophy for forty years and, for at least the last thirty of them, she was a true believer in Moritz Schlick's take on the meaning of life — she taught it, and more importantly she lived by it. In case you don't know it (I only half did until I had to clean out her office): Schlick, the Vienna Circle guy, wrote this little essay in the late 1920s on the meaning of life. His answer was basically that meaning doesn't come from purposes. If you justify your life by what it's *for*, you just push the question back a step — you reach the goal, and then what? So meaning isn't in the striving. He thought it's found in play: activity that is its own point, done for its own sake, the way children play or the way a musician loses an entire afternoon in practice and doesn't notice. Work has value only as a means; play doesn't need to be *for* anything. He also thought youth feels more meaningful than age because there's more play in it, so the whole art of living is keeping play alive while the responsibilities pile up.

That was her, exactly. She played recorder badly, on purpose, in a group that only ever performed for itself. She did jigsaw puzzles of paintings she didn't even like. She collected weird words in a notebook labeled "evidence."

I'm giving the eulogy, and I need help. The room is mixed: half the family thinks the meaning of life is the grandkids (she adored them, but that framing would have made her cringe), a couple of cousins are seriously religious and will bristle if the eulogy quietly declares the universe pointless, and most of them have never read a page of philosophy and just want to be comforted. So: write it so its true to her Schlickian view — no cosmic purpose snuck in, and no framing her life as "fulfilled" or "complete" in the teleological sense she'd have rolled her eyes at. It has to land as warm, not as a lecture. And one hard constraint she'd insist on: the comfort should come from evoking her play itself, not from talk of legacy — legacy is exactly the "meaning by proxy" she considered a dodge.

Keep it around five minutes spoken. Then, after the eulogy, add one last line on its own in the form \answer{three to six words} naming the single image the whole thing turns on — that's going to be the caption under her photo in the program, so it should work standing alone.
