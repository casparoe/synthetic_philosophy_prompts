I teach a 300-level ethics seminar, 15 students, one 75-minute session in a room with movable desks. We're about to hit particularism vs. generalism, and my students can recite the positions fine but they don't *feel* why anyone would give up on moral principles — or why anyone would trust a judgment that can't point to a rule. I want a game that produces both feelings before I name anything.

Rough idea I had (improve on it or replace it): an admissions tribunal for a fictional housing co-op. Phase 1, half the class (the "codifiers") get a training set of about 8 past applicant cases with verdicts already attached, and 15 minutes to write an explicit rulebook for future decisions. The other half (the "deliberators") get the same 8 cases but just have to absorb them — no writing rules down. Phase 2, both groups get 6 new cases that I've engineered so plausible rules misfire: the codifiers will probably write something like "lying on your application → automatic rejection," and then one new case is a woman who lied about her address to hide from an abusive ex, while another liar is otherwise great. The deliberators judge the same cases with nothing but their sense of the thing. Phase 3 is an appeals round where each group has to defend one of its own rulings to the other group — and this is where I want the deliberators to get cornered into saying out loud that the *same* fact ("she lied") counted differently in different cases, and to notice that this is not the same as having no reason at all.

I need the details actually worked out: case sketches (short, a few sentences each, the kind I can print and hand out), a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, some mechanism for keeping the deliberators from quietly inventing a rule on the sly (I was thinking the other team gets a limited number of "precedent!" objections, but you tell me), and a minute-by-minute plan that fits 75 minutes including debrief. In the debrief, map what actually happened onto the real positions: holism of reasons on the particularist side, Ross-style prima facie duties as the obvious "third way" students will propose, and please take seriously the generalist's best reply — rules as public, teachable, contestable standards, and the deliberators' unexplained judgments as exactly the thing you can't appeal. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which part of the actual philosophical dispute no 75-minute game can reproduce.

Format: give me the whole design as a single JSON object with these fields — "brainstorm" (short paragraph where you think through two or three candidate mechanics and say why you kept the one you kept), "materials", "phases" (array; each entry has name, minutes, setup, what_participants_do, what_should_emerge), "case_sketches" (array; id, text, and what_its_engineered_to_do), "debrief_map" (array of moment_in_game / idea_it_illustrates pairs), "failure_modes" (array of problem / fix pairs), and "cant_reproduce" (one honest paragraph). You're an experienced classroom-game designer with a philosophy background — think carefully before committing to the details, definately brainstorm before writing the JSON, and don't make the deliberator side secretly easier than the codifier side: the whole point is that both sides walk away having paid a real cost.
