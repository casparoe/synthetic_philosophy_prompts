Two of my friends, Elena and Rafi, spent most of a recent camping trip arguing about whether insect welfare should be treated as a top moral priority, and since I was the only one who heard the whole exchange, they've both asked me to say who won. I can't, so I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move. Elena, who works at an animal-welfare nonprofit, opened with the scale argument: there are on the order of ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, vastly more than all vertebrates combined, so even if you assign only a small probability to insects being sentient — she used one in a thousand — and even if their capacity for suffering is slight compared to ours, the expected total still dwarfs the expected value of spending the same money on human or mammalian causes. A fixed charitable budget, she concluded, should therefore go overwhelmingly to insects. Rafi, an ecology PhD student, objected first to the number itself: he said "one in a thousand" is not a probability but a confession of ignorance dressed up as one, and that multiplying an arbitrary figure by an astronomical count just launders ignorance into a confident moral verdict. Elena replied that probabilities under partial ignorance are exactly what expected-value reasoning is for, that refusing to quantify uncertainty is itself a decision with consequences, and not a humble one. Rafi then pressed what he called the fanaticism worry: if Elena's method is sound, then any hypothesis positing vastly more suffering than we can directly observe deserves the whole budget — if insect minds might matter, why not nematodes, which may be even more numerous, or any number of increasingly speculative cases — so the framework seems unable to resist betting everything on a minuscule chance of a stupendous payoff.

Elena distinguished her case from that kind of fanaticism on the grounds that the insect credence isn't pulled from thin air: it rests on ordinary empirical evidence about nociception, behavioral responses to injury, and centralized nervous systems, so it is not like an unverifiable stranger's threat. Rafi answered that this cuts against her, not for her, because the empirical evidence is thin and contested enough that reasonable experts land all over the map, and the result of the calculation swings wildly depending on where in that range you start — the input is doing all the work while looking like a mere parameter. Elena's last move was a retreat of a kind: she said that even if no precise probability can be defended, there are interventions for insects (for instance, raising welfare standards on insect farms that already exist for feed production) that look worthwhile across a broad band of credences, so the practical conclusion survives even where the exact calculation does not. Rafi said that might be right, but that it is a far more modest claim than the one she started with, and that once you grant it, it is no longer the scale argument doing any of the work — a billion insects each with a tiny chance of mattering is a different argument from "this particular farm improvement is robustly worth funding."

Please go through the exchange and say who is right at each step — I'd like ratings of each move rather than just an overall verdict, since both of them won some steps, I think. I'm especially interested in two things: whether Rafi's objection to using a made-up probability is a real problem for decision theory or just discomfort with numbers, and whether Elena's final retreat saves her original conclusion or quietly abandons it. And if there is something both of them are missing — some distinction that would dissolve part of the dispute rather than settle it — I'd like to know what it is.
