Keeping the rhyme scheme while bending the meaning slightly: fine. Keeping the rhyme while wrecking the meaning to do it: not fine. Dropping the form entirely and rendering perfect prose sense: also not fine, though I feel less betrayed, which embarasses me. Replacing a pun with a completely different pun that changes what the character is literally saying: fine, honestly great. Taking a poem that hammers the same plain word five times and varying it into five prettier synonyms because the repetition reads clumsy in English: not fine. Is there a principle that saves all of these — something like "in constrained writing the constraint is itself content" — and if so, why does the wrecked-meaning verdict still bug me so much? And what does the principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: rendering a strict fixed-form poem with a rough approximation that keeps the stanza shape and the mood but only occasionally manages the internal music. Answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each — maybe your best principle, the verdict it can't save, a rival principle, your call on my flip-flop case, and a further case that would decide between the principles — no intro or conclusion please.
