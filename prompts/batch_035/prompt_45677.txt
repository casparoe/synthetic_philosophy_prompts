I teach philosophy at a large state university, with a research interest in transformative experience, and this year our undergraduate advising office asked me for help with a problem they keep mishandling. Students come to them weighing choices the students cannot yet evaluate from where they stand — spending a year at a foreign university, leaving a science program for religious life, having a child before finishing the degree rather than after — and the advisors swing between two bad habits: asking "what do you want?" as if the student could answer that before making the choice, or quietly substituting the advisor's own conception of a good life for the student's. I would like a decision procedure the advising staff can actually run in a thirty-minute appointment, with no training in decision theory: an ordered set of questions that first sorts the choice into its class (merely novel, epistemically transformative, personally transformative, or both), then specifies what testimony from people who have made the choice can and cannot establish about what it will be like, then asks whether any cheap partial preview of the experience exists, and finally hands back to the student the one question that is about values rather than facts. The procedure must mark, honestly, the points at which no method can help — where the student must simply choose in the dark — and it must not let the advisor's own preferences pass as expertise. Please state up front which distinctions from the literature had to be dropped or compressed to make it usable, and where you expect it to misfire. Then run it on the three cases named above, including at least one in which it returns an answer the advising office will find uncongenial, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do.
