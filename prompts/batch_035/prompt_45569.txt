Last night a close friend and I spent four hours arguing about Humeanism on the laws of nature, and the argument has left me dissatisfied with the way the topic usually gets taught and examined. She conceded the standard textbook dialectic — best-system analysis on one side, Armstrong-style necessitation on the other, each with its rehearsed objections — but claimed that the real crux sits somewhere it is rarely put: the Humean's standards of simplicity, strength, and balance must themselves be held fixed by something, and whichever answer she gave, the position seemed to wobble. If our cognitive limitations and interests fix the standards, then what the Humean calls the laws looks like a projection of our epistemic situation rather than a feature of the world, and it becomes obscure why the laws deserve the deference we pay them in science. If instead the world itself fixes the standards, in a way that is not just another summarizable regularity of the mosaic, then the view seems to have smuggled in a governing or structuring element it officially rejects — at which point the difference from its opponent is far smaller than advertised. Her further claim, which I half-resist, was that the anti-Humean faces a mirror-image problem: the necessitation relation is a primitive whose instantiation we can never check against anything, so the appearance of extra metaphysical content may be illusory.

I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I want to put this on the final exam. Please draft a single essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, that presents students with this "problem of the standards" in the form I have given it — not a labeled objection they could recognize from lecture notes — and requires them to determine whether it genuinely divides Humeans from anti-Humeans or whether, once properly formulated, it is an epistemological problem that afflicts both camps equally and settles nothing about governing versus summarizing. Along with the question, write a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, organized around five must-hit points, with a one-line note for each point on the mistake that would keep a competent B answer from reaching an A.
