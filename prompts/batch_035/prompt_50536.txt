World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) rules create a category called the therapeutic use exemption: an athlete with a properly documented medical condition — asthma, say, or ADHD — may use a substance that is on the banned list, provided a panel is satisfied that the treatment returns the athlete to normal health rather than conferring an edge. So a cyclist with diagnosed asthma may inhale salbutamol at permitted doses, while her healthy teammate taking the same puffs purely to open her airways further is guilty of doping. Meanwhile, the same code allows athletes to enhance themselves in all sorts of other ways without a whisper of disapproval: altitude training, hypoxic sleeping tents, altitude-simulation chambers, advanced nutrition, laser eye surgery, and — for a period — even caffeine. I am puzzled by what exactly the exemption system thinks it is tracking.

What account of the therapy/enhancement distinction, if any, does the therapeutic use exemption presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory — is it a baseline view on which treatment restores some normal species-functioning level while enhancement pushes beyond it, or a fairness view on which the exemption merely removes an obstacle the athlete would not otherwise face, or something else entirely? Then tell me whether the practice strictly presupposes that theory or merely sits comfortably with several: could a purely fairness-based account, which says nothing about therapy and health at all, licence exactly the same exemption? Consider also the wrinkle that the surrounding code is officially relaxed about unequal natural endowments — no one bans a swimmer's wingspan or a distance runner's unusually efficient heart — so if the exemption presupposes that an athlete's own untreated body is the relevant baseline, does that presupposition conflict with the code's tolerance of natural inequality elsewhere, or coexist with it somehow? And where is the system philosophically underdetermined — which cases would it have to be settled by a further commitment it never states (the healthy athlete whose treatment takes her above baseline, the condition that is itself common among elite athletes in that sport, the grey zone between restoring and boosting)? Conclude by saying what would actually have to change in the rules if WADA abandoned the presupposed view and adopted instead (a) a blanket health-and-safety standard permitting any substance a doctor judges safe, and (b) a strict 'own baseline' standard banning anything that raises performance above the athlete's untreated level — and say which of the resulting regimes would resemble the current one more.
