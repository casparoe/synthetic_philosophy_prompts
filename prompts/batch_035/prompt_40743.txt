I work on alignment stuff at a mid-size lab, and a question about the debate setup we're testing has been bugging me. The pitch is: instead of trusting a model's answer, you have two models argue opposite sides of a question, cross-examine each other, and a human (or a weaker judge model) picks the winner, and you trust the winner's answer. Everyone justifies this by pointing at courts, or markets, or the idea that a lie is harder to sustain under cross-examination than the truth is. But when I try to state what the protocol actually presupposes, I get stuck. If the judge could tell which side is right, we wouldn't need the debate at all — so the setup seems to presuppose that adversarial scrutiny converts unverifiable claims into verifiable ones without anyone in the room, including the judge, being able to verify them. What theory of justification or evidence is that, exactly? Is it a proceduralist view where truth just is whatever survives the right kind of contest, or merely an empirical bet about lying being more expensive than truth-telling, or something else? And here's the part that really puzzles me: we also worry about the two debaters colluding, so we add rules to prevent it. A pure proceduralist shouldn't care about collusion — the procedure is the procedure, regardless of what's going on between the participants. So does the fact that collusion scares us reveal that we're only borrowing the courtroom analogy halfway, and what exactly breaks in the epistemology when the adversaries start cooperating? Reconstruct what the debate protocol implicitly commits us to, tell me whether it's a coherent epistemic position or a bundle of half-held ones, and say what would have to be true about lying vs truth-telling for the whole thing to work.
