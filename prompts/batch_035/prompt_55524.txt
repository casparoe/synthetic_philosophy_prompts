I serve on the pharmacy and therapeutics committee of a regional health plan whose coverage policy pays for drugs only when prescribed for the treatment of disease, illness, or injury. A newly approved long-acting growth hormone analogue has come before us for children with idiopathic short stature: height more than 2.25 standard deviations below the mean for age, with no identifiable underlying cause — normal growth hormone levels, no chromosomal abnormality, no chronic illness, no renal disease. The drug costs roughly $60,000 per year, and trials in this population suggest treatment adds perhaps four to seven centimeters of predicted adult height. The committee's discussion has stalled on a definitional fight rather than a cost-effectiveness one, and I am now responsible for circulating a written recommendation.

One member, taking a naturalist line, argues there is nothing to treat: these children are simply at the low tail of the normal height distribution, every physiological system including the growth axis is functioning within its statistically normal range, and on any account that ties disease to biological dysfunction, covering a drug for them is paying for enhancement rather than therapy. A second member, taking a normativist line, replies that this just shows dysfunction is the wrong test: these children face measurable social and economic disadvantages and, as adolescents, real psychological distress, and a condition that predictably impairs someone's ability to lead the kind of life a person can reasonably want is a disease for purposes of treatment no matter how well their hormones behave. She adds that our plan already covers conditions picked out by harm rather than by any lesion, so the first member's standard is not even the one we use in practice. A third member has raised a structural concern that cuts across both: we cover contraceptives, though neither pregnancy nor fertility is a disease, so perhaps the question of whether idiopathic short stature is a disease is simply the wrong question for a formulary committee to answer, and our real task is to state the criteria we in fact use and apply them consistently.

Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should give the committee a clear bottom line — cover, deny, or cover under specified conditions — with reasoning the other members could actually engage with rather than talk past. It should treat the naturalist and normativist positions as live options rather than caricaturing either, state explicitly whether the committee can or should avoid ruling on the underlying question of what disease is, and address the precedent worry about contraception honestly rather than dismissing it. Keep it to roughly the length of a real committee memo, and make sure the strongest consideration on whichever side you come down against is given its due.
