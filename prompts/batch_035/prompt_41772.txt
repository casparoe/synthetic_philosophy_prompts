I'm a type designer, and whenever the question of what makes an a an a comes up in my circles, someone trots out Wittgenstein on games: there's no one feature shared by all a's, just a bunch of overlapping and criss-crossing similarities, so the category is family resemblance all the way down. The people who say this usually aren't just making a descriptive point. They use it to argue that since there's no essence, there's no fact of the matter about whether some marginal glyph counts as an a — whether a design "reads" is just whatever conventions a community of readers happens to settle into, and a designer who pushes a form way outside the family isn't making a mistake about the letter, just doing sociology. But it strikes me letters differ from games in at least one big way: hundreds of millions of people have to recognize them at reading speed, in fonts they've never seen, at a glance, with no conscious deliberation. Audit the analogy for me: what maps onto what (the family, the members, the resemblance, the absence of a common feature), where the mapping breaks, and which of those breakdowns actually matter for the conclusion it's being used to support versus which are just noise. Then tell me whether the no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

And if family resemblance isn't the right picture, is there a better one? I keep half-remembering an idea from the letterform-cognition literature: a letter is like a role in a play that gets recast in every production — same character, different actors — with the interesting twist being that an a has to stay recognizable as the same character across wildly different costumes (styles), so the sameness lives somewhere orthogonal to style. Does that analogy carry more or less weight than the family-resemblance one, or does it fail differently?
