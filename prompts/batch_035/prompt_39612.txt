I write a long-form column on science and ideas, and I'm at work on an essay about what the current fashion for simulation talk has done to Descartes' old worry. At the heart of the essay I want to run an argument that I have not seen made in quite the form I want, and I would like you to make it for me.

The argument is this. The classic brain-in-a-vat scenario — a brain envatted from the first moment of its conscious life and fed a perfectly consistent world-simulation — does not show that the envatted thinker's beliefs about its world are false; on examination it shows that most of them are true. If a lifelong-envatted thinker's words and concepts get their content from the life it actually leads, then its word for tree refers to the tree-shaped structures of its virtual environment, and its belief that there are trees is true, much as ours is. Properly analyzed, the scenario supports a kind of realism about the envatted world rather than doubt about it. What survives of the skeptic's worry is narrower and, I think, more interesting: recent envatment. A thinker envatted last night, its memories fabricated wholesale, can have every present belief vindicated by ongoing experience while everything it believes about its past lies permanently beyond confirmation or correction. So the brain in a vat is not, in the end, an argument for doubt about the external world; it is an argument that our exposure to doubt is radically asymmetric in time — the present is checkable, the past is not — and that the residual skeptical problem is a problem about memory, not about the world.

Please make this argument in the following sections, in this order and at roughly the indicated lengths:

1. Thesis (about 150 words) — state the conclusion in its strongest form, including the temporal-asymmetry claim.
2. The skeptic's case, at its strongest (about 200 words) — present the standard reasoning from indistinguishable experience to global falsehood as charitably as you can; do not weaken it in order to beat it.
3. Lifelong envatment and the preservation of truth (about 450 words) — develop the claim that most of the lifelong-envatted thinker's beliefs come out true, and explain what "true" amounts to here without collapsing into metaphor.
4. Recent envatment and the genuine residue (about 350 words) — explain why the recent-envatment case resists the same treatment, and why the surviving doubt attaches to the past rather than to the present environment.
5. The strongest objection (about 250 words) — state and press the best reply available to the skeptic; for instance, that the argument silently assumes semantic externalism, or that virtual objects are not "real" in the sense that the debate was about — and say honestly how much damage it does.
6. Where this leaves the reader (about 150 words) — what someone who accepts the argument should conclude about the simulation chatter my essay is aimed at.

Throughout, argue rather than survey. You may draw on Hilary Putnam's anti-skeptical argument and on recent work that treats virtual worlds as genuine worlds, but do not present the thesis as theirs: the split between lifelong and recent envatment, and the relocation of the problem from the world to the past, are elements I am introducing, and I want them argued on their merits. Flag openly any step that depends on a controversial assumption rather than smuggling it through. And please do not append a bibliography or list of references; I will handle sourcing myself.
