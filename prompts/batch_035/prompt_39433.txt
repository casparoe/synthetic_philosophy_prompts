I'm a journalist — I mostly cover science and ideas for a general audience — and last month I was at a conference on ethics and criminal law to file a story about "neurolaw," which turned out to be less interesting than what happened at the hotel bar afterwards.

Two panelists got into it there. One is a philosopher who works on moral responsibility; the other is a law professor who used to be a prosecutor. The case they fought over: two drivers, both equally drunk, both equally careless, both texting. The first gets a flat tire two blocks from the bar and calls it a night. The second keeps going, and three blocks later a kid chases a ball into the road at exactly the wrong moment. The kid dies. Same blood alcohol, same phone in hand, same everything — except one of them had luck intervene before anything could happen.

(1) The philosopher said this is the cleanest case of what she calls resultant luck: the difference in how much we can blame the two drivers is produced entirely by factors outside their control, and she claimed that blameworthiness which depends on luck isn't really blameworthiness at all — it's the world grading you, not you.

(2) The professor said hold on, the law doesn't grade character, it grades acts plus outcomes, and it always has; attempt is punished less than completion in every legal system she knows of, and that's not a bug. Deterence works on outcomes, she said, because outcomes are what people actually calculate with.

(3) The philosopher said the deterrence story was convenient but that even the professor doesn't believe it, because if deterrence justified the gap, then a failed attempt that failed only by fluke — a jammed gun — should get the same sentence as a fired one, and nobody proposes that. Then she went bigger: even the driver's character, the thing we'd want to blame in both cases, is itself luck — temperament, upbringing, all of it. Control runs out everywhere, so if luck excuses, it excuses everything, and if it doesn't, the flat tire doesn't matter either. She seemed to think this was a trilemma the professor had to pick a horn from.

(4) The professor picked what she called the boring horn: yes, character is luck all the way down, and no, that doesn't dissolve responsibilty — it just means responsibility is something we do to each other rather than something we detect, like a quantum measurement (the philosopher winced at that one). The kid's death makes a claim on the second driver that the flat tire simply doesn't make on the first, and the asymmetry in how those two men will feel for the rest of their lives isn't an error to be explained away. The philosopher wants blame to be clean, she said, and it isn't, and the mess is the point.

I had to go file my story and couldn't stay for the end of it, and honestly I couldn't tell who was winning anyway. Could you referee it for me, move by move?
