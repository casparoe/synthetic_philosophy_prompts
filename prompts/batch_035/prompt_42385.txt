I'm a PhD student in political philosophy preparing a dissertation prospectus, and I have a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into a full proposal for a twelve-month graduate fellowship. Please don't include any bibliography or references; I'll supply those myself, and the proposal should stand on the quality of its framing rather than on citations.

The idea concerns the "equality of what?" debate — whether justice requires equalizing welfare, resources, or capabilities. My suspicion is that all three sides share a hidden assumption that has received far less scrutiny than their disagreements: that the correct metric of egalitarian concern is invariant across distributive units. The main positions are usually stated, and argued for, at the level of whole lives. But egalitarians also routinely make claims about shorter units — the workday, the school year, the decade — and my working hypothesis is that the argument for one metric over another may not survive the change of unit. Resources may be defensible as a lifetime metric but not a time-slice metric, because the conversion of resources into genuine capabilities takes time, is path-dependent, and interacts with people's prior holdings in ways a snapshot cannot capture; conversely, capability measurement may be tractable at the level of a life while hopelessly fine-grained at the level of an afternoon. If that is right, then some portion of the disagreement between resourcists and capability theorists may be an artifact of arguing at different temporal granularities without noticing it.

Here is what I'd like the proposal to contain. First, a precise statement of the question: what exactly would it mean for the correct metric to be unit-relative, and what would it mean for it to be invariant? Second, an explanation of why the existing positions leave this open — not a survey, but an account of which specific premises in the standard arguments for each metric are left unstated at which temporal grain. Third, a method section describing what I would actually do, month by month: I want it to be recognizably philosophical work (constructing cases, testing metrics against them, checking for internal coherence), not a disguised empirical study. Fourth, a statement of what a successful result would look like — including the possibility that the hypothesis fails, i.e., that the arguments for each metric turn out to be unit-invariant after all, and why that negative result would still be worth having. Fifth, an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for a fellowship year: which parts could be cut without gutting it, and which part is the indivisible core. Finally, since I want to submit this with my eyes open, add a closing section in which you identify the single weakest link in the proposal as you've constructed it — the step where my half-formed idea is most likely to fail, and what consideration, if it turned out to be true, would most undermine the case for funding the project rather than merely shrink it.
