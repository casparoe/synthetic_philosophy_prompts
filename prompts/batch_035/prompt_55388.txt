I'm a novelist, and the book I'm currently drafting turns on a decision my protagonist has to make in the dark, quite literally. She is a liaison officer who has parachuted into marshland she doesn't know, on the wrong side of her own maps. The country she's landed in is bisected by a river: everything east of it is held by the Red army, everything west by the Blue, and the Blue side is itself divided, by a canal, into a headquarters sector and a Second Company sector, with the headquarters sector making up about a third of the Blue side by area. Before the drop she had no reason to prefer either bank of the river. Then, on a crackling radio link, the only thing her operator can tell her is conditional: "If you're on the Blue side, you're twice as likely to be in the headquarters sector than in the Second Company sector." No unconditional news, no hint about which side of the river she's on. What I need to know, for the scene to be honest, is what her credence that she's on the Red side should be after that message — and I've discovered it's surprisingly contested.

A mathematically inclined friend of mine, to whom I owe the following, tells me this is a dressed-up version of what philosophers of probability call the Judy Benjamin problem, about updating on a bare conditional. Ordinary conditionalization is silent here — the message doesn't tell her any plain proposition is true — so one has to reach for a further rule. One candidate, which I'd describe as constraint-plus-nearest-distribution, treats the message as a constraint on her new credences and, among all distributions satisfying it, picks the one that stays closest to her prior in the relative-entropy sense; the scandal is that this rule lowers her Red-side credence below one-half, even though the message spoke only about Blue territory, which my friend considers a reductio of the rule. A rival family, which I'd call ratio-preservation or minimal mutilation, rescales the two Blue cells to honor the new two-to-one ratio and leaves the Red cell untouched, so her Red-side credence stays exactly one-half. My own credence that the correct update keeps Red at one-half is about 0.7; my friend gives it 0.3 and accuses me of mistaking psychological inertia for a norm. I need a third opinion, and I need it committed.

Please give me your own number for the proposition that the rational update leaves her Red-side credence unchanged at one-half — a definite figure with a definite derivation, not a survey of update rules that leaves the choice to me. Decompose it: the probability that there is a uniquely correct update rule for purely conditional evidence at all (as against the problem being underdetermined and several answers permissible); conditional on such a rule existing, the probability that it falls in the ratio-preserving family rather than the entropy-style family; the probability that the intuition "evidence exclusively about Blue can't tell against Red" is a genuine requirement of rationality rather than a defeasible heuristic that a legitimate rule may override; and any further terms you think belong, such as how to model the reliability of the radio operator or whether the message should be read as fully certain. Then tell me which component the final number is most sensitive to, where my 0.7 and my friend's 0.3 most plausibly disagree, and what argument or consideration would move your number the most. Finally, since this is for a novel and the scene must end somehow: in one or two sentences, state what your protagonist should do at the riverbank and whether she is being rational or merely conservative — commit to a verdict, even an unflattering one for my draft.

Please deliver all of this as a single JSON object with the following schema: "final_credence" (a number between 0 and 1 for the proposition that the correct update leaves the Red-side credence at one-half); "components" (an array of objects, each with "name" (string), "probability" (number), and "justification" (one line of string)); "combination" (a string explaining how the component probabilities yield the final number); "most_sensitive_component" (a string naming the component and why); "disagreement_location" (a string diagnosing where the 0.7-versus-0.3 split most likely lives); "evidence_that_would_move_me" (a string); and "scene_verdict" (a string of one or two sentences committing to what the character should do and whether that is rational or conservative). If a field requires more than a couple of sentences, keep it tight — the value of the JSON is that the reasoning is compressed into named, inspectable parts.
