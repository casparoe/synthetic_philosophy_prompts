I'm preparing a short position statement on vagueness for a graduate seminar on the sorites paradox, and before I circulate it I want a consistency check on the views themselves. Here is the package as I hold it:

1. Bivalence holds without exception across the statements involved in a sorites: for any fixed number n, the statement that a pile of n grains is a heap is either true or false, however borderline the case.
2. Vagueness is a feature of meaning itself, not a species of ordinary ignorance. Our not knowing whether a 57-grain pile is a heap is unlike our not knowing the number of moons of Neptune: it is rooted in how the word works, not in missing information that is simply out there.
3. The meaning of a vague predicate is exhausted by its use — speakers' dispositions to apply and withhold it, the penumbral connections they respect (e.g., that if a given pile is not a heap, then no pile with fewer grains is), and the conversational purposes at hand. Nothing beyond these facts could locate a boundary.
4. That said, our use contains no sharp cutoff: there is no number n at which the pattern of application switches over from heap to non-heap. The dispositions themselves are graded, wavering, and context-sensitive.
5. The sorites argument is valid, and it is unsound because the conditional premise fails: there is some adjacent pair of piles such that one is a heap and the other is not.
6. Even so, tolerance tracks something important: for any adjacent pair, no competent speaker — however reflective, however ideal — could be in a position to know that one pile is a heap and the other is not. Any difference between them is not accessible from within the language.
7. Semantic competence brings with it at least in-principle access to truth conditions: someone who fully understands a sentence must be able, in principle, to know what the world must be like for that sentence to be true.
8. When two fully competent speakers flatly disagree about a clear borderline case — one asserts of the pile that it is a heap, the other denies it — neither of them has made a mistake.

Tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be, and for each genuine tension, which of the implicated commitments has to give and what it costs to give it up elsewhere in the set. Order the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and where a pair looks incompatible but isn't, explain what dissolves the appearance. If the set is consistent as it stands, say so and show me how the hardest pairs fit together. I honestly don't know where the worst problem lies, so don't read anything into the order of the list.
