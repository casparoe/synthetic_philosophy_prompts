I'm a PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of environmental ethics and animal welfare, and I have hit a wall trying to figure out what the slogan "nature knows best" is actually claiming when it gets deployed in debates about wild animal suffering. Last term I sat in on a workshop on wild animal welfare where the following happened. One participant had been pressing a view with a long pedigree in the animal welfare literature: because most wild animals, counted by individual, are small, short-lived, and highly fecund — r-selected species living at population densities where famine, parasitism, and predation are near-universal — the average life in an undisturbed hectare may well contain more suffering than wellbeing, so that preserving or restoring wild habitat could, counterintuitively, make the world worse for the individuals living in it, while converting land to uses that support few or no wild animals could reduce aggregate suffering by preventing miserable lives from starting. A restoration ecologist closed the discussion by appealing to the slogan: nature knows best, so left alone the system settles into a state that is, if anything, better for its inhabitants than anything we could plan for them. Later in the same session, someone on the opposite side reached for exactly the same one-liner: nature knows best, and nature is red in tooth and claw; the best we can do for wild animals is to keep them from being born into it. Two directly opposed conclusions, one proverb.

That symmetry is what I want you to unpack. Please lay out, as a list I can bring back to the group, the distinct precise theses "nature knows best" could be expressing in this context — from readings so weak they are platitudes, through substantive but defensible claims, to readings that are substantive and probably false. As you go, I'd like you to pay particular attention to what I've started thinking of as the fitness–welfare gap: natural selection maximizes inclusive fitness, not happiness, and I suspect the philosophically interesting question is whether any reading of the slogan licenses crossing from the first to the second. A system can be exquisitely well-adapted at every level and still consist mostly of lives not worth living, if adaptation tracks reproduction and welfare is even partly decoupled from it.

A few readings I half expect to appear, though don't feel bound to my menu: something about self-organization and homeostasis in unperturbed systems (plausibly true, but note what it is true of — biophysical variables like nutrient cycling, not valenced states); something epistemic about our terrible track record with unintended consequences when we intervene; something axiological about natural states being intrinsically better or more fitting; and something much stronger about ecosystems being at or near an aggregate-welfare optimum. Classify and organize these as you see fit, and flag any further reading the two debaters were probably conflating.

Then close with the part I care about most: which reading the ecologist actually needed for his argument, which reading the pessimist needed for hers, and whether either of them could honestly rely on it. If the honest answer is that no reading available in this domain can support the strong conclusion either of them wanted, say so plainly — I want to know whether "nature knows best" is doing real work in the debate over whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering, or whether it is strictly a device for ending arguments. No reading lists or citations, please; I want the analysis itself.
