In a reading group on the future of work, one analogy keeps resurfacing whenever AI-driven automation comes up, and I want a proper audit of it rather than the usual slogans traded back and forth.

The analogy, as its proponents put it: when the internal combustion engine arrived, nobody retrained the horses. On their telling, the American working-horse and mule population stood at some twenty-odd million in the 1910s and 1920s, and within a few decades of the tractor and the lorry it had collapsed; the animals were not redeployed into growth sectors, and no adjustment process ever found them one — they simply ceased to be bred. The lesson drawn is that the human worker facing AI is in the position of the horse, not of the farmer or the driver: the machine is now taking over judgement and communication, not merely muscle, so there is no rung left on the ladder for displaced labour to climb to. From this the proponents infer that the reassuring historical record — every past wave of automation ended with new kinds of employment — is a story about a period in which humans were the only source of general-purpose competence, and that we should therefore plan seriously for a world in which most people cannot sell their labour at all, whatever we think that means for the meaning work gave their lives.

The other side of our group answers with the textbook account: the tractor did destroy farm work, and the grandchildren of farmhands became factory hands, clerks and programmers; the lump of work to be done is not fixed, and neither, historically, has been the kind of thing people would pay other people to do.

Please write your answer in British English, and structure it under the following headings, in this order:

1. **The mapping** (roughly 250 words) — set out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the horses, the tractor, the farmer, the breeder who decided the herd's size, and the horse's fixed, biology-given set of skills. Note which elements of the source case have no obvious counterpart in the target.

2. **Load-bearing disanalogies** (roughly 450 words) — identify where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for the conclusion it is being used to support. I am especially interested in which hinge the conclusion actually turns on: is it that a horse's occupational repertoire was fixed by its biology, that horses owned nothing and bought nothing, or that horses had no political standing and no say in whether the herd shrank? Test each candidate, and consider in passing whether the historical claims as stated are even sound (e.g. whether the herd's collapse was purely a mechanisation story).

3. **Incidental breaks** (roughly 200 words) — the disanalogies that do not matter for this use, and a line on why each is excusable.

4. **A better analogy, if there is one** (roughly 200 words) — a comparison that fits the target case more closely, and an honest statement of what it would and would not license.

5. **Verdict** (roughly 200 words) — whether the proponents' conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if so in what weakened form.

Please do not simply recite the lump-of-labour lecture or the standard talking points of either camp; the task is to determine what this particular comparison can and cannot carry.
