The exam question was:

"Explain why self-deception has appeared to some philosophers to be conceptually impossible. Distinguish the problem about the beliefs a self-deceiver would have to hold from the problem about the intentions she would have to have, and explain why each is supposed to generate a paradox. Can either paradox be resolved while retaining the assumption that the self-deceiver literally believes both that p and that not-p? Discuss at least one deflationary alternative to that assumption and assess whether it preserves enough of the phenomenon to still count as an account of self-deception."

The student's answer reads as follows:

"Self-deception appears paradoxical because deception is an intentional activity. If A deceives B, then A intends to bring it about that B believes something false, and for this A must believe the truth himself, since one cannot knowlingly [sic] transmit a falsehood without registering that it is false. When the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, this generates two paradoxes. First, the static paradox: the self-deceiver must believe p (the proposition she is deceiving herself into) while also believing not-p (the truth she needs in order to do the deceiving), which is a flat contradiction if belief is a single unified state. Second, the dynamic paradox: to carry out the deception, the agent must manage her evidence strategically — avoiding certain sources, misinterpreting others — and this management requires that she know which things are dangerous to look at. But knowing what the truth is defeats the whole project, so the intention cannot be executed.

The dynamic paradox is the deeper one. It shows that no one can set out to deceive himself in the fully deliberate way one deceives another person, and once we see this, the static paradox dissolves as well: if there is no intentional project of self-deception, then there is no agent who needs to hold both p and not-p in order to carry it out. What remains are non-intentional processes, and these do not require contradictory beliefs.

Some philosophers have tried to keep contradictory beliefs in the picture by partitioning the mind. Davidson is the representative here: he argued that the mind is divided into semi-autonomous centers that are only partially communicating, and on this basis he defended the deflationary account of self-deception, on which the self-deceiver never holds contradictory beliefs at all but merely ends up with the unwarranted belief p through biased evidence-gathering — selectively attending to favorable evidence, avoiding unfavorable evidence, and applying unequal critical scrutiny to the two sides. On this kind of view the self-deceiver is like a biased defense attorney building a case for her client, and garden-variety self-deception — the spouse who comes to believe her husband is faithful, the parent who believes his mediocre child is gifted — is explained without paradox. Some intentional behavior is involved in the bias, but the agent does not intend to deceive herself, which is exactly what the dynamic paradox demands. One might worry that this is just wishful thinking under another name, and that the deflationary account has preserved the paradox only by changing the subject; but the bias is responsive to evidence in a way ordinary wishful thinking is not, and so the phenomenon is preserved."
