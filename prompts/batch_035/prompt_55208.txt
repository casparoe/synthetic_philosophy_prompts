I'm a novelist, and my current book follows a fictional New England mill town from 1975 to 2000, tracing its decline through one family. Over dinner, two friends of mine got into an argument about the causal story in my outline. The sociologist said it was wrong in principle: what felled the town was the textile industry moving overseas, a structural fact that no account of what any particular person wanted could explain, since the individuals involved were largely interchangeable. The economic historian objected that structures don't do anything — only people do — and that "the industry moved" is shorthand for thousands of owners, bankers, and officials making specific decisions for specific reasons; a causal history that never bottoms out in those reasons is just bookkeeping. The sociologist answered that this is like explaining a traffic jam by each driver's wish to get home: true of every driver, and it explains no jam, because aggregate outcomes turn on how decisions interact, which is a fact about the system rather than about anyone's motives. The historian conceded something there, but said that once you ask what the system is made of, you're back to individuals doing things — so the aggregate level can compress the story, yet cannot add a causal link that isn't already present at the level of people. The sociologist's closing move was personal: as a novelist I can only write at the individual scale, so I'm professionally biased toward the historian's side, and the real question isn't which of them argued better but which level of description my book can honestly carry. I stayed out of it, but I'd like a verdict. Could you go through their exchange and tell me who is right at each step, whether either of them is missing something, and whether the sociologist's point about my bias is fair?
