I've been reading my way into the formal literature on Occam's razor — Kolmogorov complexity, Solomonoff's universal prior, minimum description length — and before I try to evaluate any particular proposal, I want the specification first: what would a theory of simplicity-based induction have to deliver to count as adequate?

Fix the purpose like this: I'm after a theory that could serve as a normative standard for how a rational agent ought to extrapolate from finite data — analogous to how decision theory is meant to say what it's right to do, whether or not any human could compute it. It does not have to be an algorithm anyone can run. But it should be more than a slogan: it should say something definite enough to have consequences, including quantitative ones (how steep the complexity penalty should be, not just that there should be one).

Write the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference I should be allowed to drop. Some candidates I want on the table; press on whether each is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of the Kolmogorov-Solomonoff camp:

1. Language-independence. The invariance theorem makes Kolmogorov complexity machine-independent only up to an additive constant, and at the small-data scales where induction actually bites, that constant can reverse every verdict. Is invariance up to O(1) a real requirement, or does it quietly concede that all the action is in the constant term — i.e., in the choice of universal machine, which the theory says nothing about?

2. Universality. The prior must assign nonzero probability to every computable continuation of the data, so that no hypothesis is ruled out a priori. But should it also cover stochastic environments, or environments that are themselves computable only relative to oracles? Where exactly does the domain of hypotheses need to end for the convergence guarantees to be more than a technicality?

3. Convergence. There should be a theorem, not just an intuition, that the theory's predictions approach those of the true hypothesis as data accumulates. How should the requirement handle the fact that known convergence results bound cumulative error but say nothing reassuring about what happens on the actual, finite prefix of data we will ever see?

4. Computability. The universal prior is lower semicomputable but not computable. If we accept idealization elsewhere, is that fine here too, or is induction different — on the ground that there's no canonical sense in which an incomputable prior is even approximated, since every computable approximation smuggles in a bias the theory doesn't track?

5. Vindication of actual practice. The theory should explain why the curve-fitting and model-selection habits of working scientists work — e.g., why parsimony enters as it does in regression and regularization — rather than merely redefining simplicity so that its own preference comes out true by construction.

If some subset of the desiderata can't be met together, that's a result in itself and I want it stated as one. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding live candidates — Solomonoff induction itself, MDL, regularization-style priors, Carnapian confirmation theory — and which requirement, if you dropped it, would let the weakest of those back in.
