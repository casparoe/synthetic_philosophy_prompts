hey, so I've been going down a rabbit hole on the automation debate, and it struck me that it's always framed in one of two ways: either (a) jobs and income (who pays the bills when the work disappears) or (b) "meaning" in this vague psychological sense — purpose, dignity, having your day structured by commitments, etc. I want to attack a different angle, and I'd like you to build the strongest case you can for it.

Here's the idea: the deepest thing lost when skilled craft and manual work gets automated is neither wages nor fuzzy "meaning," it's a kind of collective epistemic capacity — society-level know-how. Skilled work is where practical knowledge actually lives and gets transmitted (apprenticeship, having your mistakes corrected, learning what "good work" looks like up close). When the doing gets handed off to machines, the know-how atrophies within a generation or two, because there's no longer a population of practitioners keeping it alive. The end state is a society that can no longer explain, evaluate, critique, or repair its own artifacts — a kind of epistemic dependence on whoever operates the systems. Think of how almost nobody can explain how a fridge actually works beyond "it moves heat around," and now extrapolate that to carpentry, machining, farming, brewing, everything.

For this, act as a philosopher of technology who has spent way too long thinking about the sociology of skill — someone comfortable with ideas like tacit knowledge and craft traditions, but don't worry about citing anyone or naming names, I don't need a bibliography and I don't want you inventing one.

Before you write anything, please brainstorm a bit: there are at least two or three ways to sharpen the claim. Is the loss about *transmission* (the pipeline that moves knowledge between generations breaks), or about *critical capacity* (you need hands-on understanding to be able to judge whether the work the machines do is any good)? Those are different arguments with different consequences. Pick the version you think is strongest and say briefly why you picked it, then write the full argument carefully.

You also need to deal with two obvious objections. First: knowledge always lived in books and institutions anyway, not in practitioners' hands, so automation changes nothing about where it's stored — the library is still there. Second: maybe the "collective" capacity was always a myth, since even before automation the real knowledge was concentrated in a small class of experts, and most people were already dependent. Don't strawman either one, respond to the best version of each.

Tone can be conversational but the reasoning should be tight, no filler. Length whatever the argument needs, roughly 600-900 words. Also one small thing: if at some point you catch yourself thinking "but new kinds of skilled work will emerge around the machines" — hold that thought, I want that treated as an open question at the end, not a knockdown reply, since my suspicion is the replacement jobs are often downstream of the lost ones and inherit less of the know-how. Thanks!
