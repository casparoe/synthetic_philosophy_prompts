**Exam question**

*Quine and the indeterminacy of translation.*

(a) Briefly describe the situation of radical translation and the gavagai example from *Word and Object*.
(b) Explain the roles that stimulus meaning and analytical hypotheses play in the radical translator's project.
(c) How, according to Quine, does the indeterminacy of translation relate to the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence? Is the former simply a special case of the latter?
(d) Does the gavagai example by itself establish the indeterminacy of translation, or is more needed to establish the full thesis? Explain.

---

**Student's answer**

Radical translation is the limiting case in which a field linguist confronts a language with no interpreters, no dictionaries, and no shared history to fall back on. Quine's linguist observes a native utter the word 'gavagai' as a rabbit runs past and naturally guesses that it means rabbit. But the evidence would be exactly the same if the word meant undetached rabbit part, rabbit stage, or some totality of rabbit parts spread across space and time. Ostension cannot break the tie either: when the linguist points at a rabbit while saying 'gavagai', the native's assent behavior will be identical on all of these interpretations, since a pointing gesture does not by itself indicate whether the whole, a part, or a momentary stage is meant. Since all possible behavioral evidence is compatible with each translation, there is no fact of the matter about which one is right. This is the indeterminacy of translation: rival translation manuals can fit all possible evidence about the natives' speech dispositions while assigning incompatible meanings.

The translator's starting point is stimulus meaning: for a sentence, the set of sensory stimulations that would prompt a speaker's assent, together with those that would prompt dissent. Sentences that are stimulus synonymous — for instance, the native's 'gavagai' and our own 'look, a rabbit' considered as occasion sentences — match in stimulus meaning. But stimulus meaning only carries the translator a short distance, since most sentences are not tied point-by-point to current stimulation. To go further the linguist must posit analytical hypotheses: free-floating conjectures correlating native words and constructions with English ones, justified only by the overall simplicity of the resulting manual and by its fit with the stimulus meanings already established. Since more than one set of analytical hypotheses can satisfy these constraints equally well, the underdetermination of the manual is total.

On the relation to underdetermination in science: the natural thought is that this is just the familiar underdetermination of theory by evidence, now applied to translation manuals rather than to physics — just as two empirically equivalent physical theories can both fit the data, two manuals can. And that is Quine's view: the indeterminacy thesis is simply the thesis that translation is underdetermined by evidence, nothing more. If behavioral data could never decide between manuals, that is exactly the situation of the physicist choosing between empirically equivalent theories, and nobody concludes from that that there is no fact of the matter in physics.

Finally, the gavagai case does establish the thesis by itself: once we see that 'gavagai' could equally mean rabbit or undetached rabbit part with no behavioral difference even in principle, the indeterminacy of translation follows immediately — that ambiguity is the indeterminacy of translation, stated for a single word. Admittedly, Quine later supplemented the picture: in Ontological Relativity he relativized reference to a background language or scheme, which resolves the worry, since once we fix a background scheme there is a determinate fact about what 'gavagai' refers to, and one of the rival manuals is simply correct. What survives is that the translator's manual is at bottom a useful fiction — strictly speaking, the translator cannot translate at all, since no translation he could offer is true.
