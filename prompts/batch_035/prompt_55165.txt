For a reading group on Darwin's treatment of morality and religion, I have been keeping track of the interpretive verdicts of a friend who has read The Descent of Man many times over. So far she has committed herself to the following readings: she takes Darwin to hold that the difference between a loyal dog's affection and a human conscience is one of degree rather than of kind; that the near-universality of belief in unseen spiritual agencies reflects facts about our psychology rather than evidence for such agencies; that Darwin saw no tension between explaining self-sacrificial altruism through competition between tribes and continuing to regard such conduct as genuinely noble; and that Darwin's agnosticism was of a piece with his suspicion that a mind shaped for other purposes is not entitled to confidence about ultimate questions.

Our next meeting turns to a new case: whether, on her reading of Darwin, the evolutionary genealogy of our belief in objective, binding moral truths — sympathy, tribal competition, habit, and the praise and blame of the community — is itself a reason to doubt that those truths hold.

Predict her verdict on this new case. Then reconstruct the interpretive rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well — at minimum, a uniform debunking rule on which any belief that can be fully explained by evolution forfeits its independent authority, and a naturalist-but-not-debunking rule on which evolutionary explanation removes the need for supernatural posits while leaving our moral convictions' claim to authority intact — and state what each rule predicts about the new case. Give your best guess together with your reasons for preferring it, and specify one further interpretive question about Darwin that we could put to her on which those rules would come apart, so that we could tell which of them she is actually running. Please keep your answer under 450 words.
