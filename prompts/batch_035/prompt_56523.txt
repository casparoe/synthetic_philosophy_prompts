I'm on the grants committee of a mid-sized research foundation, and we have a genuinely divisive proposal in front of us that I'm supposed to write the circulating opinion on before our next meeting. The application is from a group that wants ~5 years of funding for a "historical dynamics lab": they want to digitize long runs of historical data (wage series, real estate prices, elite biographies, tax records, famine and revolt frequencies, that kind of thing, mostly Europe and China over the last two millennia), fit quantitative models of what they call secular cycles -- the long multi-generational swings where population growth pushes wages down and elite numbers up until instability boils over, then a crash resets things -- and then use the fitted models to issue actual forecasts, including a public instability index for the US and EU over the next 30 years.

The committee is split roughly down the middle. One faction thinks this is exactly what rigorous social science should look like: enough data, enough cycles observed, mechanistic models with feedback loops rather than just narrative storytelling, and testable predictions. The other faction thinks it's physics envy -- that societies aren't the kind of thing with stable enough parameters for this to work, that fitting a cycle to ten-ish historical cases tells you almost nothing, that every "prediction" is really a retrodiction dressed up, and that cycles are just a modern excuse for the oldest mistake in historiography, assuming the future rhymes with the past. The PI, for what it's worth, insists their field has already scored one big public win by calling rising US instability around 2020 well in advance, but a couple of us privately think that was closer to a stopped clock situation (anyone forecasting "instability" in a 250-year window was going to hit eventually), and the memo should be honest about whether that track-record claim can carry weight.

So: draft the decision memo the committee will circulate -- fund fully, fund with conditions (e.g., only retrodictive work, or shorter horizon, or only as a data-infrastructure project), or reject -- with reasoning the applicants and the dissenting committee members could actually engage with. It has to grapple with the real methodological question underneath the politics of the grant: what would it even take for quantified macrohistory to count as predictively successful rather than as elaborate curve-fitting? Things like how many independent cycles you'd need, whether societies that know about the model change their behavior, whether "instability" can be defined well enough in advance to make a forecast falsifiable, and whether the alternative (qualitative comparative history) does better or just hides its vagueness. Please take the strongest version of the pro side seriously rather than treating them as naive, since a memo that just says "history isn't physics" will convince no one and I still half think the pro side is right anyway.
