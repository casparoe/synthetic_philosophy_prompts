I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I've been chewing on a column about what AI is doing to how we understand things. Not the usual "will the machines replace us" stuff — more the quieter worry that offloading explanation and synthesis to chatbots leaves us with what I want to call "phantom competence": you can produce a competent-sounding take on quantum tunneling or the causes of WWI without being able to rebuild any of it from scratch. Below is my rough draft. I'd really value your comments — specifically on whether the central argument actually holds up, whether my analogies are doing the work I think they're doing, and whether anything here is just wrong. Don't hold back on the argument itself, I'd rather fix it now than get roasted in my inbox later.

Draft:

Every generation offloads some thinking to its tools, and every generation gets lectured about it. Socrates supposedly worried writing would destroy memory. The calculator killed mental arithmetic and we mostly agreed it was fine. So when people tell me that leaning on AI chatbots for explanations is "just like calculators," I want to agree — but I don't think it is, and the difference matters.

A calculator offloads a narrow, well-defined operation. You still have to know what to calculate, and you still hold the problem's structure in your head. A large language model offloads something different: the integrative, connective work of understanding — the part where you take scattered pieces and build a structure you could walk around in. When you ask a chatbot to explain inflation or the double helix, you get back something that feels like understanding, delivered so smoothly that your brain happily files it as understood. I call this phantom competence: the sensation of grasping a thing you could not actually reconstruct, defend, or apply in a novel situation.

There's a decent parallel in the GPS literature. Studies of London taxi drivers found enlarged rear hippocampi after years of navigating the city by memory; follow-up work suggested that people who rely on turn-by-turn navigation show weaker engagement of those same spatial circuits. The tool takes over the mental mapping, and the mapping capacity quietly atrophies. My worry is that LLMs are turn-by-turn navigation for everything: philosophy, history, statistics. The skill being hollowed out isn't a specific one like arithmetic, it's the general practice of sitting with confusion long enough for a genuine structure to form.

The counterargument I keep running into is that this is just what tools have always done, and that the smart people will use AI like a sparring partner rather than an oracle, coming out sharper than before. Maybe. But sparring requires already knowing how to fight. For someone who never built the underlying structure, the chatbot isn't a sparring partner, it's a ventriloquist. And unlike the calculator, which handed us back an answer we could check against the world, the chatbot's fluency is itself hard to check — fluency is exactly what we mistake for knowledge.
