I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of death, and for our final session on the Epicurean challenge I want a tier list rather than a survey. Rank the following five positions into tiers, from those you'd most trust to survive close scrutiny to those you'd expect to collapse, based on how they handle three matched cases I plan to run against each of them: (a) Lucretius' symmetry case — someone who dies at 80, after not existing for the billions of years before her birth; (b) a marginal-deprivation case — someone killed in a crash such that, had the crash not occurred, she would have lived exactly one further hour of ordinary, unremarkable life, then died anyway; (c) a no-attachment case — a severely depressed person who, before her death, had no future-directed desires at all and no interest in anything further happening to her. The positions: (1) orthodox Epicureanism, on which death is not bad for the one who dies because there is no subject or time at which it could be bad for her; (2) comparativist deprivationism, on which death is bad for you when, and because, the life you would otherwise have had contains more welfare than the one you actually get; (3) the desire-thwarting view, on which death is bad because it permanently frustrates categorical desires such as wanting to finish one's work or see one's grandchildren grow up; (4) subsequentism, on which death can be bad for you at times after it, in virtue of the goods your life then lacks; and (5) the hybrid view that grants Epicurus that death is bad for you only if you have future-directed interests, so that a being with none can lose nothing. For each position, give a one-sentence diagnosis of which of the three cases it handles worst and why, plus a sentence on why it earns its tier. Then close with a final line of the form

\answer{tier ranking: [position numbers ordered best to worst, with '=' between any that genuinely tie]}

so I can tabulate the seminar's answers against yours.
