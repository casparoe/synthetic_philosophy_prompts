Next month I'm defending a chapter in front of our philosophy of economics reading group, and I need a prep sheet for the Q&A. The audience: about half behavioral economists (nudge people, mostly, who are skeptical of the rational agent on empirical grounds but still use utility talk all day), two hardline neoclassical types who think Samuelson basically settled this in the 40s, and one philosopher of science who works on measurement and will absolutely come at whatever I say about utility from the invariance/identifiability angle. No decision theorists, so the really technical stuff can stay light.

My thesis: revealed preference, read strictly, gives you choice data and nothing else. The step from "she chose x when y was affordable" to "she prefers x to y" is not a definition dressed up as one — it's an empirical hypothesis, and a pretty bad one for a lot of ordinary data. My sketch of the argument, roughly:

1. Every choice under a budget constraint is a forced triage. The dataset records what you gave up, not what you wanted. If I grab my laptop in a fire, the data "reveals" I prefer the laptop to the painting, but calling that a preference gets the phenomenology exactly backwards. Budget lines are milder fires, but the structure is the same.
2. Consistency axioms like WARP are supposedly testable, but whether a person's choices come out "rational" depends on how finely you describe the goods. Split "coffee" into size, temperature, barista, time of day and a mass of apparent violations evaporates; lump things coarsly and they appear. So revealed irrationality is relative to a grain of description, and nobody has a principled way to fix the grain. The test isn't testing the person, it's testing the bookkeeper.
3. The fitted utility function is like a center of mass: a real number you can compute from the data, fine for prediction and aggregation, but with zero claim to be sitting inside anyone's head. Nothing wrong with that! The problem is only the slide from "u(x) > u(y)" to "choosing x made her better off," which the theorems never license and which everyone makes anyway.
4. So the welfare interpretation is a seperate normative leap, and it should be defended as one or dropped.

What I want from you: the objections in roughly the order they'll come at me, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them (the behaviorists and the neoclassicals will be annoyed at me for opposite reasons, which I expect to be funny). For each one: a two-minute reply, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Flag which objections have real force versus which are turf defense. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — you know, the one where the honest answer is "yeah, my grain-of-description point probably applies to a lot of the stuff I say in part 3 too." Tell me if thats actually fatal or just awkward.
