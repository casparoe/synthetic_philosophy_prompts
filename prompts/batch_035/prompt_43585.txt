Last week my team shipped a release with a couple hundred small changes in it, every one individually reviewed and tested. Right before the deploy I said I'm confident each individual change is correct, and I'm also confident the release as a whole has at least one bug in it somewhere. Maya (philosophy minor in college) said that's basically the preface paradox, and that set off an argument between her and Ravi that I couldnt follow to the bottom, so I'm bringing it here.

I'll reconstruct it in order. It went back and fourth but I can break it into five moves.

(1) Maya started by explaining the two paradoxes. Lottery version: a fair 1000-ticket lottery. For each single ticket you're 99.9% sure it loses, so if belief is just being really confident, you believe of each ticket that it will lose. But you're just as confident that some ticket wins. If belief is closed under conjunction — believe each member of a set and you believe them all together — you'd believe every ticket loses AND believe some ticket wins. Flat contradiction. The preface is the mirror image: an author checked every claim in her book and is confident in each one, but she's also confident (because books like that basically always contain at least one error) that at least one claim in it is false. Maya said these are the same problem in different clothes, and her solution was: binary belief is a fiction. What really exist are degrees of confidence, degrees aren't closed under conjunction, and once you accept that, both paradoxes dissolve.

(2) Ravi said the dissolution was way too fast. We run the whole engineering org on binary beliefs — a review is approved or it isn't, a release gate passes or it doesn't — and the contradiction shows up exactly when you put any threshold on confidence ("treat it as believed once you're 99% on it"). Thresholds plus closure give you the contradiction, so one of them has to go, and his vote was closure: rational binary belief just isn't closed under conjunction, deal with it. He also said Maya was wrong that the two cases are interchangeable. In the lottery, your confidence that ticket #7 loses comes from one global fact about the draw — the individual beliefs are faceless and interchangeable. In the preface, each claim got its own individual check. So the standard escape from the lottery — refuse to call purely statistical, symmetric evidence a belief at all — works there, but it can't be the whole story for the preface, which is what makes the pair interesting.

(3) Maya said the "purely statistical" test backfires. The author's confidence that somewhere in the book there's an error is itself purely statistical — "careful authors almost always ship at least one error" is a base rate, nothing more. So if purely statistical support can't ground belief, the author doesn't believe there's an error in the first place, and the preface case loses its second ingredient. Fine, no contradiction — but you got there by deleting the belief that generated the puzzle rather than by rescuing closure, and the same move applied to the lottery deletes the belief that some ticket wins, which is dissolving the puzzles by refusing to have the beliefs that make them puzzles.

(4) Ravi said he'd take that trade happily, and made what he called his real point: the difference between the cases that matters isn't the evidence, it's the number of propositions and what you do with them. With two or three claims you'd just look at the conjunction directly. At a couple hundred, you threshold each one, ship, and accept that your overall state is openly inconsistent — believe each change, believe the release has a bug — and that this is the rational state to be in, not a failure. He said insisting on closure at any cost is a leftover from treating belief like knowledge, and knowledge isn't closed under conjunction either: you can know each of two facts, he said, without knowing their conjunction, because knowing the conjunction would require knowing there's no undermining connection between them.

(5) Maya's last word: fine, but then say that. If rational belief is sometimes openly inconsistent, the interesting question isn't closure anymore, it's when you're allowed to act on an inconsistent set — and she admitted her "degrees of confidence" story in (1) was never a solution, just a relabeling. She said they'd both been circling the same thing the whole time: neither of them had actually said what makes it ok to ship the release. Then lunch ended.
