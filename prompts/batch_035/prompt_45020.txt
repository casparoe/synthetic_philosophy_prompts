A popular science book I have been reading summarises the Sleeping Beauty problem as follows: 'On Sunday, researchers put Beauty to sleep and flip a fair coin. If it lands heads, they wake her once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, they wake her on Monday, put her back to sleep, and wake her again on Tuesday, having given her a drug that erases her memory of any earlier awakening. Each time she wakes, she is asked how likely it is that the coin landed heads. The "thirders" reason that when Beauty wakes up she could be in one of three situations -- Monday under heads, Monday under tails, or Tuesday under tails -- and since all three awakenings are equally likely, the probability of heads is one third. The "halfers" object that Beauty knew on Sunday that she would be woken at least once, so on waking she learns nothing she did not already know, and she must therefore stick with one half; this objection is now generally regarded as having settled the issue in the halfers' favour. The deeper lesson of the puzzle is that there is no fact of the matter about self-locating belief: two perfectly rational agents with exactly the same information can legitimately assign different probabilities, which shows that probability theory simply does not apply to questions about where you are in time.'

I found this fairly gripping, but I have no way of telling which parts to trust, and the book's confident tone makes it sound as though the whole debate is closed. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on self-locating belief would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong? I am especially unsure about three things: whether the three-awakening argument is presented fairly, since it is not obvious that the three awakenings really behave like three equally likely, mutually exclusive outcomes of a single trial; whether the halfer 'no new information' point really counts as a decisive refutation, or whether thirders have standard replies to it; and whether the grand conclusion about probability theory not applying to temporal self-location follows from anything that is actually true in the summary. Please answer in British English.
