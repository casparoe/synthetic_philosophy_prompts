I write a column for a mid-size magazine covering science and ideas for general readers, and I've been noodling on a piece about the simulation argument. Readers always come away from these pieces either convinced we're literally in the Matrix or dismissing the whole thing as dorm-room sci-fi, and I want something better for the companion quiz we'll run online after the piece posts. So: write me a "which position on the simulation question do you actually hold?" quiz — around ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

The reason I need help rather than just doing my usual thing: I don't want any question that just asks "do you think we're in a simulation?" I want questions that sort people by the underlying commitments, so a reader discovers they actually hold, say, the view where simulations are basically guaranteed but it changes nothing, when they'd have sworn they were a skeptic.

Build it out of these commitments (roughly): (1) substrate independence — could a computer running the right program have conscious experiences exactly the way you do, or does something about the physical stuff matter; (2) whether posthuman descendants would bother running ancestor simulations at all, and whether "would want to" even matters if they could; (3) what follows if we ARE simulated — does it change what's real, what matters morally, what we should do with our lives; (4) self-locating probability — if the vast majority of people with experiences like yours are simulated, should you conclude you're probably one of them?

The leaves I'd like in the key, though rename them however reads best for lay readers: the "we're probably simulated" position that takes (1), (2), and (4) all seriously; the other two horns of the classic trilemma — civilizations like ours die before they can simulate, versus descendants just don't bother — which I'd like to be seperable by at least one question if you can manage it; the deflationary "even if we're simulated, tables and people are still perfectly real, nothing to panic about" position; the precautionary view that if you take the argument seriously you should be careful about investigating it, since simulators might not enjoy being noticed; and flat-out rejection, which ideally should be reachable from more than one direction (rejecting substrate independence, or rejecting the self-locating probability move, or just rejecting that the argument proves anything either way).

One question has to be about morality: if our simulators are watching, does that change our obligations? I keep meeting readers who think that's obviously no, and others who think it's obviously yes but in a weird direction — like we owe the simulators something, or their purposes give our lives meaning. If those people land somewhere identifiable in your key, great; if not, tell me why the quiz can't pull them apart, since that's honestly the part of the piece I'm least sure about.

Flag anywhere the quiz can't cleanly distinguish two leaves rather than fudging it. Keep the language casual enough for a general audience — no "trilemma" without a quick plain-English gloss. Ten questions, two or three options each, plus the key, with maybe one sentence per position on its most awkward commitment. My editor loves that bit.
