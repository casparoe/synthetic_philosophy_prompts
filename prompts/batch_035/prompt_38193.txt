Two people in my cohort (I'm a PhD student in political theory) got into a genuinely long argument after our department's grad colloquium committee meeting last week, and they finally asked me to referee, but I keep going back and forth so I'm outsourcing it. The case: our committee (5 of us) had to decide whether to invite a speaker back next year. Our standing rule says we reinvite only if (a) the talk was well attended, (b) the Q&A went well, and (c) the visit wasn't a logistical nightmare for the organizers. We voted on each condition separately, and here's how it came out: conditions (a), (b), and (c) each passed 3-2, though by different coalitions — only one person voted yes on all three. Then we took the actual up-or-down vote on reinviting, and it failed 4-1. So a majority endorsed every single stated requirement for reinvitation, and a supermajority voted not to reinvite.

Maya said the committee obviously decided to reinvite: our own rule says reinvite iff (a) and (b) and (c), a majority accepted each, so the conclusion follows from premises the committee committed itself to; the 4-1 vote was basically a formality we botched. Jonas said that's nuts — a committee can only decide what it votes on, and the only thing it voted on as a committee was the outcome, which failed 4-1; the premise votes were just straw polls of individual members, and the committee as a body never voted "all three jointly." Maya replied that this leaves the committee committed to p, q, r, and not-(p&q&r), which is flatly incoherent, so something in Jonas's picture has to give. Jonas said nothing gives, because groups don't have beliefs in the first place — only members do, and members were never individually inconsistent; the "committee's opinion" is just whatever its decision procedure outputs, and ours outputs the outcome vote. Maya then said that if the group's decision can completely ignore that a majority of its members accepted every stated ground for that decision, the decision isn't responsive to reasons at all, and maybe we should just always vote premise-by-premise, like a court deciding issues. Jonas's last word was that premise-by-premise voting is a gerrymanderer's paradise: whoever gets to phrase the premises controls the outcome, and outcome voting at least lets members weigh the considerations holistically (someone might think attendance matters enormously and logistics barely, and no premise vote captures that).

Can you adjudicate this move by move — who's right about which specific claim, and where exactly each of them goes wrong if they do? I'd especially like your view on the central question they never quite isolated: did our committee actually decide to reinvite this speaker, and what does that mean for whether the 4-1 vote is the decision, a mistake, or neither? And if you think one of their proposed fixes (always vote on premises vs. always vote on outcomes) is right, say how it would handle the case where the premise votes and the outcome vote conflict, since that seems like it's going to keep happening to us.
