I'm a third-year sociology PhD student and I run the department's film night, which I've basically been using as an excuse to make other grad students watch things I want to talk about. This time it's Dr. Strangelove, and I want to frame the discussion around something I keep tangling with in my own work on historical causation: Great Man theory vs structural explanation — you know, the classic "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler" debate, methodological individualism vs path dependence and world-systems stuff, whether individuals are genuine causes or just the interchangeable placeholder the structure happens to route through.

Here's why the film seems perfect for this. On the surface it's the ultimate Great Man story: one general, Jack Ripper, goes off the rails and single-handedly triggers nuclear war — one individal, one paranoid fixation, and the world ends. Remove Ripper and there's no war, full stop. But the longer the film runs, the more it looks like Kubrick is showing you that Ripper is the least important part of the causal chain: the fail-safe system that lets one man do this in the first place, the deterrence logic everyone in the War Room accepts as just obviously rational, the doomsday machine nobody thought to announce, the fact that even the President and the entire command structure trying their hardest cannot stop what the system's own design has set in motion. By the end Ripper feels almost like the coin flip that happened to come up, with the structure sitting there waiting for any coin at all.

So the ask: read Dr. Strangelove as a dramatization (or maybe a reductio) of individual vs structural explanation of historical outcomes. Does the film actually come down on the structural side, or does the comedy secretly depend on Ripper's idiosyncrasy in a way that undercuts the structural reading — could a properly structuralist story even be funny? Where does the film resist the reading, and which scenes or plot devices should I point the group to (I'm thinking about the fact that the recall basically works and the war still happens because of one damaged bomber, which seems important)? And since the historians in my cohort will inevitably bring up counterfactuals — the "no Hitler, no Nazi Germany" question — I'd like to connect the film to that debate: is Kubrick running the same thought experiment, and what does the film suggest the answer is? I want a reading I can actually defend to both the Weberians and the world-systems people in the room, not just a cute parallel. Also, do you think the ending — the whole "stop worrying and love the bomb" arc, the mineshaft survival plan getting bureaucratically organized about five minutes after the apocalypse — is the film saying structure wins even after the catastrophe, i.e. that even extinction just gets absorbed into organizational logic? That feels like the strongest version of the structural claim and maybe too strong to actually attribute to the film, so tell me honestly if it's me overreading.
