I teach a graduate seminar on collective action and rational choice, and we have just finished Scott Alexander's essay "Meditations on Moloch" (2014). My students have absorbed the canonical picture of the multipolar trap: no actor can profitably deviate, no contract can be enforced, and the race to the bottom leaves every participant worse off than some coordinated alternative they can all see plainly. They have also absorbed the complacency that comes with it — the assumption that the logic always points the same direction, toward waste.

For next week I want a thought experiment that cuts against the grain. Construct a scenario in which a genuine multipolar trap is, ex ante, better for the very actors caught in it than the coordinated alternative would be — a race to the bottom that beats the escape hatch.

The scenario must satisfy the following constraints:

1. Realistic setting: firms, labs, sports leagues, city governments — no science fiction, no exotic enforcement technology, and no actors with unusual altruism or foresight. The racers should be ordinary self-interested parties who can read a balance sheet.

2. It must be a genuine trap on its face: given that the others race, racing is strictly dominant for each participant; no binding contract, cartel, or regulator is available; and in the one-shot static version of the game the race equilibrium is Pareto-inferior to a feasible coordinated alternative. That alternative must be honestly described — a working consortium or standards arrangement that credibly shares the gains — not a strawman utopia.

3. The twist: measured ex ante, from a point before anyone could commit, the total expected welfare of the participants themselves must be strictly higher under the trap than under the coordinated alternative. Third parties may not carry the argument; the racers themselves must be the beneficiaries.

4. The metric being raced on must be locally valid: each individual step down must look, at the time and to the actor taking it, like a genuine improvement in her own position — the trap must not depend on anyone being mistaken about her incentives or sacrificing knowingly.

Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. Then demonstrate that each constraint is met: exhibit the dominance reasoning, explain why enforcement fails, and explain why the trap wins on expected welfare despite losing the static Pareto comparison. Next, identify the single feature of the case that does the work — the thing whose removal would collapse it back into an ordinary wasteful race — and describe what happens when you remove it. Finally, give the strongest objection available to someone committed to the standard Moloch picture; I anticipate the charge that the case merely shows the raced metric was mis-specified from the start, and I want to know whether that reply is substantive or merely begs the question against the possibility the case is meant to establish. Assess whether the objection succeeds.

End your answer with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed only by a short phrase naming the feature that does the work.
