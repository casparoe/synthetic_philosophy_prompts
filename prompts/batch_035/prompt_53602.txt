I teach a course on philosophy of art with a unit on the Harlem Renaissance debates, and next month I've been invited to give a guest lecture to an MFA cohort at an art school — mostly studio artists in their late twenties, a couple of community muralists, one performance person who does explicitly political work and is basically already a Du Boisian without knowing it. My thesis, which I'm going to defend straight up: Du Bois's claim in his 1926 address (the one published in The Crisis as The Criteria of Negro Art) that all art is propaganda was not a philistine blunder he should be embarrassed about, but basically correct given the situation he was speaking into — when a group is despised, its art inevitably functions as testimony about the group whether the artist wants that or not, so the real choice isn't between propaganda and purity but between propaganda you own and propaganda that gets done to you. I want to frame it as Du Bois pressing this against what he saw as the flight into mere beauty, and I'll note that his own examples skew literary and that the beauty/truth dichotomy he works with is doing a lot of quiet work.

I know roughly the room. Several of them will have absorbed the stock line that Du Bois was the propaganda guy and Alain Locke the sophisticated one who wanted art judged as art, and a few will have personally experienced the thing where a gallery or a grant committee expects work by Black artists to be legibly about race — so my thesis will sound to them like a defense of exactly the box they're trying to climb out of. One of the muralists will say the propaganda frame hands critics a shortcut for dismissing anything they don't want to look at. Somebody quieter, probably one of the abstract painters, will raise the Kantian-ish point about aesthetic autonomy without using that vocabulary, and the performance artist may come at me from the other side and say Du Bois didn't go far enough, or that his version of uplift is itself a kind of respectability politics.

So what I need from you is a proper prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they could do to my thesis, each phrased the way a working artist would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would. For each one: a two-minute reply I could actually deliver at a podium, a note on where the reply is soft or depends on stipulations I haven't earned, and what I should just concede rather than fight. Flag the single objection that has to be answered well for the whole thing to survive, and tell me the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks — and what I should say if it comes anyway. If it matters for any of the replies, assume I can gesture at the Locke side of the debate and at how the surrounding argument in the address actually runs, but I don't want the prep to turn into a literature review.
