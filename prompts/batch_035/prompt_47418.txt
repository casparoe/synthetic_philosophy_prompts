I'm a science journalist writing a long-form piece on the controversy over evolutionary psychology, and I've hit a wall on the framing. When I described the field to a developmental biologist I know, she waved it off: evolutionary-psychology explanations are just-so stories, in the sense of Kipling's tales — you observe a trait, invent a pleasing Pleistocene scenario to explain it, and nothing could ever count against the story. She invoked the tradition running from Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper onward. When I put this to the evolutionary psychologist whose work I'm profiling, she didn't just reject the label; she substituted her own analogy: what she does is reverse-engineering, like inferring the purpose of an unfamiliar tool from the details of its design, and she claims that the design features she points to — the shape of a jealousy response, the timing of morning sickness — constrain her hypotheses in a way Kipling never was constrained. I want to audit both analogies rather than pick a side by vibes. Lay out what maps onto what in each: the story, the author, the reader, the constraint, the possibility of being wrong; and the tool, the engineer, the design brief, the marketplace of the manufacturer. Then tell me where each mapping breaks — whether adaptationist hypotheses about the mind really can fail, and against what; whether story-telling in evolutionary explanation is a defect of method or just how historical inference has to work (we tell origin stories about the Grand Canyon too, without anyone calling them unscientific). And does the biologist's dismissive conclusion survive the audit, or was the Kipling frame doing the work of the argument all along? I'd like to be able to quote something sharper than "critics say, proponents respond" in my piece.
