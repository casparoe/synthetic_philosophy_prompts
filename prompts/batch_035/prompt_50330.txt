"Rawls claims that utilitarianism 'does not take seriously the distinction between persons'. Explain what this objection amounts to and assess whether it succeeds."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Rawls's objection is that utilitarianism results from taking the principle of rational choice for a single individual and applying it to society as a whole. A prudent individual can accept a loss now — visiting the dentist, say — because the very same person is compensated later by healthy teeth. Utilitarianism, Rawls says, is 'the consequence of extending to society the principle of choice for one man, and then, to make this extension work, conflating all persons into one'. The problem is that there is no social entity that receives the compensation: if a policy imposes a loss on A and a benefit on B, A simply bears the loss and B gets the gain, and 'utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons' because it treats B's gain as cancelling A's loss just as one person's later gain cancels their own earlier loss.

Nozick made the same point earlier in Anarchy, State, and Utopia, arguing that using one person for another's benefit fails to respect the fact that his is the only life he has, since he receives no overbalancing good from his sacrifice. Together Rawls and Nozick show that between persons there is no compensation, and therefore no aggregation of welfare across distinct lives can be legitimate: it can never be better that one person lose their life so that five others be saved, since the one is not compensated by the survival of the five. Rawls's own difference principle reflects this anti-aggregative stance, refusing to sum welfare across persons the way utilitarianism does.

Since utilitarianism can only be arrived at by extending the principle of prudence for one man to society, and this extension illicitly conflates persons, utilitarianism itself is refuted. The objection succeeds."
