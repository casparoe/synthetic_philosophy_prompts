So, quick background on me: no academic training, I do network admin work, but for the past year or so I've been hanging out in effective-altruism-adjacent online spaces, and there's one move I keep running into that I can't shake off. People there argue that the far future could contain an absolutely staggering number of lives, so even a 0.0001% reduction in the chance that civilisation makes it through this century matters more than everything we could do for people alive today — and they'll bite any bullet for it: tinier probability, bigger payoff, they're fine taking the trade. People online call this "fanaticism" about the far future, not always as a compliment. I think I've found a reason it mostly doesn't work, and I wrote it up as a short draft essay. I'd like comments on it, but I want real comments — don't give me "there are interesting considerations on both sides." Take an actual position on whether my argument holds up, and defend it.

Here's the draft:

---

**Title: Does the simulation argument make far-future fanaticism crazy?**

The people I argue with online (call them the longtermists) reason like this: the number of people who could exist in the future is so large — orders and orders of magnitude beyond everyone alive today — that when you weigh outcomes, the far future swamps the present. Saving a few million lives now is a rounding error next to a one-in-a-million shot at securing a future with, say, 10^30 happy people spread across the galaxy. The smaller the probability they'll accept, the bigger the payoff they'll chase, and they never say "this probability is too small to chase." That's the fanaticism part.

Here's my counterargument. Nick Bostrom (2003) showed that at least one of these has to be true: (1) civilisations at our stage almost never reach the technological maturity needed to run lots of detailed simulations of their history, (2) mature civilisations almost never bother running such simulations even when they can, or (3) we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The reasoning is that if lots of ancestor-simulations ever get run, then almost everyone with experiences like ours is simulated, and we have no special reason to think we're the lucky few real ones.

Now consider what each branch does to the "astronomical future" that the longtermist's whole calculation rests on. On branch (3), our universe's far future exists only for as long as whoever runs the simulation lets it. We know nothing about these simulators, but a simulation of the early 21st century is, most naturally, a study of the past — and the people running it have little reason to faithfully simulate ten thousand more years, let alone millions. At minimum, there's an off-switch on our entire future held by parties about whom we have zero information, and a probability of early shutoff that no amount of x-risk reduction down here moves even slightly. So conditional on being simulated, the 10^30-person future should get a heavy discount, plausibly close to zero. On branches (1) and (2), the far future also doesn't happen in the way the longtermist pictures: either we never make it, or civilisations that do make it turn out not to run ancestor-simulations — and Bostrom's whole point is that the most natural reading of avoiding (3) without going extinct is that something about mature civilisations keeps them from wanting to run us, which tells you their future isn't the expansionist one the longtermists bet on. Either way, the gigantic payoff event — quadrillions of descendants actually existing — is unlikely on every branch. The expected-value tower the longtermist builds was mostly load-bearing on futures that, once you take the simulation argument seriously, probably never occur. So the fanaticism should dampen way down, and the near term deserves most of the weight after all.
