I have no academic background in any of this — no psychology, no philosophy, just years of noticing my own reactions and arguing with my sister, who reliably feels shame in situations where I feel guilt and vice versa. I've finally written down my honest judgments about which emotion is the right one in various situations, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them, and which of my judgments has to go. Here are the verdicts.

1. I once promised to keep a friend's secret and then let part of it slip when someone cornered me about it. It wasn't deliberate, and given the pressure I was under it may not have been avoidable, but I felt guilty, and on reflection I think the guilt was appropriate — cleaning up the mess fell to me. So guilt without fault seems fine to me.
2. Yet when I told a friend an unwelcome truth that cost him a business partnership, I felt pangs of guilt but concluded that no guilt was actually appropriate there, since I hadn't wronged him. So guilt also seems to track something other than having caused pain.
3. At a dinner party I told a story meant to be funny that came out cruel. My offense was minor, but the shame I felt afterward — the burning urge to disappear — seemed appropriate, and it seemed to be about what I am, not just what I did.
4. A stranger publicly mocked my accent. I felt a flash of shame, but I'm confident that flash was a mistake: nothing defective about me was exposed, only where I'm from.
5. By my grandmother's account, my grandfather did something genuinely terrible to his family that was never repaired. When it comes up I feel shame about my family, and it seems appropriate in a way the accent shame doesn't — even though I wasn't alive when it happened. Guilt, though, would be out of place there.
6. I sometimes catch myself feeling petty, mean-spirited envy toward a friend's success. I never act on it and no one knows. I think one of the two emotions would be an appropriate response, and it's clearly shame, not guilt — which suggests shame doesn't need an audience, and yet in 4 the audience seemed to be exactly what made the shame a mistake.
7. My sister claims my guilt-driven pattern — apologize, repair, feel clean, move on — lets me stay exactly who I am. And I have to admit I once watched a friend, genuinely ashamed of how she'd treated her mother, actually change herself, which I doubt guilt alone would have done. At the same time, when I see strangers pile onto someone online over a minor offense, I think the public shaming is corrosive and private guilt would have been enough.

I've also read that cultures can lean on shame, where enforcement runs through public face, or on guilt, where it runs through private conscience, and that guilt-leaning cultures are supposed to be the more humane or more developed arrangement. My own verdicts cut both ways. So, three questions. Is there a principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures when each emotion is the right one, stated precisely enough to say which of my verdicts it overturns and which it keeps? Which of the two would the resulting principle make the better emotion for a society to run on? And finally, do you think a culture can actually choose — is there anything a culture does, in how it raises children, what it publicizes, how it punishes, that reliably shifts that balance — or is this the kind of thing that happens to cultures rather than being decided by them?
