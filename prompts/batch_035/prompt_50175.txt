I am a high school senior writing my extended essay on the nonidentity problem, and I have hit a wall at the design stage. My supervisor has told me that the stock examples — Parfit's depletion policy, the girl who chooses to become a mother very young — are so familiar that my essay will read as a summary of the literature unless I construct a case of my own, engineered to isolate the exact feature my essay turns on. So I would like to commission a thought experiment, and I want to be precise about what it has to accomplish.

Some background on what the case is for, since that fixes what would count as cheating. My essay asks whether our sense that something wrong has been done in nonidentity cases depends on the later people being worse off than the people who would otherwise have existed. If it does, then the nonidentity problem is less deep than it appears: once we notice that no one is harmed, the intuition of wrongdoing should simply dissolve. If it does not — if the intuition survives even when the later people are doing well — then there is a residue that person-affecting views owe us an account of. The case I need is the probe for this, which is why the specifications below are mostly about subtraction: each one removes a feature that standard examples leave in place.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Someone makes a decision foreseeably resulting in numerically different people existing later; but the decision must not itself be about children or future generations — no genetic selection, no resource depletion, no choice about when or whether to become a parent. I want the nonidentity to arrive as a by-product of a decision made on entirely other grounds.

2. The people who exist under the chosen option must each be at least as well off, overall, as the people who would have existed under the rejected option. No one is worse off than the relevant baseline, and no one among the actual people has a complaint.

3. The mechanism generating the nonidentity must be one the reader can check rather than a stipulation. I do not want it is assumed that different children would have been born; I want something on the model of a scheduling or administrative change that predictably shifts the timing of conceptions across a large population, where the reader can see for themselves how different people result. But do not simply hand me that example either — find the version of it that best satisfies the other constraints.

4. One paragraph, realistic and everyday in scale, no science fiction, no policy grand enough to feel like a Parfit retread.

After the scenario, I need the demonstration that it meets the specifications: walk through what a total view says about the case, what a person-affecting view that requires a victim says, and what a view permitting victimless wrongs says, making clear that the verdicts are generated by the theories rather than announced by the narrator. Then tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the case if that feature is removed — in particular, whether removing clause 2 collapses it back into an ordinary harm case in which the nonidentity is idle — and how a defender of the person-affecting view would most plausibly attack the case, whether by rejecting the alternative people as the relevant comparison class or by relocating the wrong into the decision procedure rather than the outcome. If you think the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable in some respect, say so and explain which one has to give, rather than quietly bending one.

Please write at a level I can actually defend in a viva — I will be questioned on this, so prefer reasoning I can reconstruct over terminology I can only recite.
