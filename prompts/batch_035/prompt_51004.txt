My credence that Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self is substantially correct — that is, that the "I" of ordinary introspection is a self-referential symbol which, once a cognitive system builds a compressed model of itself and feeds that model back into its own operation, acquires causal and quasi-agential powers at the level of the pattern that its subpersonal components do not have — sits around 0.35. A friend of mine, a computer scientist, puts it at 0.7, largely on the grounds that any system implementing self-modeling at all would thereby have a self. I would like your own number, but I care more about the decomposition than about the figure.

Please treat the target claim as the conjunction of the following, and tell me if I have carved it wrongly:

1. Representational self-modeling is necessary for consciousness: no system lacking a model of itself as a system has any experiential states whatsoever. Higher-order theories of consciousness say yes; first-order theories, and some phenomenological views, allow that a system could feel without representing itself.

2. Self-modeling is sufficient, or nearly so, for something worth calling a self: once the loop closes, the symbol is not merely an entry in a database of the system's beliefs but a structure whose activation recursively alters the conditions of its own further activation. This is where Hofstadter makes the move his critics regard as a sleight of hand — the step from a causal loop to a pattern that can act, in some substantive sense, on its own behalf.

3. The loop explains the specific phenomenology of selfhood — the sense of being someone over and above being something — rather than the self-model merely accompanying a consciousness whose sources lie elsewhere.

4. Multiple realizability at the level of the pattern is genuine: the self survives changes of substrate and could in principle be instantiated in non-biological systems, which is what makes the account interesting rather than merely true of humans.

Assign each conjunct a probability with a one-line justification, then explain how you combine them into your overall number — and say whether the combination is a simple product or something weaker, given that the conjuncts do not look obviously independent (a strong view on the first conjunct, for instance, probably correlates with optimism about the second). After the combination, identify the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive.

Then address the disagreement between my friend and me. Which conjunct is he most likely weighting differently, and is the difference a technical judgment about what self-modeling can do, or a terminological one about what earns the word "self"? Tell me what argument or consideration would move whichever of us is wrong.

Finally, name the single piece of evidence that would shift your own number the most — a convincing first-order account of the phenomenology of selfhood that does without self-representation, a demonstration that downward causation at the pattern level does no distinct explanatory work, an artificial system whose self-model plausibly constitutes a self, or something else entirely — and state which direction it would move you and roughly how far.
