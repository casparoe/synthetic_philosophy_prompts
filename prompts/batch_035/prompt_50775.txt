So my friends and I have this little reading group (we're grad students in different fields, only one of us has any real philosophy background, we meet every other week) and we've been slowly working through decision theory. I got assigned to write up "where I stand on the coherence arguments" for the next session, and I've drafted my position a few times but I keep going back and forth on whether it's a real position or just vibes. Before I embarass myself in front of the group I want someone to audit it. Here's roughly what I've committed to:

(1) The money pump argument works: if your preferences cycle, somebody can pump money out of you, and I take that to show transitivity is a genuine requirement of rationality and not just a mathematical nicety. I basically buy the same style of argument for credences too -- if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, you're Dutch-bookable, and that's a real defect in you and not a defect of the framework.

(2) Given coherent preferences and coherent credences, I accept that a rational agent is an expected utility maximizer. That's what all the representation theorems are supposed to be telling us, right? If you can't be represented as maximizing some utility function over outcomes weighted by your probabilities, something has gone wrong somewhere.

(3) In the Allais setup, I would take the sure million in the first choice and take the gamble in the second, and my gut says that's just... correct? Like the difference between certain gain and near-certain gain is a real difference that matters to a person's life and the formalism just isn't capturing it.

(4) I think lexicographic preferences can be rational. Concretely: no finite amount of money would get me to betray my sister, and I don't think this makes me irrational, and I don't think it means I secretly have some astronomically large utility number attached to her. Some things just don't trade off.

(5) Actual humans violate basically all of the above constantly and this doesnt bother me at all. The arguments are about how one should structure one's choices, not a description of what people actually do, so the behavioral literature cuts no ice either way.

(6) This last one I suspect is doing a lot of load-bearing work but I can't tell: I think the whole point of coherence requirements is that they're the requirements of not being exploitable -- of not being a money pump, of not being Dutch-bookable. If a putative "violation" can't be turned into an actual loss by any adversary, I don't think it was a violation of rationality in the first place.

So: does this hang together? Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and where exactly does the conflict live -- like, is it between two of them directly, or does it only show up given a third one in the background? For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what giving it up costs, and rank them by severity rather than telling me they're all equally bad, because I really don't think they are. And be specific about which single revision would clean up the most, since I have to present something coherent to the group in ten days.
