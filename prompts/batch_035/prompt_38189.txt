Exam question: "Popper claimed that falsificationism, combined with his notion of verisimilitude, can explain scientific progress despite the fact that (on his view) even our best theories are false. Explain what verisimilitude is, why falsificationism seems to need it, and what the Miller-Tichý result showed was wrong with Popper's original definition."

Student's answer:

Since Popper thinks no scientific theory can ever be verified, and all past theories have eventually been falsified, he owes us an account of how science makes progress. If Newton was wrong and Einstein was wrong too, in what sense is Einstein better? Popper's answer is verisimilitude, or truthlikeness. The idea is that a false theory can still be close to the truth, and later theories have a higher probability of being true than earlier ones -- that's what progress consists in.

Popper defines verisimilitude in terms of a theory's consequences. The truth content of a theory is the set of its true consequences, and the falsity content is the set of its false consequences. Verisimilitude is then the ratio of true consequences to total consequences, so a theory with all true consequences has verisimilitude 1, and adding false consequences lowers the score. This means a theory with fewer consequences overall can score better, which fits the intuition that vague theories are less truthlike.

In the mid-1970s, Miller and Tichý independently showed that this definition doesn't work. On Popper's definition, no comparison between two false theories is ever possible -- the ordering is undefined for any pair of theories that are both false. Since almost every interesting scientific theory is false, this makes the whole apparatus useless, and later work had to start over from scratch with completely different tools.
