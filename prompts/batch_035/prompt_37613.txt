I run a rationality workshop for the local EA-adjacent meetup — about 14 regulars, mostly longtime readers of the usual blogs and forums, one 90-minute session — and I'd like a structured game that makes them feel the force of an idea most of them have only ever met on the page: evidential cooperation in large worlds. The idea, in my own words, since the group will need it stated plainly: in a sufficiently large world — Everett branches, regions beyond our cosmological horizon, whatever else is out there — there are plausibly other agents whose decision procedures resemble mine closely enough that what I choose is evidence about what they choose, even though no signal can ever pass between us. If their values are even weakly aligned with mine, I can treat some of my costly choices as partly made on behalf of them and their values, expecting that somewhere a counterpart of me is making symmetric choices that benefit agents like me. No payoff ever travels causally; the entire case rests on my decision being information about theirs. My group can recite all this, but I don't think they've felt why it's tempting, or where it's fragile, and that's what I want the game to produce.

Design the mechanics so the acausal structure is built in rather than announced. My sketch, which you should refine or replace if you see something better: pair everyone through a short similarity questionnaire, then have each participant make a costly choice that determines the payoff of the person they were paired with, while their own payoff is fixed by the choice of whoever was paired with them — so you always choose for a stranger and are always chosen for by a stranger, with no communication, no feedback, and no way to find out who was who until after everything is over. I need the full package: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that works without real money, questionnaire items, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps each mechanic onto a piece of the idea — what plays the role of the horizon, of the correlation between decision procedures, of the weak value alignment. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect some participants to try to establish conventions anyway, and a few to defect for the fun of it), and be frank about which parts of the real idea no classroom game can reproduce — in the game the facilitator manufactures the similarity by fiat, whereas in the large world you have to infer it under uncertainty, and that inference is exactly where the whole view lives or dies.

One last request: in the debrief material on the idea itself, please keep what is settled separate from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know the area. That my choice can be evidence about a distant similar agent's choice is elementary. Whether decision theories license cooperating on that basis — whether evidential reasoners really cooperate in these settings, whether the required degree of similarity is at all realistic, and whether the expected gains are non-negligible rather than lost in the noise — is disputed, and I don't want the debrief presenting the optimistic reading as if it were consensus.
