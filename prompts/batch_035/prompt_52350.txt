For a seminar on post-Gettier theories of knowledge, I need a thought experiment built to the following specifications. The case must be a genuine Gettier case: a belief that is justified and true yet intuitively not knowledge. However, the subject's justification must be non-inferential — the belief must not be deduced or otherwise inferred from any proposition, so that the standard "no false lemmas" repair finds no false premise within the subject's own reasoning to point at. Further constraints: the setting must be ordinary and realistic (a workplace, a commute, a family kitchen — nothing science-fictional); the case may not use the stock devices, meaning no lookalike objects of any kind, no stopped clocks or malfunctioning instruments, no misleading publications, and no character in the scenario may lie or intend to deceive; and the coincidence that makes the belief true should be the kind that plausibly occurs in ordinary life rather than a one-in-a-billion accident.

After presenting the scenario in a single paragraph, demonstrate that it satisfies each specification. State precisely what the subject believes, in virtue of what the belief is justified, and in virtue of what it is true; then explain why the belief nonetheless fails to be knowledge. Show why the no-false-lemmas diagnosis has nothing to grasp inside the subject's own epistemic position, and consider whether a defender of that diagnosis can redirect it outward — for instance, toward a false belief held by someone whose word or behavior the subject is tracking — and whether that extension is principled or merely ad hoc. Then evaluate the case under a safety condition: identify the method by which the belief is formed, say whether the belief counts as safe in the relevant sense, and judge whether safety explains the anti-knowledge intuition any better than no-false-lemmas does. Conclude by naming the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and describe what happens to the example if that feature is removed.
