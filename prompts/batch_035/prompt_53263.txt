Hey, so I've been going round in circles on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe, and I'm hoping you can untangle one specific step for me, because popular explainers just sort've gloss over it.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: you and a distant observer glide past each other at some decent fraction of c. Because of the relativity of simultaneity, the distant observer's plane of "now" is tilted relative to yours, and it slices through events that, in your frame, haven't happened yet. If whatever is on that plane is in some sense already there / settled / determinate for them, then chunks of your future are determinate too. And since for any event you can find some observer whose "now" contains it, everything is determinate — the block.

The bit I actually want explained: the standard move is that determinateness cannot itself be frame-relative — it's supposedly not like simultaneity or length, which we've learned to relativise. Once any observer's present contains an event, the event is determinate full stop, simpliciter. But defenders of the argument mostly just assert this, and I don't see why the presentist can't shrug and say: "fine, 'determinate relative to your simultaneity slice' is a coordinate artefact, my foliation is the metaphysically privileged one, and the distant observer's 'now' tells me nothing about what's really settled." Is there anything more to the reply than insisting that 'determinate' was always meant as an absolute notion?

There's also a related worry I half-grasp: people point out that no observer can actually witness my future event and get that information back to me before the event — light cones constrain real observation in a way that simultaneity conventions don't. Doesn't that suggest the argument leans on treating "what some observer calls present" as "what has been verified", which relativity never gives you?

So: give me the strongest, gut-level version of the "determinateness isn't frame-relative" step, and explain whether it can carry the weight the argument puts on it. Don't give me a hedge-fest — commit to a verdict on whether the argument works, and say specifically what consideration or objection would flip you to the other conclusion, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
