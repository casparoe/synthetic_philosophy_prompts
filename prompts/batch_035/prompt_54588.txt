So I'm a grad student and something about how math actually gets established has been bugging me, and I want the philosophy of it spelled out. Here's the practice: a journal sends a two-hundred-page proof to two referees, they check what they can in a few months, and then the entire community treats the theorem as settled — people cite it, build on it, use it as a lemma in papers that then get rubber-stamped the same way. Everyone knows this is fallible: gaps get found a decade later, the classification of finite simple groups sat for years with no single human having verified all of it, parts of the community just decided to act as if it was done. So the everyday institution quietly treats mathematical knowledge as something like a credit rating — confidence accumulated through chains of partial checking — even though the official story is that a theorem is known by deduction, full stop, and it makes no sense to be 90% sure of a necessary truth.

What does the institution actually presuppose? I want someone to reconstruct the implicit epistemology here: does treating a published theorem as a usable premise require some notion of logical uncertainty — genuine credences about mathematical claims — or can a strict deductivist rescue the practice as just economical shorthand? And here's the part that looks inconsistent to me: the same community was queasy about the four-color proof and the Kepler conjecture precisely because a machine checked the cases and no human had, but is now pretty relaxed about Lean-verified proofs that also no human has gone through line by line. Do those two reactions presuppose different things about what "understanding" a proof consists in, or is there a principled line there? Finally, if the probabilistic picture is what the practice really runs on, tell me what would actually have to change — would we owe each theorem a confidence level, and is there a threshold below which we should stop calling things theorems and start calling them well-supported conjectures?

Also, and I know this is a seperate question, feel free to say if the whole framing is off because peer review isn't doing epistemic work at all — that its just a quality filter and the real guarantee of mathematical knowledge is something else entirely, like the fact that errors get found when consequences are used downstream. I'd want to know if that's a better story.
