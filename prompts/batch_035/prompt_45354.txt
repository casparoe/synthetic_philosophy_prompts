Longtime lurker of the rationalist blogosphere here (lesswrong, the SSC-adjacent diaspora, etc.), and I finally ran into something I can't dissolve to my own satisfaction.

The paradox of fiction gets presented as three claims that can't all be true: (1) we genuinely feel emotions for fictional characters, (2) we believe those characters don't exist, (3) genuine emotion toward something requires believing it exists. And my immediate reaction was... this seems trivially solved? Emotions are motivational machinery shaped by natural selection, and evolution is a cheap hack that never bothers to gate emotional responses on verified ontological commitments. Crying at a death scene is the same cognitive module that fires at real deaths, just triggered by a vivid simulation — basically a misfire, like getting hungry from a food commercial or flinching at a horror movie jumpscare. So isn't the "paradox" just an artifact of philosophers writing before evo psych was on everyone's radar? There's no contradiction, just an explanation waiting to be filled in.

But then two followup questions, because something still feels off:

First, if the evolutionary story fully dissolves it, why do actual philosophers of art keep insisting there's a residue left? Am I missing a distinction here, e.g. between explaining why an emotion occurs and whether it's rational or appropriate to have it (the way explaining why we fear spiders doesn't settle whether the fear is warranted)? Is the paradox supposed to be about the rationality of fiction-directed emotions rather than their causal etiology, and would that make my "it's just a misfire" answer a category error?

Second, and this is the part I actually care about for my own life: if my fiction-driven emotions are misfires of modules designed for real situations, should I be treating them as a bias to correct? Concretely — I teared up at the end of a novel last week, and part of me wants to file that under "motivational system receiving noise, discount accordingly," the same way I'd refuse to let a novel's unflattering portrayal of some group update my beliefs about that group. Is that the right move, or do the people who study this think there's something dumb about refusing to count your own emotions as data just because their object is fictional?

Where exactly does my dissolution go wrong, if it does?
