I edit a small blog read by an STS-adjacent reading group, and for our next discussion cycle on Langdon Winner's 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" I want to build an interactive sorting instrument: a decision tree that takes a reader from their honest reactions to concrete cases to the position on technology and politics they actually occupy -- regardless of what they'd call themselves. The tree should start from a neutral entry question and work down through commitments, with each branch node phrased as a question about a case that a reader can answer without knowing which school it leads toward. I want the leaves to include at least: plain instrumentalism (artifacts are neutral, all politics resides in users and institutions), social-constructionist views (the politics people find in an artifact is a contingent effect of interpretive flexibility and social interests, and the artifact itself settles nothing), Winner-style political artifact theory (some arrangements genuinely embody political relations, either as flexible tools of a political order or as systems that require or strongly select for certain forms of authority), strong technological determinism, and substantivist views of the Heidegger/Postman/Ellul stripe where the political question isn't about individual artifacts at all but about technology as an encompassing mode of revealing or a culture in itself. If you think critical-constructivist positions that split the difference belong as separate leaves, include them; if some leaves can't be cleanly separated by cases, say so explicitly rather than inventing a diagnostic question that doesn't really cut.

For the branch nodes, I'd like you to build from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first the questions that genuinely divide these positions -- for instance, whether an artifact's design features, considered just by themselves, can ever settle questions of power and access; whether such settlements are necessary (no re-arrangement preserving the function avoids the politics) or merely contingent; whether the relevant unit is the artifact, the whole sociotechnical system and its required forms of organization, or the culture of efficiency and control as such; and whether a design's politics is fixed at creation or continuously renegotiated in use. Then locate the actual positions in that space. Each node should be anchored in a concrete case a reader can react to: the overpasses allegedly built low to keep buses off a parkway, a nuclear plant or pipeline that seems to demand centralized authority and security apparatus just to function, a household appliance that shifts domestic labor rather than eliminating it, a mechanical harvester that only pays off with large consolidated farms, or a weapon whose mere existence reshapes an entire strategic situation -- you pick the mix. Please note any combination of node answers that no known position occupies, with a sentence on whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

A few constraints on format: aim for seven to ten branch nodes total, keep every case description to a sentence or two so the tree stays readable as a handout, and mark at each leaf one or two of the positions' most awkward commitments -- the concession a reader at that leaf should acknowledge before they feel too comfortable there. Since my readers will take the tree before reading anything for the cycle, it should be self-contained: no citations, no reading list, and no jargon a smart layperson couldn't parse in context. If you think the Moses-style bridges example is so contested as an anchor case that it would mislead readers at the first node, tell me what to substitute and why, rather than quietly building the tree around it.
