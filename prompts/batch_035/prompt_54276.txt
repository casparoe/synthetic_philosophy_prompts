So here's an episode I want analyzed through a pretty specific ethical lens, and I want it done carefully, not the standard take. The episode: the long American shift from beef toward chicken, roughly from the 1970s to now. Over that stretch, per-capita beef eating flattened and then slid, while chicken eating kept climbing, and at some point in the last couple decades chicken passed beef in per-capita pounds -- the exact crossover year doesn't matter, just the shape. Behind this was mostly the broiler efficiency revolution: mid-century breeding contests and after, birds that hit slaughter weight in ~6-7 weeks instead of the ~12 of the 1950s, barns packed tighter, feed conversion pushed down. By most counts the US now kills on the order of 9 billion broilers a year, world total somewhere north of 70 billion.

The lens: a straightforward anti-speciesist, aggregative utilitarianism of the kind you find in the effective-animal-advocacy world. On that view, the ethical arithmetic of meat runs roughly per-calorie: how many animals die, and how badly do they live, per unit of food. And there's a well-known implication people in that world repeat: eating chicken is much worse than eating beef, because it takes on the order of 200 chicken deaths to deliver the calories of one cow, and a broiler's 6-7 weeks are, on most welfare accounting, worse than a day at a slaughterhouse. So the dietary advice that falls out is counterintuitive: if you're going to eat red meat anyway, drop the chicken first.

Now map the episode onto the theory, both directions. Direction one: what does the theory say happened? The beef-to-chicken shift was, on the per-calorie death arithmetic, maybe the single largest retrograde step for farmed-animal welfare in modern history -- a dietary change sold as healthful and cheap, which the aggregationist has to count as a disaster of many billions of worse-off lives, while nearly everyone else (nutritionists, consumers, even most animal advocates at the time) counted it as progress or at least neutral. Direction two -- and this is the part I actually care about -- is where the theory gets stress-tested, because there's a wrinkle inside the same framework that the standard chicken-vs-beef advice never mentions: feed conversion. Chickens are something like 5x more efficient at turning feed into meat than cattle. So per calorie of beef, you're dragging way more acres of cropland through tillage, pesticides, and harvest, and if you take the wild-animal-suffering-during-crop-cultivation worry seriously (the Norwood-style / crop-death argument, which its own proponents admit is speculative), the beef-vs-chicken ranking possibly inverts, or at least the confident advice becomes mush. Same framework, two considerations, opposite verdicts, and no agreement on the exchange rate between a broiler's weeks and a field mouse's death.

So what I want from you: (1) the mapping done honestly -- who/what the theory says the relevant victims are, what the actual intervention was (breeding, not policy), why nobody inside the episode was operating with anything like this arithmetic; (2) the explanation question -- does the aggregationist framework explain why the shift happened (it doesn't, right? cheapness and health marketing explain it; the theory is silent on causes), and whether that silence matters for a theory meant to guide diet advice; (3) the anomaly -- does the chicken-vs-beef case show the per-calorie framework generating unstable advice whenever you toggle which consideration you count, and is the right response to count both (and how would you even), or to admit the framework only speaks when you feed it contestable weighting assumptions; and (4) tell me straight whether this episode is, on reflection, a confirmation of aggregative anti-speciesism (it did at least register the shift as bad when common sense didn't) or a case the theory never anticipated and handles by hand-waving. Don't force the theory to speak where it's silent -- I'd rather hear that it has nothing to say about causes than a just-so story. Also, if you think the wild-animal wrinkle is too speculative to carry any weight, say that and defend it; don't just split the difference to be safe.
