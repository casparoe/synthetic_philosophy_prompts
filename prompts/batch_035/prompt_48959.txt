I sit on the ethics advisory board of a mid-sized fertility clinic that performs roughly two thousand IVF cycles a year. Our lab director has proposed that we begin offering polygenic embryo screening: when a couple has several viable embryos, they would receive each embryo's polygenic risk scores for common diseases and, at the contested end of the menu, predicted percentiles for non-disease traits — educational attainment, cognitive performance, height. Disease risk panels are, by our board's lights, settled. The live issues are the non-disease traits, and also a separate request already on file: a couple in which one partner carries a dominant gene for hereditary deafness, both partners being Deaf themselves, who want our help selecting an embryo that has the mutation — selecting for deafness, in effect — so that their child belongs to their Deaf community rather than to ours.

The director's pitch is essentially the principle of procreative beneficence: parents have a reason, perhaps an obligation, to select, of the children they could have, the one expected to have the best life, and withholding the information is paternalism. Several board members reply with the standard counters — the child's future autonomy, the objection to designing another person, the disability-rights worry about what screening against a trait says to people who live with it. My own view, which I would like the memo to take seriously but not simply assume, is that both sides share a flawed picture: they argue as if the moral weight of the choice rests on the child who will result, when the non-identity problem seems to me to gut every version of the harm-to-the-child argument for embryo selection outright. The considerations that actually move me all operate elsewhere — on families already raising children with the traits being scored against, and on what the clinic endorses and thereby trains parents to want. But I recognize I may be wrong that the child-centered arguments are empty, and the memo should not stack the deck.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should make a concrete recommendation — approve the trait menu, reject it, or approve with conditions — including where, if anywhere, the boundary between medical and non-medical screening should be drawn and how the Deaf couple's request should be handled within that framework. Give the reasoning in enough detail that the lab director and the skeptical board members would each recognize their own position fairly stated, and include an honest treatment of the strongest argument for whichever recommendation you reject. The board acts on memos it can respond to, so flag explicitly any point on which you think the philosophical literature is genuinely indeterminate and the decision must fall back on institutional values rather than argument. No citations, please — we attach those separately.
