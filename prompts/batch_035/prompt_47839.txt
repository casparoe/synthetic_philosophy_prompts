In my thesis chapter on liberty I keep returning to a dispute that has essentially not moved since the late 1990s: whether republican freedom as non-domination is a genuinely distinct third concept of liberty, or whether it collapses into one of the two familiar ones. Ian Carter and Matthew Kramer press the first horn: if you let threats, conditional interference, and the mere capacity to interfere count against freedom, then non-domination is just non-interference with a wider, more sensitive accounting of interference. Critics on the other side press the second horn: since saying which powers count as arbitrary seems to require substantive judgements about interests and status, non-domination smuggles in a positive conception after all. Pettit's own response, as I read it, is to concede that part of the argument about whether republicanism offers a distinct *concept* of freedom is terminological, while insisting that the underlying ideal of not being subject to another's arbitrary will is distinct and important regardless of how we taxonomise it. My working hypothesis, which I can't quite get to hold together, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side individuates concepts of liberty by their extension — which concrete states of affairs count as a loss of freedom — while the other individuates them by their theoretical role, i.e. by what work the concept is supposed to do in explaining why arbitrary power is bad even when it is benignly exercised. On the extensional criterion, maybe non-domination does reduce to a fine-grained non-interference view; on the role-based criterion, maybe the negative and republican concepts differ in kind, because the former is a comparative measure of constraint and the latter is a threshold condition for standing. What I'd like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on republicanism itself: is this a substantive disagreement, a merely verbal one about the word 'liberty', or two parties tracking different questions about concept individuation? Which, if either, of my two criteria is actually in play in the literature as written, and does the concession Pettit appears to make — that some of the dispute is about labelling — leave anything of philosophical importance still contested? Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress here: a stipulative convention, a worked-out case that forces the two criteria to deliver different verdicts on the same person's freedom, or an argument that one criterion of concept individuation is the right one for political philosophy's purposes. Please answer in British English.
