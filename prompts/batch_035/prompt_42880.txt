hey, need some help prepping for a talk. In about three weeks I'm presenting at a colloquium where I defend a deflationist view of truth, and specifically the version that goes through the prosententialist idea: the truth predicate doesn't ascribe a property to beliefs or sentences, it lets us endorse claims indirectly and generalize over them (saying it is true that snow is white is just a way of agreeing that snow is white, and saying everything the boss said is true lets you endorse a bunch of things you never got in front of). The twist I'm pushing this time: everyone thinks deflationism can't explain why truth matters for belief -- why belief aims at truth, or why a true belief is better than a justified but false one. I want to argue that once you see what the truth predicate actually does, you get the normative role for free: calling a belief true is endorsing it, and endorsement is already normatively loaded, no substantive property needed.

the audience is going to be hostile-ish, though. The department is big on epistemology and the normativity of belief. One senior guy is a straight correspondence theorist and thinks deflationism is so thin it can't even state the realism vs anti-realism debate. Two people work on epistemic normativity and will come at me from the direction that truth is constitutive of beleif, so if truth is thin the whole normativity of belief has to be rebuilt from scratch and they'll want to know what I put in its place. A couple of grad students just finished Wright on truth as a norm of assertion and are dying to try it out on somebody. And there's a formal epistemologist who I suspect will raise the objection that if truth doesn't explain anything, then the fact that reliable methods track truth can't do any explanatory work anywhere in epistemology -- which, if it lands, cuts into a lot of the room's research program, not just mine, so it might get everyone piling on.

What I want is a prep sheet. List the objections I'll realistically get from these specific people, roughly ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumble it. Phrase them the way that kind of philosopher would actually phrase them, so I recognize them in the wild. For each one give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus a flag on where my reply is honestly soft. Tell me which objection I should partly concede up front to buy goodwill, and what's the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. And if you think the twist itself is a mistake -- that the normative role really does need more than endorsement -- say so and tell me what the more modest fallback line is.
