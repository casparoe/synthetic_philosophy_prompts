In the evening philosophy course I teach at a community college, most of my students are adults returning to education — some devout, some militantly secular — and next week's topic is the Euthyphro dilemma. I want to teach it in reverse of the usual order: students should first meet the bare structure in non-religious cases (is a chess move illegal because the arbiter penalizes it, or does the arbiter penalize it because it is illegal?), and only then watch Plato apply the pattern to piety. Don't write the explanation for me; plan it. Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the sequence the pieces must go in and what depends on what; (2) the single carrying example for the whole session, including why I should not open with Abraham and Isaac, which is what I keep being tempted by; (3) the misconceptions I should expect — my own guesses are that students will hear it as an argument for atheism, and that they will think picking the second horn is simply the right answer that dissolves the dilemma — and where in the sequence each must be headed off; (4) whether the "good because it reflects God's nature" response should be presented, deferred, or left out entirely at this level, and what else I should openly refuse to cover — divine simplicity, the Leibniz–Clarke exchange?; and (5) one question to close with that would show they grasped the structure rather than the theology — I am considering asking each student to generate a fresh instance of the dilemma from their own line of work, but tell me if that ends up testing something else.
