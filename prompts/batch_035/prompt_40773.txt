Had a huge blowup argument with my roommate last night and I want to be better armed for the rematch. Context: I manage a small team and I floated tracking a couple of metrics (ticket resolution time, feature adoption) just to get visibility into whats going wrong with our product. She went off on Goodhart's law — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — and by the end her position was basically that putting a number on something is how you ruin it: managers game the numbers, devs build to the metric, and she swears it happened in her own life too (she used to track her runs on Strava and says she started picking flat routes to flatter her pace, so she deleted the app). Conclusion: no metrics, or at least no metrics anyone knows they're being judged on.

Something about her move felt off but I couldn't pin down what. Can you untangle the different precise claims hiding in that one-liner? One reading seems almost statistical — if you select on a noisy proxy, the thing you actually care about regresses (a model that scores high on a benchmark partly by luck isnt as good as the score suggests, and conditioning on a high score breaks the correlation). Another is causal and strategic — agents who are judged on the proxy will optimize the proxy, maybe at the goal's expense. And there might be a reflexive social one where the very act of measuring changes the thing measured even without anyone consciously gaming anything. For each reading: is it true, and does it actually get her to "no metrics at all"? Because the statistical one seems to just say "be careful interpreting", and even the gaming one might only tell you to keep the metric and not make it a target — which is interesting given her Strava story, since she was the measurer AND the gamed agent, and she could've just... not looked at the pace field. Lay the readings out as a list with verdicts, flag which one she needs for her conclusion, and say whether that one survives contact. Also — does it matter that the original law was apparently about central banks and monetary aggregates, not performance reviews? Does the popular version even follow from that, or is it doing totally different work?
