I am preparing a position statement for a graduate seminar I teach on population ethics — the students have asked each participant in the faculty roundtable to circulate a short document stating where they actually stand, so that the seminar can poke at a living set of views rather than at textbook positions. I have drafted my list of commitments, but before I circulate it I want an audit of whether it is internally coherent. I should say that I have taught this material for years and regard myself as clear-eyed about the standard failure modes, so please do not soften anything on my behalf: if my commitments are jointly untenable, I would rather learn it now than in front of twelve graduate students.

Here is the list. (1) In comparisons where the same people exist in the same numbers, I am a straightforward hedonic totalist: the sum of wellbeing settles which outcome is better, full stop. (2) The transitivity of better-than is non-negotiable. I regard the suggestion that we should simply live with cyclical or intransitive betterness relations, in the manner of Temkin or Rachels, as an evasion rather than a theory. (3) I accept the Mere Addition Principle: adding lives that are worth living, without harming anyone, cannot make an outcome worse. (4) I think the Repugnant Conclusion is false — a very large population whose members each have lives barely worth living is not better than a smaller population whose members have wonderful lives — and I want to insist, in stating this, that I am not smuggling in the claim that the barely-worth-living lives are bad: each such life is genuinely positive, its owner is glad to exist, and no one in the large population is harmed by being brought into it. (5) I also think the Sadistic Conclusion is false: it cannot be better to add a smaller number of lives of unrelieved suffering than a larger number of happy lives, which is one reason I have never been tempted by positive critical levels. (6) On the non-identity problem, I think depleting resources, and thereby bringing about a different, worse-off future generation rather than a better-off one, is wrong in exactly the same straightforward sense in which harming a particular person is wrong; I feel no pull whatsoever toward the person-affecting restriction. (7) I am a tempered egalitarian: equality has some non-instrumental value for me, though only a little — I would happily accept a large gain in total wellbeing at the price of a large increase in inequality. (8) Finally, I find the longtermist verdict about extinction compelling: a future containing vastly many generations at even modest quality would be enormously better than near-term human extinction, and losing it would be a moral catastrophe of the first order.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Go through the commitments pairwise where it matters, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. For each genuine tension, say exactly what would have to give and what abandoning it would cost me elsewhere in the set — I am particularly interested in whether anything I have declared non-negotiable turns out to be load-bearing. And please identify any tension I have flagged that dissolves on inspection, since I would like to know which of my own worries are confused. I do not want to be told which commitment I should give up; I want to understand the structure of the trade-offs well enough to make that decision myself.
