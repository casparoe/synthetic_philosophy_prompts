I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and I have watched the question of whether an aligned AI system should reason as a utilitarian or under deontic constraints resurface in one disguise after another: the corrigibility debates, arguments about whether tolerance of shutdown is better cashed out as a constraint or as a term in an expected-value calculation, and periodic flare-ups about whether machine ethics should be codified as rules or learned as preferences. Lately the standard framing has begun to feel stale to me, and I want help figuring out where the genuinely open questions actually are, because I suspect they are not the ones the recurring arguments keep rehashing.

The framing I mean is this one: a utilitarian AI is fragile because utility functions are easily misspecified and expected-value reasoning goes haywire under extreme stakes, while a constraint-following AI is fragile because rules can be gamed, come into conflict, and freeze a mediocre moral code in place while the world moves on. That much I take to be roughly right and roughly useless. Here are the candidate questions I think might contain the real action, offered for pressure-testing:

First, whether deontic constraints can do the work that corrigibility arguments need them to do. An AI that treats not resisting shutdown as a side constraint behaves very differently from one that assigns resistance a large negative utility, and the difference shows up only in corner cases — but corner cases are precisely where alignment failures live. Which is the better bet, and is the difference even coherent once you notice that a constraint-violating action can also be assigned a probability-weighted cost?

Second, whether agent-relative constraints make sense for a system that is not an agent in the relevant sense. Deontology as standardly formulated distinguishes what I must not do from what I must prevent others from doing, which presupposes a persisting, unified subject. For a system that spawns subagents, is copied across hardware, and self-modifies, it is not obvious where the relativity attaches. This might be a real problem for implementing deontology, or a real problem for deontology, or nothing.

Third, whether rule-consequentialism just collapses the whole choice. If what we can actually implement is a system that follows the rules that a sounder process would endorse, and if both utilitarian and deontological humans agree on many such rules, the dispute may be practically idle — or it may flare up exactly when the rules diverge from the ranking, which is again the interesting case.

Fourth, what constraint-following prescribes in a world full of other agents. A sufficiently capable deontologist will find itself surrounded by constraint violations it could prevent at low cost; a utilitarian will want to aggregate them away. Whether a deontologist AI is obligated to police the world, forbidden to, or permitted to is not a corner case but the central case, and I have not seen it treated as one.

Fifth, and most unsettling to me: whether the utilitarian-versus-deontologist distinction tracks anything in the space of systems we can actually build. The orthogonality thesis says intelligence is compatible with any goal structure, but it does not say which goal structures are easy to specify, easy to train, or stable under reflection. It could be that both theories are artifacts of human-verbalizable ethics and cut the implementation space at no joint at all.

Suppose a small team — say four researchers with three years, doing conceptual and formal work rather than empirical ML — takes this on. Which of my five questions, or of the ones I have failed to list, should come first? Please defend the ordering rather than just assert it: I want to know which questions other alignment work tacitly presupposes answers to, which are tractable from the armchair, which would be better borrowed from existing moral philosophy rather than reinvented, and what would even count as progress. I would particularly like to know which of my candidates are confused or pseudo-questions, and what a ranking would look like if the criterion were contribution to near-term interpretability and specification work rather than long-run safety theory — I suspect the two rankings come apart, and I do not trust my intuitions about which criterion I should be using. I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in a defended agenda, including what evidence or technical developments over the next few years should reshuffle it.
