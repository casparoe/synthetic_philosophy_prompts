I am revising a short essay for a graduate epistemology workshop and would like serious comments on the draft core pasted below, before I expand it into full prose. Please be blunt: I would rather hear the objections now than from the audience.

Working title: The Meno Problem Is a Social Problem.

Thesis: The question of why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief has no answer at the level of the individual believer, because at that level there is nothing over which knowledge could be better. The surplus the Meno puzzle seems to reveal appears only when we consider systems of believers, because knowledge — and only knowledge — is an epistemic state that is stable under transmission: it can be passed from one person to another through testimony without loss of its status, whereas a true belief that is merely lucky degrades into mere belief when told. The value of knowledge is thus not a property of a person's mental state but of an economy of informants; lucky true belief is, by contrast, a private good that cannot serve as the currency of collective inquiry.

The argumentative core, as it currently stands:

First, I set up the problem by distinguishing three questions that I claim get run together: whether knowledge is better than true belief, why it is better, and whether the difference in value is instrumental. I then argue that the standard style of answer — knowledge is reliably produced, or safe, or non-lucky, and therefore more likely to yield further true beliefs — answers only a usefulness question, and a contingent one. Reliability explains why a knower is a better bet going forward; it does not explain what is better about the state right now, in the very case where the lucky true believer gets things right once and the knower gets the same thing right once.

Second, the central move, via two cases. (1) The dowser: in a valley where water underlies nearly every field, a dowser's detections are almost always followed by finding water, and the villagers' beliefs about where to dig are true beliefs acquired through a method with no connection to hydrology. Their wells succeed. If I ask only after the fate of each individual villager, well by well, the dowser's customers do just as well as the geologist's. (2) The two informants: a geologist knows that there is an aquifer under a particular field; a dowser in the valley above happens to hold a true belief to the same effect, acquired by the same bogus method as before. Each tells a third party, who comes to believe truly that there is water under the field. I claim the third party's state differs in kind depending on which informant she heard: testimony from the knower can make for knowledge in the hearer; testimony from the lucky true believer cannot, and at best delivers a belief that inherits its lucky status derivatively. Knowledge is a fixed point of the telling-operation; lucky true belief is not. On this basis I argue that the menonic surplus — whatever it is that makes the geologist better off, or better to consult, than the dowser — lives entirely in the relation between the two parties. A single knower, considered in isolation from all potential hearers, is no better off than a single lucky true believer; the difference is that only one of them can found a practice.

Third, I close with an analogy I am fond of but unsure about: knowledge is to true belief as a sound currency is to a bilateral IOU. A private IOU between two friends works perfectly for exactly those two people; it just cannot circulate. Both are bits of paper with the same words on them, considered one at a time, and yet only one can underwrite an economy.

Comments I would especially welcome, in rough priority order:

1. The deepest worry I have is that I have smuggled the reliabilist answer back in by another route. Is transmission-without-loss anything more than reliability of a specific process, namely testimony? If so, I have answered only the usefulness question after all, and the essay collapses into the standard view with extra machinery. Can this be repaired, or should I concede it and reframe the essay as offering only a diagnosis of why the Meno problem feels unsolved at the individual level?

2. A circularity worry I anticipate: if knowledge is partly constituted by facts about warrant transmission, then explaining the value of knowledge by appeal to transmission would seem to presuppose the very notion whose value was in question. Does this bite? I have not yet found a reply I believe.

3. Does the second case actually hold up? I assert that the hearer's state differs in kind depending on the informant, but a hostile reader might insist that the two hearers are in subjectively identical states, and that the difference only reappears when we ask what the hearer can go on to tell a fourth party — which would make my claim about transmission into a claim about iterated transmission, and possibly into an infinite regress or a covert appeal to the usefulness of the whole chain.

4. Are there counterexamples in the other direction: items of knowledge that are not testimonially transmissible at all, if there are any? If some knowledge cannot be shared, then transmission cannot be the source of knowledge's value in general, and at best I have identified one vector of it.

5. Does the currency analogy earn its place, or is it decorative? If it is doing real work, tell me what; if it is a trinket, say so plainly, because my fondness for it is impairing my judgment.

6. Finally, where would a workshop audience of mainstream epistemologists — people who think in terms of justification, Gettier cases, and the value problem literature, but who have no patience for hand-waving about collectives — push back hardest? Identify the single weakest paragraph, the one you would attack first if you were in the audience, and what I should do about it: shore it up, soften the thesis, or cut.
