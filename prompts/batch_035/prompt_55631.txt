I teach a graduate seminar on formal and informal epistemology, and two of my students have spent the term arguing past each other about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia — roughly, the state of believing that p while also believing that your evidence does not support believing that p, or assigning credence 0.7 to p while believing that the credence your evidence warrants is 0.3. One student holds that akratic combinations are always irrational: rationality requires coherence between one's first-order view and one's view of what one's evidence supports, so higher-order evidence always carries the day. The other defends a level-splitting position: first-order evidence is grasped directly, and one can sometimes permissibly retain one's object-level credence even while judging, on the basis of the higher-order evidence, that it is not the credence one ought to have.

Rather than another round of position papers, I have asked them to run an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement they should produce. Structure it as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what higher-order evidence is, about the difference between defeating first-order evidence and merely indicating that one has mishandled it, about the distinction between irrationality, blameworthiness, and mere sub-optimality, and about whatever else you judge both parties could sign.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the anti-akrasia party would accept for its own view and once in wording the level-splitting party would accept for its own view.

3. A list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or test both parties would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance.

To keep the collaboration from staying at the level of stock examples, please work through at least one novel case of your own construction along the following lines: a domain expert whose object-level judgment is challenged by an audit or algorithmic check with a known and imperfect track record, where the expert can inspect the source of the challenge but cannot locate any specific error in her own reasoning. Make the case specific enough (give the parties names, numbers, and stakes) that the two views yield different, checkable predictions about what the expert is permitted or required to believe.

Finally, if you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal — for instance, a standoff over whether "irrational" picks out incoherence, blameworthiness, or failure of responsiveness to reasons — say which part and why. Append a short closing note in which you state how confident you are in your characterizations of the two positions and of the dialectic, and single out the point in the document you are least sure about, explaining what would make you revise it.
