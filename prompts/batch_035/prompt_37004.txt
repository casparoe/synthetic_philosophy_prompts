I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about dream research that's gotten stuck on a methods question I can't shake. The standard way sleep labs study dreams is to wake people up during REM and ask what they'd been experiencing — and a few years ago some labs went further and got lucid dreamers to answer simple math questions with eye movements, while apparently still asleep, which made headlines everywhere. My editor loved that one. But all of this treats the dream report as a window onto something that was unfolding in real time during sleep, a little movie the brain plays while the body's offline. Isn't that assumption just smuggled in with the mehtod? I remember reading about a philosopher — Norman Malcolm, I want to say — who argued you can never actually verify anything happened during sleep, since the only evidence for a dream is what someone says after waking, and the whole point is they were unconscious at the time. And then a second guy, Dennett I think, floated the idea that maybe the dream gets composed at the instant of waking, like a story written backwards from the telling, with the memory feeling like a replay. If either of those views were right, would the REM-awakening data look any different? It seems to me like it wouldn't, which is exactly what worries me about the piece.

So here's what I actually need for the article: what does mainstream dream research methodology presuppose about what dreams ARE? Does treating dream reports as scientific data commit you to dreams being experiences had during sleep, or could a scientist honestly run the same studies while believing the whole thing is confabulated at the moment of waking? And is there anything in the science itself — the lucid dream two-way communication stuff, say, or even just the timing of the awakenings — that could in principle discriminate between the "movie playing in real time" picture and the "written at the door on your way out" picture? I want to make sure I'm not overclaiming in either direction, and also if I've garbled Malcolm's or Dennett's positions above, please correct me, because I'd much rather hear it from you than from my inbox after publication.

One more ask: since this is going out to a general audience under my name, I need to know where the actual lines of dispute are. Please separate what's more or less settled among people who work on dreams — scientifically or philosophically — from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me some neat consensus if there isn't one. If the honest answer is that nobody knows whether dreams are experiences in the relevant sense, and the scientists just proceed as if they are because it works fine for their purposes, then say that plainly, because honestly that might be the most interesting part of the story.
