For about two years I've been thinking about Robert Nozick's entitlement theory entirely on my own — I have no academic background, and I've been working from summaries and secondary discussions rather than from the original text of Anarchy, State, and Utopia. What keeps me occupied is the Wilt Chamberlain argument: we start from a distribution that everyone agrees is just, a thousand spectators each voluntarily pay twenty-five cents to watch Chamberlain play, and we end up with Chamberlain rich. As I understand Nozick, the point is that if the starting distribution was just and each transfer was voluntary, the ending distribution must be just as well — so any principle that demands restoring the original pattern either has to forbid the voluntary transfers, making liberty answerable to the pattern, or has to keep re-redistributing without end. My closest friend, who thinks of herself as a Rawlsian, has been arguing with me about this for a year, and we have finally admitted that we have been talking past each other. We would like to do a written adversarial collaboration of the sort where both parties sign the final document even though it records their residual disagreement.

Please draft the joint statement the two of us could sign. Begin with a numbered section of claims both sides accept — covering what the Chamberlain example does and does not show, the distinction between historical principles of justice and patterned or end-state principles, and the fact that actual histories of acquisition and transfer in the real world fall far short of Nozick's own three-part theory (acquisition in accordance with the proviso, voluntary transfer, and rectification of past violations). Follow this with a disagreements table, in which each remaining disagreement is stated in wording that the side holding the view would itself accept. I suspect the live disagreements are these: whether the baseline for the Lockean proviso should be a state of nature without property or some feasible alternative arrangement; whether transfers remain genuinely voluntary when the starting distribution reflects accumulated past injustice that no one alive can trace; and whether the market value of exceptional talents is produced socially in a way that limits the entitlement to their full market earnings.

For each disagreement, identify the crux beneath it, and then describe a concrete case or argument that both of us would agree bears on that crux, recording each side's prediction in advance. Two cases we would specifically like included: first, a variant of the Chamberlain scenario in which the spectators' quarters reach them through a long chain of earlier voluntary transfers whose first link was an appropriation that violated the proviso, though no one living remembers it; and second, a scenario in which Chamberlain's grandchildren inherit the fortune and the question is whether their holdings inherit its legitimacy. If part of our dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, the statement should say which part and explain why.
