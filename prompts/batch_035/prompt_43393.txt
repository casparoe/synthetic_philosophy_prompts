I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy of science course this semester, and there is a pattern in our unit on scientific realism that I keep running into and cannot find a term for. Whenever my professor argues that the evidence by itself cannot decide between two theories, the second theory is never one that anybody actually believed. It is always manufactured on the spot from the first one: take whatever theory we already accept — the kinetic theory of gases, say, or general relativity — and then bolt on a clause. To give a few examples of the kind of thing I mean, all of which have shown up in our seminar at one point or another: a theory exactly like the accepted one except that the quantities in its equations denote something subtly different; the accepted theory plus a universal force whose effects precisely cancel; the accepted theory with the stipulation that its laws switch off in the year 3000. These second theories have a family resemblance. They are generated by a recipe from the winner, they could never have been proposed first, no one has ever come to believe them in response to evidence, and the recipe can be applied to absolutely any theory whatsoever. My professor treats them as perfectly good rivals, but they strike me as too cheap: the underdetermination argument seems to lean entirely on theories whose only role in life is to be rivals.

What I would like to know is whether there is an established name for this kind of machine-made rival — and, correspondingly, for underdetermination arguments that rest exclusively on such rivals. If there is, please tell me what it is and how it is standardly defined. If there is not, I would be grateful if you would coin a term, with a precise definition and some boundary cases, since I care more about the carving than the label. In particular, I want to understand where a recipe rival ends and an independently motivated rival begins. The Lorentzian ether interpretation, which makes the same predictions as special relativity, had real proponents with independent reasons for wanting an ether; does that put it on a different side of the line from the switch-off-in-the-year-3000 variant? Would something like epicycle astronomy count as a genuine rival on this way of drawing things, or a parasitic one? And finally: does the fact that these rivals are derived rather than discovered affect the strength of the underdetermination argument itself? I have a feeling there is a distinction waiting to be made here between a trivial version of the argument and a version that should actually worry the scientific realist, but I do not know whether the literature has already made it.

Please write your answer in British English. And please do not simply give me a bare name and stop — I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
