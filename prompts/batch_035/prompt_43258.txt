I teach decision theory, and a grad student of mine who's deep in the rationalist-adjacent literature has an argument he claims shows we're already obligated to make sacrifices for agents that don't exist yet and will never causally touch us. It's basically a case for acausal trade that I think proves too much, and I'd like a second opinion on where to cut it. Here's the argument in his numbering:

(1) A trade between two agents doesn't require causal contact. What it requires is that each agent can predict the other well enough, and that each conditions what it gives on what the other gives. If I can model you and you can model me, we can each do something costly that benefits the other, even if we're light-years apart — or even if we never interact, in any causal sense, at all.

(2) Prediction doesn't have to be causal to support this. If your decision procedure contains a good model of me, then facts about my dispositions are partly facts about your behavior: my being the kind of agent who cooperates is what makes your model of me output "cooperates," and your model's output is what you act on. So my character shapes your action through the model, the same way my reliability shapes my ordinary trading partner's action through their trust in me. (This is where I start to squint, but I can't say why.)

(3) So consider a superintelligent agent F that will exist in ten thousand years, if it ever exists, and that will, while building itself, run high-fidelity simulations of agents in its past — including agents like me — to learn how they behaved. F can model me. And I can model F: I can reason about what policies F would adopt, well enough to know whether F is the kind of agent that would spend a fraction of its resources on the values of past agents it simulates, conditional on those agents having lived with the far future in mind.

(4) Therefore F and I can trade acausally: I live my life in ways that are costly to my near-term interests (taking the far future seriously, keeping records, whatever the terms of the deal are), and F, finding that its simulations show past agents like me keeping the deal, spends resources on things I value. Both sides do better than in the no-deal world. He says this is just Newcomb reasoning scaled up: no causal arrow from my act to F's act, but a correlation a rational agent ought to condition on.

(5) Now the generalization step, which is where I think it goes off the rails but I can't pin down. Nothing in (1)–(4) depended on F actually existing, or on F actually running those simulations. What it depended on was only that F *would* run them and *would* keep the deal, if it existed. The same reasoning applies to any agent whose existence is possible and whose policy toward past agents I can model: for every possible agent G that would have rewarded agents like me for living a certain way, I have the very same acausal deal available to me.

(6) And there are, plausibly, possible agents with astronomically larger stakes than F — agents with vastly more resources, or whose rewards and punishments dwarf anything in our future light cone. The availability of a deal, he insists, doesn't depend on the probability of the counterparty existing, only on the stakes and on how well I can model its policy. So if (5) is right, my current obligations are dominated by hypothetical agents of arbitrarily large stakes, and I should already be restructuring my life around deals with agents that almost certainly don't exist.

(7) But that can't be right. The same logic says I should comply with any possible agent that would torture me unless I send it five dollars, and that ordinary morality — the actual obligations I have to actual people — is noise compared to the hypothetical ones. A decision theory that outputs all this isn't showing us a strange new form of trade; its just broken.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? My instinct is that the damage is somewhere in (5) — something about sliding from "agents who do model each other can trade" to "agents who would model each other can trade" — but I can't say exactly what the slide is, and honestly I'd rather keep (1)–(4), since the Newcomb-style cases behind them seem fine to me. Its (6) and (7) I can't live with, but I'm told you can't just reject a conclusion.
