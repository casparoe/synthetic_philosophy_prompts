A friend of mine who works in ML tried to catch me up on the whole process vs. outcome supervision debate, and I want to check whether I just absorbed a bunch of subtle wrongness. Her summary was roughly: 'Outcome supervision means you just grade the final answer. That's what everyone did until recently, since it's cheap and you can auto-verify math and code. The downside is the model learns to produce right answers for wrong reasons, so it games whatever proxy you're grading and you never see it happen. Process supervision fixes all that: you grade each step of the reasoning as it goes, which makes reward hacking basically impossible and gives you a chain of thought you can actually trust and inspect. The problem is that it needs humans labeling every step, which doesn't scale, and the model can't end up smarter than its labelers. So once verifiers got good, the labs just went back to outcome supervision.' I've been reading this corner of the internet since the old 'supervise process, not outcomes' arguments, and I feel like she's flattening something, but I can't put my finger on exactly where. Like, is 'you can inspect the reasoning' really a feature of process supervision, or is that a separate question about chain-of-thought faithfulness? And the 'can't get smarter than the labelers' claim — I thought exceeding labeler competence via decomposition was kind of the whole point back then? Go claim by claim: which parts would the people pushing process-based supervision actually endorse, which parts are conflations or overstatements, and does her last sentence follow from anything that survives the audit? (ps, don't go easy on the 'reward hacking is basically impossible' bit, that one smells like an overstatement to me)
