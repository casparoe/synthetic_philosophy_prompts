I'm a senior in high school, and our math club has been spending a few weeks after school on decision theory. Last week our coach gave us a puzzle she called the absent-minded driver, and it produced the longest argument we've had all year, between two members I'll call Daniel and Priya. Since neither would budge and I'm supposed to present something at the next meeting, I promised I'd write up the exchange and get an outside referee's read, so I want to be scrupulously fair to both sides. Here is the setup exactly as we received it. A driver leaves a friend's house late at night and must get home along a one-way street containing exactly two intersections, one after the other, both reached in order. At each intersection he can either exit (turn off immediately) or continue (go straight). If he exits at the first intersection, he ends up somewhere awful (payoff 0); if he exits at the second, he arrives home (payoff 4); if he continues through both, he reaches a hotel where he can safely sleep (payoff 1). The catch is that he is absent-minded: by the time he reaches any intersection he has no memory of having passed one, and the intersections look identical, so from inside the car the two situations are indistinguishable. He knows the whole setup, the payoffs, and his own absent-mindedness.

The argument ran as follows. (1) Daniel said the answer is trivial: before setting off, the driver should adopt the plan "continue at the first intersection, exit at the second," which gets him home for a payoff of 4; since that plan beats every alternative available at the outset, it is simply what he should do, full stop. (2) Priya replied that the plan is unexecutable: an absent-minded driver standing at an intersection cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, so the only rules he can actually follow there are "always exit" (payoff 0) and "always continue" (payoff 1, since he will continue at both and end at the hotel). She argued the best he can genuinely do is randomize — carry a biased coin, flip it at each intersection, continue with probability 2/3 — which our coach worked out with us to have expected payoff 4/3. (3) Daniel said this was rationality throwing away two-thirds of the value for nothing: if randomizing beats both pure rules, that only shows the driver should use a commitment device, such as a navigation app that announces "second intersection," and in any case "should" means "should follow the best available plan," so the answer remains his plan. (4) Priya answered that with a working app there is no absent-mindedness and hence no puzzle — the puzzle is precisely about what "should" means when the agent cannot distinguish his own situations — and, worse, that Daniel's attachment to the ex-ante best plan goes strange from inside the car: a driver following her coin-flip policy who finds himself at an intersection should reckon he is at the second one with probability 2/5, making an immediate exit worth 8/5, which beats the 4/3 the policy promises; yet if he always gives in to that temptation he exits at the first intersection and collects 0. (5) Daniel's last word was that a policy you are tempted to abandon the moment you try to follow it cannot be what rationality demands, so if her 2/3 rule is not even self-supporting, his plan, however unexecutable, is still the right answer to the question actually asked — which he insisted was about what the driver should do, not about what some coin should do.

Neither they nor the rest of us could sort out who was right about which step. Would you adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is right, wrong, or right about something smaller than it claims, with your reasoning — and then explain what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relationship between assessing a decision from before the drive and assessing it from inside it? I have another week before the meeting, so a careful answer is worth more to me than a quick one.
