I've come to hold that the Pasadena paradox — a St. Petersburg-style coin game whose payoffs alternate in sign and shrink in just such a way that the "expected value" converges, but only conditionally, so that its value depends on the order of summation and natural truncations oscillate without settling — reveals no deep fault in expected-utility theory. The right response, I think, is conventional: fix a summation method or truncate the series at some finite stage, treat that choice as a policy decision rather than a discovery, and carry on; once the convention is set, no substantive question about rational preference remains. What would count against this? I want the cases and results that should move me: pairs of gambles where one weakly dominates the other in every outcome yet the chosen convention values them oppositely; arguments that some summation methods are rationally compulsory rather than merely conventional (for instance, appeals to dominance reasoning or to the long-run behavior of the partial sums); and reasons to think the case class is not a curiosity — heavy-tailed, Pascalian decision situations where the convention changes what one should actually do. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed conventionalist should concede from what they would dismiss, and assess honestly whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that matters for a normative thesis of this kind.
