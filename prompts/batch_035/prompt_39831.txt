I teach an upper-level seminar called "Learning What Humans Want" — sixteen students, a mix of computer science and philosophy majors, one 75-minute session. Next week's topic is inverse reinforcement learning: inferring an agent's preferences from its observed behavior, rather than from what it says it wants. On paper, students find this comfortable — "revealed preference," they say, like they learned in economics. What I want them to feel in their bones is the part that isn't comfortable: the inference from behavior to preference is radically underdetermined, and the assumption that the observed agent is rational — or approximately rational, or rational-in-what-way — is doing enormous, mostly invisible work. Two agents with entirely different preferences can produce identical trajectories; the same trajectory supports incompatible preference hypotheses; and what you learn about preferences is boxed into the situations the agent actually happened to be in.

Design an in-class activity that produces these difficulties experientially rather than presenting them on a slide. Here is a rough shape you can improve on, but the essentials should survive: a demonstration phase in which the class observes a confederate "agent" (a student I've briefed beforehand, or me) making a sequence of choices in some simple structured scenario — I'm imagining a foraging or commuting setup where the agent makes a dozen or so decisions, some in ordinary situations and a few in edge cases like a shortcut that passes near a hazard, or a choice between a reliable small reward and a risky large one. The observers record the trajectory, then work in small teams to commit, in writing, to a hypothesis about the agent's preferences — a full ranking, not just a single favorite. Then comes the prediction phase: I present a set of novel situations the agent never encountered, and each team predicts what the agent will do. Scoring should be for correct predictions only, not for plausible-sounding stories — that's the payoff scheme that makes underdetermination sting. And there should be a twist I can actually run: for example, revealing that half the teams were told the agent is a careful planner and half were told the agent is impulsive and habit-bound, so that the same data got different preference labels depending on the rationality assumption smuggled in with the framing; or engineering the demonstration so that two teams with genuinely incompatible committed hypotheses both turn out to fit every observed choice perfectly, and only diverge on the novel situations.

The activity should be runnable without computers, without money, and without the words "inverse reinforcement learning" appearing anywhere until the debrief. Please give me: the full rules and materials; a concrete scenario with enough choice points to be interesting but few enough to run live; the briefing script for the confederate agent, including how to keep the behavior consistent across sessions; the set of novel prediction situations and what each one is designed to discriminate between; a timing plan for 75 minutes; the scoring scheme; and the debrief, which should map specific moments in the activity onto the formal ideas — non-identifiability of the objective from the data, the rationality prior as a hypothesis-shaping device rather than a neutral assumption, the dependence of what you can learn on where the demonstrator actually went, and the everyday-life parallel of inferring what someone wants from what they do. The debrief should also connect to why this matters for AI alignment, since that's the course's through-line: if we want machines to learn human values from human behavior, the ambiguities the students just wrestled with are ones the machine inherits, scaled up.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — confederates drifting off-script, teams colluding with the confederate, students treating the preference hypotheses as a guessing game about a hidden answer rather than an inference problem — and how to handle each. And include an honest note on which features of real inverse reinforcement learning no classroom activity at this scale can reproduce: real inference happens over enormous parametric hypothesis spaces rather than four or five candidates, real demonstrators are not briefed by the person scoring the predictions, and no debrief can replicate the feeling of a learning algorithm confidently outputting a precise wrong objective. For those, I'll need the lecture anyway.
