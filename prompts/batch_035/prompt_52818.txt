At a dinner party last weekend the conversation turned to a contemporary novelist whose personal conduct many at the table condemned, and when someone said she could no longer in good conscience recommend his books to her book club, another guest waved this off with the phrase "separate the art from the artist," as though that settled the matter. When pressed, he seemed to mean several different things at once: that the man's behavior is irrelevant to whether the novels are any good, that we should judge the work on its own terms without consulting the biography, and that enjoying a novel is not an endorsement of its author. I have also heard the same phrase used in the opposite direction, to argue that we can and should keep reading problematic authors precisely because the aesthetic verdict and the moral verdict come apart, and once, in a literature seminar, to defend something stronger — a claim about interpretation itself, namely that the author's life and intentions have no bearing on what the work means. That last use struck me as very different from the dinner-party use, and it made me suspect the phrase is doing a lot of ambiguous work.

I would like you to take the phrase apart. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "separate the art from the artist" could be expressing — as a menu, please, not a single gloss — and for each one say whether it is trivial, substantive, defensible, or false. Distinguish carefully, for instance, between claims about the work's aesthetic value, claims about its meaning or correct interpretation, claims about the moral status of consuming or enjoying it, and claims about whether aesthetic and moral evaluation are conceptually independent. Then tell me which of those readings would actually support the move my dinner-party acquaintance was making, which would support the seminar's use, and whether any of the readings, if true, would really license the conclusion that the biography is irrelevant. I want something I could bring back to that table.

Please write your answer as continuous prose only, in the style of a letter or an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Keep it in American English, and please be rigorous rather than merely balanced — if some readings are confused or untenable, say so plainly.
