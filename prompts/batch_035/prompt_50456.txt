Consider the history of artificial neural networks as a case study for Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, and give me a disciplined mapping rather than a victory lap. The episode: Rosenblatt's perceptron in the late 1950s, accompanied by extraordinary public hype about machines that would soon walk, talk, and learn; Minsky and Papert's 1969 demonstration that single-layer perceptrons cannot even represent XOR, followed by the collapse of funding and the first AI winter; the connectionist revival in the mid-1980s driven by the popularization of backpropagation; the disappointment of the 1990s, when deep networks stubbornly refused to train, statistical methods like SVMs captured the field's attention, and a reasonable observer would have judged the programme dead; and then the succession from GPU-era breakthroughs around 2012 through the transformer architecture of 2017 to large language models and the scaling laws that now predict their performance in advance.

Run the analysis in both directions. First, theory to case: identify the hard core of the neural network programme -- is it that intelligence is learning from data by adjusting connection weights, or something else, and did it stay fixed across these decades or quietly change, as when biological plausibility was abandoned for whatever gradient descent could exploit? Identify the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses (architectures, training tricks, initialization schemes, scale) and say which revisions were legitimately protective-belt work and which were ad hoc. Then classify the phases: which were progressive problem shifts, which degenerating, and what, concretely, counted as a novel prediction at each stage. Second, case to theory: a good Lakatosian in 1975 or 1995 should presumably have advised abandoning a degenerating programme, and would have been wrong. Does that show the degenerating-phase criterion is useless in real time, that the winter was a hibernation your framework cannot distinguish from death, or that the eventual success belongs to a successor programme with a different hard core -- and if the hard core did change, is Lakatos's apparatus even able to say that one programme replaced another rather than that one programme progressed? Finally, a question I care about: the scaling laws predict benchmark performance years in advance, but they are predictions about artifacts we construct rather than about nature. Does Lakatos's standard of novel prediction make sense for an engineering-driven field at all, and if not, what should replace it in judging whether the current phase is genuinely progressive or a sophisticated degeneration that happens to be profitable? Tell me where Lakatos fits this history, where he strains, and where he is simply silent.
