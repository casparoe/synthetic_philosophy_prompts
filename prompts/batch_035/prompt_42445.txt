I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've somehow let the organizers of a summer ideas festival talk me into running an interactive 75-minute session — call it "the strange logic of nuclear brinkmanship." Crowd will be about 30 adults, mixed ages, mostly there for fun but the kind of people who'll actually engage. I want them to *feel* deterrence logic in their stomachs, not hear a lecture about it: the Schelling-style stuff where it can be rational to visibly burn your own escape routes, where backing down gets harder the higher you climb, and where a threat can work precisely because part of the outcome is out of everyone's hands.

My half-baked plan so far: pairs of players face off over several rounds where each can hold, escalate, or back down, and every escalation level raises the chance that a die roll ends the round with both players losing everything. Payoffs in raffle tickets for a decent prize, so the stakes are small but real-ish. I also want a commitment mechanic of some kind — a card or gesture that publicly destroys your own option to back down — and maybe a rule that escalation can never be fully unwound, so the slide toward the cliff keeps happening even when both players would love to freeze it.

Design the full thing for me: rules I can teach in under five minutes, the payoff scheme, how the randomness should work so it feels like genuine risk rather than pure chance, and the commitment mechanic. Then the debrief: map the moments they actually experienced onto the deterrence ideas — the value of losing options, why threats become credible when some of it is left to chance, the difference between deterrence working and everyone just getting lucky. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people treating it as a joke, refusing to escalate at all, collusion within pairs?), and be honest about which parts of real nuclear deterrence the game simply cannot reproduce — nobody actually dies, everyone can leave the tent — and what I have to supply myself to bridge the gap. One more thing: I'm worried the session accidentally teaches the comfy but wrong lesson that brinkmanship is basically a bluff that never gets called. How do I structure it so that takeaway can't form?
