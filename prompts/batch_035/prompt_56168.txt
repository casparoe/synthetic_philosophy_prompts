I teach philosophy and, through an accident of institutional history, sit on the scientific advisory panel that reviews species listings for our state wildlife agency. Our next agenda item is whether the state should continue to protect the red wolf population in our eastern counties as an endangered species. The complication is well known to everyone on the panel: red wolves interbreed readily with coyotes, and the recovery program spends much of its budget sterilizing territorial coyotes as placeholders precisely to keep the wolf line from dissolving into coyote ancestry — while recent genomic work has suggested that the red wolf itself may have originated as a coyote–gray wolf hybrid rather than as a long-independent lineage, a conclusion other geneticists contest. Our enabling statute protects species, and it presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about which populations fall inside a listed species and which are merely hybrids or local variants. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — continue protection as-is, continue under a redefined conservation unit, or discontinue — and it should make explicit which species concept the recommendation rests on (biological, phylogenetic, or some pluralist or lineage-based alternative), since the choice between them does real work here rather than being a label applied after the fact. It should also engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side, including the worry that choosing a species definition for its conservation consequences is not taxonomy but advocacy.
