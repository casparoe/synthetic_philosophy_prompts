so, novelist here. my next book hinges on a character who does something terrible more or less by not doing anything: during a drought she reroutes the village's water allocation downstream to save the town there, and people in her own village die. my editor keeps insisting "she didn't kill them, she let them die," and it's starting to sound like he thinks that settles the moral weight of the whole chapter. I genuinely don't know whether the doing/allowing distinction holds up, and I don't want to build a character's entire moral arc on a philosophical shortcut that actual philosophers have already torn apart. so before I commit the book to any position, I want the spec first.

what I'm asking for is the list of requirements: what would an adequate theory of the doing/allowing (or killing vs letting die) distinction actually have to deliver? things it has to get right — make sure you include cases like mine, where harm comes from redirecting something already in motion rather than initiating anything, and also the messier kind where a bad outcome only happens because a hundred people each fail to do some small thing, so no single person is plausibly "allowing" all of it — plus the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (refraining vs enabling, doing harm vs redirecting harm vs merely relocating it), and whether the theory has to track the legal line between act and omission or is allowed to say the law is just wrong. for each requirement, tell me what case or consideration is doing the work behind it, and say which of the requirements you'd treat as non-negotiable versus which are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a constraint. you can mention the trolley stuff since its the obvious reference point, but don't lean on it — I'm more interested in the weird edges than the famous case.

two things I especially want: flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (if the list itself is impossible to meet, that's actually the most useful thing you could tell me), and identify the single requirement that knocks out the most of the standard accounts, whatever you take those to be.

and last thing — tell me how confident you are in the pieces of this as you go, and single out the point you're least sure of. a character's redemption arc is going to hang on whatever I take from your answer, so I'd much rather have honest uncertainty than a confident lecture. if the honest answer is "nobody knows whether the distinction survives," I can work with that too — ambivalence is basically my medium.
