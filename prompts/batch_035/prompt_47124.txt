I'm a philosophy professor who's spent most of my career on ethics and decision theory, and next month I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on AI alignment where I'll be defending a claim I'm pretty invested in: coherent extrapolated volition — the old Yudkowsky idea that a machine ought to do what humanity would come to want under more idealized conditions, knowing more, thinking faster, being more like the people we wish we were, and having developed together — isn't the sci-fi handwaving that its critics (and honestly some of its fans) make it out to be. My line is that it's a species of preference idealization that moral philosophy has been doing quietly all along: reflective equilibrium, ideal observer theory, the kind of autonomy Rawls gestures at with the original position, even the way economists talk about "true" preferences behind noisy choice data.

Here's my twist, and this is where I expect to get mauled: I want to argue that the extrapolation step should NOT be read as a prediction about what actual humans would converge on if you somehow ran the tape forward. It should be read as a regimentation — on analogy with ideal gases and frictionless planes in physics, or Quine's notion of regimenting ordinary language into a canonical notation. The justification for the idealization is pragmatic (does it systematize, does it reduce friction in our deliberation) not predictive accuracy. If nobody would actually converge on the extrapolated volition, I want to say, that's fine — ideal gases don't exist either.

The audience is the problem. It's a small workshop, maybe fifteen people: mostly empirical psychologists and behavioral decision researchers who spend their careers showing that human preferences are constructed on the fly by anchoring, framing, question order, whatever was on the previous survey page — they think the whole notion of a stable "what we would want" is an artifact of bad elicitation methodology. Plus two serious Kantians who I suspect will say the entire setup bakes heteronomy into the foundations: it's still our contingent, given wishes in the drivers seat, just laundered through an idealization, and laundering doesn't make it autonomy. And there's a political theorist who will tell me this is just deliberative democracy theory with a gadget fetish, and that I should go read her field before reinventing it.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually land, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them — the methodologists in terms of elicitation artifacts and constructed preferences, the Kantians in terms of autonomy and heteronomy, the political theorist in her vocabulary. For each one: a reply I can give in about two minutes, and an honest note about where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections have genuine force and which ones I should concede up front rather than pretend I have an answer to — I'd rather spend my preparation time on the real fights. And please, include the one question I'm quietly praying nobody asks — the kind where the honest answer knocks out a load-bearing wall of the whole talk. I need to know what it is before they do.
