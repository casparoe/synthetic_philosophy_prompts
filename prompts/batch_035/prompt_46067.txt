In an online writing community I help moderate, a well-liked member was caught passing off other people's work as his own. When the condemnations came in, he replied with the old proverb that people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones, noting that several of his loudest critics had committed lesser offenses over the years — recycled ideas without attribution, some flattering self-description in their bios — and demanding that they withdraw their accusations. The community is now split between those who think he has a genuine point about standing and those who think he is weaponizing an old saw to dodge accountability, and I've noticed something that worries me: if the proverb is read strictly, as saying that anyone with faults of their own forfeits the right to blame others, it seems to abolish blame altogether, since none of us has clean hands. I want to know what the proverb actually commits us to before we make a ruling. Please lay out exactly five distinct precise claims the proverb could be making, each in a sentence or two, and for each say whether it is plausible and whether it would actually entitle our plagiarist to demand withdrawal of the accusations rather than mere silence from his critics. Make sure the menu includes the merely prudential reading; a reading on which the proverb concerns the blamer's fairness or standing as something distinct from the truth of what he says; a reading that takes the glass seriously — a claim about transparency, about what the blamer must already know regarding his own conduct when he blames; and the radical reading that generates my abolition worry, with your verdict on whether it follows from any of the more plausible ones. Give me the list as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction and no conclusion.
