Two friends of mine have been having the same argument for about two years and finally agreed to do one of those adversarial collaboration things where you write up a joint document. I've been tasked with drafting it, and I want a decent first draft out of you.

The setup: one of them is a hardline expected-utility person who bites the bullet on St. Petersburg — the gamble has infinite expected value, so you should be willing to pay any finite price for a single play, and he thinks anything short of that is just people flinching. The other thinks that's obviously wrong: the gamble is worth, at most, a modest amount, and she doesn't think she's being irrational, she thinks the infinite expectation is an artifact of a formalism being applied outside its domain. They've tried all the standard moves on each other. He says her finite valuation needs a principle: risk aversion? diminishing marginal utility of money? a cap on utilities? — and every principled version she offers either sounds ad hoc or destroys expected-utility reasoning elsewhere, and then it's her turn to flinch. She says his position licenses absurd behavior — paying your life savings for one flip — and that no real agent could ever act on it, so "infinite expected value" is doing no work except generating paradoxes like Pascal's mugging. They also go in circles about repeated plays: he leans on the fact that averaging many independent St. Petersburg payoffs diverges, so any finite price looks like a steal in the long run; she thinks looking at "the long run" for a game you'll play twice is a shell game. And every once in a while someone brings up the Pasadena game — the variant with alternating signs where even the expected value is undefined — and they can't even agree on whether that's a problem for the bullet-biter or extra ammunition against him, because it's unclear whether infinity is the disease or just one symptom of deeper trouble with unbounded payoffs.

Write the draft of their joint statement. What I need:

1. A numbered section of claims they can BOTH sign off on — about the setup itself, about what expected-utility theory actually commits you to, about which of the usual "resolutions" they jointly think fail (they both, for instance, think the "casinos go broke so can't offer it" reply is a dodge, since the question is what you'd rationally pay if it were offered). Don't let this section be vacuous — the whole point is finding the non-trivial stuff they agree on.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated precisely, in wording each side would actually accept for their own view. So the bullet-biter's position has to be stated in a way he'd sign, not in a strawman way like "he thinks you should ruin yourself on a coin flip."

3. The cruxes — the subquestions such that settling them would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether there's a principled, non-ad-hoc way to get a finite valuation, and another is what exactly "rationality" is doing when no agent can distinguish infinites, but they may have others I haven't spotted. For each crux, give a concrete test, argument, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, and write down what each side would predict in advance — that's the part that makes an adversarial collaboration real, and it's the part neither of them will do for their own argument.

4. If any chunk of their dispute turns out to be verbal — e.g., about whether "worth" in "what the gamble is worth" means fair price, willingness to pay, or something else — say which chunk and why, and separate it from the genuinely substantive part.

Keep it fair. I know both of them and if either one reads the draft and feels their side got weakened to make the other look good, the whole exercise dies and I get to listen to round three of this argument at Thanksgiving.
