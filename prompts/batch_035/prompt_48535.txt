In a philosophy of science seminar, a classmate keeps invoking the slogan that the evidence underdetermines the theory, and most recently used it to defend a friend practicing psychoanalysis: when a patient's behavior seems to refute a prediction, the friend adds a further assumption to absorb the anomaly, and my classmate says this is no different from a physicist who blames a failed measurement on the apparatus rather than the theory under test, saving it to fight another day. I suspect the slogan is packing several distinct claims together, but I can't sort them out. Please lay out, as a numbered list I can take back to seminar, the different precise theses the slogan could be expressing — for instance, that any finite body of data is compatible with infinitely many hypotheses, that a failed prediction refutes only the conjunction of a theory and its auxiliary assumptions, or that there are always distinct theories that no possible observation could decide between — and for each thesis, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or outright false, and whether it actually supports my classmate's further conclusion: that because empirical considerations can never settle the matter, it is legitimate to choose between rival theories on non-empirical grounds such as simplicity, or even ideological preference. In particular, I would like to know whether any reading strong enough to get that conclusion is one a reasonable philosopher of science could accept.
