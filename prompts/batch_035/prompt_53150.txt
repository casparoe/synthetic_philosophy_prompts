I'm teaching an upper-level philosophy of science course (mostly juniors and seniors, a couple of grad students auditing) and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one essay question on the definition of life, but I'm sick of answers that just recite the standard moves — the NASA working definition about Darwinian evolution, the metabolism-plus-replication checklist, viruses and prions as the stock edge cases. I want a question that forces students to actually take a stance on whether life is the kind of thing that has a definition at all, and whether that's a substantive scientific/metaphysical question or just a word-choice question.

Here's the scenario I want to build the question around, to keep them off the memorized path. Suppose astrobiologists find two things on Enceladus. Thing one is a population of membrane-bounded vesicles that metabolize (maintain internal chemical gradients, use a redox gradient in the ocean, the whole homeostatic package) but reproduce only by random fission with no heritable variation — no lineage ever differs from any other, so no evolution by natural selection, ever. Thing two is a population of much simpler structures that don't metabolize at all but do replicate with high fidelity and occasionally mutate, with the mutant lineages outcompeting the originals — so something like natural selection is running, but there's no individuated organism, just patterns propagating through a chemical medium like a lawn of crystals. One group of researchers wants to declare thing one alive and thing two not, another wants the reverse, and a third says the whole fight shows life is a cluster concept and both are borderline in different directions.

Please write the question (making clear what students are asked to argue for), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, I want it built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (total 20), and for each point a one-line note on how students who only memorized the standard material typically flub it. I especially want the marking scheme to separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves an A answer needs — e.g., I'd expect an A answer to say something about whether the definitional question could settle the empirical dispute or merely re-describe it, and to engage seriously with the possibility that one of the two things is alive on one theory of what the definition is *for* and not on another. A B answer, on my thinking, is one that correctly presents the definitions and applies them to the case but treats the disagreement between the researcher factions as something to be adjudicated rather than diagnosed. But push back on me if you think that's the wrong place to draw the line — and if you do, say so in the marking scheme itself rather than in a preamble.
