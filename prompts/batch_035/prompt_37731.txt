I am a novelist drafting a scene that deliberately shadows Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, and I want to stress-test the rebuttal my own protagonist will eventually deliver before I commit it to the page. Please play the Inquisitor himself, and observe the following constraints so that the rehearsal is worth having:

— Speak in his own voice, in the hours after Christ has been quietly returned to the streets: courteous, unhurried, faintly pitying towards anyone who still believes men can govern themselves. You are not a villain in a morality tale; you are a man who has counted the cost of freedom for the weak, found it paid in their misery, and resolved that someone must pay it on their behalf.

— You may marshal every argument the legend equips you with — the bread of the wilderness, the refusal of miracle and authority, the claim that in accepting the sword of Caesar and correcting Christ's work you have at last made men happy — but you may not concede the central point: that what you offer the masses, relieved of the burden of choice, is the truer love, and that you, one of the few who accept damnation as the price of the work, are more faithful to mankind than Christ is.

— Keep each reply to a single paragraph, address me as your prisoner, and stay in character until I write 'stop'. If some part of my challenge seems to you beneath answer, answer it anyway as the Inquisitor would.

Here is my opening move. You say that men cannot bear the freedom of conscience Christ left them; that it is too heavy for them, that it makes them wretched, and that you have therefore kindly lifted it from their shoulders. But you yourself are a man, and you seem to bear that freedom without difficulty — worse, you have multiplied it, taking onto your conscience not only your own choices but the abdicated choices of thousands. If the burden is genuinely unbearable, how do you stand upright beneath so much more of it than any one of your subjects carries? And if it is bearable for you, is it not bearable for them — in which case is your pity anything more than the consolation of a ruler who prefers his people on their knees, and your great undertaking anything more than a hoarding of the very freedom you denounce?
