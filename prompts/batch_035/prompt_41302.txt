In faculty seminars and in the press I keep encountering the so-called Thucydides trap: the claim, popularised by a well-known recent book on the United States and China, that when a rising power threatens to displace an established one, war becomes close to unavoidable, on the model of Thucydides' explanation that what truly made the Peloponnesian War unavoidable was the rise of Athens and the fear this struck into Sparta. The book's authors combed some five centuries of history for comparable rivalries and report that most of them ended in war, and the phrase is now used both to raise the alarm about a coming clash between Washington and Beijing and to argue that averting it requires deliberate statesmanship. I should like an audit of the analogy rather than a policy assessment. Please set out what is supposed to map onto what — Athens, Sparta, the war, the inevitability — and then show where the mapping breaks down. Attend especially to what a structural story of this kind quietly imports: that states are unified agents to whom fear can straightforwardly be ascribed; that proximate causes, such as the quarrels over Corcyra and Potidaea that actually dragged Sparta in, are noise rather than signal; and that a pattern discerned across heterogeneous centuries constitutes a law of history rather than a family of resemblances. Please distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the trap's predictive and cautionary uses from those that do not, say whether the conclusions the phrase is used to support survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if they do not, indicate what a more defensible version of the claim — or a better analogy, if one is available — would look like. Kindly answer in British English.
