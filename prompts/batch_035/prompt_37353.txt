I'm teaching a one-off evening session at our local rationality meetup on the interpretations of probability — frequentism vs subjectivism vs propensity — and I want help planning it, not the content itself. Audience is ~15 people, mixed backgrounds (a couple of software engineers, some humanities folks, no statisticians), 90 minutes, whiteboard only. Most of them have never realized there's even a question here, they just think "probability is probability."

The thing I keep going back and forth on is the sequence. Part of me wants to do frequentism first since it's the intuitive default, but then I worry they'll fossilize into thinking that's just What Probability Is and the later interpretations will feel like weird add-ons rather than live alternatives. Is there a better entry point — maybe starting from a case where the frequency definition flatly fails, like a one-off event? I also want to decide the carrying example in advance, and I'd like it to be one scenario that gets genuinely different answers (or at least different glosses) under all three interpretations, not one example per interpretation, since that feels like it lets everyone off the hook. Something like a weather app saying 30% chance of rain tomorrow, or a forecaster putting odds on a specific election — I'm not sure which is better or whether there's something that works harder.

Then the misconceptions. I'm guessing the big ones are: that frequentism is the "objective scientific" one and subjectivism means vibes or wishful thinking; that propensities are just frequencies in disguise; and that the interpretations disagree about the math (they don't, which I suspect is crucial and easy to miss). Where in the sequence should I head off each? And relatedly, what should I openly refuse to get into at this level — I'm thinking things like the measure-theoretic foundations, dutch book arguments in full detail, maybe anything about quantum propensities — and how do I flag that without it sounding like hand-waving.

Please structure your answer like this: (1) "The sequence" — the order to introduce things in and why, roughly 300 words; (2) "The carrying example" — which single scenario to build everything on and what it needs to have and not have, roughly 150 words; (3) "Misconception map" — the predictable confusions and the exact point in the sequence to defuse each, roughly 300 words; (4) "What to leave out" — roughly 100 words; (5) "The exit check" — one question I can ask at the end that would show me whether they actually got the distinction rather than just nodding along, roughly 50 words.
