I'm writing a position paper on the cogito for a graduate seminar on Descartes, and before I commit anything to print I'd like a consistency audit of my overall position. I hold the following:

(1) The cogito is indubitable in a distinctive way: any sincere attempt to doubt my own existence is self-defeating, because doubting is itself a mode of thinking.
(2) Nevertheless, the cogito is not an inference. I don't think its certainty can rest on a syllogism with the suppressed premise that whatever thinks exists; the certainty of the particular judgment seems prior to any general principle.
(3) I'm largely persuaded by the Lichtenberg-style objection: all the cogito really establishes is that thinking is occurring — it thinks, as it were — not that there is a substantial, persisting self doing the thinking.
(4) Even so, I maintain that when I run the cogito I gain genuine self-knowledge: I learn something about me specifically, not merely about the occurrence of some anonymous thought-event.
(5) I want to use the cogito to anchor at least one substantive Cartesian conclusion: that I know, with certainty, that I am a thinking thing.
(6) I also hold that the truth of the cogito is contingent — I could very well never have existed — and this has never struck me as a problem for its certainty.

Please take the role of a careful professional philosopher specializing in early modern philosophy and the epistemology of self-knowledge. Think through my commitments methodically — you might begin by briefly brainstorming which pairs could conflict — and then tell me: which of these commitments are in genuine tension with each other, and which tensions dissolve on closer inspection? Rank the real tensions by severity, say what would have to give in each case and what that concession would cost me, and identify the single cheapest revision that would make the whole package cohere. Please be specific and honest rather than diplomatic; I'd rather discover the problem now than in the seminar discussion.
