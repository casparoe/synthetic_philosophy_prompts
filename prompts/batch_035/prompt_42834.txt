I recently joined the commemorative review panel of the mid-sized city I moved to five years ago, and the first major case on our docket has an awkward personal dimension I'll explain below. I need a decision memo I can circulate before the panel's next session, and I'd like your help drafting it.

The case: our city's central square holds a bronze statue of Edwin Vail, who in the 1910s and 20s founded the hospital where I now work as a nurse, endowed the public library, and essentially built the mill economy on which the town's survival once depended. He was also, we've learned from a recently digitized archive, a principal investor in the realty companies that redlined the city's east side for three decades — the neighborhood where my husband's family was steered into a declining housing stock and from which his grandmother watched the hospital Vail built refuse some of her neighbors. A grassroots coalition wants the statue relocated to a museum courtyard with interpretive signage. A preservation society wants it left in place, arguing that the statue is the square's visual anchor, was paid for largely by worker subscription rather than by Vail himself, and that stripping every morally compromised founder from public space leaves us commemorating only the bland. The city council has kicked the decision to our panel with instructions to return a recommendation with a concrete implementation plan and budget band, not a set of options.

The panel has deadlocked on two positions that don't seem to actually engage each other. The removalists treat the statue as a standing act of honor, so Vail's redlining record disqualifies him now as it would have then; keeping it is a continuing endorsement. The preservationists treat the statue as a historical record — evidence of what this city chose to celebrate in 1931 — and argue that removal is a kind of forgetting dressed up as accountability. I notice that both sides concede the other's premise about what the statue *is doing* on its plinth, and I suspect the whole fight turns on that unargued question: does a figurative statue in a position of civic prominence honor, or merely record, or does it do something in between that neither 'honor' nor 'record' captures — perhaps something more like constituting a civic identity that later generations inherit whether or not they endorse it? There is also a proposal on the table from a local artist to keep the statue but recontextualize it in place with a counter-installation, which strikes some panelists as a genuine third way and others as a way for the city to have it both ways, morally speaking, at reduced cost.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. It should: (1) recommend one course of action among relocation with signage, retention with a counter-installation, retention unchanged, or removal to storage — with a proposed timeline and a rough sense of resources involved; (2) do the philosophical work in the middle of the recommendation rather than as a preamble to it, i.e., take a position on what the statue is doing and show how that position, if right, settles the case; (3) engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly enough that its supporters could recognize their view in the memo and see what would have to be true for them to win; and (4) address my conflict-of-interest disclosure — my hospital's foundation bears Vail's name and my husband's family was among those harmed — recommending whether I should vote, recuse, or participate with a disclosed stake, and on what principle. You may assume any further facts about the city's history you need, as long as you make the assumptions explicit rather than smuggling them in.

Two final requests about the memo's epistemic character. First, where the memo's claims are matters of judgment rather than application of an agreed principle, it should say how confident it is and why, rather than adopting the flat tone of settled conclusions. Second, it should close by singling out the single point on which the recommendation most depends and which the author is least sure of — the place where I should press hardest when we deliberate. I'd rather circulate a memo that knows where it is thin than one that hides it.
