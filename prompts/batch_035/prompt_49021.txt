I teach an upper-level bioethics seminar, and next month I've been invited to lead a half-day workshop for the clinical ethics consultation service at a large teaching hospital on whether care ethics has anything to offer their actual practice. I should describe the room. The committee's working methodology is effectively principlism in the Beauchamp and Childress mold — autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice — and several of its members have fifteen or more years of casework organized around balancing those four considerations when they conflict, so they will hear anything I say as a proposal to amend or attack that framework. The chair is a retired internist who opens every discussion by asking what the recommendation would be by end of day; there is a palliative care physician who I suspect is quietly sympathetic to the view I'll be defending; a hospital attorney sits in on everything and thinks in terms of liability and precedent; and there is a social worker who told me in advance that she is torn — she finds the committee's framework too abstract for the situations she actually sits in on, but she is also wary of anything that romanticizes "care," because in her experience the follow-through on the committee's recommendations, the actual tending of patients at home, falls disproportionately on wives and daughters. My thesis, roughly, is that care ethics is neither a fifth principle to be tacked onto the four nor a rival decision procedure, but a reordering of what counts as the relevant features of a case: a consultation about withdrawing ventilation goes differently if you begin by mapping who has been sustaining the patient's life day to day, what those ongoing relationships of dependency already require, and what attention the situation itself demands, rather than by asking what the patient would have chosen. I want to argue this with a concrete running example — an elderly ventilated patient with a devoted but exhausted daughter who insists she is not asking that anything be withdrawn — rather than in the abstract.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. I want the six or so objections I am most likely actually to face from this particular room, phrased in the vocabulary they would use (casework and clinical ethics, not the vocabulary of a care-ethics colloquium), ordered by how much damage each would do to my thesis if badly handled. For each, give me a two-minute reply I could deliver in the meeting, plus a candid note on where the reply is soft — places where I should concede rather than push, and what exactly to concede. I am fairly sure the chair will open with the action-guidance objection: that what I am describing is a sensibility, not a method, and that at four o'clock the committee still has to write a recommendation with a reason attached to it. I have a reply I like — that principlism's "balancing" is itself under-determined and the caseworkers already smuggle in the relational facts — but I want to know where that reply is weakest when a room full of people who have balanced principles under time pressure for years pushes back. I also want you to think hard about the social worker's worry as an objection to my view specifically: that reframing consultations around relationships of care could make it easier, not harder, for the committee's recommendations to quietly assign the costs of care to families, and to women within families, and that a framework with no theory of justice built in gives her no vocabulary for resisting that. Tell me whether that is an objection I can answer in the room or one I should concede and build into the talk as a stated limit, and how to tell the difference without losing the audience. Finally, include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks — the one where my running example is most vulnerable — and what I should have ready to say if the palliative care physician asks it in front of everyone.
