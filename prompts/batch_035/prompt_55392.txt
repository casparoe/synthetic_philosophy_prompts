I'm a science journalist and I've spent the past few years on the consciousness beat -- hard problem stuff, anaesthesia studies, the occasional eccentric neuroscientist claiming his parrot is sentient. A mate of mine who did philosophy at Bristol has been on at me for ages that I should write down my actual position instead of paraphrasing other people's in feature pieces. So here it is, warts and all, and I'd like you to tell me whether it hangs together:

1. The what-it's-likeness of experience isn't reducible to physics. I can coherently imagine a creature physically identical to me with no inner life whatsoever, and I don't think that's just a failure of imagination on my part -- at minimum it shows you have to argue for the identity theory rather than assume it.
2. The physical world is causally closed. Every physical event, including every twitch of my fingers on this keyboard, has a sufficient physical cause. In all my years covering neuroscience I've never once needed a ghost in the machine to make a story make sense.
3. So I land on epiphenomenalism: brain processes generate experience, but experience doesn't push anything around downstream. Consciousness is the whistle on the steam train -- driven by the engine, not driving it.
4. I think the neuroscience of consciousness is making genuine progress. I've written warmly about the big collaborative projects hunting the neural correlates of consciousness, and I believe we are slowly working out which brain states go with which experiences. This is real science, not vibes.
5. People sometimes ask whether I believe in free will and I say yes and I mean it: I act for reasons, I deliberate, I could have phrased this paragraph differently, and that's all the freedom I actually care about. I'm not claiming my qualia overrule physics.
6. I trust my own introspection more or less across the board. When I'm in pain, I know I'm in pain and I know what the pain feels like. If somebody told me my beliefs about my own experience might be systematically wrong, I'd want to see a very good argument indeed.

My mate read this over my shoulder, said something like "oh that's juicy, there's at least one real contradiction in there and another clash that only looks like one," and then refused to elaborate, which I thought was a bit rude honestly. So: tell me which of these are in genuine tension and how badly, which only look like they clash, and what the cheapest fix would be in each case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me a list where everything is equally doomed, and be specific about which of the six would actually have to bend. Don't be shy about the fact I write about this stuff for a living -- if I've been filing copy that's incoherent, better I hear it from you.
