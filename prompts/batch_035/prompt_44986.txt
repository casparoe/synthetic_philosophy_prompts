Two of my colleagues — both broadly sympathetic to effective altruism — got into a long argument about whether we should be fanatical about the far future, and I'd like a careful adjudication.

Dana's opening position: the expected-value case is decisive. Even a small reduction in the probability of existential catastrophe this century protects a potentially astronomical number of future beings, so essentially everything should be devoted to it.

Marcus replied that Bostrom's simulation argument deflates this. Roughly: either civilizations go extinct before reaching technological maturity, or mature civilizations run few ancestor simulations, or we are almost certainly living in one. If we accept the argument's usual upshot — that we're probably simulated — then the far future isn't ours to protect. We're an experiment running in a posthuman's computer; the base-level future is causally beyond us; and when the run is switched off, the future beings Dana wants to save simply never exist. So her fanaticism should deflate in proportion to her credence that she's simulated.

Dana came back on two fronts. First, if simulated minds have moral status, then the far future inside a simulation matters exactly as much as the base-level one — Marcus can't coherently treat us as morally considerable while treating the simulated future as worthless. Second, and she thought this her stronger point: the simulation argument is itself a claim about the far future. The probability that we're simulated is fixed by what our descendants (or our simulators' descendants) will choose to do, so a high credence in simulation is a high credence that humanity, or something descended from us, survives to run vast numbers of simulations — which is close to the very outcome her fanaticism aims to safeguard. Rather than damping her view, her simulation credence seems to be evidence for it.

Marcus's final word: Dana is being selective. If we're simulated, the vast projects belong to the simulators, not to us; we influence at most what happens inside the run, and the simulators can end it whenever they like. The astronomical stakes are real, but they are not the stakes of our choices, and expected-value reasoning that ignores this is misapplied.

Please adjudicate move by move: who's right about what, and where does each argument overreach? In particular, I want to know whether Marcus's deflation argument works even if simulated minds matter morally; whether Dana's recursion point is sound or subtly confused; and whether the dispute ultimately turns on something other than the simulation argument — for instance, on what fanaticism about tiny probabilities of astronomical value actually requires.
