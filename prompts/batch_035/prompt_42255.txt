Every Christmas my family watches It's a Wonderful Life, the old Frank Capra one with Jimmy Stewart, and this year I couldn't stop thinking about it as a philosophy thing instead of just enjoying it. George Bailey spends the whole movie having no idea what his actions actually caused -- he doesn't know Potter stole the $8,000, he doesn't know saving his brother from the ice as a kid would later save a transport full of soldiers, half his life's biggest effects are invisible to him. Then Clarence the angel shows up and gives him the one thing nobody in real life gets: a full tour of the counterfactual. Bedford Falls without him, a.k.a. Pottersville. So in a sense the movie is about a guy who was clueless about the effects of his own choices and then gets to stop being clueless, at least for one night.

Here's where I got stuck. A while back I read an argument (on some forum, can't find it now) that even if you think the right thing to do is just whatever does the most good overall, you're basically sunk, because none of us can trace the effects of our choices more than a step or two out. Save a kid from drowning and maybe he grows up to be a war criminal, buy a bicycle and decades later the nickel you didn't spend on gum changes everything. The movie seems to take the opposite as given: Clarence's tour presents the no-George world as fully knowable and reliably awful, so George gets to read the ledger and feel good about his life. But is that tour actually the way counterfactuals work? It looked to me like the movie quietly assumes George is irreplaceable -- that nobody else would've stopped old Mr. Gower from putting poison in that capsule, nobody else would've built the affordable housing, nobody else jumps in the river after Harry. In real life other people step up when someone's absent, or don't, and you can't know which. Also the tour is curated by an interested party -- an angel trying to talk a man out of suicide -- so of course it only shows the bad ripples and none of the good ones from the no-George world. And one more thing that bugged me: the counterfactual is "George never existed," which is a strange thing to show a guy who's deciding what to do. It answers whether his existence on net was good, not whether his choices were.

So here's what I want from you: a real reading of the film that takes both the movie and this cluelessness problem seriously. Where does the film actually dramatize the problem (it seems telling that even with divine assistance, the "answer" to George's despair isn't a calculation at all -- it's the whole town showing up with cash at the end, which feels like the movie changing the subject from consequences to something else entirely), and where does it cheat to avoid the problem it raised? Does the ending amount to an answer to the "you can't trace effects" worry, or a refusal to answer it, and is the refusal maybe the better position? I want you to push back on my own reading too if you think I'm wrong about the irreplaceability thing. Please be upfront about how confident you are in the various claims, and point out the single spot in your reading you're least sure about. And write it in American English, since that's what I'm used to.
