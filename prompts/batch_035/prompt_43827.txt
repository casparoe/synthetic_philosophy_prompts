I'm a graduate student writing a term paper on the problem of logical omniscience — the fact that standard epistemic logic, on its usual possible-worlds semantics, entails that an agent knows every logical consequence of what it knows — and before I commit to a line of argument I want to check whether my own picture of the problem is even coherent. Here is where I've landed. First, I hold that closure under known logical consequence is a genuine norm of rationality: an agent who knows p, knows that p entails q, and yet fails to believe q is being irrational, full stop, even if deriving q from p would take more compute than exists in the universe. Second, I nonetheless think the logical omniscience "problem" is a harmless idealization, exactly analogous to frictionless planes in mechanics: we model the perfectly rational agent, and we can always add the resource bounds back in afterward, the way physicists add drag back in. Third, I think ordinary knowledge attributions to real agents are perfectly in order as they stand — when I say that a chess novice didn't know the winning fork was available while the grandmaster did, I'm saying something true and important, and not something about some idealized surrogate. Fourth, the right technical fix is the implicit/explicit distinction: implicit knowledge (everything that follows from what you have) is closed, explicit knowledge (what you have actually represented) is not, and explicit knowledge is what does the real descriptive work. Fifth, I believe rationality is constitutively bounded: an agent who burns ten years of compute verifying a trivial consequence, purely to avoid the "irrationality" of not having deduced it, is not approximating an ideal but malfunctioning.

My question is whether these five commitments hang together. I have a nagging feeling that the "harmless idealization" line and the "closure is a genuine norm" line pull against each other, and I also suspect something about the novice/grandmaster case is hard to square with the implicit/explicit distinction — but I honestly cannot tell whether these are real tensions or artifacts of my phrasing, and I suspect at least one pair that worries me dissolves on inspection (perhaps something about the Kripke semantics treating any two agents with the same information as having the same knowledge, which has always struck me as being either the entire problem or no problem at all). Please go through the commitments pairwise, tell me which tensions are genuine and rank them by severity, say for each genuine one what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set, and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most if I were willing to make it. I would much rather hear that my package is unstable in a specific, nameable way than receive a diplomatic survey of the literature.
