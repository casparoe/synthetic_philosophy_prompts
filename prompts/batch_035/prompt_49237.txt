Below is the draft of a short essay I have written for a graduate seminar on skepticism. I would like your comments on it.

The essay, provisionally titled "Proof Without Ascent: What Moore's Hand Proves," runs as follows. In Proof of an External World, Moore sets out two conditions on a proof: the premises must be known with certainty, and the conclusion must be distinct from the premises. The famous argument — here is one hand, here is another, hands are things external to my mind, so there is an external world — plainly satisfies both. Critics who concede the formal point usually complain that the proof is somehow dialectically useless: it cannot convert anyone, because anyone who doubted the conclusion would doubt the premises. My essay argues that this complaint misses what the proof is doing. Proofs, as Moore conceives them, are not instruments of evidential ascent: the conclusion of a valid proof need not be better supported after the proof than before, and in Moore's case it plainly is not, since the conclusion was never in doubt for anyone who accepts the premises. The proof's real function is ad hominem in the classical, non-fallacious sense: it demonstrates that the skeptic's demand for a proof of the external world is one the skeptic's own standards make unsatisfiable — any proof adequate to convince him would have to rest on premises his standards would reject too — and so the demand is exposed as a demand for new standards rather than for new evidence. In the final section I bring in the later Wittgenstein, who held in effect that Moore was right to insist he knows such things but wrong to say it, since claims about hands function as hinges on which empirical criticism turns rather than as empirical claims within it. I argue that the hinge picture, far from undermining Moore, supplies the positive account of why the premises are certain without proof, and thus completes the ad hominem reading: the skeptic is not short of evidence but trying to criticise from outside the very frame within which criticism gets its sense.

Please respond with exactly five numbered comments on the draft, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. At least three of the five should be substantive objections or corrections (for example, concerning my reading of Moore's conditions on proof, or of the ad hominem strategy), and at least one should assess whether the concluding claim about Wittgenstein genuinely supports my thesis or merely changes the subject.
