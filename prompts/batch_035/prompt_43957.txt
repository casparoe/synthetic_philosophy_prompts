I've ended up pretty convinced that acausal trade/cooperation is a real thing, not just a formal curiosity — the whole family: superrationalist cooperation on Newcomb-like problems, functional-decision-theory style "refuse the counterfactual blackmail, pay the counterfactual mugger" reasoning, multiverse-wide cooperation with causally disconnected agents, even "trade" with a superintelligence running a simulation of you. And the claim I actually care about is stronger than "this is all internally consistent": it's that this stuff can be action-guiding right now, that I should behave differently today because of correlations with agents I will never causally interact with.

What would count against that? Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Some I can already half-think of: maybe predictors good enough to create the relevant correlations just don't exist and never will; maybe the whole framework quietly depends on a decision theory that's itself unproven or just wrong; maybe the measure problem over large/infinite worlds makes the expected gains undefined rather than merely hard to compute; maybe committment races make acausal bargaining unstable in practice. Which of these would committed proponents themselves concede as counting against the view, and which would they bat away? Also be honest about whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable — if no observation inside my lightcone could ever count against it, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or just the price of admission? No citations or reading lists needed, I just want the map of where the view is exposed. One thing I do insist on: please separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among the people who know this stuff (e.g. whether one-boxing intuitions transfer to these cases, whether simulation-based trade is even coherent), and don't hand me contested positions as if they were consensus.
