At a dinner party, two friends of mine — Jonas, who grew up in Munich, and Dana, who grew up in Ohio — argued about something my neighbor did. At 3 a.m., on a completely empty street, the neighbor crossed against a red pedestrian light rather than stand there for ninety seconds waiting for nothing. Jonas said the neighbor did wrong, full stop: the rule is the rule, it binds everyone equally, and order of exactly this kind — rules that hold even when no one is watching and no one is harmed — is what makes public life predictable. Dana replied that an act which harms no one, risks nothing given the street was provably empty, and serves no purpose cannot be wrong; taking the rule to bind in that situation is empty formalism, and a culture that drills people to obey empty forms is training them not to think. Jonas said the rule isn't empty: it is a coordination device whose value depends precisely on not being renegotiated by each pedestrian at 3 a.m., and that Dana's "use your own judgment" standard, applied by everyone, yields not freedom but the rule of each person's confidence in their own case. Dana's retort was the obvious one: this is the mentality that lets people say they were only following rules when the rules were monstrous, and individual conscience has to be the final court, or rule-following is just obedience with good manners. Jonas called that a cheap shot: his case concerns a pedestrian light under a democracy whose rules you can campaign to change, not a criminal regime, and Dana's conscience-centered picture has its own failure mode — everyone who bends a rule is certain their exception is the reasonable one, so her standard licenses exactly the self-serving discretion it claims to replace. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move: say who is right at each step, identify what each of them misses if anything, and say who has the better of the overall question — whether morality lives more in shared rule-following or in individual conscience.
