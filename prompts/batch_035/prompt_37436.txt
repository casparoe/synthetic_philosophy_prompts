I've somehow ended up running a small conceptual research agenda at a safety org — it's me plus two other people who are basically philosophers/formal methods types, no ML engineers, and roughly two years before someone expects to see results. The remit I was handed is vague to the point of uselessness: "debate and adjacent approaches to scalable oversight." So I get to pick what we actually do, which is great except now I have to defend the pick.

What I want from you is a prioritized list of the open conceptual questions in this space, and — this is the actual ask — a defense of the ordering, not a survey. For each question: why it's upstream of other work in the area (what other things people are doing that quietly assume one answer to it), whether it's tractable for people who can't run experiments and won't be training anything, and what would make you bump it up or down over the next year or so.

To make it concrete, here are some candidates I've been chewing on, plus room for ones I haven't listed:

- whether the self-play story for debate only buys you unexploitability rather than truth-tracking, and whether that gap actually matters for oversight purposes;
- how much of the case for debate survives when the two "debaters" aren't symmetric (different base models, different knowledge, one side compelled to be honest);
- what happens in settings where the thing being debated isn't zero-sum for the AI systems, or where a debater can win by making the judge's evaluation itself do something harmful;
- the obfuscated-arguments problem — cases where the argument for the right answer is correct but so long and tangled that a bounded judge can't check it even with cross-examination — and whether that's a fundamental ceiling or just a current-model artifact;
- how to even state the success condition: are we trying to get the judge to the truth, or just to catch lies, and does conflating those two screw up most of the formal work that exists.

Rank maybe four to six of these (or your own alternatives) and tell me what we should drop and why. I care most about the dependency structure — which of these, if settled wrong, would make two years of everything else wasted. Also flag anything you'd deliberately deprioritize even though it's interesting, and what that costs us.

Fair warning: if the answer reads like a literature map I'll be annoyed. I want the reasoning, including where you think the standard framing of the field is just wrong and a question everyone works on shouldn't be on the list at all.
