Over the last year or so I've gotten quietly obsessed with anthropic reasoning — the puzzle of how much you should update on the fact that you exist, and that you exist as this particular person at this particular time. It started when I stumbled across the Doomsday Argument somewhere online and couldn't sleep afterwards. Since then I've pieced things together from podcasts and forum threads, and as far as I can tell the two main positions are the self-sampling assumption, where you reason as if you're a random sample from the people in your reference class, and the self-indication assumption, where finding that you exist at all is evidence for hypotheses on which more observers exist. My problem is that each one wins and loses in exactly the wrong places: the self-sampling assumption seems to give you the Doomsday Argument, which I find creepy but worry I can't just dismiss, while the self-indication assumption seems to dissolve Doomsday but then lets a philosopher who merely suspects there are vastly more people in one possible world than another confidently bet on the bigger world without doing any experiments — that's the "presumptuous philosopher" case, if I have the name right — and that feels like a straight-up betrayal of how evidence is supposed to work.

While turning this over at work (I'm an ICU nurse, so I have long stretches of walking in circles at 3 a.m. to think), I came up with what I admit is a half-baked third option: maybe the reference class isn't a fixed thing you get right or wrong, but a dial you set relative to the question being asked — narrow when you're asking about your own future, wide when you're asking about the history of the cosmos, and with some principled rule for when to widen it. I can't tell whether this is a genuine position or just the self-sampling assumption with extra steps and a way of dodging hard choices.

So here's what I'd like from you. Please rank the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and my dial idea into tiers based on how much you'd trust each of them, as a guide to real reasoning, to give sane answers — and give each a score from 0 to 10, with a sentence on why each lands where it does. Then tell me two further things: which of the three test cases (Doomsday, the presumptuous philosopher, and the Sleeping Beauty problem, if you think it belongs in the same family) should carry the most weight in deciding between the assumptions, and whether that weighting is itself a philosophical choice that a neutral judge couldn't make. Finally, be honest about my dial idea: does it deserve a place in the ranking at all, or should I file it under "sounds soothing, does no work"? I'd rather have my idea taken apart carefully than praised vaguely.
