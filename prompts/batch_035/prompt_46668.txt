I'm a retired radio astronomer who fell into the philosophy-of-probability rabbit hole a few years back, and my old officemate (still working, a signal-processing guy) and I have been argueing about the two-envelope paradox over coffee for months now. Let me set up the two sides, because we've become very precise about them. I'm on the "it's just a fallacy" side: the famous switching argument computes (1/2)(X/2) + (1/2)(2X) = 5X/4, but that formula is only legitimate if X is the smaller of the two amounts, and if you instead condition properly on what's actually in your envelope and average over what could be in the other one, the expected gain is exactly zero — which is what symmetry demanded all along. The apparent 25% gain is an accounting trick where the variable quietly changes meaning halfway through the derivation. My friend concedes the closed-envelope version but thinks I've missed the real content: he keeps pushing the open-envelope variant, and he claims (and I've checked the algebra and hate that he's right) that you can pick a heavy-tailed prior over the amounts — roughly, probabilities of amounts 2^k falling off like 1/2^k per step, so total probability just barely behaves — such that whatever amount you see when you open your envelope, the conditional expected gain from switching is strictly positive. His line is that the paradox isn't a solved fallacy but a window onto what breaks when expectations are infinite or plain don't exist.

We've decided the only dignified exit is to write up the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between our two camps, and since I type faster, I'm commissioning it. Write that document. Start with the numbered claims both of us can sign — including what we agree about regarding the closed-envelope switching argument, about the role of symmetry, and about what simulations with truncated finite distributions would show. Then a table of our exact disagreements, each phrased so the person holding that view would accept the wording of their own row: in particular whether "the conditional expected gain from switching is positive at every observed amount, yet switching twice returns you to exactly where you started" is a genuine problem or an artifact of manipulating a nonexistent expectation as if it were a number; and whether the always-switch prescription can be exploited or whether the aggregation argument (a room of N people all handing envelopes to the right can't all come out ahead) already shows something must have gone wrong upstream.

Then the cruxes, and for each crux one concrete case, worked example, or argument that both of us would agree bears on it, with my prediction and his prediction recorded in advance — for instance, what happens to the always-positive conditional gain when you truncate the heavy-tailed prior at a finite ceiling and then let the ceiling grow, and whether the average gain over a large population of players tends to zero, stays positive, or oscillates. I want each of us on record before the "experiment" so nobody can quietly retcon afterwards. And if part of our fight turns out to be verbal, definately say which part and why: he suspects that our disagreement over the word "resolved" (his sense: every proposed diagnosis has some variant it fails on; my sense: the original argument has a demonstrable flaw, QED) is doing more work than either of us admits, and I want that suspicion either vindicated or squashed precisely, not hand-waved. Keep it fair — I'll know if you've stacked the deck for my side, and so will he.
