I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and lately I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the old Meno problem — the question of why knowledge is supposed to be better than a merely true belief — by running it against cases from my own working life and recording my gut verdicts. Here they are. A colleague traced a race condition through the logs, isolated the faulty lock, and predicted where the crash came from; a second colleague, shown the same symptoms, said "it's the lock, I can feel it" with nothing behind it. Both turned out to be right, and my verdict is that the first one knew and the second one merely believed something true. Our team veteran, on the other hand, calls production incidents correctly maybe ninety percent of the time and cannot articulate her reasons any better than "I've seen this shape before" — and I'm inclined to say she knows, even though her belief looks structurally just like the second colleague's. Then there's an engineer I've worked with who refuses to deploy on Fridays "because Fridays are unlucky," and on one occasion a Friday deploy did fail (for reasons that had nothing to do with the day of the week); I'd deny that he knew anything. And once I was convinced a performance problem was in the database because I'd misremembered a profiling rule of thumb — a rule that happens to be wrong as a rule but pointed at the database, which genuinely was the problem in that instance — and my verdict there is a firm no, yet somehow it feels like a less clear no than the veteran case is a yes.

So what I'd like from you is the principle behind these verdicts, if there is one. It looks like it can't just be "right for the right reasons," since the veteran can't give reasons and I still credit her with knowledge; it can't be bare reliability either, or else the unlucky-Friday engineer, whose belief was in a sense reliable that week, should get some credit, which I'm not willing to grant — unless you can convince me one of those verdicts has to go. Please state the principle precisely enough that it rules on each of my cases, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I should abandon if forced, and then apply it to two cases I haven't decided: first, whether the unlucky-Friday engineer's belief would count as knowledge if it turned out that Friday deploys really did fail far more often, because everyone was exhausted at the end of the week — his superstition accidentally tracking a real mechanism he knows nothing about; and second, whether a static analysis tool that flags the same race condition reliably, but with no more insight into it than the veteran has, could be said to know anything in the sense I've been using the word. If parts of this have established names in epistemology, feel free to mention them, but please don't assume I know any of the literature — explain from the ground up.
