I'm a PhD student in mathematical logic, and this semester I'm teaching the last segment of an upper-level course called "Set Theory and the Foundations of Mathematics." The audience is mixed — philosophy majors who can write but needed the first month to get comfortable with transfinite induction, and math majors who eat Zorn's lemma for breakfast but have never had to defend an axiom in prose. By the end of the course everyone has worked through ZF, the standard equivalences (choice, well-ordering, Zorn, maximality principles), the construction of a nonmeasurable set via a choice function on the Vitali equivalence classes, and Banach–Tarski, plus a seminar week on weaker principles like countable and dependent choice and what they buy you in analysis. For the final exam, I want a single essay question on the epistemology of the axiom of choice, answerable in about ninety minutes, that cannot be answered by reciting the equivalence proofs or listing the famous pathologies.

There's a well-known quip to the effect that the axiom of choice is obviously true, the well-ordering theorem is obviously false, and Zorn's lemma is anyone's guess. I want a question that treats this as a real puzzle rather than a joke: since the three are provably equivalent over ZF, the differential intuitions must be tracking something other than mathematical content — perhaps the vividness of one formulation's consequences, perhaps a confusion between a principle's plausibility as a rule of reasoning and the plausibility of its remote corollaries — and a strong answer has to say what, and then take a stance on what the tension does to the case for accepting full choice over its weaker fragments. I'd like students to have to engage the distinction between justifying an axiom by its intrinsic fit with the iterative conception of set and justifying it by its fruitfulness; to say something careful about the difference between a consequence being surprising and a consequence being a cost; and to confront the awkward fact that some attractive set-theoretic projects, like determinacy assumptions about sets of reals, are flatly inconsistent with full choice, while ordinary mathematics outside set theory is largely content with dependent choice.

Please write (1) the exam question itself, in a form I can put directly on the paper; (2) a model answer of roughly six hundred words — one that takes an actual position rather than surveying options; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth four marks each, out of twenty, where for each point you name the specific move a top answer must make and add a one-line note on how students typically botch it. I particularly want the marking scheme to separate answers that merely cite Banach–Tarski from answers that explain why "it entails something bizarre" is not, by itself, a refutation — and to catch the common student slide between rejecting the axiom and merely reporting that it has counterintuitive consequences. If you think an A answer has to concede something uncomfortable to the other side — for instance, that the quip's "obviously true" verdict on choice itself gets harder to sustain once we notice the axiom asserts a set whose members we can't specify in any canonical way — build that in as one of the five points.
