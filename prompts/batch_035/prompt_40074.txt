I hold that teleosemantics — the family of views, in the lineage of Millikan and Neander, according to which mental content is fixed by the evolutionary functions of our cognitive mechanisms — is the most promising naturalization of intentionality. Tell me what would count against it. Beyond the familiar objections (the disjunction problem, Swampman, the apparent indeterminacy of what selection-based functions assign as content), I want you to consider a case that worries me in particular: if selection history is what confers content, what does teleosemantics say about a language model trained by gradient descent? Training is a selection process of some kind — it filters and amplifies behavioral dispositions — but there is no obvious lineage of copied-and-varied mechanisms serving functions, and no ancestors in the relevant sense. Does teleosemantics then entail that such a system has no genuine content, and if so, is that a defensible verdict or an embarrassing bullet to bite? Alternatively, can selection be construed broadly enough to include optimization by training, and if it can, has the view lost its grip on the biological cases it was built to explain? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those committed teleosemanticists would themselves concede as counting against the view from those they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding, and give an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a naturalization project as opposed to, say, a normative theory.
