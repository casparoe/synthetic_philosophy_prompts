I've come to hold a position I'd call moral asymmetry from moral psychology: the research program of moral foundations theory finds that political liberals draw mainly on the care and fairness foundations, while conservatives respond across the fuller set — loyalty, authority, sanctity, and later a liberty/oppression foundation — and I take this breadth asymmetry to be evidence in moral disagreements, roughly as a person with functioning hearing is better placed than a deaf person in an argument about a sound. On my view, a sensibility that registers more moral dimensions is, other things equal, better positioned to get moral questions right, and the liberal narrowing is a genuine (if partial) moral deficit rather than mere moral difference. Tell me what would count against this. I don't want only the obvious objection that descriptive psychology can't be normatively loaded — I want the strongest version of it, plus the rest of the exposure map: what survey and cross-cultural findings would have to turn out to be true; whether evidence that liberals do register loyalty, authority, and sanctity content, just redescribed in care/fairness terms, would undercut the asymmetry; whether showing that some foundations track coalitional psychology or submission to power, rather than moral perception, breaks my hearing analogy; and whether adding foundations to fit recalcitrant data counts against the underlying theory as ad hoc retrofitting. Distinguish what a committed proponent would concede as counting against the view from what they'd dismiss, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether the retreat from "better at morality" to "differently morally attuned" has insulated the view — and whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind, or a reasonable humility. Please answer in American English.
