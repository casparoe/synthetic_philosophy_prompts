My housemate Dev and his brother Marcus got into a huge argument after watching some documentary about Atlantis (the kind that starts off respectable and then an hour in you realise the narrator thinks the survivors built the pyramids). I mostly just listened and now I cant stop thinking about who was actually right, so I need someone to go through the whole thing for me.

Dev opened by saying this stuff fails the most basic standards of evidence. There's no city, no artefacts, no trade goods, nothing. Every time a claim gets knocked down, the story just mutates — it was an island in the Atlantic, then it was "really" Santorini and the Minoans, then it was a global seafaring civilisation that conveniently left no trace because, well, it left no trace. That's not a hypothesis, that's a shape-shifter.

Marcus answered that the standard Dev is applying is a moving target dressed up as neutral. Loads of now-accepted ideas once looked like shape-shifters: geologists laughed at continental drift for decades, and Troy was considered pure myth until someone actually dug the place up. If the rule is "no mainstream support, no traction," then orthodoxy becomes self-sealing — the people inside the citadel decide who gets let in.

Dev said that's hindsight bias in reverse. Wegener wasn't dismissed for being heretical, he was dismissed because he had a mechanism problem, and once real evidence arrived — seafloor speading, the fossil matches across continents — the field changed its mind. That's the whole point: science eventually flips. Show me the seafloor spreading for Atlantis. Pseudohistory never produces its seafloor spreading, it just produces more story.

Marcus said Dev is defining "evidence" so that it can only come from accredited professionals, and then noting approvingly that the accredited professionals all agree with each other. He also brought up this Russian mathematician — Fomenko? — who claims big stretches of written history are duplicated or reflected copies of other stretches, and who says statistical analysis of old texts backs this up. Marcus said: look, the guy is probably wrong, but he's using numbers, he makes claims you can go check, and he still gets called a crank. Which suggests "does he use evidence" isn't actually the criterion — "is he one of us" is.

Dev's last word was that this proves too much. If Fomenko's methods count as evidence, then any numbers generated to reach a pre-chosen conclusion count as evidence, the distinction between reasoning and rationalising collapses, and Marcus has no argument left that doesn't also license every crank alive. Marcus just said Dev was being patronising and they dropped it.
