Two centuries on, per-capita food production rose enormously and mass famine became rare, yet defenders still say the theory was 'right but early' -- the crunch is merely postponed, and every famine that does occur somewhere counts as confirmation of the mechanism. And here is the student's answer, more or less verbatim (typos theirs, I transcribed fast):

"Malthus held that population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically, making misery inevitable. Since the industrial revolution this clearly has not happened globally -- food per person is way up. But that falsifies the prediction, not the theory, because the prediction only follows from the theory together with auxiliary assumptions, for instance the assumption that agricultural technology stays roughly fixed. This is the Duhem-Quine point, which Popper later adopted as his demarcation criterion: a single failed prediction can never refute a theory on its own. So the 'right but early' defense is legitimate in principle, as long as the rescuing auxiliary assumption is itself testable. If famine never arrives, ever, we just conclude the auxiliaries were false, and that is fine.

Where the defense overreaches is elsewhere. Local famines like the Irish famine fit the theory perfectly, so those should honestly be counted as confirming instances -- the theory predicted misery somewhere and misery occured. Still, a theory that predicts catastrophe at no specified time risks being unfalsifiable in principle, since one can always wait longer. This is essentially what Lakatos called a degenerating problem shift: a program that only reacts to its failures and never makes any new predictions at all. On the informative question: Malthus was right but early about the geometry of growth, exponential versus linear, and this is the part that survives, while the timing claim is what failed. The ecological revival does not change the verdict much, because carrying-capacity arguments use the same underlying model, so their predictive successes are really the original theory's successes and should be credited to it."
