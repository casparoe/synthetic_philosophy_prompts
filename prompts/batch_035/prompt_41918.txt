I'm teaching an introductory ethics unit on moral responsibility. We've just covered Frankfurt's challenge to the principle of alternative possibilities — his thought that responsibility need not require having been able to do otherwise, and that what matters is whether the will you acted from was really your own. For the final session I want to screen Kubrick's A Clockwork Orange and use it as a test case. Please write me a set of teaching notes that reads the film through that debate.

Here is the angle I want developed. The Ludovico treatment is like a Frankfurt case run in reverse: instead of someone secretly standing by to ensure that you do what you were already going to do, the state removes your alternatives wholesale — and not just the ability to act violently but the ability to want violence at all. So the questions: is the treated Alex non-responsible for his subsequent "good" behavior in the way Frankfurt would say a manipulated agent still is responsible, or is he responsible for nothing, because nothing he now does issues from his will? Does the prison chaplain's objection — that a goodness one is compelled into isn't genuine moral goodness — vindicate the principle of alternative possibilities, or only a hierarchical view of the self, given that pre-treatment Alex arguably never endorsed his violent impulses either, but was simply driven by them? And consider the endings: Kubrick's Alex, conditioned back to his old appetites and visibly delighted about it, versus the final chapter of the original British edition of Burgess's novel, which was cut from the American edition the film was based on, and in which Alex simply grows out of violence and starts imagining a settled life. Which ending better serves the Frankfurt debate, and what should each lead my students to conclude? One further complication worth addressing: after his release, Alex is beaten and humiliated by people he once victimized and cannot even defend himself, and the film seems to invite us to pity him — does that pity show we treat the capacity for violence as partly constitutive of the person, and does it cut against or for the hierarchical view?

Please write the notes in American English, and do not quote dialogue — cue scenes by description only, since I want the students to recognize the moments rather than check citations. End the entire document with a single last line of the form "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating whether the film, as a whole, vindicates the principle of alternative possibilities or the hierarchical alternative.
