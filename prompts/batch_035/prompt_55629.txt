I am a second-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course with a cognitive science component, and I am preparing a seminar handout on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness. Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from a starting commitment — that after complete commissurotomy the two cerebral hemispheres can process information independently under lateralized stimulus presentation — to a named position on how many subjects of experience a split-brain patient is. The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: define the branch questions first, then locate the positions in the space the answers generate, rather than designing the questions to reach predetermined labels.

Each internal node should be a question a reader could answer without knowing which position it leads to, and the questions should turn on concrete cases and commitments rather than on labels. Include cases and issues along these lines, selecting and rephrasing as you see fit: whether a patient whose two hands pursue contradictory goals, one stacking matches while the other sweeps them aside, strikes the reader as two agents or as one agent with a motor conflict; whether cross-cuing, in which one hand gestures so as to feed information to the opposite hemisphere, is better described as communication between two subjects or as a single subject using two channels; whether the patient's ordinary life — driving, cooking, joking, apparently unimpaired — is better explained by subpersonal coordination between disconnected systems or by genuine conscious unity; whether the interpreter-style confabulation, in which the speaking hemisphere invents an explanation for the other hand's action, is evidence of one rationalizing subject or of a subsystem whose products happen to be voiced; whether the number of subjects of experience in a given brain is a determinate fact or partly a matter of descriptive convention; and whether unity of consciousness admits of degrees rather than being all or nothing.

The leaves should include, where the tree's structure permits: the straightforward one-subject view, on which the apparent duality is an artifact of how responses are organized; the single-stream view associated with Gazzaniga's earlier work, on which one consciousness operates at a time, alternating between hemispheres; the two-streams-one-person view, on which a single person entertains two partially independent streams of experience; the two-persons view defended by Roland Puccetti, on which every human brain houses two persons and the surgery merely reveals this; Nagel's view that these patients fall below the threshold of unity required for a single mind, so that neither one nor two is the right count; and a deflationary Dennettian leaf, on which counting subjects of experience is a badly posed question because unity is an effect of narrative construction. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot reliably separate rather than forcing them apart, and note any combination of answers that leads to no occupied leaf, with a brief diagnosis of whether that region of the space is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Keep the tree to at most eight internal questions, and write it for fellow students who have some background in cognitive science but no prior exposure to this literature. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of this request.
