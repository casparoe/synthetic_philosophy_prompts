Two colleagues of mine — a political philosopher working on global justice and a development economist sympathetic to the effective altruism movement — have spent three years arguing past each other about what rich countries owe poorer ones, and I have been asked to mediate. The philosopher holds that the obligations of rich countries are primarily remedial and institutional: the duty is to stop participating in, and to restructure, an unjust global order — trade rules and tariff regimes, sovereign-debt enforcement practices, the colonial legacy of arbitrary borders — and that transfers of aid without such reform are at best palliative and at worst a way of buying permission for the underlying arrangement to continue. The economist holds that the obligations are real and stringent but best discharged through well-evidenced transfers — insecticide-treated bed nets, unconditional cash transfers, vaccine procurement — and that the institutional critique, whatever its abstract merits, gives little guidance about what a donor agency or finance ministry should do with the next billion dollars.

Draft the joint statement the two could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, to be circulated to a graduate seminar on global justice. The document should contain: a numbered section of claims both sides accept — including the moral stakes they agree on, any empirical record they can jointly state, and any points each side concedes to the other; a table of their remaining disagreements, each entry phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes — subquestions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each crux, a concrete case, policy, or study both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance; and a short closing section flagging any part of the dispute both agree is merely verbal, such as whether they are using the word "obligation" in the same sense.

To keep the exercise from collapsing into generalities, the document must work through at least the following specific issues, which I know divide them:

1. The fungibility question: whether a donor's earmarked spending on health quietly relaxes the recipient government's budget constraint, and whether that should count for or against the transfer camp's moral accounting. Each side should state how it would treat a case in which a documented budget release was spent on something the donor camp considers bad.
2. The no-harm-first crux: whether rich countries' discontinuation of identifiable harms — tariff peaks against processed goods, certain debt-collection practices, arms exports to specific recipients — is lexically prior to any positive assistance, and whether the two sides can converge on a shared list of such harms, with any disagreement about list membership stated precisely.
3. The attribution question: how much of a given recipient country's poverty is attributable to global structure versus domestic governance. Each side should commit to a percentage range and to what kinds of evidence would lead it to revise the range, so the commitments are falsifiable rather than rhetorical.
4. The marginal-dollar test: given one additional billion dollars controlled by a rich-country ministry with no ability to change trade or debt rules, what should be done with it? If the philosopher's side cannot answer in a way the economist would accept as action-guiding, the statement should say so candidly; if the economist's side cannot say whether its favored interventions remain worthwhile under a hostile government, it should say that too.

The agreed-claims section should be substantive enough that a reader can see what each side gave up in order to sign; the document is a failure if those claims are platitudes, and a different failure if either side's position is worded so that it would refuse to put its name to the entry attributed to it. Please write the entire answer in British English.
