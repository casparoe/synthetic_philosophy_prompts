I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of economics — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and I'm designing a unit on the socialist calculation debate. The pedagogical target: my students keep reading the debate as being about whether the equations are solvable, and conclude it was settled once computing got cheap. I want them to feel the difference between the computational version of the problem (solving for a full price vector is hard arithmetic) and the epistemic version I take Hayek to be pressing: the relevant information — local production conditions, shifting preferences, tacit know-how — doesn't exist anywhere in collected form and has to be elicited from dispersed people who have reasons to misreport it.

Design an in-class simulation with at least three conditions: a market condition, a planned-economy condition, and a hybrid condition where a planning board has a designated "solver" with genuine computing power (a spreadsheet, an announced iterative rule, as much time as the market side gets) so the class can see for itself whether the bottleneck is arithmetic or something upstream of it. Give each participant private, partly hidden local knowledge — secret production costs, idiosyncratic preferences, and a rule allowing preferences to shift mid-game when new local information arrives — so that planners must elicit data rather than receive it, and strategic misreporting to obtain favorable quotas is a live option. Please include the concrete setup (materials, roles, what each participant's private card says), a timeline, a point-based payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the Mises/Hayek side versus Lange's trial-and-error proposal, including whether eliciting demands at announced prices answers the epistemic version or only the computational one. Be honest about which parts of the real historical debate the game cannot reproduce, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
