Is there a name for this thing I stumbled on? Background: I'm a backend dev, no philosophy training, but I like logic puzzles and I got into an argument with a teammate about "in principle discoverable" claims. I was defending something like: there's no bug in our codebase that a sufficiently careful reviewer could never in principle find — every true claim about the system is knowable by someone at some point. He pushed back, and somewhere in the back-and-forth I whiteboarded an argument that ends somewhere insane, and I can't find the hole in it.

Roughly:

1. Assume every truth is knowable in principle (could come to be known by somebody).
2. There's clearly at least one truth that isn't currently known — e.g. "bug #4471 exists" when nobody has found it yet.
3. Now take the conjunction: bug #4471 exists AND nobody knows it exists. That's also a truth. But notice it's *unknowable*: if someone knew the conjunction, they'd know the bug exists, contradicting the second half.
4. By (1), every truth is knowable, so the conjunction must be knowable. But (3) shows it isn't. Contradiction.
5. So (2) must be false — every truth is already known?! By somebody?

I assume I've reinvented a wheel here, it was too easy to trip over. What I'd like to know:

First, does this argument have a name, and what exactly does it show — is it a proof that the knowability claim is false, or a proof that some step (the one letting me form that conjunction, maybe) is doing something sneaky? What are the standard escape routes people argue about, and where do they actually break the argument vs. just deny the premise?

Second — and this is the part I actually care about — I tried to rescue my original claim by weakening it, and I want to know if any of these dodges work. (a) Restricting the knowability claim to a domain: "every truth *about the codebase* is in principle knowable" rather than every truth whatsoever. Does the paradox need the unrestricted version, or does it still bite within a domain? (b) Making it multi-agent: "every truth is knowable by *some* member of some team at some point" rather than knowable by a single knower — does spreading the knowing around change anything, or does the same blowup happen one level up? (c) Distinguishing "could be known" from "could be known without anyone acting differently" — the conjunction in step (3) seems to become unknowable exactly because finding out changes things.

I don't just want the label. I want the carving: which formulations of "everything is in principle knowable" survive this argument and which die, and what the boundary cases are. If some of my dodge attempts are known not to work, tell me why, and if one of them is actually an open question, say that too.
