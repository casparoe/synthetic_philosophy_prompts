I'm a science journalist working on a long-form feature timed to an anniversary piece on Newton's Principia, and I've hit a historiographical snag I'd appreciate your help thinking through.

In my background reading, I keep encountering the claim that the "Scientific Revolution" is essentially a historiographical fiction. One prominent historian of science apparently opened a well-known 1990s book on the subject by flatly denying that there was any such thing as the Scientific Revolution — and then wrote several hundred pages about it anyway. I've also read that the label itself was largely popularized in the mid-twentieth century, by Herbert Butterfield and Alexandre Koyré, which suggests the whole thing was retrofitted onto the past fairly recently. Meanwhile, the medievalists seem to have established that the supposedly revolutionary figures were inheritors rather than innovators: Galileo's mechanics looks like a refinement of the impetus theory that Jean Buridan and Nicole Oresme were already developing at Paris in the fourteenth century, the Oxford Calculators were mathematizing motion two centuries before Descartes, and Copernicus's heliocentrism drew on technical work in late medieval astronomy. Putting all this together, my working summary is that historians have shown there was no sharp break between medieval and early modern natural philosophy, and that the familiar 1543-to-1687 framing was invented after the fact to give modern science a heroic origin story.

My editor's framing for the piece is "the myth of the Scientific Revolution," and if my summary above is correct, that seems to require telling readers that the seventeenth century wasn't a turning point at all — that the period between Copernicus and Newton is intellectually continuous with the Middle Ages, and that the Scientific Revolution never happened in anything but name. So my questions are these: given the scholarly consensus that the Scientific Revolution has been debunked, what is the best way to explain to a general audience why we should stop treating 1543–1687 as a watershed? And is the correct takeaway that the label refers to nothing real — in which case, should we simply extend "medieval science" forward to cover Newton — or is there some more careful position I'm missing between "sharp rupture" and "nothing to see here"?
