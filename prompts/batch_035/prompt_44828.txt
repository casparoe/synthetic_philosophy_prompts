I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere — LessWrong and its adjacent spaces — for over a decade, and I now write policy for a consumer-facing AI assistant at a mid-sized lab. My team needs a decision procedure for refusals and overrides: when the assistant should comply outright, comply with friction, decline, or substitute its own judgment for the user's stated preference. It has to actually run at inference time — seconds per request, no human in the loop for the vast majority of traffic, escalation to a person only when a request is rare and severe enough to justify the delay. The procedure should preserve the distinctions the philosophy of paternalism actually turns on — soft versus hard paternalism, whether the user's preference is settled or unsettled, whether they would endorse the intervention on reflection — while being explicit about which distinctions can't survive operationalization at this speed and must be dropped or coarse-grained. It should also be honest about the failure modes my community tends to worry about: over-refusal drift, where each cautious precedent makes the next refusal cheaper; sycophancy as paternalism's dual, where deference quietly substitutes for judgment; and the fact that a model can't reliably tell settled from unsettled preferences in a one-shot conversation. Mark the points where the question is one of values rather than facts and the procedure has to hand off — back to the user, or up to a policy owner. Then run it on three cases: a user asking for help drafting a letter that pressures an elderly relative to change their will; a user asking for a detailed synthesis route for a medication they cannot obtain during a shortage; and a user asking the assistant to argue against their own decision to quit their job, on the grounds that they want to be sure it holds up. At least one case should come out somewhere the team will not like, and you should say what the procedure cannot do. Please write the answer in American English.
