I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a feature about how platforms watch their users. Nearly every source I interview — privacy lawyers, sociologists, former moderation staff — eventually reaches for the same comparison: social media, I'm told, is a digital panopticon. The comparison is doing real work in these conversations. It licenses the conclusion that users, like Bentham's prisoners, who can never be sure they are unobserved, internalize the gaze and discipline themselves: editing their posts, muting themselves, performing conformity. And it quietly suggests the remedy is the remedy for the panopticon — to block the gaze, to demand opacity.

Before I repeat this in print, I'd like to interrogate it. Please give me a systematic audit. First, a correspondence table: in the social-media case, what plays the role of the central tower, the unseen guard, the inmate, the backlighting of the cell that makes the prisoner visible while the watcher stays dark, the prisoner's permanent uncertainty about whether he is being watched at any given moment, and the automatic, decentered character of the power Foucault described — power that functions no matter who occupies the tower, or whether anyone does. Second, the disanalogies, sorted by whether they matter for the intended conclusion. The ones I keep running into: platform surveillance is largely retrospective and predictive rather than simultaneous; it is partly consensual and even exhibitionistic, with users competing for the gaze rather than hiding from it; there is not one tower but many, and each prisoner is shown a different corridor, since ranking and personalization mean there may be no single visible norm to internalize; and the apparatus seems aimed at engagement and prediction rather than at normalizing conduct in Foucault's sense. Tell me which of these break the mapping in a way that matters for the self-discipline conclusion and which are superficial. Third: stripped of the parts that don't carry, does that conclusion survive at all — are users being disciplined into self-censoring conformity, or only into some narrower subset of careful behavior, or into something that isn't usefully called discipline?

Finally, if the panopticon is the wrong ancestor, what is the better one? Note that Bentham's design also allowed the public to inspect the prison, and some of my sources prefer that inverted arrangement, in which the many watch the few — celebrities, leaders, each other's feeds — more than the few watch the many. Others say the right comparison is not a prison at all but something more like a scientific instrument that samples its subjects in order to predict them, with discipline merely a leftover metaphor from an earlier technology of power. Which, if any, of these framings does what we actually know about platform behavior over the last decade better support? And be explicit about what the panopticon frame smuggles in that a careful piece should not grant for free — for instance, the assumption that the watcher's power is centralized, deliberate, and aimed at correction, rather than distributed, mechanical, and aimed at something else entirely.
