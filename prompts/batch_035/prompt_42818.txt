My closest friend and I spent an entire road trip arguing about why promises bind, and neither of us left the car satisfied. The argument started concretely — I once promised my dying uncle that I would never sell the family cabin — but quickly became about the underlying question. We'd each like a verdict, move by move, on who was right at each stage.

(1) She argued that promises bind by convention: promising is a social practice, like money or chess, and once you invoke the practice you can't then opt out of its rules — just as you can't take a bet, lose, and refuse to pay because you've reconsidered the odds. The obligation comes from the practice itself.

(2) I objected that conventions explain the form of the obligation but not its force: a convention can be immoral or pointless (imagine a town whose central practice is promising vendettas), and obligations there would be null, so something else must ground why a normal promise binds. I proposed that it's induced reliance: it's wrong to deliberately invite someone to arrange their life around your word and then withdraw it.

(3) She replied that reliance can't be the ground, because promises seem to bind even when no one relies, expects, or is even around anymore — including, she noted pointedly, my promise to my uncle, who cannot now rely on anything. A promise made in secret, to someone who forgets it, still seems to bind.

(4) I then retreated to a different idea: a promise transfers a kind of authority — the promisee acquires a claim over a slice of your future conduct, the way a landlord acquires rights over property, and that's why it can survive the promisee's absence or indifference. She said this merely renames the mystery: it labels the result ("authority") without explaining how a string of words can conjure authority out of nothing, which was the original question.

(5) Finally, she suggested the whole question is confused: promises bind the way the rules of chess bind — you simply cannot be promising and ignoring promises at once — so there is nothing further to explain. I answered that this can't be right, because promissory obligation outlives the practice: if promising fell out of use, yesterday's promises wouldn't evaporate, the way your checkers obligations would if checkers stopped being played.

Please adjudicate each of the five moves — say who is right about what, where either of us talked past the other, and what, if anything, both of us missed about why promises bind.
