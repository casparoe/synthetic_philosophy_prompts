You are a senior epistemologist who has taught upper-level epistemology for many years, and you are known for writing exam questions that separate genuine understanding from memorized labels. I am writing the final for my upper-level seminar on knowledge and evidence, and I want a single essay question on the dogmatism paradox — the puzzle Gilbert Harman raised in his book Thought, where the plausible claims that if you know that p, you are entitled to dismiss putative counterevidence to p, and that apparent counterevidence is still evidence bearing on whether p, jointly seem to undermine the knowledge you were supposed to have. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard labels. In particular, a student who writes "this is just skepticism, and Cohen says to deny the second premise" should earn a middling mark at best: the paradox is not skepticism about knowledge in general, and the interesting question is what exactly a rejection of each plausible-seeming premise commits one to elsewhere in epistemology.

Here is the material I want the question built from, since I'd rather it hinge on fresh cases than on the worn examples. Case one: a structural engineer certified a bridge design; last month she personally ran the load simulations that convinced her the design is safe, and she knows it. Today a colleague hands her a manuscript claiming to demonstrate a flaw in designs of exactly this type. Case two: the same manuscript arrives on the desk of a junior engineer who believes the design is safe only on the say-so of the senior, and whose belief is true and safely formed. Case three: the manuscript is peer-reviewed and published, and the senior engineer now encounters it in the literature rather than from a colleague's hand. I want the question to require students to say, for each case, whether the engineer still knows, what entitlement to dismiss the counterevidence amounts to, and why the cases come apart if they do — and to evaluate at least two general response strategies to the paradox (for instance, denying the entitlement, restricting the evidential principle, or making entitlement conditional on how the knowledge was acquired), saying for each what unpalatable consequences it carries for ordinary knowledge, closure, or the rationality of inquiry.

Please produce three deliverables. First, the question as it would appear on the exam, worded so that a student who has merely memorized the terminology cannot fake their way through, and so that the three cases do the work of forcing principled answers. Second, a model answer of about 600 words that a strong student could realistically produce under exam conditions, not a distillation of the literature. Third, a marking scheme totaling 25 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on the mistake that most commonly costs students each point, and an explicit statement of the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer — where the B answer, for instance, correctly describes the cases but never states the general principle it is applying, or evaluates responses to the paradox only by intuition-pumping rather than by tracing their costs elsewhere in epistemology. Before writing, think carefully about what the crux of the question should be: the aim is that the exam separates students who grasp why the paradox arises from students who can only name it.
