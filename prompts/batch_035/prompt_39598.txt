Two people in my game theory reading group, Dana and Marcus, spent basically a whole session arguing about the centipede game and it never got resolved. Some quick setup so the argument makes sense: in the game, players alternate choosing "take" or "pass." The pot roughly doubles each pass; whoever takes gets the bigger share, and at the last node taking is forced. We used a six-node version where taking at node 1 pays $4 to the taker and $2 to the other, growing to $128/$64 at node 6. The structure is that at every node, taking gives you more than you'd get if you passed and the other player took immediately, but passing twice makes both players better off than taking now. Backwards induction then says: at node 6 the mover obviously takes, so at node 5 taking beats passing, so at node 4 the same, ... all the way down, take at node 1 for $4.

Dana said that's the unique rational play and there's no paradox, just a theorem plus empirical noise. Her case: (1) at the final node it's a simple choice, no beliefs involved, take the $128; (2) each earlier step only needs that at the later node both would take, that both know it, and that both know that both know it — common knowledge of rationality delivers exactly that; (3) the fact that people pass in experiments just means the assumption fails in the lab, and that's a fact about subjects, not a problem with the theory.

Marcus said the assumption doesn't just fail, it eats itself. His case for taking at node 1 leans on how we'd both behave at nodes 4, 5, 6 — nodes only reachable after several passes. But a history with several passes is precisely evidence against the common knowledge the argument needs. If I'm sitting at node 3 facing an opponent who just passed twice, I'm not facing someone about whom the induction's premises hold. So the conclusion at node 1 was derived using premises that the game itself can falsify once play starts. He compared it to the surprise exam, where the student's backwards induction proves there can be no exam, and the exam then surprises them anyway — same self-defeating structure, and there we all agree the student's reasoning is bad, not the calendar.

Dana replied: at node 1 nothing has been falsified yet — beliefs about off-path behavior come from the game's description plus the common knowledge assumption, and play doesn't get to check them before you move. Also, that "irrationality can pay" is old news: being able to credibly commit to passing would help you in lots of games, which shows commitment is valuable, not that the rational move is pass.

Marcus pushed back: then rationality has two tiers — optimize on path, and off path the theory just shrugs. But the on-path choice at node 1 is entirely determined by the off-path beliefs, and the theory only licenses them when they've never been tested. He also pointed at our own session as a data point: we ran ten pairs with real money and nobody took at node 1; most took around node 3, and one pair that passed to node 5 split $64/$32. His punchline was that a standard of rationality whose adherents reliably do worse than its violators, in the very game it was built for, is broken as a standard — and if the reply is "that's because they were playing against non-CKR agents," the theory has quietly admitted it can't say anything about any game actually played by humans.

Don't give me the "both perspectives have merit" buffet — commit to a position and give your reasons.
