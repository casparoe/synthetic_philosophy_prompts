I'm designing a unit for an applied ethics course on the ethics of eating animals, and I've noticed that almost every argument my students will encounter runs in one direction: consequentialist reasoning about animal suffering is taken to push monotonically toward plant-based diets. I'd like a thought experiment that complicates this without any contrarian framing, built around a structural fact that is easy to state but rarely worked through: a single cow supplies hundreds of meals while a chicken supplies only a few, so the number of animal deaths per meal — and, on some credences, the suffering per meal — can be lower for beef than for chicken, and possibly lower even than for certain plant-based meals once you count the wild animals killed in crop cultivation by harvesting machinery, pesticides, and habitat destruction. Please construct a case that makes this bite.

The protagonist should be Maya, a hedonic act utilitarian who gave up meat solely to minimize animal suffering and who accepts all of the standard welfare claims: that chickens fare terribly in modern production, that beef cattle fare somewhat less badly per individual life though still badly, and that the wild-animal death toll of crop cultivation is large but deeply uncertain. The setting should be an ordinary restaurant or workplace cafeteria — nothing futuristic, no perfect information, no contrived lab conditions. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph. The crucial constraint is that the verdict — that, by her own lights, the beef dish minimizes expected suffering relative to both the chicken dish and the vegan dish — must fall out of her credences and the arithmetic of animals-per-meal rather than being stipulated by narrative fiat. After the scenario, show the expected-suffering comparison in rough terms, flag exactly where the uncertainty about wild-animal sentience and field death tolls enters, and indicate how strongly the result depends on that uncertainty — a case that only works if field deaths dominate everything else should be presented as such, not disguised.

Then add a second stage: Maya is lunching with fellow volunteers from a campus animal-welfare group she helps run, several of whom are wavering about their own diets, and her order will be noticed and remarked upon. Show how the very same theory, applied to the full set of consequences — what her companions order afterward, the group's morale and credibility, the longer-run effects on her persuasiveness as an advocate — might reverse the verdict of the first stage. Finally, tell me which feature is doing the work at each stage (the per-meal arithmetic in the first, the observational entanglement in the second), what happens to the case if each feature is removed, and how a utilitarian critic would attack the accounting — for instance by pressing per-life rather than per-meal reasoning, by raising the entanglement of beef with the dairy industry, by weighting uncertainty about invertebrate suffering differently, or by questioning the moral weights assigned across species. My aim is pedagogical: I want students to see the gap between the local calculus and the full one, so a scenario that merely asserts that beef is defensible would defeat the purpose. No references are needed; reasoned argument and honest uncertainty are what I'm after.
