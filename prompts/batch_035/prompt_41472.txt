I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, working comparatively on early Chinese theories of naming, and I want a diagnostic decision tree for the interpretive readings of the White Horse Dialogue (the Baima lun chapter of the Gongsunlongzi, where the advocate defends the thesis bai ma fei ma, conventionally rendered "a white horse is not a horse"). The tree is for a handout in a graduate reading group of about twelve people; it should be walkable in roughly twenty minutes, and it must classify readers by what they say about cases, not by whether they can attach a scholar's name to their view — someone who has never read a page of secondary literature should still end up at the right leaf.

The tree's shared starting point is a charitable commitment: the reader grants that the advocate's replies within the dialogue are internally coherent, i.e., that the standard objections the interlocutor raises ("if there is a white horse, one cannot say there are no horses") are answered rather than dodged. From there, branch nodes should be phrased as concrete questions answerable without knowledge of the labels. I want the branching to be built from the logical space of readings, not from a list of names: first the axes — at what level does the thesis operate (grammar, ontology, or the adjudication of classifications), and what genre is the dialogue (doctrine, demonstration, or forensic exercise) — and only then the named readings located as leaves.

Leaves that must appear, in some formulation: (1) the Graham-style semantic reading, on which the sentence equivocates between the "is" of identity or class membership and the "is" of predication, and the advocate is deliberately exploiting that; (2) the Hansen-style reading, on which classical Chinese nouns behave like mass nouns picking out stuffs or kinds, so "white horse" and "horse" non-synonymously denote, and a translation like "white horse and horse do not name the same kind" dissolves the paradox; (3) the Fung Yu-lan universalist reading, on which "horse" names the abstract universal horseness, which is not the composite picked out by "white horse"; (4) a mereological reading, on which "horse" names a part of the content of "white horse," and a part is never identical to the whole; (5) the eristic deflation, on which the dialogue trains court disputers in winning either side and is not doctrine at all; and (6) a Mohist-style resolution, on which the advocate trades on the ambiguity and the Mohists' own apparatus (their affirmations that, e.g., riding a white horse counts as riding a horse, and their treatment of compound terms like "ox-horse") shows exactly where the equivocation lives.

At least four branch nodes should be fixed in advance, as follows. One node is the translation decision: asked to paraphrase fei into English, does the reader produce a sentence that is false, one that is true but trivial, or one they refuse to paraphrase at a sentence level at all? A second node is the sibling-sophism test: the catalogue of the disputers' theses preserved in the final chapter of the Zhuangzi includes the claim that dogs are not hounds — two different words for dog, invoked exactly the way "white horse" and "horse" are — and the Mohist Canons wrangle with the ox-and-horse compound; the reader must say whether, on their view, the white horse dialogue gets its depth from something these sibling puzzles lack, and if so what. A third node is drawn from inside the dialogue itself: the advocate's central observation that when one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may be delivered, but when one seeks a white horse they may not — the reader should be asked whether that observation registers a difference in the world, a difference in classification, or a fact about substitutability of names. A fourth node concerns genre and sincerity: is the advocate asserting a doctrine he holds, exhibiting a technique, or staging a reductio of loose usage?

Two further requirements. First, the tree should flag leaves it cannot genuinely separate — my suspicion is that the Graham and mereological readings, and perhaps the Hansen and Graham readings, will collapse together at the node level, and I would rather that be stated openly than papered over. Second, combinations of node answers that leave no one at the resulting leaf should be noted and diagnosed in a sentence each: is the position unoccupied because it is incoherent (e.g., a reading on which the English paraphrase is plainly false yet the advocate is a sincere metaphysician), because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it? Please keep the whole instrument self-contained: no citations, no bibliographies, no untranslated passages — every node must be decidable by someone with the dialogue's content summarized in the handout but no access to further sources.
