I spent forty years in human physiology before retiring, and I have lately been reading philosophy of medicine in the evenings, the way one picks up chess late in life. The debate over whether "disease" names a natural fact or partly a human value judgment strikes me as the medical analogue of an argument we never finished in the lab: whether our reference ranges discover a boundary or draw one. I now facilitate a monthly discussion group of retired physicians and scientists, and I want to hand them a classification instrument rather than a lecture.

Please write a decision tree that takes a participant from the starting commitment "there is a fact of the matter about whether a given condition is a disease, discoverable by sufficient biomedical research" down to a position on the naturalism–normativism spectrum in the philosophy of health and disease. The tree should be built from the logical space first and the named positions second: that is, decide which questions actually partition the terrain, then locate the standard views in the resulting cells.

Each branch node must be answerable by a physiologist who has never read a word of philosophy, phrased as a concrete case rather than as a request for self-labeling. Please include, at minimum, nodes turning on the following cases, since these are the ones my group will actually fight about:

1. Average age-related decline in lung function in an eighty-five-year-old who is asymptomatic relative to her peers: pathology, or normal function for the reference class of healthy eighty-five-year-olds?
2. The historical drift of the hypertension threshold: the same blood pressure reading that was deemed normal in 1960 is stage-one hypertension today, with no new mechanism discovered in between.
3. Grief after bereavement that satisfies the symptom checklist for major depression: whether the tree can even pose this case as a question about disease rather than about treatment is itself diagnostic.
4. A gene variant that sharply raises lifetime risk of a condition the carrier will never develop: does the person carrying it have a disease, a risk factor, or neither?
5. Whether the analysis is meant to apply to livestock and laboratory animals, where the "species-typical functioning" and the breeder's or experimenter's purposes come apart.

The leaves should include, at least, Boorse-style biostatistical theory, Wakefield-style harmful dysfunction, hybrid accounts that add judgments about reasonable expectations to functional facts, thoroughgoing normativism, and the eliminativist or deflationary option that "disease" should give way to specific mechanistic description. Include any other leaf you think the logical space requires, and aim for eight to twelve leaves in total.

Please also do the following three things, which I regard as the point of the exercise rather than an afterthought: first, mark every unoccupied combination of branch answers and say whether the view sitting there is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored; second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate — I suspect reference-class selection cannot be asked in a way that divides the positions it is supposed to divide, and I would like your tree to expose that problem rather than conceal it; third, tell me which single branch you would delete if forced to, on the grounds that a participant's answer to it tracks familiarity with the literature rather than genuine conviction.

Please write in American English, and keep the register of the branch questions suitable for reading aloud to a group of intelligent people who have never heard the term "metaethics."
