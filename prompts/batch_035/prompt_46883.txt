I am a novelist, three chapters into a book about a family that discovers something alive in the walls of their house — something whose status, vermin or lodger or child, they cannot settle — and as I draft I keep circling back to Kafka's The Metamorphosis, which I have slowly come to think of as the great pest-control narrative in the Western canon. I would like a serious critical reading of the novella through the ethics of pest control, one that takes both the story and the philosophy as equally worthy of respect.

Here is the angle that interests me. The philosophy of pest control is usually argued on two fronts: whether insects have the kind of sentience that grounds moral status at all, and, setting that aside, what level of threat to health, food, or livelihood suffices to license killing them. But Kafka's novella stages something the treatises tend to skip: the creature's moral status is not discovered by the family, it is administered by them, in real time, and it tracks their convenience and their disgust far more closely than any settled fact about what Gregor is. The sister who fed him and protected him becomes the one who insists they must get rid of him; the father, whose thrown apple lodges and rots in his son's back, is arguably acting out ordinary extermination logic with extra force; the charwoman treats his corpse as something to be swept out; and the family's closing excursion into the sunny countryside reads as the relief of a household after a successful clearing of an infestation. So my first question: does the novella show that our actual treatment of pests is governed by sympathy and threshold-of-tolerance rather than by any principled line, and does it do so more honestly than the philosophical literature?

But I want the reading to be faithful in both directions, so please press the disanalogy too. The novella never doubts Gregor's inner life — the reader is locked inside his perspective from the first sentence — whereas ordinary pest ethics rests precisely on doubting it. Kafka has to work a fantastic premise to make the extermination horrifying. Is the horror he produces, then, just the horror of a category error, a family misfiling a son as vermin, in which case the novella tells us nothing about how to treat actual insects? Or is the fantastic premise doing something else — showing that the question we ask about pests, namely what may be done to a creature that disgusts and inconveniences us, is the same question the family answers wrongly, so that the wrongness of their answer contaminates ours? A related thread: the family does have something like a real pest-control justification — Gregor's condition costs the father his jobless ease, risks the lodgers who pay rent, and forecloses the sister's future. If killing pests is permissible when they threaten livelihood, the family's case is formally in order. Is the novella's deepest horror that the logic we accept every spring in the garden runs exactly this way when the creature can think?

I would like the reading to end by saying what Kafka adds to the debate over insecticides and pest control that the philosophy papers don't: my working guess is that he demonstrates how moral status is socially conferred and withdrawn rather than metaphysically fixed, and that a family, a village, or a species can run through the stages of that withdrawal — sympathy, habituation, disgust, removal — without ever once making a decision. If you think that guess is wrong, say so and show me what the novella is really doing. Point me to the specific scenes that carry the argument, and be candid about the places where the pest-control reading strains the text rather than illuminating it.
