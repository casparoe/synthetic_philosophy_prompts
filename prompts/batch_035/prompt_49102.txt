I teach an intro development econ / economic history seminar, and every year the same fight breaks out: why are some nations rich and others poor? We get roughly three camps. The institutions people say it's all about inclusive vs. extractive rules, property rights, constraints on elites — they lean on stuff like the "reversal of fortune" pattern, where places that were prosperous around 1500 are now relatively poor, which they read as evidence that European colonization imposed different institutional packages and the institutions stuck. The geography people say the whole story is ecological: disease environments, crop suitability, latitude, access to coasts and navigable rivers, and they'd insist the institutionalists' "institutions" are just downstream of geography anyway, so finding that institutions predict income doesn't separate the causes. And then one student always brings in the culture side — norms, trust, work orientation, time preferences — pointing at things like the persistence of attitudes even among migrants' descendants generations later.

Here's what bugs me. These debates have been running for decades, extremely smart people on all sides, everyone using the same broad historical facts, and to my eye nobody has moved in twenty years. When an institutionalist shows an identification strategy (some natural experiment, some instrumental variable), the other side just says the instrument is contaminated or the mechanism travels through their favored channel, and vice versa all the way down. It starts to feel less like an empirical dispute and more like a structural one — maybe they disagree about what "cause of national wealth" even means when we're talking about a causal chain running from glaciation → soil → settlement patterns → property rights → GDP, where everything is both a cause of one thing and an effect of another. Maybe one side is answering "what's the most policy-relevant node in the chain" and another is answering "what started the chain," and they don't realize those are different questions.

So that's my hypothesis, but I want an actual diagnosis, not a survey of the literature. Is this disagreement substantive (there really is a fact of the matter about, say, the counterfactual "would the Congo be rich under different institutions, holding geography fixed"), or is it partly verbal, or is it a fight about standards of evidence where one camp accepts cross-country regressions as evidence and the other thinks N is effectively 5 or 10 when you're talking about continents? And crucially: what would even count as progress here, given that we can't rerun history? New data? Micro-estimates aggregated up? A sharper vocabulary for talking about proximate vs. distal causes? Or is this a question where the honest answer is that the limits of causal inference from one observed history are binding, and the field should admit it? Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is and whether anything could settle it.
