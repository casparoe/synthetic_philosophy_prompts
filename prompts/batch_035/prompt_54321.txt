Write a villanelle on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory. The two refrains should be spoken by the evidence itself, and they must never change a word as they return — one refrain should say, in effect, that it was already known, that its probability is one; the other should press the intuition that it nevertheless counts in favor of the theory that explains it. Let the form do the philosophical work: the refrains, fixed and unupdatable, play the role of P(E) = 1, while the tercets between them introduce successive new hypotheses (you might move from something like Mercury's perihellon to whatever else you like) so that the same unchanged lines stand in different confirmational relations each time they recur. A philosopher of science who knows Glymour's objection and the general-relativity case behind it should recognize exactly what is at stake; a reader who doesn't should still get a good poem about a fact that seems to matter differently depending on when one learns the theory that explains it. Do not resolve the problem cheaply — if you gesture toward a reply (the counterfactual about not yet having known the evidence, or the thought that what is really new is the theory rather than the fact), earn it in the final quatrain rather than merely asserting it.
