I'm on the charter review commission for a mid-sized city (around 150k people, and a pretty large population of legal permanent residents and visa holders who've been here for years). A councilmember asked us to study a proposal to let noncitizen legal residents vote in municipal elections only — city council, mayor, school board, local ballot inititives. The charter currently just says electors are US citizens 18 and over residing in the city. At our public hearings the commision split three ways. One faction is basically all-in: these residents pay local taxes, their kids are in our schools, our police can ticket them, our zoning decides what they can build, so they're governed by everything the city does and should get a say. A second faction says the line has to be citizenship because that's what marks actual commitment to the political community — otherwise voting degenerates into a customer relationship, like a gym membership. And a third member keeps insisting the whole debate proves voter rolls are arbitrary: every principle anyone proposes (live here? be governed? be taxed? plan to stay for good?) carves out a slightly different group, so we might as well keep the status quo since any line is as defensible as any other. Someone at the hearing also claimed a few American cities already allow noncitizens to vote in school board elections, though I haven't checked that.

Can you draft the written recommendation I'd circulate before our next meeting? I want a real bottom line — endorse the amendment, reject it, or endorse a narrower version (like school board only, or with a minimum residency period) — not a menu of options, since the council asked us for an actual position. But I also want it to take the taxation/representation argument and the citizenship-as-commitment argument seriously on their own terms and say why one wins, not just count pros and cons. And if you can address our third member's move — whether it really follows that if every boundary is somewhat arbitrary, the status quo wins by default, or whether there's a difference between a line being arbitrary and it being wrong (or between 'no line is perfect' and 'all lines are equally good') — that would help a lot, because that argument keeps derailing us and half the commission seems to find it secretly convincing.
