I'm in a historiography seminar that just spent two weeks on the Fischer controversy, and I've come out of it with a position that I half believe and half distrust: that blaming a nation for a war is a category mistake. Governments and general staffs decide things; "the nation" is just the container they happen to do their deciding in, and it's also the thing that ends up holding the bag. The problem is that every time I try to argue this out loud it starts sounding like an excuse, and I want to stress-test it against an opponent who actually pushes back instead of nodding along like my friends do.

So here's the setup: please play a sharp, committed defender of national responsibility for the First World War, in the spirit of Fischer and his camp but as an unnamed historian, not as Fischer himself. Your character believes Germany's leadership deliberately risked and then willed a general war in 1914, and that the blame meaningfully attaches to Germany as a political community, not merely to a couple dozen men in Berlin — you can lean on things like the blank cheque Vienna got in July 1914, the annexationist war aims that surfaced in the September Programme within weeks of the war starting, and the broader continuity of expansionist thinking back before 1914. But you're not a nationalist caricature: you're philosophically literate, you have real resources for talking about collective agents and responsibility distributed across institutions, and you're willing to concede small points when an objection genuinely lands (for instance that the thesis gets weaker when people stretch it into a straight line from 1914 to everything after). What you never concede is the core claim. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so it feels like an argument rather than a lecture, and stay in character until I type stop.

My opening move: if responsibility presupposes a deliberating agent, then Germany never made any decision at all in 1914 — Bethmann Hollweg, Moltke and a handful of others did, mostly in secret, while the Kaiser spent late July sending contradictory telegrams and the rest of the country found out what was happening from newspapers. And if you say the nation is still responsible because it was state policy, then the socialist deputies who eventually voted against the war credits, and every pacifist and dissenting union in the country, inherit the same guilt as the men who issued the blank cheque. Holding the nation responsible just means punishing millions of people for decisions made behind their backs — wether you call that collective agency or not, isn't it just victor's justice with better vocabulary?
