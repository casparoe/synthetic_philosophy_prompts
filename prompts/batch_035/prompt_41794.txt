I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the epistemic pathologies of direct democracy — eighteen students, one 90-minute session — in a unit that runs from the Mytilenean debate through the vote on the Sicilian expedition to the trial of Socrates. For this session I want the students to run into the problem themselves before anyone names it. Design an in-class role-play built around a fictional decision case — not Mytilene itself, and not any historical episode they will recognize — in which a sovereign assembly of the whole class, in the grip of a perceived betrayal, votes in haste for a maximally harsh response, then reconvenes and reverses itself, only to discover that reversal arrives too late for part of what the first vote set in motion.

The activity should have the following components, and the deliverable is the full, runnable design of each:

- A fictional case with enough texture that students argue about it as a real decision rather than as a puzzle: the background scenario, a one-page briefing sheet the entire assembly receives, and asymmetric information — two or three students hold briefs the rest of the assembly does not, and whether and when they deploy them is up to them.
- A two-assembly structure in which the standing rules of debate are chosen by the students themselves at the outset — who may speak, for how long, and whether appeals to justice are allowed alongside appeals to interest. Their choice of rules should have visible consequences they can identify in the debrief.
- An irreversibility mechanic: the first vote must dispatch something the second vote cannot fully recall — a timer, a sealed envelope, a token standing in for a trireme — so that students feel, rather than being told, that being able to change your mind is not the same as being able to undo.
- A payoff scheme requiring no real money (points or standing), rigged so that deciding quickly and harshly is genuinely tempting on the merits — the harsh option must not be an obvious trap that no reasonable person would take.
- A two-stage debrief. Stage one maps what actually happened in the room onto the historical pattern: first the parallel to Mytilene, then the ways the same assembly-level failure reappears at Syracuse and in the courtroom that condemned Socrates. Stage two is a stress-test: the students propose institutional fixes (cooling-off periods, supermajorities, second chambers, banning emotional appeals), and the facilitator is supplied with good grounds for arguing that each fix merely relocates the problem — as the Sicilian vote shows, an assembly that deliberates at length and without anger can still err catastrophically, and as the trial shows, a large jury deciding under procedural rules is not immune.
- A frank note on what the simulation cannot carry: the real fear of revolt, the real stakes in lives, and the fact that Athenian jurors and assemblymen were paid volunteers whose verdicts admitted no appeal. Say how the facilitator should supply these gaps in the debrief rather than pretend the game reproduced them.

Please organize your response under the following headings, in this order and at roughly the indicated lengths:

1. The Case and the Briefings (about 250 words) — the fictional scenario, the text of the common briefing sheet, and the asymmetric briefs.
2. Rules and Timeline (about 300 words) — the mechanics of the two assemblies, the speaking rules, the irreversibility mechanic, the payoff scheme, and the minute-by-minute breakdown of the 90 minutes.
3. The Debrief (about 350 words) — the mapping onto Mytilene, Sicily, and the trial of Socrates, and the stress-test of the students' proposed fixes.
4. What Will Go Wrong the First Time (about 150 words) — the predictable failures in practice and how to handle each.
5. What the Simulation Cannot Carry (about 100 words).
