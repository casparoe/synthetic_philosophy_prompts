I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and one story I keep circling without ever being able to land is the one about what cross-cultural variation in moral judgment does to moral objectivity. The empirical side is by now well rehearsed: surveys and economic games show the populations that psychologists disproportionately sample — Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic — are global outliers on things like impartial fairness, the weighting of harm to strangers, and the treatment of individual rights as moral trumps; elsewhere, kinship obligations, sanctity, and loyalty carry real moral weight, and the same "harmless offense" vignettes that a secular Western undergraduate shrugs at are treated as serious wrongs by people who are, by any other measure, entirely sensible. There is even an argument I have reported on that part of this pattern traces to the medieval Church's dismantling of extended kinship networks in Europe — that WEIRD impartiality is downstream of a specific marriage-and-family regime, not a discovery of the moral truth.

But when I ask researchers what follows from all this, the conversation always forks into the same two channels and then stops moving. One side says: if our moral intuitions are calibrated by local ecology — markets, kinship density, church history — rather than by the facts, then the intuitions are like instruments with uncorrectable systematic error, and a confident WEIRD moral universalism is just parochialism with better manners. The other side says: variation in judgment has never impugned objectivity in any other domain — people disagreed about the shape of the earth, and still disagree about history — and the debunking move only works if you smuggle in the premise that intuition is our only route to moral truth, which the objectivist rejects; moreover, there is a thin but real core of norms (against unprovoked harm within the group, for some reciprocity, for some sharing) that shows up nearly everywhere, so the variation is disagreement at the margins, not chaos.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the stall is not about the data at all: the two sides are using the same variation as evidence under two different metaphors, one treating intuition as a measuring device whose cross-sociological spread indicates calibration error, the other treating it as testimony whose spread is mere disagreement, and neither metaphor is argued for — it is inherited from the side's broader commitments. I would like to know whether that diagnosis holds up, or whether the real fault line is something else: a burden-of-proof dispute about what counts as the default, a hidden empirical question about how much convergence there actually is once you fix the grain of description (thick local norms versus thin universal ones), or simply two projects — the sociology of moral systems and the epistemology of moral belief — traveling under one headline. And given whatever diagnosis you favor, I want an honest account of what progress would even look like here: a shared measure of cross-cultural moral convergence at each grain? A principled criterion for when variation is debunking evidence and when it is not? Or a well-argued verdict that the question, as posed, cannot be settled and why.
