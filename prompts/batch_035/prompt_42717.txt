I sit on the national council of a professional association of about forty thousand members, and at next month's meeting we must vote on a motion to affiliate formally with a broad electoral coalition ahead of the general election. The coalition's terms are concrete: affiliates adopt the coalition's full platform rather than picking planks, route intra-affiliate disputes through the coalition's arbitration body rather than resolving them ourselves, contribute a share of dues, and commit to mobilizing members for canvassing and get-out-the-vote. Our current charter commits us to positions on professional regulation, licensing, and public funding of our field, but we have never taken a collective stance on the general political program of any party or bloc, and nothing in the coalition's platform concerns our field directly.

The motion has split the council three ways, and the split has an unmistakably philosophical edge. The leading opponent is a longtime councillor who has been circulating a two-page summary of Simone Weil's early-1940s essay on the abolition of political parties. Her Weilian claim, as best I can reconstruct it: Weil held that a party is a machine for producing collective thought in place of individual thought — that it operates by pressure on members, rivalry with other collectives, and an implicit goal of unlimited self-propagation, so that joining one means handing over your conscience and ceasing to think for yourself. Our councillor argues that an association with forty thousand members, a paid staff, a line it can enforce, and now a platform adopted wholesale is exactly such a machine in miniature, and that our professional authority would be spent making members think as a bloc. The pro-affiliation side replies that this is a category error: Weil was describing mass parties competing for state power in a nation at war, not voluntary professional bodies members can quit at will, and besides, our association already takes collective positions and disciplines members who violate professional norms — so if the Weilian logic were right, we would already be a party, and the objection proves too much. A third group wants a compromise: affiliation without teeth — endorsement and shared platform, but no arbitration clause, no dues, no mobilization quotas, explicit protection for members who dissent publicly.

I have to draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote, and I'd like you to write it. It should recommend one of the options — full affiliation as proposed, rejection, or the toothless-compromise variant — with reasons the losing factions could actually recognize as responsive to what they said. Please engage the Weilian objection at its strongest: specify what would have to be true about our association — about how members actually form views, about what we can credibly enforce, about the exit costs of leaving — for the "machine for collective thought" worry to bite here rather than being an eloquent anachronism. And if you recommend a conditional approval, spell out which conditions do the real work and why dropping any one of them would change your answer. I'd also appreciate a candid paragraph on what the Weilian side should concede: if voluntary exit is easy and no one is disciplined for dissent, has their objection been answered, or has it merely been deferred to the day the coalition's arbitration clause is quietly reinstated?
