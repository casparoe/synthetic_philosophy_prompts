I teach a third-year module on free will and the sciences of the mind at a UK university, and I've been landed with writting this year's final exam. The topic I want to test is the interpretation of Libet-style experiments, and I'm completely sick of the two stock answers I get every year: either "Libet showed the brain decides before we do, so free will is an illusion", or the equally canned "the readiness potential is just an artefact, so Libet was refuted". Both are recitations with a name attached. What I actually want to test is whether students can reason about what a specific finding would need to show about the timing of a decision before any conclusion about freedom could follow from it.

To force that, I want the question built around a fictional study, which the exam paper would describe to the students roughly as follows. Subjects do a standard Libet task: they watch a spot rotate around a clock face, press a button whenever they feel the urge, and afterwards report where the spot was when they first felt that urge (the W judgment). In seperate blocks the presses are externally timed by a cue instead. The study reports three findings: (1) averaged EEG shows the usual rising potential before the free presses but nothing comparable before the externally cued ones; (2) the shape and onset of this ramp is the same however early or late the subject reported the urge — trials where W came a whole second before the press look identical to trials where W came only 100ms before it; (3) in a second session, subjects are told they'll be interviewed afterwards and asked to justify why they pressed when they did, and in that session the reported W shifts earlier by about 300ms while the EEG ramp is unchanged.

What I need from you is three things. First, the exam question itself, written for a 90-minute answer and phrased so it cannot be answered by naming Libet and Schurger — it should demand that students say precisely what each of the three findings does and does not show about when, if ever, a decision occurs in this task, and what follows, if anything, for the freedom of these subjects' choices. Second, a model answer of about 700 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points and the marks each is worth, that separates a first from a 2:1 by naming the specific moves each class of answer has to contain, plus a one-line note on how students typically mess up each point — that'll save me sanity during marking. Please write it all in British English, since this is going straight into the paper.
