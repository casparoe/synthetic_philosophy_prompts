I teach a grad seminar on cultural property ethics, and next week we're finally doing the Benin Bronzes — you know the story, looted in the 1897 punitive expedition, dispersed by auction, most of them ending up in places like Berlin and London where they've now been for over a century. In the faculty discussions beforehand I keep hitting two analogies that people are using as actual arguments, and I want to figure out how much weight either of them can bear.

The director of our university museum insists the bronzes are like children evacuated in wartime who grew up in foster homes: Berlin is where they've lived for 120 years, that's part of what they now *are*, and shipping them "back" to a Nigeria they've never known doesn't restore anything, it just relocates them for the sake of a bloodline. One of my postdocs counters that they're just stolen goods, full stop: if someone steals your car and drives it for twenty years it's still your car, and nobody asks whether the car has "settled in" to the thief's garage. Both lines do real work out there in the actual restituion debate — the grown-up-elsewhere story from museum people, the stolen-is-stolen story from restitution advocates.

Act as a professional philosopher specializing in applied ethics and cultural property. Think carefully before you write anything, and maybe start with some quick brainstorming on what each analogy is really trying to get you to accept. Then audit both of them: what maps onto what (what plays the role of the parent, the foster home, the car's owner, the passage of time), where each mapping breaks, and what each one smuggles in — I'm especially interested in whether artifacts can have anything like interests or attachments at all, and in who "the owner" is supposed to be when the original victims were a kingdom annexed in 1897 and the current claimant is a modern state with successor institutions. Then tell me which conclusion, if any, either analogy can actually support on its own, and whether there's a better analogy lurking. I have a suspicion the whole "who owns it" framing is doing more work than either side admits — if you agree, say so, but if you think that's a cop-out, push back.
