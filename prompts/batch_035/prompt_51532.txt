I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on moral epistemology and the ethics of artificial intelligence, and for our unit on value alignment we've been discussing coherent extrapolated volition — the proposal, originally due to Eliezer Yudkowsky, that a powerful AI system should be guided not by humanity's current, contradictory, often ill-informed stated preferences, but by what we would want it to do under substantially idealized conditions: knowing much more, thinking faster and more coherently, having reflected at length, and having developed together rather than in isolation. For my term paper I want to test this idea against historically important positions in metaethics, and I've chosen Hume. What do you think Hume's considered verdict on coherent extrapolated volition would be?

I can see the case going both ways, and I'd like your help thinking it through. On the one hand, Hume is famous for holding that reason cannot generate, correct, or rank our ultimate ends — it merely works out the means to whatever ends our passions happen to set. One might therefore expect him to reject any procedure that lets what we would want if we were smarter and more reflective outrank what we actually want, since the extrapolated preferences are not the preferences of any passion actually occurring in any actual mind. On the other hand, Hume himself appeals to something like idealization when he argues that moral judgment requires correcting for the distortions introduced by our particular situation — we must judge from a steady, general point of view rather than from wherever we happen to be standing — and he elsewhere suggests that what passes for the influence of reason on action is really the operation of certain calm passions. If correcting for one's situation is legitimate for Hume, it's not obvious why correcting for ignorance and hasty reflection wouldn't be.

In your answer, please address three things. First, give your overall prediction of Hume's verdict: would he accept, reject, or qualify the extrapolationist picture, and why? Second, explain whether you think Hume would regard the extrapolated preferences as normatively authoritative for the actual person — as genuinely that person's own values, suitably tidied up — or as the values of a different, better-informed agent to whom the actual person has no special obligation to defer. Third, consider the calm-passions reading: if the more reflective, better-informed version of ourselves just is the version in which calm passions dominate the violent ones, does coherent extrapolated volition become not merely compatible with Hume but an extended application of his own method? Please be explicit about which parts of your answer rest on textual evidence you're confident in, and which are reasonable reconstruction filling gaps in Hume's texts, since he obviously never considered anything like this.
