I am a novelist at work on a book in which the definition of death is not a theme but a piece of machinery: the plot must actually turn on it, the way a plot turns on a locked door. I have read enough to know that the interesting line runs between the neurological criterion of death — the irreversible loss of all brain function — and the circulatory criterion — the irreversible stoppage of heart and lungs — and that these two usually coincide but need not. What I cannot yet see is how to make them diverge in a way that is both medically ordinary and narratively consequential.

Construct the scenario to the following specifications. It must be set in the present day, in an ordinary hospital, with no speculative technology and no theatrical rarity invented for my convenience; everything in it should be the sort of thing that has actually happened in intensive care units. The divergence between the two criteria must concern not merely whether my character is dead but when she dies, and the difference in timing must be long enough — hours at least — to matter legally. The stakes must be concrete and non-medical: I want the timing of death to decide something a lay reader will grasp at once, on the order of whether a beneficiary has outlived a testator by the period a survivorship clause requires. Each criterion should be applied by a competent professional acting in good faith; the story is not about negligence or villainy, and I refuse to rig the scene by making one side a fool.

After the scenario — which should be no longer than a paragraph — walk me through the mechanics. State precisely what each criterion delivers in the case and at what clock time; identify which single clinical fact is doing the work of separating them; then show me how a thoughtful defender of the criterion that comes out inconveniently for my plot would argue, and what a defender of the other would say back. Finally, tell me what becomes of the scenario if that separating feature is removed — whether it collapses into the standard brain-death case that every reader of bioethics has already encountered, and if so, what the least contrived way to avoid that collapse would be. What I am after, in the end, is a scene that a reasonable physician, a reasonable lawyer, and a reasonable grieving relative could each read and come away on different sides.
