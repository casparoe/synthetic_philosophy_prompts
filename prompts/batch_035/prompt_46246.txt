I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a computationally ordinary mathematician — brilliant, but no faster at checking entailments than you or I would be if we sat down with pencil and paper for a few decades. She has spent years working toward a proof of a certain long-standing conjecture, and I have been drafting a chapter in which she confesses to a colleague that she is "about seventy percent confident" the conjecture is true. My editor, who studied philosophy, objects: mathematics is not like the weather. Either the conjecture follows from the axioms or it does not, and there is no remaining fact about the world for her to be uncertain about — so a probability like seventy percent is, on his view, a rhetorical flourish, not an honest state of mind. A friend of mine then pointed me toward the literature on logical uncertainty: the problem of logical omniscience in epistemic logic, and more recently the "logical induction" research program, which claims that computationally bounded agents can assign coherent probabilities to mathematical statements by treating successive reasoners as a kind of prediction market for theorems, in such a way that they cannot be exploited by a Dutch book strategy even though no computable agent can know all logical consequences of its beliefs. My friend believes this settles the matter in my character's favor.

I would like you to write an essay adjudicating this dispute. The core question is whether it makes sense — metaphysically, epistemologically, and not merely as a stylistic convention — for a bounded reasoner to hold graded credences in propositions whose truth value is fixed by logic alone. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides: the argument that logical uncertainty is somehow counterfeit or merely psychological, lacking the truth-conducive grounding that ordinary empirical uncertainty has; and the argument that from the agent's own standpoint, unproven mathematical claims function in deliberation and action exactly as contingencies do, so that a normative theory of rationality which idealizes them away misdescribes her situation. In doing so, assess what the logical induction framework actually vindicates — whether it shows that probabilistic attitudes toward mathematics are rational in themselves, or only that certain formal procedures avoid exploitations, leaving open the possibility that my editor is right that nothing in the world corresponds to my character's seventy percent. I am also curious about the so-called paradoxes this territory generates, such as the difficulty any consistent agent faces in assigning sensible probabilities to claims about its own future beliefs.

Please do not end on a noncommittal note: take a side on whether my character's confidence is philosophically respectable as written, or whether I should revise the chapter so that her attitude toward the conjecture is presented as something other than ordinary graded belief. Finally, since I will be sending your essay to my editor, include a closing discussion of where your reasoning is most fragile — which argument, if it failed, would flip you to the opposite verdict, and what would have to be true of the logical induction framework (or of the notion of credence itself) for that to happen.
