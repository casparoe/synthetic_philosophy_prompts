So I've pretty much fully bought into the Humean view of laws of nature. There's just the vast mosaic of local facts — this particular having that property, here and now — and the laws are nothing over and above a summary of it: whichever system of axioms best compresses the mosaic, on a balance of simplicity and strength, gets to be called "the laws." Nothing governs anything; laws describe rather than legislate. Lewis's thing, more or less. I used to think the anti-Humeans (powers, nomic necessitation, laws as things in their own right) were bolting on spooky machinery with no explanatory payoff, and honestly I still think that.

But I want to stress-test my view properly: what would actually count against it? And I don't mean the generic "but laws must govern!" hand-waving. There are a few concrete things I want sorted:

- Lewis himself granted there could be worlds where the best-system competition goes badly — no stable winner, or a winner so gerrymandered it says nothing any scientist in that world would use. Does that concession damage the view, or is it fine because our world clearly isn't like that? And if a gerrymandered system somehow won in OUR world, would I have to bite the bullet and treat its ugly axioms as the laws? Should that bother me?
- Someone told me Humean chance has a problem where the chances are fixed by the whole mosaic, including the events themselves, so if some wildly improbable event occurs that would itself change what the chances are, the usual rule for relying on chances (the Principal Principle) gets caught in a loop. Is that a genuine crack in the view, or a technicality Humeans have allready answered?
- If the standards the "best system" is judged by — simplicity, naturalness of properties — are themselves only definable relative to us or our language, hasn't the view smuggled mind back into the laws? That seems like it should worry a view whose whole selling point is being the sober one.

Rank the defeaters for me: which would be fatal if they went the wrong way, which merely awkward, and for each, say whether a committed Humean should concede it counts against the view or dismiss it — and on what grounds. Also tell me what Humeanism predicts or rules out that its rivals don't, because if nothing distinguishes them empirically, I'm not sure what my confidence in the view is even tracking. And be honest at the end about whether "balance of simplicity and strength" has insulated the view so it can never lose — and if so, whether that's a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind or just the price of admission.
