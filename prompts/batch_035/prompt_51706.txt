My colleague Ruth and I co-teach a seminar on vagueness, and I'm trying to anticipate her line on a couple of new cases before Thursday. Her track record on the old cases: a man with 3 hairs is bald, flat out; a man with a full head of hair is not bald, flat out; a man with 40,000 hairs (call him Sam) gets nothing either way — she won't say he's bald and she won't say he isn't; and when Sam lost a single hair, going from 40,001 to 40,000, she said that made no difference to how she'd rule. She also refuses to sign off on "if someone with n hairs is bald, someone with n+1 hairs is bald" as a blanket claim.

The new cases I have to prep for: (1) I ask her to set aside what anyone could ever know, and tell me whether there's a fact of the matter about whether Sam is bald; and (2) I ask whether anything actually changed about Sam's baldness the day he lost that one hair. Epistemicism and supervaluationism both seem to fit her record as far as I can tell — so what does each predict on (1) and (2), which is she more likely to run with, and is there some further case that would split the two even if she dodges (1)? Related: would just asking her "is there some exact number of hairs where baldness flips?" do the job? I keep feeling like it should, but something tells me it wont.
