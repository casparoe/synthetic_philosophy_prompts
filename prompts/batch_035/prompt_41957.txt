I teach an introductory typography course at an art and design school, and next term I want to devote one ninety-minute session to a question my students have never been asked but are oddly well positioned to think about: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? These are first-year design students, not philosophy students. They spend their days copying letterforms, and they arrive with two convictions I'd like to disturb: that there is a correct 'a' they were taught in school (the double-storey print 'a' from their handwriting workbooks, or the one in whatever typeface they think of as neutral), and that the rest — the single-storey 'a', the blackletter 'a', the 'a' in a distressed poster font — is just "styling" applied to that core shape.

Plan the session for me rather than explaining the philosophy directly. I want a teaching plan whose spine is the philosophical literature on categories and essences, translated into something designers can grip: Plato's Forms as the "master template" view, Wittgenstein's family resemblance, prototype theory of the sort Eleanor Rosch developed, and the thought — which I take to hover behind Hofstadter's Letter Spirit work on letterform perception and analogy — that recognizing an 'a' in a typeface you've never seen before is a small everyday miracle of categorization that no list of necessary and sufficient features seems to capture. I'd also like the plan to touch the historical-causal idea: that a glyph is an 'a' partly because it sits at the end of a long chain of copying and modification running back through the Latin alphabet to the Greek alpha, so that identity might be inherited rather than intrinsic — a notion my students will find strange until they realize it's what a revival typeface designer does for a living.

What I need from you:

1. The order of ideas, with the dependency structure made explicit — what has to be established before the next piece can land. For instance, I suspect students must first be confronted with the sheer variety of things they already accept as 'a' before the "master template" view can be put on trial, but tell me if you'd sequence it differently and why.

2. The one carrying example or exercise that holds the whole session together, and what it must have and what it must lack. I've considered having students deform a single 'a' step by step until it becomes an 'o' or an 'e' and asking where it stopped being an 'a' (a sorites-style slide), versus showing them a glyph from an unfamiliar or invented typeface and asking how they know what letter it is at a glance. Tell me which to use — or propose something better — and explain what each version fails to teach.

3. The misconceptions to expect, in the order the students will form them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I anticipate some of them: that the essence of 'a' is a specific shape; that recognition is template-matching against the schoolbook form; that once essences fail, it's all arbitrary convention, so any squiggle could be an 'a' if we agreed; and, from my sharper students, that the answer is simply "family resemblance" stated as a slogan they don't actually know how to apply.

4. What to leave out at this level and say so honestly — I assume the computational details of Hofstadter's architecture, the neurophysiology of letter recognition, and the semiotics they'll get in a later unit, but push back on my list if something I'd cut is actually load-bearing.

5. One concrete check at the end of the session that would tell me whether they've genuinely grasped the problem of letter categorization rather than merely found it charming — a question or micro-task they couldn't pass on either the "there's a right 'a'" view or the "it's all convention" view.

If it sharpens the plan: the session is a studio session, so students have pencils, tracing paper, and scissors on the table, and I'm far more interested in them leaving with the question lodged in their hands than with the correct theory in their notes.
