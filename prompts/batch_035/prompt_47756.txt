I am a recently retired developmental biologist — most of my working life was spent on early embryogenesis, which is perhaps why the question has gripped me — and I have lately been reading metaphysics, including Kripke and Parfit. I have gradually assembled a position on the necessity of origins: the question of whether I could have come into existence from a different sperm and egg. I should like to know whether what I have assembled hangs together, and I would be grateful for a careful audit rather than a survey of the literature.

My commitments, as near as I can state them, are these. (a) I accept the Kripkean claim that my origin is essential to me: I could not have originated from a different pair of gametes, so that a child of my parents produced by different gametes would have been a sibling of mine, not me. (b) The timing of conception seems to me inessential: the same egg fertilised by the same sperm a fortnight later would still have been me. And here I must confess to a stronger pull. My parents might have married earlier, in which case a child would very likely have been conceived some years before my actual conception — in the late 1940s, say — and my inclination is to say that that child would have been me, living a rather different life. As a developmental biologist I am aware that the same egg could in principle have been involved, since my mother's oocytes were all formed well before her own birth, though the same sperm certainly could not have been. (c) I accept Parfit's reductionism about persons: personal identity consists in physical and psychological continuity and is not a further fact over and above it, and what matters for my future is psychological connectedness rather than identity as such. (d) On cases: a clone produced from one of my somatic cells would not be me; and I take the monozygotic-twin case to show that a single origin can be shared by two people without collapsing them into one. I mention this because someone has suggested that if one origin can give two people, my refusal to allow one person two possible origins looks like special pleading; I do not feel the force of the suggestion, but I cannot say quite why. (e) Finally, as a matter of method carried over from the sciences, I distrust modal intuition as evidence: where I accept a necessity claim — as in (a) — I should want it grounded in facts about the actual world, biological or otherwise, rather than in what we find ourselves able to imagine.

Please treat this package as the object of examination. Set out which pairs of my commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad; and for each, indicate what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the package. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I honestly do not know where the trouble lies, if anywhere, and I have tried not to signal which commitment I should be most willing to surrender. Please write your answer in British English, and be specific rather than exhaustive.
