My team is weighing a change to how we approve risky database migrations: instead of a single senior-engineer sign-off, a panel of engineers would each review the migration independently and we would ship on a majority verdict. At the design review where this was proposed, two of our staff engineers got into a sustained argument about whether it is actually justified, and since I took notes I can reconstruct the gist of the exchange.

(1) Marcus, arguing for the panel, invoked what he called the Condorcet jury theorem: if each reviewer independently reaches the right call with probability 0.6 — barely better than a coin flip — then a majority of 21 reviewers is right about 82% of the time, and the probability climbs toward certainty as reviewers are added. Since our engineers are well above coin-flip quality, he said, the panel is not merely a modest improvement over one expert but approaches infallibility as the panel grows, so more reviewers is strictly better.

(2) Elena objected that the independence assumption is fantasy in this setting: our engineers share the same training, the same runbooks, the same architecture documents, and therefore the same blind spots, so their errors correlate. With correlated errors, she argued, a majority vote just reproduces the average reviewer's judgment, amplified by a false sense of statistical legitimacy, and can be worse than relying on our single best reviewer. She said Marcus's 82% figure was smuggled in by an assumption the setting flatly violates.

(3) Marcus replied that Elena had misread the independence condition: the theorem does not require reviewers' verdicts to be unconditionally independent — of course they agree more often when the migration is genuinely sound — it requires their errors to be independent conditional on the truth of the proposition under review, and shared documentation and shared priors do not by themselves create that kind of error dependence; each reviewer either finds the real flaw or misses it for reasons of their own. He added that the theorem cuts both ways in a manner that should reassure us: if individual competence fell below 0.5, majority voting would amplify the incompetence and converge on the wrong verdict with near-certainty as the panel grows — but we can measure our reviewers' historical hit rate on past migration decisions, it sits comfortably above 50%, and so we know we are on the right side of the threshold.

(4) Elena answered that the symmetry point was the strongest argument against the panel rather than for it: the measured hit rate comes only from the subset of past decisions where we eventually learned the truth — the migrations that shipped and failed visibly — while the dangerous cases are the silent ones, where the damage is diffuse or delayed and we never find out, so we do not actually know the competence parameter in the regime where the panel matters most. She also said Marcus's claim that shared documentation does not create error dependence was optimistic: a wrong diagram in the runbook makes every reviewer miss the same flaw, and that common-mode failure is exactly the case the panel was supposed to catch. She concluded that the theorem is practically useless as a justification — it either assumes away the problem or certifies the conclusion we would have reached anyway.

(5) Marcus ended by conceding the runbook point but insisting the upshot was merely that the gains saturate: correlation slows the climb toward certainty, but as long as reviewers' errors are not literally identical, the majority still beats the average reviewer, and the panel still dominates solo sign-off in expectation. He closed by repeating that adding reviewers can never make the decision worse — only more expensive.

Neither would budge before the meeting ran out of time. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say who is right, what is subtly off, and what each party deserves credit for. In particular, I would like to know (a) whether Marcus's handling of the independence condition in move (3) reflects the standard reading of the theorem, or whether he is exploiting an ambiguity; (b) whether his final claim in move (5) — that adding reviewers cannot make the decision worse — survives Elena's common-mode-failure and silent-feedback points; and (c) whether there is a better-grounded way to structure the panel (for instance, weighting reviewers by track record, or something that changes the individual competence rather than just the head count) that would dissolve much of the dispute.
