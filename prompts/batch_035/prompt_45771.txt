I'm a PhD student TA-ing a graduate seminar on collective agency and social choice, and I'd like a second opinion on a short midterm answer I have to grade. The exam question was:

"Present the discursive dilemma, using the three-judge court example. Explain how premise-based and conclusion-based procedures can diverge, and state what List and Pettit's general impossibility result adds to this observation. Finally, identify which of the conditions in their result majority voting itself fails to satisfy."

The student wrote:

"The discursive dilemma—a term coined by Kornhauser and Sager in 'Unpacking the Court' (1986)—arises when majority verdicts on a set of interconnected propositions are jointly inconsistent with the majority verdict on a conclusion drawn from them. Suppose a three-judge court must decide whether a defendant is liable, where doctrine says the defendant is liable if and only if the defendant committed the act (p) and had a duty not to commit it (q). Judge 1 accepts p, q, and liability; Judge 2 accepts p, rejects q, and rejects liability; Judge 3 rejects p, accepts q, and rejects liability. Majorities accept each premise—p is accepted by Judges 1 and 2, q by Judges 1 and 3—so the premise-based procedure yields liability. But a majority of judges, namely 2 and 3, individually rejected liability, so the conclusion-based procedure yields not liable. If the court votes by majority on every proposition on the agenda, the collective outcome is the inconsistent set {p, q, not liable}. List and Pettit generalized the point: for agendas with this structure, no aggregation function satisfies all of universal domain, anonymity, systematicity, and collective rationality. Majority voting satisfies universal domain and anonymity, so it is systematicity that it violates, and this violation is what generates the inconsistent collective set. The result parallels Arrow's theorem, though it concerns logically connected propositions rather than preferences. It shows that some condition must be relaxed—by narrowing the agenda, or by allowing different propositions to be treated differently."
