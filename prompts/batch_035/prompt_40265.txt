I'm a third-year PhD student in machine science, working on multi-agent systems, and a running disagreement with another student in our reading group has turned into a wager we would like to settle properly. The topic is what the formal decision theory and AI-safety literature calls commitment races, and since neither of us can have evidence in the ordinary sense, what I want from you is a structured credence, not an opinion.

The setup, so that the question is self-contained: two agents are about to interact — bargain, trade, threaten. Schelling's observation is that in such interactions the ability to visibly bind your own future behavior can be a strength rather than a handicap: if I can irreversibly commit to walking away unless you concede seventy percent, and you know I have so committed, your best response may be to accept thirty. The trouble starts when both sides have this capacity and commitment is cheap to deploy but expensive to verify. Each agent then has an incentive to commit early, aggressively, and on the basis of little information, because the value of committing first is that it shapes the other side's best response before their commitment shapes mine. If both reason this way, the result can be a race to mutually destructive commitments that neither can back out of, even though both would have done better to commit more cautiously and later. Some writers worry that machine agents bargaining with one another — or with us — are especially exposed to this dynamic, since self-modification and verifiable disclosure may be far cheaper for them than constitutions and treaties are for us.

My credence that this is a genuine structural phenomenon — that there exist realistic conditions under which independently rational agents with access to commitment technology end up in mutually worse outcomes specifically because each rationally commits early rather than late — is roughly 0.75. Hers is roughly 0.25; she thinks the race is mostly an artifact of idealizations: perfectly transparent commitments, one-shot interactions, and agents assumed to reason about each other in a way real actors, human or machine, will not sustain.

Give me your own credence, but the number is the least of it; I want the decomposition. Some terms you might use, though you should restructure them if you think they carve the question wrongly: (i) the probability that commitment of this kind is actually available in realistic settings — cheap to make and credible to the other side, rather than just talk; (ii) the probability that the first-mover advantage is real, rather than an illusion generated by modeling both agents as simultaneously rational and simultaneously ignorant; (iii) the probability that mechanisms outside the model — iteration, reputation, third-party enforcement, or agents deliberately remaining ignorant of each other's commitments — dissolve the race often enough that it stops being a structural threat; (iv) any residual term you think belongs, for instance the probability that independently rational even picks out a coherent joint policy when agents model each other modeling each other. Assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into your final number, and then tell me which single term that number is most sensitive to — the one where a modest change in its justification would swing the answer across the gap between her 0.25 and my 0.75 — and what argument, theorem, experiment, or class of historical cases would move it most. If you think our disagreement most likely lives in one term rather than being spread across them, say which; and say whether one of us is making a category mistake — whether she is right that the phenomenon, if real, is a fact about decision theory rather than about the world, or whether I am right that a fact about decision theory can thereby be a fact about the world. Please do not hedge into refusing to give numbers, and do not give a number so qualified that it could mean anything.
