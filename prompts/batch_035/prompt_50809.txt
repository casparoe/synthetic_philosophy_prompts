I'm a doctoral student co-organising an interdisciplinary reading group — half philosophers, half NLP researchers — that has spent this term on the question of whether machines understand language. We've worked through Searle's person-in-the-room who manipulates Chinese symbols by rule without knowing any Chinese, Harnad's worry that a symbol system whose connections run only between symbols, and never out to the world, is ungrounded, the more recent thought experiment about an octopus that, having tapped into an undersea communication cable, responds so fluently to a shipwrecked sailor's distress call that it passes as a fellow human, and the claim that today's large language models, trained only on the form of text, are at best stochastic parrots — pattern-completers whose grip on form tells us nothing about any grip on meaning. The recurring failure in our sessions is that everyone argues from a single pet case: one person brandishes the Chinese Room, another points at a model answering an unseen physics question, and the meeting ends in a duel of intuitions. For our final session I want to replace that with a specification.

Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of what it is for a system to understand language has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp — I am particularly suspicious that several requirements people treat as obvious (anything demanding a biological origin, sensorimotor embodiment, or participation in genuine communicative exchanges) may be prejudices of one side smuggled in as criteria. Requirements I would expect the list to cover include at least: what the understanding is for, so that the theory can say what success would even look like; whether it can extend to non-biological systems without the debate being settled by definition; how it distinguishes producing contextually appropriate text from actually grasping what the text is about; whether it must account for systematic, productive generalisation to genuinely novel utterances, not just fluent continuation of familiar ones; whether it has to be testable without relying on the system's own reports of what it understands; and how it handles the cases each camp treats as decisive — the rule-follower without Chinese, the unobservant octopus, the model that answers well about situations it can never perceive or act in.

Finally, do two things with the list. First, tell me whether any pair of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: my own suspicion is that a demanding reading of the grounding requirement and a serious commitment to being non-anthropocentric cannot both be had, and if some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the part I most want to see, since it would show the debate turns not on future evidence about the models but on which criterion each party is prepared to abandon. Second, briefly — a paragraph or two, no more — score the two familiar extremes against the list: the deflationist view that understanding just is the capacity to generate appropriate text in context, and the grounding-first view that nothing understands language unless its symbols are anchored in perception and action. The weight should sit on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on the scorecard. Please write the whole answer in British English.
