I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of practical epistemology and social epistemology, and I'm trying to work out whether the Iraq WMD episode is a good test case for pragmatic encroachment or whether I'm forcing it. Help me think this through.

Quick facts so we're on the same page: in 2002-03 the US and UK governments claimed to know that Saddam had active WMD programs and stockpiles. The public case was Colin Powell's UN presentation (satellite imagery, intercepted communications, the aluminum tubes that were supposed to be centrifuge parts, accounts from defectors — most famously the source later known as Curveball — and drawings of mobile bioweapons labs). The internal record was messier: some analysts, e.g. at the Energy Department, thought the tubes were probably for conventional rockets, and a few people raised doubts about Curveball's reliability, but those doubts never made it into the top-level claims. After the invasion the Iraq Survey Group found no stockpiles and no active program, so the "knowledge" turned out to be flat false. I'm not trying to adjudicate who lied — I want the epistemology.

The theory: pragmatic encroachment (Fantl & McGrath's version, or Jason Stanley's) says whether a subject knows that p depends partly on what's at stake for them in acting on p and on their practical options. The standard intuition pump is the bank cases: same evidence, different costs for being wrong, and we're inclined to attribute knowledge in the low-stakes case but not the high-stakes one. Contextualism (DeRose and others) instead says the truth of "S knows that p" shifts with the attributor's conversational context, e.g. which error-possibilities have been made salient.

So the naive application: the stakes couldn't have been higher — a war. Hence the bar for the administration knowing there were WMDs should have been about as high as bars get, and by the encroachment lights they plainly didn't clear it, so the "we know" claims were false at the time, not just falsified later. That's the tidy story I want to complicate, and here's where I'm stuck:

1. Who is the knowing subject? Powell said "we know," but Powell himself hadn't seen most of the raw intelligence — he was relying on the system. The encroachment literature is almost entirely about individuals; does stakes-sensitivity apply to a distributed institutional believer whose "evidence" is whatever survives an aggregation process that has already been shaped by the very decision it's supposed to inform? It looks like the practical interests operated backwards: instead of high stakes raising the evidential bar, the desire for the case for war lowered what counted as sufficient — the analysts' doubts got filtered out exactly where the stakes were highest. Is that just ordinary motivated reasoning that encroachment never claimed to model, or is it a counterexample to the idea that stakes and evidential standards move together?

2. The contextualist angle. Same speakers, same content, different audieces: the internal briefings (the slam-dunk style assurances to the president), the UN presentation where the audience was adversarial, and the domestic speeches where the context was a rally. If knowledge attributions shift with the attributor's context, are the UN and domestic attributions even supposed to have the same truth value? And the relevant-alternatives thing: the alternative "our main defector is a fabricator" was in some sense always live, but the conversational context treated it as not worth considering. Did the context make that alternative irrelevant, or was the context engineered to? Because if a motivated speaker can engineer the context to lower the standards, contextualism seems to be describing a lever, not an epistemic fact.

3. What I want in the end is the both-directions analysis: where each view illuminates the episode and where it goes silent rather than being forced to speak. My working hypothesis is that encroachment goes silent because the case involves institutional aggregation and chains of testimony, which its individualist machinery wasn't built for, and contextualism goes silent (or worse) because the interesting question is whether the attributions were true, and contextualism can look like it's changing the subject to how talk of knowledge is properly used. But tell me honestly if you think the episode does more damage than that — e.g., whether it shows that stakes don't track evidential standards at all in institutional settings, or whether I'd be better off analyzing the whole thing as a case about group knowledge, or about the dismissal of the dissenting analysts, rather than about either of these views. And if you think there's a cleaner real episode for stress-testing encroachment (something where the stakes clearly moved and the standards visibly moved with or against them), I'd take that too.
