Beat reporter who migrated to the ideas desk here, midway through a long piece on why publics in different countries complied with pandemic rules so differently, and before I write the framing section I want a consistency check on the package of views I've been writing from for years. Here it is: (1) I think culture genuinely explains a lot of cross-national behavioral differences — it's not just economics or institutions, some peoples really do have different default levels of deference, fatalism, risk appetite; (2) I think "national character," meaning stable average psychological traits of a nation's people, is a basically discredited idea and I'd never let the phrase into a piece; (3) I've reported the stat that personality variation within any country swamps the variation between country averages, and I beleive it; (4) as a matter of professional ethics I refuse to generalize about peoples in my copy — no "the French are like this" — even in analysis where the generalization seems to be doing real explanatory work; (5) I treat the fact that immigrant kids' habits converge to the host country's norms within a generation or so as one of the strongest arguments against biological explanations of national differences, and I lean on it a lot.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which ones only look like they conflict, and what's the cheapest fix for each? I don't want a history lesson on national character studies — I want an audit of my set, with the tensions ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad, and for each one a note on what giving in would cost me editorially. And please tell me what would change your ranking: what argument or piece of evidence would make a tension you rate as mild look serious, or vice versa, since I suspect my own sense of where the problem is is skewed by which of these views I'm most attached to.
