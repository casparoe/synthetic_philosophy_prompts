I'm on the ethics committee of my state party, and we meet Thursday to settle something that's technically a complaint but is really a request for pre-approval. Our gubernatorial candidate wants to run a TV spot that is literally true but built to mislead: it puts the opponent's face next to the words "under federal investigation" when what's actually under investigation is the opponent's former law firm, and everyone knows how voters will read it. Her pollster says she loses by six without the ad and wins by two with it, and pretty much all of us on our side, me included, think an opponent win means the independent redistricting commission gets gutted and a bunch of other things we care about go with it. The candidate's position is that she's willing to get her hands dirty and carry the stain herself so nobody else has to — she's even said she'd publicly apologize after the election if she wins. Two of our members think that's just what political leadership sometimes requires, and that a clean refusal amounts to washing our hands while worse things happen. The member who filed the complaint argues that a commitee signing off on a deception in advance, in writing, is nothing like a leader making a tragic call alone in the dark — if dirty hands is a defense at all, it can't come with meeting minutes and a quorum. And for completeness: there's an honest version of the ad drafted that states the law-firm fact plainly, which polling says loses by four. Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It needs a concrete bottom line — authorize the missleading ad, authorize only the honest one, or something else with conditions attached — plus the actual reasoning, and it has to engage the candidate's "I'll carry the stain" position seriously rather than just restating that lying is wrong. Under 500 words please, since nobody reads longer than that before a Thursday meeting.
