I am writing a dissertation prospectus in democratic theory on the boundary problem: the question of who belongs to the demos entitled to make a given collective decision. Rather than adding yet another rival principle to all-affected interests and all-subjected-to-coercion, my half-formed idea is that the familiar objections to those principles — especially the regress objection, that applying any boundary principle already presupposes a demos competent to apply it — trade on conflating two questions: who is a member of a decision-making body, and on which occasions that body may decide. In law, standing answers the second question without requiring a prior adjudication of the first for each claimant; I want to examine whether a standing model of demos membership, on which membership is conferred decision by decision under rules justified impersonally, dissolves the regress or merely relocates it. Please turn this into a prospectus of roughly 1200 words. It should state the research question precisely; explain why the existing positions leave it open, including the skeptical view that boundary-drawing is unavoidably arbitrary; describe the method chapter by chapter, saying specifically how I would distinguish genuine dissolution of the regress from its mere relocation; state what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure — for instance, if the standing model proves to be a notational variant of the coercion principle, or if impersonal justification of the standing rules turns out to be unavailable without presupposing a demos; and give an honest assessment of which parts risk bloating beyond dissertation scope. Do not include a bibliography; I will supply citations myself.
