I run a little weekly discussion group at our senior center — mostly retired folks, sharp as anything but zero philosophy background. I got into Indian philosophy on my own a few years back after my yoga teacher wouldn't stop going on about it, and lately I've been chewing on Nyāya epistemology, the whole thing about pramāṇas, i.e. the different sources of right knowledge. Next month I want to do one session on it, and this time I want to actually plan it instead of rambling like I did with the Vedanta session (never again).

So don't write the session for me — help me plan it. Here's what I keep going back and forth on:

Order. Do I start with perception and climb up to inference, or do I start with the big framing move first — that Nyāya thinks knowledge comes in countable kinds, and that the count itself is the claim — and only then fill in the kinds? I feel like the second one is what makes it feel like a real system and not just a list, but I might be wrong about what has to come first. Also, where does comparison fit, that one where you learn what an unfamiliar animal is by being told it resembles one you know (the wild-cow thing)? Is that worth a slot for this crowd or is it just a curiosity I like?

Carrying example. I refuse to use smoke means fire, everyone has heard it a thousand times. I was thinking of using my pressure cooker — the whistle tells me the food's done, and I can walk the group through which pramāṇa is doing the work at each step (seeing the steam, hearing the whistle, inferring from past dinners). Would that one example carry the whole session, or does it need something to cover the parts it can't?

Misconceptions. My guesses: (1) they'll hear "valid knowledge" and think it just means true, (2) they'll think the pramāṇa list is just the five senses relabeled, (3) someone will ask about hallucination — if perception is on the list, doesn't that sink it? Where exactly in the sequence should I head each one off — and is there a fourth one I'm not seeing coming? Related: for inference, do I show them the full five-step schema Nyāya uses with all that repetition at the start, or will they just think it's a syllogism with extra steps and tune out?

What to flat out leave out. I assume the debate about whether a pramāṇa's validity proves itself, and probably the whole God-as-cause part? Tell me if I'm wrong.

And finally: one question I can end the session with that would show me they actually got it — not just that they sat there politely for an hour.
