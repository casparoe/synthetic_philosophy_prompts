I'm a third-year PhD student in moral philosophy, working on a chapter about objections to aggregation, and I've become uncomfortably aware that my department splits on the utility monster in a way that doesn't track the usual demographic lines — the two people whose judgment I most trust sit at opposite extremes over whether the objection, if it works, refutes total utilitarianism or merely dresses up a distributional intuition that also threatens their own preferred theories. So I'd like your credence on a precisely stated proposition, decomposed into the sub-questions it actually depends on.

Here is the proposition: if a utility monster — a being such that maximizing total welfare requires directing essentially all available resources toward it, with everyone else left near the zero baseline — is metaphysically and psychologically possible, then classical hedonistic total utilitarianism is false, because a correct moral theory could not entail that we ought to sacrifice everyone else's welfare for it. My own credence in the conjunction, understanding it as requiring both the possibility of the monster and the falsity of the verdict that we should serve it, is roughly 0.45; a colleague whose opinion I respect is at 0.85.

Give me your number, but more importantly decompose it. The terms I have in mind are roughly these, though I'd welcome corrections to the carving: the probability that interpersonal cardinal comparison of wellbeing is coherent enough for the monster scenario to be well-formed at all, rather than an artifact of treating utils as a substance; the probability that the monster is genuinely possible given the best theory of wellbeing, since it seems to matter a lot whether we picture an intensity monster, whose single experiences are enormously more valuable per episode than ours, or a volume monster — a vast AI, say, or a billion barely-superhuman minds — whose advantage comes from having vastly more episodes of merely ordinary quality, in which case the objection starts to look structurally like a mirror image of the repugnant conclusion rather than a distinct one; the probability that the utilitarian's standard escape, namely that a criterion of right action is not refuted by cases that cannot arise, is principled rather than ad hoc, given that monsters of the volume kind seem merely unlikely rather than impossible; and the probability that the intuition pumping against the monster is specific to unweighted aggregation, such that even a prioritarian or egalitarian-weighted view would face a weakened version of the same verdict and the objection loses its dialectical point. Assign a rough probability to each term with one line of justification, show how they combine into your overall figure, and then tell me which term you are least confident in and what argument or thought experiment would move it most. If you think my colleague and I are most likely disagreeing about a term I haven't listed — for instance, whether the possibility of the monster is doing independent work or is parasitic on one's theory of value — say so, because that's the disagreement I actually need to locate.
