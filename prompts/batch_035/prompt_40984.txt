The question I set was this: "The simple conditional analysis of dispositions says that an object is disposed to give response r to stimulus s if and only if, were it to undergo s, it would give r — so a glass is fragile iff it would break if struck. Now consider two cases. (1) Martin's electro-fink: a fragile glass has a device attached which, were the glass ever struck, would instantly alter its molecular structure so that it is no longer fragile, doing this before the glass could break. (2) Bird's packing case: a fragile glass is surrounded by styrofoam; struck, it does not break, but the glass itself is unchanged and remains fragile throughout. (a) Explain the difference between a fink and a mask. (b) Explain why each kind of case threatens the simple conditional analysis. (c) How did Lewis amend the analysis in response to finks, and does his amendment also take care of masks?"

And here's what the student wrote:

"The simple conditional analysis identifies being fragile with breaking-if-struck, so a counterexample needs the left-hand side true and the right-hand side false. Martin's electro-fink provides exactly this: the glass is fragile, but it is false that it would break if struck, because the fink would strip away the fragility the instant striking begins. In effect the fink does its work in advance: from the moment it is attached, the glass has already lost its fragility — the fink just hasn't physically rewritten the molecular structure yet — and that is why the conditional comes out false while the glass is still fragile. Masks differ from finks mainly in timing. A fink acts before the stimulus, removing the disposition ahead of time; a mask acts after the stimulus, in the interval between it and the manifestation. On this way of seeing things, Bird's styrofoam is really a slow-acting fink: like Martin's device, it would ensure non-breaking upon being struck, it just operates without touching the glass's molecular structure. Both cases are therefore counterexamples to the simple analysis: in each, the glass is genuinely fragile yet would not break if struck. Lewis's response was to amend the counterfactual used in the analysis: when we evaluate whether the glass would break if struck, we hold fixed everything about the actual world up to the moment of the strike. That way the fink's fragility-stripping never makes it into the closest worlds, so the glass breaks there and counts as fragile, as it should. And since the styrofoam likewise doesn't touch the glass's intrinsic properties, the very same amendment handles the mask case — in the closest worlds where the glass is struck, it is no longer packed, so it breaks there too, which is the right verdict."
