As a science and ideas journalist, I've been commissioned to build an interactive feature for our magazine's website: one of those online self-assessment widgets, this one tied to a long-form piece on the science of emotion. The conceit is that readers arrive at the article already knowing roughly which theory of emotion they intuitively hold — cognitive, perceptual, pure feeling, constructivist — rather than discovering it three thousand words in. I've asked our web developer for a decision-tree format, since the widget can then display each question's answer options as clickable cards and route readers along branches, which tests far better than a point-tally.

What I need from you is the content of that tree. Please design a decision tree whose branch points are questions a reader can answer from ordinary experience, without knowing any of the technical vocabulary — no question like "do you believe emotions are appraisals?" Instead, each node should be a concrete case or thought experiment on which the theories genuinely come apart. I'd like you to construct at least some of the cases yourself rather than only recycling the famous ones, so please devise a few novel scenarios — for instance, something involving a medication that suppresses the bodily response while leaving the appraisal intact, or an emotion a person has toward a situation they fully know to be harmless, or a mood shift produced by blood sugar rather than by anything the person thinks about. Aim for roughly eight to twelve branch questions and keep the tree answerable by a general reader in four minutes.

The leaves should be the major positions: theories on which emotions are evaluative judgments, perceptual or quasi-perceptual experiences of one's own bodily state or of the world, essentially non-cognitive feelings, and constructivist views on which the categories themselves are assembled. Please locate each position in the logical space generated by your questions rather than the reverse — that is, first fix the branch questions, then say which answer combinations lead to which leaf — and explicitly note any combination of answers your tree cannot separate (for example, whatever real differences exist between feeling theories and bodily-perception theories, if your cases don't force them apart). I also want a short section, maybe two hundred words, on the unoccupied corners: which answer combinations correspond to no standard theory, and your best guess as to whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has bothered. Since our editors insist the feature be accurate even where it is blunt, please flag any question you think unfairly stacks the deck against a particular theory, so we can decide whether to keep it.
