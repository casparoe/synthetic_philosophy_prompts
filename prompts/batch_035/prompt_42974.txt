I'm a graduate student in a department where a mereology reading group has been meeting for over a year, and the debate over composition has now stalled in a way I find genuinely puzzling. I don't want a verdict on which side is right; I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is.

The object is the special composition question: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? Three positions are represented in the room. One member is a universalist: any two or more non-overlapping things compose something, so there is a fusion of my left shoe, the Moon, and every electron in the Andromeda galaxy. Another is a nihilist: composition never occurs; only simples exist, and when I say there is a table I speak loosely — strictly, there are simples arranged table-wise. Two others are moderates, one defending van Inwagen's own answer that composition occurs exactly when the things jointly constitute a life, the other sympathetic to some criterion of causal integration.

The arguments are old and everyone can recite them. The nihilist presses the universalist with the scattered fusions — the fusion of a shoe, a snowball, and the northern half of the Eiffel Tower — not by denying that it exists but by asking what explanatory work positing it does. The universalist replies that any restriction on composition draws a sharp line between things that compose and things that merely sit near each other, and that wherever that line is drawn, composition would be vague — and it cannot be vague whether something exists. The moderates keep refining cutoffs that nobody else accepts and are squeezed from both directions. Nobody has moved in months.

Here is what makes me suspect the diagnosis is not the obvious one. At some point, for the sake of argument, the group conceded the paraphrase thesis: that every sentence the nihilist accepts can be systematically translated into a sentence the universalist accepts, and vice versa, with no practical loss in explanation, prediction, or ordinary life. By the usual standards, that would make the dispute merely verbal. Yet nobody felt the dispute was over. The universalist insisted something substantive survives translation: the existential quantifier must mean something, and one of the two available meanings carves nature at the joints, so there is a fact of the matter about which quantifier is the correct one, and it is the universalist's. The nihilist, joined by one moderate adopting a quantifier-variance line, answered that joint-carving is precisely the sort of property that cannot be tested, stipulated, or argued for except by more philosophy of exactly the kind that has just stalled. A third member holds that the residual dispute is really metaontology in disguise and should be settled, or dissolved, up there rather than down here in the fight about tables.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two different questions traveling under one heading? If verbal, why does it not feel verbal to the participants, and is that feeling evidence of anything, or just attachment to their own sentences? If substantive, what fact could make it so given the shared paraphrases — a fact about joint-carving, about fundamentality, about what a quantifier must be like to do its job? What, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result, a criterion nobody has tried, an empirical discovery, or an agreement to speak differently? I have my own working hypothesis — that the residual disagreement is not about composition at all but about the epistemology of metaphysical structure, that is, about whether a metaphysical posit can be vindicated by theoretical virtues alone or requires contact with something independent of them — but I want it tested, not endorsed. Please ground the diagnosis in what each side actually maintains, and be honest about whether anything could move this debate, or whether the stalemate is itself the finding.

Please write your answer in American English.
