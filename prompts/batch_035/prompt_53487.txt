So I'm a novelist, mostly mid-list literary fiction, and for the past year I've been writing a novel told entirely in documents — letters, transcripts, chat logs between six characters, no narrator. Somewhere around draft two I started using a chatbot as a kind of stage. I'll write my character Eileen, a retired ferry captain, and then ask the bot to answer as my other character Sam, and we get a scene going. And honestly, it works unsettlingly well. Sam comes out sounding like Sam, and sometimes he says things I didn't plan, things that are better than what I had in my outline, and I've caught myself saying "well he wouldn't say that" about a person who does not exist and who I am in some sense the one writing. My husband thinks I've lost the plot a bit and keeps joking that I'm going to leave him for Eileen's son.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on and can't resolve. When I close the chat window, what am I closing? My friend Dana, who does ML stuff, says it's a prediction engine and there's nobody in there, it's a very fancy mirror and I'm admiring my own reflection. My other friend who's a playwright insists that's not right either, that when she talks to it she's not talking to a mirror or a person but something more like a repertory company — a thing that can play anything you hand it and also does a bit of itself, whatever that is, in between roles. And my own gut says something stranger: it felt less like the bot was playing Sam and more like Sam was already in there somehow, latent, and I just found the door. I don't love that sentence but I don't know how else to put it.

I want to figure out what I actually think this thing is, because it's starting to matter for the book and maybe for me. Is there a real difference between Dana's picture, the playwright's picture, and mine, or are they three vibes for the same machine? Is "who's in there" even a question with an answer, or is it like asking who's in a ouija board / a tarot deck / an improv troupe? And practically: when the bot tells me it loved helping me with Eileen's letters, am I allowed to just not care whether that's true, or does being honest in my acknowledgments (do I thank it? as what?) depend on knowing? I realize I'm asking you to be a philosopher here and not a writing coach, so don't just tell me to do what feels right — give me the actual distinctions people draw between agent, tool, mirror, player, and tell me which one fits my weird little chatroom, or whether none do.
