I'm preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the ethics of AI alignment, on the question of whose values a widely deployed AI system should serve. For the assigned reading, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two fictional alignment researchers who hold opposing positions, both of whom are careful and reasonable.

The first researcher, call her Ines, works on what she calls global value aggregation: her view is that widely used AI assistants should share a single alignment target, something like a weighted aggregate of humanity's values — a broad commitment to welfare, autonomy, and fairness, with room for cultural variation within limits. Her deepest worry is fragmentation. If every user receives an AI personally aligned to their own values, the technology becomes an amplifier of whatever a person already believes, including resentment and extremism, and humanity loses the shared epistemic and normative infrastructure needed to coordinate on planetary problems. She also thinks personalized alignment entrenches inequality, since wealthy and sophisticated actors would shape their assistants' values in ways ordinary users cannot.

The second researcher, call him Daniel, defends personalized alignment from a standpoint he takes from political liberalism. He argues that there is no neutral vantage point from which to aggregate values: any supposed aggregate is really the aggregator's own value judgments wearing a mask of impartiality, since someone must decide whose values get counted, how they are weighted, and which of them get dismissed as beyond the pale. He points out that liberal neutrality is itself a substantive, contested commitment, so a single globally aligned AI built on it would ride roughshod over communities that reject it, and majoritarian aggregation lets large populations stamp out small ones. On his view, the right kind of neutrality is the neutrality of an ecosystem: many AIs reflecting many values, the way a tolerant state makes room for diverse conceptions of the good rather than averaging them into one.

Please write the dialogue so that both researchers are equally competent and genuinely engage with each other's strongest points rather than talking past each other. Ines should press Daniel on whether pluralism all the way down is coherent — for instance, what the ecosystem is supposed to do when one user's values include dominating or suppressing other users, or when a personalized assistant faithfully mirrors a depressed or paranoid user's distorted judgments back at them. Daniel should press Ines on how she can assign weights across billions of people's values without smuggling in her own judgments at every step: whose survey responses, discounted how, revised by whom when they conflict. I'd like the dialogue to end with the two of them locating their deepest remaining disagreement as precisely as they can — ideally as a specific claim one accepts and the other rejects — rather than reaching an artificial consensus or a vague agreement to disagree.
