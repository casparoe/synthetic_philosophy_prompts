I teach intro to free will every fall, and this semester it finally hit me that my own gut verdicts on the responsibility cases don't obviously add up to any one theory, and I'd rather sort that out before I stand in front of thirty undergrads pretending the reading assignments settled it. So here's my pattern of verdicts, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, captures it, and which verdicts I have to give up to get a clean one.

The drunk driver who kills a pedestrian is more culpable than the identical drunk driver who gets home with the same bad luck of no one being on that road — my gut insists on that even though I know the luck objection is coming. The person who develops a brain tumor that produces violent impulses and hurts someone: not responsible, full stop. Someone raised in horrific circumstances who mugs a tourist: diminished but not off the hook — and I can't for the life of me say why deprived circumstances only discount while a tumor zeroes out, since neither causal history was under the person's control. Someone who acts with a gun to their head: not responsible, even though they did choose, which seems to tell me that mere choice can't be the whole story. A psychopath who genuinely cannot grasp that others' suffering counts: I keep wanting to say he's not responsible, but I also don't want him walking around, and I suspect my verdict there is contaminated by practical worry rather than any actual judgement about desert.

So: is there a principle — some capacity-based thing, reasons-responsiveness, control over character, whatever — that gets all or nearly all of these? Say precisely what each clause of your principle is doing for which verdict, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save, since I suspect the drunk-driving pair and the tumor/upbringing pair can't both come out clean. Then tell me what the principle predicts on a case I deliberately didn't list: someone who knowingly takes a drug that makes them reckless, and years later, still on it, badly injures a friend — responsible for what exactly, and does your answer change if the same recklessness instead crept in gradually from choices that were fully his? I'd also like one paragraph on whether my whole pattern of verdicts is quietly compatibilist or not, since I honestly can't tell and my students ask every year.
