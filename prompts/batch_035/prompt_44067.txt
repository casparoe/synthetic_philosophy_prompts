Third-year PhD student in environmental history here, and I want your read on a fight that's been going in circles for as long as I've been in the field, because I've started to suspect it isn't really an empirical disagreement at all.

Rough shape of it. On one side you've got the big-picture people — the guns-germs-and-steel guy, the Little Ice Age people, the folks who wrote the books on the Tambora eruption of 1815 and the year without a summer. For them nonhumans are basically the lead actors of history: a microbe decides who colonizes whom, a volcano deletes a growing season and argubly a few political orders along with it. On the other side, the social and cultural historians, who say this is determinism with a fresh coat of paint: it flattens people into weather gauges, and anyway when you zoom in on an actual catastrophe the interesting variance is institutional. The potato blight of the 1840s hit most of northern Europe, but the famine happened where a particular political economy was, not where the blight was. Same pathogen, wildly different death tolls depending on who governed where.

And then there's the third position where I think the whole thing actually stalls, which is nonhumans as actors in the network-theory sense — a glacier or a germ gets agency without determining anything, it just participates. Everyone can live with that formulation and nobody is moved by it, which makes me suspect it's doing diplomatic work rather than explanatory work: one crowd hears the word actor as causally decisive, the other hears it as attention-worthy, and the word papers over the gap.

My hypothesis, and please tell me if its stupid: the two sides aren't disagreeing about causes at all, they disagree about the unit of explanation. The environmental-causes crowd is answering why did the world get shaped this way at the century scale, and their critics keep hearing that as an answer to why did things go the way they went in one particular county in 1846 — a scale at which institutions really do absorb most of the climate signal. So the same claim comes out true and false depending on which question you meant, and the fight under the fight is about which question history is for. If that's right, then no amount of new climate reconstruction data will ever move anyone, because neither side is wrong about the data; they're wrong to think they share a question.

So what I want from you: is this actually a substantive disagreement hiding somewhere (and if so where), or a scale mismatch, or a covert fight about the discipline's identity? What, if anything, would count as progress — would a shared protocol for when a climate shock counts as a cause versus a background condition do it, or is the protocol itself the thing people would fight over? And be honest with me about whether the nonhuman-agency language is earning its keep or just letting everyone keep publishing.
