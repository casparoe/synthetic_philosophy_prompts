Each year I teach a graduate seminar that pairs statistical inference with practical reason, and I like to close the term with a staged debate about Simpson's paradox — not the arithmetic of the reversal, which my students can already do, but the genuinely contested question of what a decision-maker should do when two inconsistent tables sit in front of them. Please write the dialogue for this year's seminar.

The setting is a hospital coffee room, an hour before a formulary committee must decide whether the standard drug for a chronic condition (call it tradamil) should be replaced by a newer one (call it novoril). Two experts have been deputed to recommend. Dr Kaur is an epidemiologist who has spent her career on stratified analysis; she holds that the marginal comparison is a weighted average whose weights reflect who happened to be given what, so it answers a question about past allocation rather than about the drug, and that stratification is how one recovers the question that matters. Dr Millar is a trialist who holds that a well-conducted marginal comparison is the only thing a committee is entitled to act on, and that stratification — especially stratification chosen after the data have been seen — is where mischief enters; a subgroup can be carved to reverse almost anything, and he has watched it happen.

They have two exhibits on the table. The first is an observational registry, stratified by baseline severity, recorded before treatment began. Among mild cases, tradamil patients recovered 90 out of 100 and novoril patients 46 out of 50; among severe cases, tradamil 40 out of 100 and novoril 184 out of 400. In the registry as a whole, tradamil looks clearly better — 130 out of 200 against 230 out of 450 — because the new drug was given almost exclusively to severe patients; yet novoril does better in each severity group taken separately. The second exhibit is a randomised trial. In the trial as a whole, novoril does better: 192 out of 400 against 63 out of 150. But when the same trial is stratified by whether blood pressure was controlled one month into treatment — a measurement taken after treatment began — tradamil comes out ahead in both strata: among patients whose pressure was controlled, 80 per cent on tradamil against 76 per cent on novoril; among patients whose pressure was not controlled, 23 per cent against 20 per cent. Novoril achieves blood pressure control in half its patients, tradamil in only a third.

Neither character is a strawman, and neither should be made to lecture the other about what Simpson's paradox is — both know it cold, and they should argue about what follows from these particular tables, not re-derive the textbook. The exchange should be genuine back-and-forth: each responds to the other's strongest formulation, not to a weakened version, and each should land at least one blow the other has visible difficulty answering. Each should also concede something concrete before the end. The argument must work through both exhibits and must not let either party off without explaining what, if anything, distinguishes the two stratifications — the one variable measured before treatment, the other after — since Dr Kaur's instincts are vindicated by the registry and embarrassed by the trial's blood-pressure table, while Dr Millar's are vindicated by the trial's margin and embarrassed by the registry's. At some point they should find themselves facing the question I most want my students to see: whether any rule statable purely in terms of the tables — in terms of the data alone — could settle which table to act on, or whether the answer always depends on something outside the tables. They may invoke familiar touchstones such as the sure-thing principle or the classic reversal examples from admissions data and the kidney-stone literature, but they should argue, not cite. The ending should be honest: either a narrow, hard-earned convergence with the residue of disagreement stated exactly, or a cleanly mapped residual dispute — no forced draw, no cheap victory for either side.

Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words. Continuous prose only, please: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, with speaker tags in plain text and at most a sentence or two of scene-setting.
