As a professor of metaphysics preparing a graduate seminar on persistence through time, I would like your help in stress-testing a line of argument that I suspect is overextended in the literature. Please take on the role of a professional metaphysician with deep expertise in the debate between endurantism and perdurantism, and think carefully before committing to a position; I would ask you to begin with a short brainstorm of the main replies open to the endurantist, and then develop your answer from there. Please note that I am looking for a genuinely argued case, not a survey: I already know the standard textbook exposition, and so do my students.

The specific task is this: argue that David Lewis's argument from temporary intrinsics, however effective it may be against certain unsophisticated versions of endurantism, does not by itself establish perdurantism. Lewis observed that a single object can be bent at one time and straight at another, and urged that if one and the same wholly present object had both properties simpliciter, a contradiction would threaten — his preferred resolution being that the bent thing and the straight thing are distinct temporal parts of a four-dimensional whole. But the endurantist has familiar replies in the neighbourhood: she may treat "is bent" as elliptical for "is bent at t," so that instantiation is always time-indexed and no object ever has contrary properties simpliciter; she may adopt an adverbialist line on which objects have their properties in temporally modified ways; or she may couple endurantism with a presentist or growing-block ontology on which only some tensed facts obtain. Your argument should give the strongest case that, once these replies are properly accommodated, the argument from temporary intrinsics at most shows that the endurantist must enrich her ideology — admitting times or temporal modifiers into her ontology of instantiation — rather than that she must accept temporal parts at all. A student in my seminar last week insisted that the argument is a straightforward reductio of endurantism, and while I am confident she is moving too fast, I want a carefully constructed case showing exactly where the inference from "the endurantist needs extra apparatus" to "the endurantist is wrong" breaks down. Please also make clear whether you are defending the stronger claim that the argument is unsound or the weaker claim that it is inconclusive, and indicate which you find more defensible and why.

Finally, please compose your entire answer in British English, and structure it so that it could be circulated to the seminar as a short discussion piece: a brief brainstorm, followed by the argument proper, followed by a concise statement of the conclusion and its limitations.
