I've been arguing with a colleague about what Avicenna's flying man argument actually establishes, and I'd like your honest credence rather than a survey of positions. The proposition, stated so it has a truth value: in its strongest defensible reconstruction, the flying man establishes that a subject's awareness of their own existence is logically independent of any awareness of the body at all — not merely independent of external sense perception. My colleague's number is around 0.65; mine is closer to 0.25. Give me your own credence, but what I really want is the decomposition that produces it.

I'd expect the decomposition to include at least the following terms, though you may restructure or add to them. First, the probability that the thought experiment is even coherent: whether Avicenna can legitimately stipulate a fully formed adult intellect created at an instant, suspended in air with limbs not touching, and ask what he is aware of — or whether the stipulation silently presupposes a subject already constituted by a history of embodiment, so that the scenario cannot be run as described. Second, the probability that, granting coherence, the case establishes the weaker conclusion: that self-awareness does not depend on current external perception, which nearly everyone concedes and which the argument does deliver fairly cleanly. Third, the probability that this weaker conclusion, together with anything else Avicenna legitimately has available, entails the stronger body-independent conclusion he wants for his incorporeality thesis. Fourth, whatever weight you give to the suspicion that the intuition pump works only because we are invited to project ourselves — creatures with a lifetime of bodily experience — into the flying man's position, so that the self-awareness we confidently attribute to him is memory-laden rather than pristinely pre-reflective.

Two constraints on how you reason. Don't simply recite the modern literature's verdicts — tell me which contemporary considerations actually bear on the decomposition and which are atmosphere: for instance, whether the notion of immunity to error through misidentification supports the flying man's conclusion or only a much thinner claim about the grammar of first-person reference; whether the clinical reports of patients with near-total deafferentation, who describe the self as threatened with dissolution when bodily feedback vanishes, count as evidence against the body-independence thesis or as irrelevant because those patients have expectations the newly created flying man lacks; and whether the argument is best read as a move in the same dialectic as the Indian debates over whether consciousness persists in dreamless sleep, where the Advaitin and the Buddhist part ways on exactly the question Avicenna needs settled. Second, tell me where you think my colleague and I most likely disagree: which single term of the decomposition does the most work in separating a credence of 0.25 from one of 0.65, and is the disagreement really about the argument, or about background assumptions regarding what a thought experiment is entitled to presuppose.

Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what kind of argument or evidence — not citations, arguments — would move that term the most.

Please write your answer as continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, no tables — just paragraphs, as one would write a long, careful letter.
