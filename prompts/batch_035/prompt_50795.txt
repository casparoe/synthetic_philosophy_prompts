I run a reading group on philosophy of mind for a mix of cognitive science grad students and ML engineers, about 12 people, 90 minutes, no lecture component. We've just worked through the classic frame problem literature (Dennett's robot story, Fodor's claim that the problem shows something deep about minds and not just about engineering). Next session I want them to actually *feel* the problem instead of nodding along to it, because a couple of the ML folks are pretty sure it's just an outdated worry that scaling took care of.

Design an in-class game for that. My vague idea is something like this, but improve it or replace it if you have something better: participants take turns being a planner agent that has to complete some mundane errand in a shared fictional enviroment (make tea, mail a package, get to the airport), and everyone else plays "the world" -- they earn points for raising consequences that genuinely invalidate the current plan and force a replan. What I want to happen is that the planners discover two things on their own: exhaustive checking is hopeless, because the consequence-raisers should never run out of ammunition; and blanket ignoring doesn't work either, because some raised consequences really do sink the plan. So the actual question becomes what licenses treating a fact as irrelevant -- and everyone will find they can *behave* as if they have a relevance rule without being able to state one. That gap is the whole point of the session.

Please give me full rules, materials, a point scheme that doesn't need real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto parts of the philosophical debate: the difference between the prediction version of the problem and the relevance version, why Fodor thought it cut against computationalism rather than just being a to-do item for engineers, and -- this is the part I care most about -- where the ML folks' instinct that "the policy just implicitly learned what matters from the training distribution" is a substantive move inside that debate rather than an exit from it.

Two things I want you to be upfront about. First, what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it: I'm guessing the consequence-raisers will escalate into full surrealism and the enviroment will stop feeling like something with physics, at which point being "genuinely invalidated" stops meaning anything -- but you tell me what the actual failure modes are and how to patch them (judges? a realism quota? card decks of pre-checked consequences?). Second, what the activity structurally cannot reproduce: my worry is that the committee format turns a single agent's real-time problem into social exhaustion, which might make the frame problem feel like a fact about committees. Related to that, tell me which version of the frame problem the game actually targets -- the logic/AI formalization, the cognitive-science one about how mortals like us cope, or the loose modern ML usage -- since they're not the same thing and I suspect any game quietly has to pick one. If the game only really delivers one of them, say so, and tell me what I have to supply in the debrief that no amount of clever rule design can.
