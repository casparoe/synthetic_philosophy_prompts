Over the last few years I have kept a private ledger of my own snap judgments about when a belief is true, when it becomes true, and when there is no truth of the matter at all. I have come to admit that these judgments do not all sit comfortably together, and I would like your help deciding what, if anything, they add up to. Please give me a theory of truth — a single principle if one can be had, otherwise a small set of candidates with a principled choice between them — that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. State it precisely, clause by clause, saying which of my cases motivates each clause; tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why; and tell me what it predicts on cases I have not listed. I have read a little Peirce and a little James, and I suspect that my two minds are roughly their two minds — Peirce, as I understand him, identifying the truth of a belief with what an ideal community of inquirers would eventually converge on if inquiry were pressed indefinitely; James holding that truth happens to a belief, that beliefs become true, are made true by events, and that the long-run satisfactoriness of a belief in the course of experience is of its essence. But I want the reasoning worked out on the cases, not deferred to the authorities.

My verdicts, stated as honestly as I can:

1. Goldbach's conjecture has been undecided for nearly three centuries and may never be decided. My verdict: it is true or false right now. Its truth value does not wait on the proof; if no one ever settles it, it will have been true (or false) all along, unbeknownst to everyone.

2. Whether Julius Caesar scratched his left ear on the morning of the Ides of March, 44 BCE. No record survives, none ever will, and no inquiry, however prolonged, could settle it. Verdict: there is a fact of the matter. Had Caesar himself formed the belief, it would have been true or false then; our permanent ignorance does not suspend the question.

3. Around 1827 a chemist believed that atoms were real physical bodies rather than useful calculating fictions, decades before anything we would now count as evidence. Verdict: his belief was true already in 1827. The later evidence discovered that it was true; it did not make it true.

4. A dying woman believes her estranged daughter still loves her. The belief steadies her final months; she dies before any contact; and in fact the daughter does not love her. Verdict: the belief was false, however much good it did. I am quite firm on this: that a belief satisfies and consoles the believer is nowhere near sufficient for its truth.

5. A religious person believes there is a God and a moral order of the universe. No decisive evidence will ever arrive; no community of inquirers will ever converge on it. Yet the belief is repeatedly vindicated in her experience, her life is in fact different because of it, and she reports the vindication in the way James says religious believers do. Verdict: I want to say this belief can be true — genuinely true, not merely useful — without inquiry ever settling it. I notice this sits badly beside my verdict in 4, and I do not currently have a principled way to hold both.

6. A team walks onto the field certain it will win, and the confidence itself is part of what carries it to victory. Verdict: the belief became true; it was made true by the events, in a way that was not true of it beforehand. Here truth seems to be manufactured rather than found, which fits James and strains Peirce.

7. A diamond is extracted and, before anyone can test it, worn down to nothing in a mill. Was it hard? My verdict: no determinate fact of the matter — nothing in the universe ever hung on the answer, and I do not think there was an answer to be ignorant of. The question merely looked substantive. This jars with 2, where equally nothing ever hung on the answer, yet I insisted on a fact of the matter.

I have tried to distill a tentative principle of my own: a belief is true just in case it would be agreed upon at the limit of a indefinitely prolonged inquiry by a community of investigators — with the rider that the satisfaction that matters to truth is satisfaction in the long run of experience, never the short run. Please test this principle against the cases, repair it where it fails, and tell me honestly whether it can be saved at all, or whether I should abandon it for something else.

Two further requests. First, decide for me between the two paths I can see but cannot walk: a principle that preserves every one of my verdicts at the price of real complexity, or a simpler principle that overturns some of them — and tell me which verdicts go, and why those rather than others. Second, tell me what the resulting view predicts for cases I have not listed: whether the universe will keep expanding forever; whether the number 2 was prime before there were mathematicians; and whether two people exchanging wedding vows, believing their marriage will last, are believing something that is thereby more likely to be true. Finally, say plainly whether the view you land on is closer to Peirce, closer to James, or a genuine hybrid, and what the honest cost of that verdict is.
