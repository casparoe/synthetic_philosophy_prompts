I teach an upper-level seminar in social epistemology — sixteen students, ninety minutes, a flat room with movable chairs — and I want an in-class experiment-cum-game that makes the class feel, rather than merely discuss, the hardest part of the manipulation/persuasion distinction: that it cannot be drawn from the inside, by the person being influenced, because manipulated change of mind is routinely experienced and endorsed as one's own reasoning.

The setup I have in mind is a decision exercise. Half the class plays "clients"; each must rank three fictional student-initiative proposals for a committee vote — I'd like you to invent the three proposals, and make them differ on cost, reach, and reversibility, so there is genuinely something to argue about. The other half plays "advisors," each of whom receives one of three technique cards, deliberately unlabeled. Card one permits only content moves: restating trade-offs in terms of criteria the client has already endorsed. Card two forbids any mention of the proposals' attributes and instead supplies motive-side techniques, spelled out concretely on the card — a small reciprocity move (perform some trivial favor for the client before the main ask), a foot-in-the-door sequence (secure agreement to something small before the substantive request), and an identity move (affirm the client's good judgment as a decider, with no reference to any feature of the options). Card three mixes the two. Clients rank the proposals, receive advice, rank again, and then write one sentence stating the reason for any change, plus a confidence rating that the shift was "their own reasoning." Include a small no-advisor control group so baseline drift is visible.

Please give me the full package: a cover story for the clients (they should not be told the session is about influence — tell me exactly what to tell them it is about, and note that this requires a complete debriefing afterwards, and that this fact is itself instructive: we will have run a mild manipulation in order to study manipulation); the exact text of the three cards; a minute-by-minute timeline; what gets recorded on the board and how to stage the reveal, so the class can see which clients' stated "reasons" were confabulated rather than inherited from their advisor's card; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the three standard criteria — bypassing of reasons, retrospective endorsement, and counterfactual dependence on the technique — showing for each criterion where it catches and where it misses what the students have just done to each other. The intended takeaway, which the debrief should land but not lecture: "it felt like my own decision" is not where the line is.

Also include, frankly: what will predictably go wrong the first time (clients smelling the design and going suspicious; advisors overplaying their cards; the mixed card muddying the clean reveal), and an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce — above all that real manipulation typically concerns matters the target cares about deeply, unfolds over longer timescales, and comes with no scheduled debrief in which the technique is named. Describe all ideas plainly in your own words; no citations and no reading list, I'll supply those myself.
