at my uni's neuro/philosophy reading group we keep coming back to mind uploading, and I want to settle one move that does a ton of work in these arguments. the pro-upload people lean on the Ship of Theseus: if you replace a ship's planks one at a time with identical new ones, it stays the same ship — so if you replace my neurons one at a time with functionally identical silicon (same inputs, same outputs, same timing), there's no step at which the "I" could disappear, and the end result isn't a copy, it's me. they use this to argue that gradual-replacement uploading is survival rather than death-plus-a-copy, and that the teleporter-style horror intuitions are confused. one of the older grad students thinks the analogy is carrying the entire conclusion. her point is that nothing in the ship case corresponds to the stuff we actually care about: a ship has no inside, nobody is riding along AS the ship, there's plausibly no fact of the matter about ship identity beyond convention (and what happens if someone reassembles the old planks into a second ship?), and planks have no experience to preserve. so the ship can't even in principle tell us whether the gradual upload would still be a subject of experience or only a behavioral twin of one.

she also offered a rival analogy I cant stop chewing on: a band where each musician gets replaced, one per week, by a session player who mimics their part perfectly. the audience notices nothing, the recordings are indistinguishable, but ask any ex-member whether it's still their band and you get a fight. meanwhile someone else in the group brought up the thought experiment where consciousness fades out gradually as the chips go in — the claim was this can't happen because you'd never notice the fading, but I lost the thread of why that's supposed to be decisive, or whether it helps the ship people or the band people.

can you audit the analogies for me? I want the correspondence table — what plays the role of the ship, the planks, the reassembled-old-planks ship, the harbor, the convention, the band, the session player, the audience, the recordings — then a clear split between the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion being drawn (survival vs copy, and whether experience rides along) and the ones that are just decorative. then: does the pro-upload conclusion survive once you take the real disanalogies seriously? does the band analogy do any genuine work the ship one doesn't, or is it just trading on prestige-of-continuity feelings? where does the fading-experience argument fit in, if anywhere? and if neither analogy is up to the job, what would a better one look like? please be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the single point in your audit you're least sure about — especially if it's somewhere in the neighborhood of "does functional equivalence settle anything about experience", since that feels like where everything is quietly hanging.
