My brother works as a nurse in an intensive care unit, and for years we have been arguing about when exactly a person dies. I have no academic background in any of this — what follows is what I have worked out from arguing with him and from what I could piece together about how hospitals actually operate — so let me lay out my understanding of the background first, and then ask for what I want.

There are two official ways to be dead. The neurological criterion: irreversible loss of all brain function, including the brainstem, even though a ventilator can keep the heart beating and the blood circulating for a long time afterwards. The circulatory criterion: irreversible cessation of circulation and breathing. What strikes me, and what my brother only half-admits, is that these two don't merely differ — they disagree in both directions. A patient declared brain-dead on a ventilator is dead by the first criterion while having a warm, circulating body by the second. A patient whose heart stops and is not resuscitated is dead by the second criterion within minutes, even though, as my brother has explained, in the organ-donation protocols that wait for circulatory death the surgeons allow only two to five minutes after the heart stops before declaring the patient dead — far too little time for the brain to have lost its functions in any irreversible sense. And then they take that heart, which was judged dead by its circulation, restart it in the recipient, and nobody calls that resurrection. I have come to suspect that "dead" does not pick out one natural boundary line; rather each criterion is right by its own lights, the first really saying that this brain is gone forever and the second really saying that this heart will never beat again on its own — a "won't" rather than a "can't," which my brother tells me the medical literature marks as the difference between permanent and irreversible cessation.

Here is what I want you to construct. A pair of matched hospital scenarios, set in the present day and of the kind that actually occur, in which the two criteria deliver opposite verdicts: each patient is dead by one criterion and not dead by the other, with the direction reversed between them. Constraints: each scenario one paragraph; entirely realistic — no speculative technology, no heads in vats, no cryonics, and no anencephaly or anything else that drags a separate controversy in with it; the two patients matched as closely as you can manage in age, underlying cause, prognosis, and family circumstances, so that the divergence is forced by the definitions rather than smuggled in by making one patient's situation obviously different from the other's; and the verdicts must be generated rather than stipulated — derive them from the definitions each time, showing exactly what the word "irreversible" is doing in each derivation, rather than announcing who is dead and assuming I will agree.

After the two scenarios, I need four things. First, a plain statement of which patient is dead by which criterion and why. Second, identification of the single feature of the pair that is doing the work — what exactly you varied between the two scenarios — and what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Third, the best attack available to a defender of each criterion: the strongest complaint that the pair is unfair to their definition as its own defenders would apply it. Fourth, your honest judgment on whether the pair supports my suspicion or whether there is a reply that preserves the idea of death as one single, criterion-independent fact.

This is for the argument with my brother, so let me say what counts as cheating: a case that merely asserts its verdicts, or one that wins by applying a criterion in a way its own defenders would reject as a straw man rather than the way they actually use it. Begin your reply with the first scenario's opening sentence — no preamble, no restating my setup — and put all of the analysis after the two scenarios.
