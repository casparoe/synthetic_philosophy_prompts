My friend and I got into a two-hour fight about the word "but" (of all things) last night. I said "but" basically means "and" plus a hint of contrast, she said the contrast is just part of the literal meaning, period. Eventually she pulled out the line her pragmatics professor apparently drills into everyone: implicatures can be cancelled, entailments can't. So she said "he's rich but he's happy, and no contrast intended there" and went see, the contrast cancels fine, so it's implicature, then declared victory and went to bed. Felt like a trick at the time but I couldn't say why.

Since then I've been wondering how much work that little slogan is even doing. Like, what does "cancellable" precisely mean here -- that the speaker can tack "and I don't mean to imply X" onto the end of anything? That seems way too easy: I can append "and I don't mean to say X" to basically any utterance, including stuff that is clearly part of what the sentence itself says, since people misdescribe their own utterances all the time. Maybe the real test is whether the cancellation sounds coherent rather than self-contradictory? Coherent to whom, though? And what is the test supposed to be a test OF exactly -- separating implicature from entailment? from conventional implicature? from presupposition? I gather these are different animals and "but" is the classic hard case, which makes me suspect my friend's professor would NOT have been on her side.

So: can you lay out the distinct precise principles that could be hiding behind "implicatures can be cancelled, entailments can't" -- from the weakest/most trivial reading to the strongest -- and for each one say whether it's plausible and whether it would actually get my friend her win about "but"? A menu-style list I can bring back to her, please. And if none of the readings settles our fight, tell me what would settle it, or whether we should just call it a terminological truce and go to bed earlier.
