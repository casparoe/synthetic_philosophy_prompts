In U.S. evidence law (Federal Rule 201 and its state analogues), a judge may take judicial notice of a fact and treat it as established without any proof, provided the fact cannot reasonably be disputed — either because it is generally known in the community, or because it can be accurately and quickly checked against sources no one could reasonably distrust. Once noticed, the matter is closed: the parties may not contest it, and in a civil case the jury must accept it. What epistemology does this practice presuppose? The second prong looks like ordinary fallibilism — trust in checkable sources. But the first prong admits propositions into the record because no reasonable person in the jurisdiction could doubt them, which suggests something else: a class of propositions that are exempt from the demand for justification in the way Wittgenstein's On Certainty describes hinges — they stand fast, not as the results of testing, but as the unargued background against which disputes are adjudicated. Is that the right reconstruction, or is judicial notice better explained by something non-epistemic, like conserving scarce trial resources? Consider the tension with the adversarial ideal that all controverted facts are put to the factfinder, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories of knowledge or evidence would license it as it stands. Finally, say what would change if the general-knowledge prong were abolished and only the verifiable-sources prong remained: what, exactly, would the practice lose, and would that loss be epistemic or merely ceremonial?
