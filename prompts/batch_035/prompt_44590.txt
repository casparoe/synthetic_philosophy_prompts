I'm organizing the closing session of a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and the final unit was on the language of thought hypothesis — mentalese, the classical/computational theory of cognition, and the connectionist backlash against it. Rather than another discussion, I'd like to end with a poem, and I want the form to carry genuine philosophical weight rather than serve as decoration.

Here is the idea I want embodied. A villanelle is built from a small stock of fixed constituents — two refrains — recombined by strict rule into ever-new lines and stanzas; and that is very close to the classicist's argument from systematicity made flesh: the same parts, rearranged by syntax, yielding thoughts you have never tokened before. So the poem should, in its very construction, run the argument that Fodor and the classicists run against connectionism. Let one refrain state the language-of-thought position in miniature — something like the claim that thought has a combinatorial syntax, which is what makes it productive and systematic — and let the other refrain state the connectionist counterposition — something like: there are no inner symbols at all, only weights and activations spreading through a network that never carves anything discrete. The refrains must return unaltered wherever the form demands (that is what makes them constituents), while the body of each stanza should voice the strongest objection available against that stanza's refrain: distributed representations, graceful degradation where brains unlike serial machines survive local damage, the fact that nobody ever explicitly taught us most of our sentence-shaped thoughts, the complaint that mentalese just pushes the mystery of meaning from English into an unlearnable inner code. The final tercet, where the two refrains finally stand together as the closing couplet, should leave it genuinely open whether their meeting is a refutation, a reconciliation, or two claims that were never really in conflict — the way the hybrid view, a language of thought implemented in neural machinery, wants to have it both ways.

Constraints: strict villanelle form, nineteen lines, the ABA rhymes, the refrains alternating through the quintains and then joined at the end. A philosopher of mind who knows the Fodor–connectionism dialectic should be able to identify each refrain's position on sight and find the middle stanzas tracking the actual moves of the debate rather than name-dropping them; a reader who knows nothing about any of this should still come away with a good poem about two voices that keep circling back to each other. Please do not quietly resolve the debate in either direction — the seminar could not, and I would like the poem to be honest about that.
