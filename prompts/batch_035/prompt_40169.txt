My co-moderator on a long-running philosophy forum and I have spent three years arguing about vagueness and want to produce, at last, the document our argument deserves. She defends Williamson-style epistemicism: for every vague predicate there are sharp boundaries, and our inability to locate them is ordinary ignorance. I defend supervaluationism: a vague term admits many admissible precisifications, and sentences in the penumbra can be true on all, some, or none of them, with truth reserved for the supertrue ones. Our original falling-out was over a thread asking at what point a language model counts as "generally intelligent," and we would like that case treated as a live test in the document, not as a joke. Please draft our adversarial collaboration's joint statement: a numbered section of claims both of us can sign (about the sorites paradox, about vagueness being a feature of representation rather than of pebbles, about higher-order vagueness being genuine); the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, including what each of us means when we deny or affirm that there is no sharp boundary between heap and non-heap; the cruxes, of which we suspect one is whether an unknowable sharp cutoff is a substantive fact about the world or an artifact of the semantics; and, for each crux, the arguments or cases we both agree would bear on it -- the model-capability threshold, say, or legal cliffs such as a regulation that switches on at a firm-size number, or Williamson's margin-for-error reasoning about why the cutoff must be unknowable -- with each side's prediction written down in advance. If any part of our dispute strikes you as merely verbal, say which part and why, in terms we would both sign.
