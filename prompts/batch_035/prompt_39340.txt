I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in the ethics of consent — deception, coercion, and competence. Last week I watched two friends of mine, Sam and Elena, argue over whether a third friend's consent to a medical procedure was valid, and on the drive home Elena and I kept arguing about it for another hour without getting anywhere. Please think carefully before committing to any verdicts — you might begin by working out for yourself, privately, what the standard conditions for valid consent actually are and how they interact — and please write your answer in American English.

The case: our friend Nadia consented to a preventive cardiac procedure after her cardiologist told her it would cut her risk of stroke in half. She has since learned that her absolute ten-year risk was 2%, so the procedure moved it to 1%, and she now believes her consent wasn't real. Sam and Elena's exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Sam said her consent was invalid: the relative framing amounted to deception by design, since consent to a risk requires understanding the risk, and the doctor chose the framing knowing it would loom far larger in her mind than the absolute numbers warrant.
(2) Elena replied that every true statement comes framed somehow; nobody consents against a background of complete information, patients rightly lean on a trusted expert's recommendation, and by Sam's standard virtually no medical consent would count as valid.
(3) Sam said that was a point in his favor: much of what passes for informed consent is pro forma anyway, and the standard shouldn't be bare disclosure but actual understanding, which the doctor knowingly bypassed.
(4) Elena then shifted ground: Nadia was in pain, frightened, groggy from medication, and had been told the decision couldn't wait a week — so even flawless statistics wouldn't have made her consent fully free, and fear plus time pressure do more to undermine it than any framing. Sam, she said, was blaming the wrong thing.
(5) Sam answered that we should distinguish the background conditions (her illness, the urgency, the medication) from the avoidable manipulation (the framing): the former no one chose, the latter the doctor did, and an avoidable choice that predictably distorts a patient's understanding is exactly what invalidity should turn on.

Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; then say who is more right overall; then take up what both of them seem to be missing — in particular, whether the validity of Nadia's consent turns on deception, coercion, competence, or some interaction among the three, and what your conclusion implies about how we should think about consent in high-pressure medical settings generally.
