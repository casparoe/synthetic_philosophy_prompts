You are a professional philosopher of science with a specialty in the metaphysics of laws, and I need you to think carefully rather than pattern-match. I'm a data scientist by training with a serious amateur interest in philosophy, and there's a recurring puzzle I can't find a name for, though I'd bet the professionals have discussed it to death. Before settling on any answer, please brainstorm the possible framings out loud, consider several candidates, and only then commit.

Here is the thing, in my own words. In my day job I fit curves to data. A well-chosen curve through a scatter of points tells you where the next point is likely to fall, but nobody says the points are where they are *because* the curve goes there; the dependence is plainly the other way around. The curve is a summary, and a summary can't push its own data around.

But when my old physics professor explained why the shattered window's fragments ended up with just the right kinetic energies, he said it was "because of the conservation laws," and it sounded like the law was doing something more than summarizing — as though it were a reason the window couldn't have done otherwise. Yet if you press philosophers on what a law of nature actually is, one honest-sounding answer is that a law is a very good curve through everything that has ever happened: the pattern you'd write down if you had to compress all the facts and cared about being both brief and comprehensive. On that answer, the law no more pushes the window than my fitted curve pushes the data. So the phenomenon I want a name for is this: if the laws are nothing over and above the totality of the facts they cover, then citing a law as an explanation seems to eat its own tail — "it happened because it belongs to the pattern, and the pattern is all the things that happen" adds nothing beyond "it happened." The explanation has gone circular without anyone appearing to notice.

Three complications that keep me from thinking this is just a confusion, and that any good answer would have to deal with:

1. Compression strikes me as genuinely explanatory. A single equation that covers millions of disparate events seems to tell me something real, even if it is "only" a summary. So maybe the redundancy worry attaches to citing a law piecemeal and not to citing the whole system at once — or maybe not. I genuinely can't tell where the line is, or whether there's supposed to be one.

2. My professor insisted laws differ from mere descriptions because they support counterfactuals — if the glass had been thicker, the fragments would have carried less energy. But a decent curve supports interpolation too. Whether the summary picture can handle counterfactuals "for free" or whether it quietly needs something extra seems to matter to whether my worry is real.

3. Someone who already knew every fact wouldn't learn anything from being told the pattern. But nobody does know every fact. So maybe the felt non-redundancy is just ignorance-management and the worry only bites for an idealized know-it-all — which would make it unclear whether it's a worry about laws, about explanation, or about us.

I've privately tried "descriptive vs. prescriptive laws" as a label, comparing physical laws to legal codes (which are genuinely rules and can be broken, unlike windows that fail to shatter "illegally"), but that feels too crude to be the professional version of the point.

So, three questions. First: does this exact worry have an established name or home in the literature, and under what heading would the professionals discuss it? Second: what are the nearest neighbors, and precisely how does my described thing differ from each — I'm especially interested in whether it's distinct from the problem of induction, since I'm happy to trust my curves for prediction and only sour on them for explanation. Third: if the literature has no single concept matching this exactly, coin one — give it a definition, and use the boundary cases above (single equations vs. whole systems, counterfactual support, the know-it-all) to say what it is and, just as important, what it is not. Please don't stop at a bare name; the label is worth little to me compared to the carving.
