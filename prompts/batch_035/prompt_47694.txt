I'd like your credence on a question in the philosophy of temporal consciousness, with the reasoning decomposed.

The setup: in the color phi phenomenon, a red spot is flashed on a screen, the screen goes briefly dark, and a green spot is flashed a short distance away. Observers typically report seeing a single moving spot that changes from red to green somewhere in the middle of its path — even though nothing was on the screen at the moment of the midpoint, and nothing was ever both moving and color-changing. There are two familiar ways of telling this story. On the Stalinesque version, the brain holds back its delivery to consciousness for a moment and, working backwards from the later information, fills in the color change before the experience is generated, so the experience never happened without the midpoint content. On the Orwellian version, the experience occurred roughly as the naive retelling suggests, with the content arriving only later, and what got revised was the memory: the record was rewritten after the fact, so the subject now sincerely reports the impossible experience. Dennett argued that these two stories are not merely difficult to tell apart empirically but indistinguishable in principle, on the ground that there is no further act of presentation to consciousness whose timing could settle which story is true; and he drew the deflationary conclusion that for some fine-grained temporal questions about experience there is simply no fact of the matter.

The question I want a number on: what is your credence that in the actual color phi case there is a determinate fact of the matter about when, and in what order, the midpoint content entered the subject's experience — as opposed to the question being unsettled in Dennett's deflationary way? Please decompose rather than giving a bare number. A natural decomposition, though propose a better one if you see one:

(i) the probability that the temporal fine structure of experience at the tens-of-milliseconds scale is fixed by something over and above the totality of the subject's discriminative capacities, reports, and later-accessible traces — that is, that there is a further fact about "when it was experienced";
(ii) the probability that the phi case is relevantly like ordinary perception, where we take the reported timing of experience at face value, rather than like the ordinary memory-revision cases in which we comfortably say the revision came after the fact;
(iii) the probability, conditional on a further fact existing, that it settles matters in the Stalinesque direction — for instance because postdictive integration in early visual processing turns out to be partly constitutive of the experience.

Give a number and a one-line justification for each, then show how they combine into your final credence. Then identify which term you are least confident in, and tell me what empirical finding — about the neural time course of the illusion, or about subjects' abilities to make fine temporal discriminations within it — would move your final number the most.

One further twist, because I suspect the standard framing rigs the answer. It is usually assumed that the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories agree completely on everything downstream: after the fact, the subject remembers the same thing either way, so no test could ever separate them. But the stories need not agree on the traces. On the Orwellian story, the memory was presumably first recorded in one form and then rewritten; on the Stalinesque story, no rewrite ever occurred, since the "false" content was there from the start. Suppose that in some future setup — psychophysical probing, or a recording of the memory trace in its earlier form — the earlier record were actually detected. Would that discovery settle the matter in the Orwellian direction, or does Dennett's indistinguishability argument survive, on the grounds that a detectable earlier trace is still just another trace and not a presentation to consciousness? And does the bare possibility of such a test change any of the numbers in your decomposition above?

For context: my own credence is around 0.4. A friend of mine who works in psychophysics puts it near 0.9, on the grounds that postdictive effects are now a routine finding in vision science and consciousness is simply where such integration shows up. I suspect our disagreement lives in term (i), but I'm not certain — if you think it lives somewhere else instead, say which term and why.
