At my university's effective altruism society I keep getting the same line, most recently from a committee member who was trying to talk me out of my plan to work on homelessness policy in my own city and into something he considered more "important," like factory farming or pandemic preparedness. The line is "do the most good you can," and when I pushed back he elaborated with the movement's standard triad — that a cause should be important, tractable, and neglected — and observed that homelessness in a rich country is crowded and well-funded, so my marginal contribution there would be small. I left convinced he was right about something and wrong about something, but unable to say which, because I realized I don't actually know what precise claim the slogan is making. It seems to be doing several jobs at once: sometimes it sounds like a moral demand (I *ought* to maximize), sometimes like a comparative heuristic (more good is better than less, all else equal), sometimes like an empirical claim about where my marginal impact is largest, and sometimes like a metaphysical claim that goodness across different causes can be aggregated onto a single scale that ranks a donation to a local shelter against a donation to a malaria charity.

I'd like you to lay out a menu of the distinct precise theses that "do the most good you can" could be expressing, ordered from the weakest to the most radical, and give a verdict on each: which readings are platitudes that no one could dispute, which are substantive and defensible, which are substantive and probably false, and which are ambiguous enough to be doing the work only under an interpretation nobody would explicitly endorse. A few places I suspect the readings come apart, and would like you to address: whether "the most good you can" refers to my marginal counterfactual impact or to the average impact of people doing that kind of work; whether the slogan commits you to interpersonal aggregation of welfare across causes and recipients, or whether "the most good" can remain vague enough to dodge that; whether "can" makes it a claim about my actual options or my idealized ones; and whether the slogan, on its strongest readings, generates the demandingness problem — the objection that maximizing morality asks more of individuals than morality is entitled to — and if so, whether the weaker readings escape it.

Finally, connect the menu back to the triad he used against me. Under which readings does the fact that a cause is *neglected* make my working on a crowded cause a worse choice, and under which readings is neglectedness irrelevant or even a bad sign (because crowded causes are crowded for good reasons — they're tractable and important)? And say explicitly which reading the committee member was probably invoking when he used the slogan to end the argument, and whether that reading, made precise, would actually support his conclusion that I should redirect my career. Please present the whole thing as an ordered list I can bring back to the society and work through with him point by point, rather than as an essay defending one interpretation.
