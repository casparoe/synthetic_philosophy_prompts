Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a retired epidemiologist (about 30 years, mostly cancer clusters and occupational exposures), and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy-of-history rabbit hole. Here's the package of views I seem to have ended up with, and I'd like someone to tell me whether it hangs together or whether I've talked myself into a corner:

(1) When I taught epi methods I drilled students on the component-cause / "causal pie" picture: outcomes come from whole constellations of jointly sufficient conditions, and no single component is *the* cause. I've come to think this is exactly right for history too — WWI, the fall of Rome, the Reformation — and that historians hunting for the one cause are asking a malformed question.

(2) I also hold a pretty blunt counterfactual view of causation: X genuinely caused Y only if, in the world most similar to ours but without X, Y doesn't happen — or at least doesn't happen then, the way it did.

(3) I think big historical outcomes are usually overdetermind: yank the assassination at Sarajevo out of 1914 and you still get a general European war within a few years, because the alliance blocs, the mobilization timetables, and German fear of encirclement were all sitting there loaded. (Though I don't think I'm speaking falsely when I tell people at dinner that the assassination started the war.)

(4) At the same time I'm a sucker for contingency — I think history is wildly path-dependent, and that small stuff (a misread telegram, a king's toothache, Cleopatra's nose) really can redirect enormous outcomes.

(5) And I have an old frequentist's allergy to probability talk about unique events. When philosophers say a cause "raised the probability" of a war, I want to ask: probability relative to what refrence class? If there isn't one, I don't see how the statement means anything.

So: is this a consistent package, or am I helping myself to something somewhere that the rest of it rules out? Please structure your answer as follows. Start with "Verdict" — one paragraph saying up front which pairs of my views clash and how badly. Then "Real tensions, worst first" — a short paragraph on each genuine clash, saying exactly what would have to give and what giving it up costs me. Then "Only looks like a clash" — any pair that dissolves once you look closer, and why. End with "Cheapest single fix" — two or three sentences on the one revision that would clean up the most. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flat, and please write in American English.
