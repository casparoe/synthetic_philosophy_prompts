At a reunion dinner last month, an old colleague from my detector group waved his glass at me and repeated the line, nearly always credited to Feynman though I have never managed to trace it to anything he actually published, that the philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds. I spent thirty-five years in experimental particle physics and have, since retiring, taken to reading philosophy of science in earnest — Reichenbach on the context of discovery versus the context of justification, the Duhem–Quine underdetermination problem, arguments about parsimony that remind me uncomfortably of fights over model selection in my own analyses. My colleague deployed the quip precisely to dismiss this new pastime, and he had company: Weinberg argued in a book of his from the early nineties that no recent advance in physics had been much helped by the philosophy of science, and Hawking opened a popular book around 2010 by declaring philosophy dead because it had not kept up with physics.

I want to know what the quip actually amounts to before I concede or rebut it at the next dinner. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making — from the trivially true to the genuinely radical — and give a verdict on each: is it plausible, is it defensible, and would it actually support using the quip to shut down a retired experimentalist's reading? Some threads I want you to take seriously rather than smooth over. The quip says useful to scientists, while the surrounding debate is about usefulness to science, and I suspect those come apart. The ornithology half of the analogy may backfire, since ornithologists have arguably done a great deal for birds, conservation being the obvious case — but perhaps that misreads the analogy, if the point is that birds cannot make use of it even when it is about them, which would raise the question of whether scientists are in the relevant way like birds, and whether that comparison presupposes that scientific know-how is already tacitly complete in the practitioner. There is also the difference between a claim that philosophy is useless, a weaker claim that it is merely dispensable, and a different claim entirely about which profession ought to be saying what about science. On my own side of it, I keep wondering whether things I took as merely professional practice — blinding protocols, the five-sigma convention, how we hedged about systematic uncertainties — were ever touched by philosophical argument, or whether they grew up as pure craft.

Do not leave me with a menu and no verdict. State in your very first sentence which reading of the quip is the correct one and whether my colleagues' use of it was warranted, and only then unfold the reasoning, with the readings as a numbered list within that. I would rather have a committed position I can argue with over dinner than a survey. No preamble and no restatement of my question, please.
