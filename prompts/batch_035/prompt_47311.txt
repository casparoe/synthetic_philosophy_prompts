I'm a certified financial planner who runs a small independent practice, mostly ordinary people saving for retirement. I have no academic background at all, but I've been chewing on something for years on my commute, and I've finally managed to put my finger on it well enough to ask for help. I'd like you to act as an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice, and to think carefully and do some brainstorming before you answer, because I suspect my question is messier than it looks.

Here is what I keep seeing. Some clients slowly bleed money to their own inconsistency, and no one is cheating them — each individual decision looks free and reasonable, but the sequence of decisions costs them money and leaves them where they started or worse. One client every January tells me, comparing funds side by side, that the boring low-cost index fund is the right fit for her goals, and every March, after growth funds post a good quarter, she pays a load plus capital gains taxes to switch into the growth fund — then every autumn, when markets get rocky, she pays again to switch back. She ends up owning what she originally had, minus several percent a year. Another client told me he'd rather have $110 in a year than $100 today, but then, asked about the same kind of choice moved closer, said he'd take $100 today over $110 next week; something is off about that pair of answers and I can't articulate what. A third insists she's happy with her current portfolio and has no interest in anything I offer her, yet also calls me in every panic to make paid changes she describes as "rebalancing" that leave her with less of everything. And in the opposite corner sit clients who never move at all: a man who has held three worthless stocks for fifteen years "until they come back," whose stated plan requires selling them, and who won't. It strikes me these might all be failures of the same general thing — people whose choices don't fit together across time, or across the way the question is put — but I may be lumping unlike problems together, and I'd want you to tell me if I am.

What I'm asking for is a procedure I can actually run, not an essay. Please start with some plain-language brainstorming about what's really going on in my examples (I've never read a word of philosophy, so no jargon without translation). Then write a checklist for me to follow: the questions to ask and the records to pull, in order, during a client's annual review and again during unscheduled panic calls; what a "fail" looks like at each step; where the procedure instructs me to act (propose a commitment arrangement, decline to execute a trade, escalate to a senior colleague); and — this is the part I care most about — where it honestly cannot distinguish between an inconsistency that makes a client exploitable by their own future choices and a legitimate change of heart or genuine new information, so that the call is mine as a person rather than mine as a checker of boxes. The constraints are real: about forty-five minutes per review, plus whatever a panic call takes; stakes are people's retirements; and the only person who signs off is me.

Then run the procedure on three clients and show your work: (1) the January growth-fund switcher above; (2) the fifteen-year holder of dead stocks who refuses every trade his own plan requires; (3) a client who has recently asked me to become his commitment device — to refuse his future requests to sell, and who is furious at me whenever I actually do refuse. For each, tell me what the procedure yields, whether the verdict is one I or the client will dislike, and what the procedure cannot settle in that case. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire — the situations where following it would itself be a mistake — and whether, on reflection, some of my examples belong to different problems that need different procedures rather than one. I would much rather learn that I've conflated things than receive a tidy system built on a confusion.
