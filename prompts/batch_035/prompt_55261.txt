I run a weekly decision theory reading group at a community college, 11 students, we meet for 80 minutes. Next session is on Parfit's hitchhiker and the whole "rational irrationality" thing — the idea that it can be rational to be disposed to act irrationally later, because a hitchhiker who really will pay up once rescued gets picked up, while the perfectly rational one who won't pay (once the threat of being abandoned is gone) gets left in the desert. They've already done expected utility, backwards induction, and regular Newcomb's problem, so they know the vocabulary, but I don't think they *feel* yet why the "obviously rational" agent loses here, or why having a disposition can matter even when you'd never voluntarily act on it.

Design me a runnable game for this. Real stakes would be ideal but no money — poker chips or IOU slips or something, with some final payout that matters even a little (snacks rights, picking next week's reading, whatever). Some things I'd love to see worked in: a way for the "driver" players to partially read the "hitchhiker" players' dispositions before choosing (a cheap version of the predictor), at least one binding-commit variant where hitchhikers can lock themselves in before the ride, and maybe a repeated version where reputation starts doing work so they can see which mechanism is actually doing the heavy lifting. The payoff numbers should make the standard hitchhiker case come out sharp: taking the detour costs the driver something real, and paying the reward at the destination is a pure loss in the moment.

Please give it to me as a single JSON object with these fields: "name" (a short title for the game), "materials" (list), "setup" (roles, group size handling, how chips/payouts work), "timeline" (array of objects with "phase", "minutes", and "what_happens"), "rules" (the full rule set, including the numbers for each variant), "debrief" (the questions that map specific moments in the game onto backwards induction, commitment, and disposition-based reasoning — please don't name Parfit or use the word "hitchhiker" until the game is over), "failure_modes" (what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each), and "limits" (an honest note on which part of the philosophical point a game with actual humans and real stakes can't reproduce — e.g. whether my students' hitchhikers are ever really "unable" to pay the way the idealized agent is). If some field wants more structure, nested objects or lists are fine, just keep the field names as given. Also, one of the students is very loud and will try to argue about free will the second anything smells like determinism — bonus points if you build in a graceful place for that tangent to land.
