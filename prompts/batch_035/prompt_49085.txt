My flatmate came back from a seminar on semantic externalism with an argument she says settles the brain-in-a-vat scare once and for all, and I can't decide whether it's a triumph or a trick. (1) What my words mean isn't fixed by anything in my head; it's fixed by the causal links between my uses of them and my surroundings. (2) A brain that has been envatted from the start has never so much as brushed against a real vat — every one of its word-uses is caused by states of the computer running it — so when it thinks in terms of vats, it is at best thinking about vats-in-the-image, simulated vats. (3) So if I am such a brain, my sentence "I am a brain in a vat" is true only if I am a simulated brain in a simulated vat, and in that scenario I am the opposite: a real brain in a real vat. (4) If I am not such a brain, the sentence is false in the boring way. (5) Either way my sentence "I am a brain in a vat" is false, and I can establish this from the armchair, without examining my hands or consulting my memories. (6) So I can know a priori that I am not a brain in a vat.

Here is my problem. The reasoning in (1)–(5) is available to the envatted brain itself: the very brain floating in the vat can run it, every step will seem just as good to it as to me, and it will conclude, truly by its own lights, that it is not a brain in a vat. If an argument can do that to the person it is true of, it hasn't ruled the scenario out so much as made it unsayable by its victim. And there is a second wrinkle: my flatmate concedes the argument fails against a brain envatted last Tuesday, since its words were fixed by honest contact with the world before the switch, but she insists that is the only amendment needed. So either she has delivered a priori knowledge of how long my causal contact with the world has been intact, or she has a trick that works only against the one textbook scenario. I suspect the damage is in the move from (5) to (6) — from my sentence being false to the scenario not obtaining — but I can't pin down the equivocation, and I'd much rather keep (1) than give up externalism. Tell me which step to give up, what it costs, and whether the Tuesday amendment exposes what the argument was really doing all along. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my setup.
