My husband died eight months ago and I'm 61, and somewhere in the last few weeks I went back to Émilie du Châtelet's short essay on happiness — the Discours sur le bonheur — because I remembered loving it in a college course and it seemed like the right companion for where I am now. I've ended up writing a personal essay, sort of her views on happiness meets widowhood, and I want to send it to a small magazine that publishes this kind of thing. Before I do, can you go through it and tell me where I'm being sloppy or unfair to her, and whether the personal angle earns its place or is just maudlin? I don't need a reading list, just your comments on the draft.

Here's the meat of it:

Opening is me, the rereading, the eight months. Then I get into her. My central claim is that she's basically a Stoic writing a century after Descartes — the essay reads like a stoic manual for how to be happy by wanting less and letting go of what you can't control. She says, and I agree this is the spine of the whole thing, that happiness is largely not up to us: you need the right temperament, decent health, and to have been born into circumstances that don't grind you down, and she's unusually blunt that people who lack these are mostly out of luck. Then the part I love: her defense of illusions. She thinks illusions are the best thing we have, that a person who sees themselves and their projects clearly is doomed to misery, and that you should actively maintain your illusions and even manufacture new ones as the old ones wear out. I connect that to the stories you tell yourself after a loss, the version of the marriage that keeps you functional.

Then a section on passions. She says the great secret is to have a passion — for her it was mathematics and physics — and that even mediocre success in it makes you happy, because it's the having-something-to-run-to that counts, not achievement. I use that for my gardening. Then love: she'd had the big passionate kind with Voltaire and concluded it's a bad bet for happiness, that passionate love burns out or torments you and what you want is steady companionable affection. Then a section on reputation, where she argues the happy person has to make themselves indifferent to glory and to what others say — another stoic note, I claim.

I also say somewhere in there that she wrote it at Cirey in her forties and published it herself in 1749, the year she died in childbirth, and that she was partly writing against Rousseau's romanticism about nature and feeling. Ending is me deciding which illusions I'm keeping.

So: where am I wrong about her, especially the Stoic framing — a friend who knows some philosophy said that's off but wouldn't say why — and is the structure working, or does the personal stuff and the exposition just take turns rather than braid? Also tell me if any single claim in there would make someone who actually knows du Châtelet wince, definately want to fix those before an editor sees it. And do be blunt about the widowhood material, I can take it.
