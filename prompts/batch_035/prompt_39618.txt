Third-year PhD student in history here, working on memory studies / national myths, and I need help pinning down a slogan that gets thrown around my department like it's obviously true: history is written by the victors. Last week in a seminar on Confederate memory someone used it to dismiss a whole line of questioning — the argument was roughly that we shouldn't worry about which version of the defeated side's story survives in textbooks and monuments, because whatever version we have is already the victors' version anyway, so there's no "authentic loser's account" to protect or recover. Everyone nodded, and I sat there feeling like the slogan was doing three different jobs at once and nobody checked whether any of them was actually done.

The thing that bugs me is that my own subfield looks like a pile of counterexamples to the obvious reading. The Lost Cause narrative about the Confederacy was written by the losers — ex-Confederate generals and their memorial organizations — and it dominated American popular memory of the Civil War for close to a century. Serbian national identity runs on Kosovo, a defeat (or at best a bloody draw) from 1389 that got built into a myth cycle powerful enough to be reactivated with a vengeance around its 600th anniversary. Masada: Judea lost, yet the account of the siege became a founding story for a state two millennia later, and the archaeology done to shore up the story ended up complicating it. And Dunkirk is just conceptually confusing for the slogan — a military retreat that got narrated as triumph, by a side that eventually won the war but decisively lost that battle, so who even counts as the victor doing the writing? At the same time I don't want to be the person who says the slogan is dumb because there are counterexamples — clearly archives get seized, curriculum boards get appointed by whoever holds power, losing sides' languages and institutions get suppressed, and in oral cultures a destroyed population's account can just die with it. So some reading of the slogan has to be true and non-trivial.

Can you lay out, as a menu I can bring to the next seminar, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing — from the weak-but-true (records survive at the pleasure of whoever holds power) through the sociological one (the profession and the publishers sit downstream of the victors' institutions even when individual historians are sympathetic to losers) up to the near-relativist one (all historical knowledge is structured by power relations, so "written by the victors" is true even of loser-written myths like the Lost Cause, since those losers were victors of some other struggle, e.g. over Reconstruction memory)? For each reading tell me: is it true, is it interesting or a platitude, and — crucially — does it actually support my seminar-mate's move that there's no loser's account worth distinguishing from the victors' version? Also flag which reading people probably mean when they say it offhand, because I suspect they equivocate between them mid-argument. And since I'm going to have to defend this in front of people who nodded along last week: tell me honestly where your analysis is most likely to fail, and what kind of evidence or argument would flip your verdict on the strongest reading. If a reading only survives by defining "victor" so loosely that the Serbs mourning 1389 count as victors, say so bluntly — I'd rather get caught out by a narrower claim than a mushy one.
