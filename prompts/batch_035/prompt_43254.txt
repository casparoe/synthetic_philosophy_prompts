You are a professional political philosopher with deep expertise in early modern thought, especially Locke, and a working knowledge of contemporary debates in urban politics. I'd like your help with a speculative exercise for a seminar paper I'm writing on the ethics of land-use regulation, and I'd like you to reason through it carefully rather than rushing to a verdict.

Imagine we could put John Locke in a present-day American suburb that is debating whether to upzone: a proposal would allow duplexes and small apartment buildings on lots that are currently restricted to single-family homes. Longtime homeowners oppose the change, arguing that they bought into a neighborhood with a certain character and that allowing denser construction diminishes the value and tranquility of what they own; they organize, show up at planning hearings, and lobby to keep the restrictions in place — classic NIMBYism. On the other side, younger renters and would-be newcomers argue that in a region with scarce housing and high prices, incumbent owners are effectively locking up land that others need, invoking something like the right-to-the-city tradition associated with Henri Lefebvre, on which the people who actually inhabit and use a city have a collective claim to shape it, not just those who hold title to parcels of it.

Where would Locke come down? Please reason from the core commitments of his property theory and political philosophy as you know them: the idea that unowned land becomes property through the mixing of one's labor; the proviso that appropriation is legitimate only so long as enough and comparably good land remains for others; the claim that governments are legitimate only by consent, and that they may regulate property only in ways the consented-to constitution permits; and his distinction between express and tacit consent. I'm particularly interested in the tensions you find: does the labor-mixing account favor incumbent owners, who developed and maintained the neighborhood, or the builders, whose improvements on their own land seem exactly the kind of activity Locke protects? Does the proviso speak to newcomers excluded by zoning, or does it lapse once land is genuinely scarce? And would Locke treat a municipal zoning code as a legitimate exercise of consent-based collective authority, or as an overreach that lets a local majority perpetualize a property rule in its own favor?

Before answering, please briefly brainstorm the strongest Lockean case for each side and note where the textual tradition underdetermines the answer. Then give your considered judgment about which side Locke would more likely favor, clearly flagging the points where you are extrapolating beyond his actual writings rather than reporting them. Since this is speculation about a historical figure confronted with a problem he never faced, candor about the limits of the exercise is part of what I'm grading.
