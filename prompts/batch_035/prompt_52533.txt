I'm constructing a scoring rubric for scientific thought experiments, and I want you to apply it to a fixed set of cases. To be clear about what I'm after: I'm not looking for yet another retelling of the debate over whether thought experiments are disguised arguments, windows onto abstract structure, or genuine experiments performed in the imagination. I want those views treated as background, and instead want a working evaluation instrument used to produce a defended ranking.

Here is the rubric, which mixes familiar ideas with two criteria of my own devising.

1. Counterfactual discipline (0–10). A good thought experiment should extract its conclusion from background beliefs the audience already holds, rather than smuggling in a substantive empirical assumption and then presenting it as derived. Low scores for scenarios whose persuasive force depends on an empirical claim that was never actually checked.

2. Simulation load (0–10). This is my own criterion, and I want you to take it seriously. Some thought experiments can be mentally executed step by step by a competent reasoner: the audience can actually run the scenario in their head and watch the conclusion fall out. Others outrun human simulation capacity entirely — they involve tracking individual molecules, inverting enormously complex functions, or observing what happens "in principle" over unbounded time. My hypothesis is that thought experiments sometimes trade on this outrunning: the audience's inability to simulate is mistaken for the necessity of the conclusion. High scores go to cases where the conclusion survives the fact that you genuinely can run the simulation.

3. Historical yield (0–10). Roughly, how much theory or practice actually changed as a result. This is the crude instrumentalist criterion, kept separate so it doesn't contaminate the first two.

The cases, in no particular order: Stevin's chain of spheres looped over a triangular prism (the inclined-plane argument against perpetual motion); Galileo's argument that a heavy and a light body tied together must fall at the same rate; Newton's bucket; Maxwell's demon; Einstein's sixteen-year-old thought of chasing a beam of light; Heisenberg's gamma-ray microscope; and Schrödinger's cat. Treat Laplace's demon as a control case: tell me briefly whether it even belongs in the same category, and if not, why the others do and it doesn't — this will help me decide where the rubric draws the boundary of the class.

For each of the seven, give the three scores with a compact justification, then an overall tier (A, B, or C) reflecting your own weighting of the criteria — and please state what that weighting is and defend it, since I don't want the scores to be silently aggregated. I'm especially interested in disagreements between the criteria: for instance, cases that score high on historical yield but low on counterfactual discipline, and what that says about whether the field rewards rigor or theatre. I'd also like a short paragraph on whether my simulation-load criterion tracks anything real, or whether it collapses into the disguised-argument view under inspection; if it collapses, say so plainly rather than protecting my hypothesis.

Conclude with a final line, machine-readable and on its own, of the form \answer{RANKING: ...} listing the seven cases from highest to lowest by your weighted total, comma-separated, with Laplace's demon appended as either "INCLUDES: Laplace" or "EXCLUDES: Laplace" at the end.
