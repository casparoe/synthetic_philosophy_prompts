I am drafting a novel set in sub-Roman Britain whose frame narrative turns on how much historical weight a reader may fairly be asked to place in the Arthur legend. When I observed to a historian friend that neither the archaeology nor the near-contemporary written sources seem to contain an Arthur, she replied that "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," pointing to Troy as her precedent — a war long dismissed as myth until the excavators uncovered a mound that plausibly matches it — and noting that post-Roman Britain is so thinly documented that the silence there proves nothing. That one line is doing a great deal of work in her argument, and before I build a frame narrative on it I want to know exactly how much it can bear. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from the trivial logical point that failure to detect something never strictly entails its nonexistence, to the Bayesian observation that a search which had some chance of turning up evidence and did not must lower the probability of the hypothesis — and, for each reading, say whether it is defensible and, crucially, whether it actually supports the use she put it to: does any of them license believing in a historical Arthur, or only suspending judgment? Finally, I would value your assessment of whether the Troy analogy strengthens or weakens her case, given what a genuinely confirming archaeological discovery looks like as opposed to a merely compatible one.
