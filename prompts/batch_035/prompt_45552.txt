I hold the interventionist view of causation: for C to cause E is for there to be some possible intervention on C — an exogenous manipulation, holding certain other factors fixed — under which E's value would differ. I came to it from the similarity/possible-worlds counterfactual analysis, mainly because it seems to make sense of the contrast-sensitivity of causal claims (the short circuit, not the presence of oxygen, caused the fire) and of causal talk at different levels of description. Tell me what would count against it.

I'd like the defeaters sorted by decisiveness, and I want more than the standard list. Please cover at least these: the circularity charge (that "intervention" is itself defined partly in causal terms, so the analysis presupposes what it analyzes); cases where the needed interventions are impossible in principle — a causally closed universe with nothing outside it to intervene from, or two variables locked in a mutually sustaining equilibrium, such as a predator population and a prey population each stabilizing the other, where intuitively neither "causes" the other; the gerrymandered-variable problem (that with enough freedom in how one carves the world into variables, the theory can be made to say nearly anything, so I want to know what it actually rules out — this is where I'd most like to hear what the view predicts that its rivals don't, e.g. its commitment that causal claims are always tacitly contrast-relative); early preemption and causation by omission; and the possibility that interventionist counterfactuals could be shown to collapse into ordinary similarity counterfactuals, which would leave my preference for the former unmotivated. For each, say whether committed interventionists would themselves concede it as counting against the view, or would wave it off, and rank the survivors from fatal through serious to merely awkward. I'd be most moved by a concrete case where the machinery delivers a clear verdict — especially about which of two mutually dependent factors causes which — and our considered judgments about that case go the other way.

Finally, the question I want answered honestly: has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by the "holding fixed" clauses and the latitude in variable choice? And if so, is that a defect in a theory of causation, or acceptable because the theory is better read as prescribing a method for using causal claims than as stating what causation is?
