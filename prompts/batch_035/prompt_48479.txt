I'm taking a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and last week we reached the independence axiom for expected utility. The professor walked us through the classic Allais choices, and I discovered that my own answers — the sure million in one choice, the gamble in the other — jointly violate the axiom I had happily endorsed on paper. Rather than simply confess to being irrational, I've spent the past week cataloguing my gut verdicts on a series of related cases, some drawn from the literature and some I made up by tweaking numbers. I'd like to know whether one coherent principle captures all of them, or whether I have to abandon some verdict, and if so which.

Here are the verdicts. First choice: $1M for sure, versus an 89% chance of $1M, a 10% chance of $5M, and a 1% chance of nothing — I take the sure million, and I take it immediately. Second choice, with everything scaled down: an 11% chance of $1M versus a 10% chance of $5M — here I take the 10% shot at $5M, even though the structure looks the same once you cancel the states where both options pay identically. Third: a certain $3,000 versus an 80% chance of $4,000 — I take the sure $3,000; but when the same gamble is shrunk to a 0.2% chance of $4,000 versus a 0.25% chance of $3,000, I take the $4,000 gamble. Fourth, and this is where my classmate thinks she has me: when the first choice is embedded in a two-stage setup — a preliminary draw determines whether I even face the choice, and I must record my preference before the draw — I find myself choosing in accordance with independence, taking the $5M-type option in both stages, and I cannot articulate why the staging should matter to me, yet it plainly does. Fifth, away from money entirely: choosing between a weekend in Rome and a weekend in Paris, where with some probability the trip is cancelled either way and I stay home — I'm confident my preference between Rome and Paris shouldn't shift as that cancellation probability moves, and I'd be embarrassed to violate independence there.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a sure $1M versus a 99.9% chance of $1M with a 0.1% chance of nothing. I can't tell whether my attachment to the sure thing in the first Allais choice is about certainty as such, in which case I should also pay a lot here to eliminate the 0.1%, or whether it's about the size and salience of the downside, in which case the near-certainty should leave me nearly indifferent. So: is there a single principle that fits all of this — some version of regret, disappointment aversion, a security-level or lexicographic preference, or something the field hasn't named — and if so, please state it as precisely as you can, show which verdicts it saves and which it cannot accommodate, and predict what it says about a case I haven't listed, including my wobbling one. If no single principle fits, tell me which of my verdicts to drop and why that one rather than another, since several of them feel about equally firm to me.
