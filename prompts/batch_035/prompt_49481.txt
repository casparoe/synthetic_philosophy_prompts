In most countries with transplant programs, organ procurement is governed by a "dead donor rule": vital organs may only be taken from patients who are already dead. But the rule is operationalized in two very different ways. For patients meeting whole-brain death criteria — tested at the bedside through absence of brainstem reflexes and apnea — death is declared while the heart is still beating, often with a ventilator still inflating the lungs, because that perfusion is precisely what keeps the organs viable. For donation after circulatory death, by contrast, a patient who will never meet brain death criteria is taken off the ventilator, and death is declared after a short interval of absent circulation — as little as seventy-five seconds in one well-known pediatric protocol, more commonly two to five minutes — a wait chosen, in part, because it is long enough that autoresuscitation is not expected yet short enough that the organs remain usable. The same institution thus declares death, in one room, in a patient whose heart is beating, and, in another, in a patient whose brain may still have residual function that a prompt resuscitation could partially restore.

What definition of death does this dual-track practice presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the practice commit its participants to a single account — cessation of whole-brain function, irreversible loss of circulation, or something else — or does it presuppose two different accounts in the two rooms, and can that be coherent rather than merely expedient? Note the awkward pressures from both sides: if death is a single biological event, the two-minute declaration looks premature and the beating-heart declaration looks late; if death is instead the loss of what matters about a person — consciousness, the capacity for experience — then neither track tracks it very precisely, and the practice's reliance on mechanical tests of brainstem reflexes and pulselessness looks like a proxy. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where it is underdetermined — that is, which competing definitions of death could each license both tracks, and what would have to be altered in the waiting periods, the testing protocols, or the dead donor rule itself if one of those rivals were adopted as official policy.
