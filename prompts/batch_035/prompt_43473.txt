I'm a nurse practitioner, and for the past few years I've served on the five-member ethics panel at our regional hospital that reviews research studies involving human volunteers. Our procedure is fixed by the hospital's policy manual: for each study we answer three yes/no questions in order — whether the risk to participants is acceptable, whether the consent process is genuinely informed, and whether the scientific value of the study is sufficient to justify that risk — and a study is approved only if a majority of the panel votes yes on each question separately. Last month we approved a study that, as far as I can tell, most of us were against. It went like this: two of us accepted all three conditions and so favored approval. One colleague voted no on consent but yes on the other two; one voted no on risk but yes on the other two; one voted no on value but yes on the other two. So on each individual question, four of five said yes, and the study sailed through — even though three of the five of us, each for a different reason, would have voted the whole protocol down if we'd been asked "approve or reject?" in one shot. A friend who went to law school told me appellate panels get into something like the same tangle, which was unsettling to hear, because our manual presents the rule as obviously fair and obviously equivalent to just letting the majority decide.

I've been chewing on what our procedure actually assumes, and I'd like a philosopher's help — someone who works on collective decision-making and on whether a group can be said to have a mind or a view of its own. As I see it, the rule only makes sense if you grant several things at once: that the three questions are genuinely separable and can be settled one at a time without reference to the others; that "the panel's view" on consent is simply whatever a majority says about consent considered in isolation; and that these majority views then compose into a further view, "the panel approved the study," that belongs to the panel as a single reasoner rather than to any of the people on it. But some of us believe the questions aren't separable at all — I for one think what counts as an acceptable risk depends on how valuable the study is, so my risk vote was already shaped by my value judgment — and if that's right, voting on the questions separately seems to quietly ignore how the members actually reason. There's also the paperwork problem: our approval letter states that the panel concluded the consent process was adequate and the risk justified, yet a majority of the panel's actual members rejected the study, and each for reasons that appear nowhere in the letter as the panel's own.

So here is what I want you to work through, carefully and in detail. What conception of the panel — of what it is and where its "view" lives — does our procedure presuppose, and which rival conceptions does it quietly rule out? Does it require the premise that the criteria are independent of one another, or can it survive the fact that members' judgments interlock? Is the procedure consistent with the letter's claim to state the panel's reasons, or does the institution end up asserting things in its own voice that no majority of its members asserted? And if the hospital decided the panel's decision should instead track what a majority of members would conclude about each study as a whole — switching from issue-by-issue voting to a single up-or-down vote — what exactly would we gain and what would we lose, especially regarding the public record of reasons? Please start by laying out the possible pictures of what a committee like ours is before evaluating them, and use our actual votes from last month as your running example. And please explain any technical terms you introduce, since I have no background in philosophy; I just want to understand what my own committee has been assuming all these years.
