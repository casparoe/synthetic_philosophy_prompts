I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar in formal epistemology — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, a room with movable desks and a whiteboard. We have just finished the unit on Bayesian idealisation, and next week we turn to the problem of logical omniscience: the way our formal models treat an agent's knowledge or credences as closed under logical consequence, so that anyone who knows a set of premises ipso facto knows everything entailed by them, and anyone with consistent credences never assigns positive probability to a logical contradiction. The students can state the problem, but I do not think they feel it. To them it reads as a technical footnote, not as something that could sink the whole modelling enterprise.

Design an in-class game in which students are scored as though they were logically omniscient, and discover from the inside what that idealisation does to them. My thought — which you are free to improve upon or discard — is to give each team a modest stock of explicit facts about a fictional scenario (I am imagining something like twenty atomic facts about the movements of characters in a small town, chosen so that the consequences are finitely computable but laborious), and then run rounds in which queries are posed and teams are penalised, under a stated scoring rule, for any consequence of their own information they cannot produce on demand. I would like at least one round in which the query has a disjunctive form — the team must commit to knowing at least one of two consequences without knowing which — so that the "ideal reasoner" model forces an answer the actual reasoners cannot responsibly give. Teams should be able to spend points to buy deductive labour from other teams, so that the market price of deduction becomes visible data for the debrief.

Please provide: the full rules and materials; the scoring scheme, including how you would score an agent who assigns credence to a proposition that is in fact a contradiction without any team being able to see that it is; a round-by-round timeline that fits ninety minutes with the debrief included; and the debrief itself, mapping specific moments in play onto specific parts of the problem — the implicit/explicit knowledge distinction, the question of whether failing to know a consequence is an instance of irrationality or merely of boundedness, and the disanalogy between mathematical and empirical intractability. I also want your honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it (teams gaming the scoring, queries that are accidentally trivial, the deduction market collapsing), and a frank note on which feature of the problem the game cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief — in particular, whether anything in the activity can get at the normative question of whether logical omniscience is a defect of our models or a legitimate idealisation, rather than merely at the empirical fact that we are not logically omniscient.
