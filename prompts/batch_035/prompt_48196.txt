Something I keep arguing about with my brother and I want to get straight what I actually think: which cohort groupings pick out a real thing and which are just labels that got reified. Here are my gut verdicts, such as they are.

People who came of age during the Depression or fought in WWII as young adults — that feels like a real generation to me. Shared formative experiences, and claims like "the WWII generation trusted institutions more than their kids did" seem to do genuine explanitory work.

"Gen Z" — I go back and forth. Half the time it feels like a marketing category that got retroactively turned into a demographic, half the time I meet people ten years younger than me and think no, there really is something different about how they treat group chat vs email. My brother says the cutoff (1997 or whatever) is literally arbitrary so the whole kind is fake, and I can't quite answer him.

The May '68 generation in France. Only a small fraction of French people that age were involved, most of the age group weren't students, and most students weren't radicals. And yet that grouping feels MORE real to me than Gen Z, which is the thing I can't square. It's like being defined by a shared event trumps being defined by birth years, even when hardly anyone in the "generation" shared the defining event.

Someone born in 1964 vs someone born in 1965 — one's a Boomer and one's Gen X, and there's obviously no real difference between them, which suggests either the boundary is fake or the whole kind is.

But also: "first-generation college students" feels like a completely real grouping with real shared features, and it's not a birth cohort at all, it's defined by an experience. So maybe what I actually care about isn't generation in the demographic sense at all?

And the weird one: in immigrant families people talk about "the generation born here" vs the parents' generation, and that feels real too, even though the birth years are staggard across decades — a kid born in 1990 to parents who arrived in 1985 and a kid born in 2005 to parents who arrived in 2000 are both "second generation."

So: is there a principle that captures which of these carve something real and which are just binning? My brother's principle is "no sharp boundary, no real kind," which handles the 1964/65 case but seems to wreck my WWII-generation verdict, since that boundary is fuzzy too. Mine is something like "shared formative experience during a critical window," but that makes the '68 generation real for sort of the wrong reason, and I genuinely don't know what it says about Gen Z. Fix one of ours or come up with something better, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts has to go. Also curious what your principle says about the COVID cohort — kids who did 7th or 8th grade over zoom — is that a generation now, or too narrow, or what?
