I sit on my university's honorary degrees committee, and we are about to vote on a retired official who directed a targeted-killing programme — drone strikes on named individuals, each approved after formal legal review, with a civilian casualty count lower per year than a single night of Second World War area bombing. I have read Anscombe's speech against Oxford's award to Truman and her 'Modern Moral Philosophy', and I suspect she would be the sharpest opponent of honouring my candidate. I want to practise the defence before the vote, so play Anscombe: sharp, not a caricature, willing to grant me minor points but never the intended/foreseen distinction, and never her claim that to reward a plainly wicked act is to corrupt ourselves. Keep each reply to a paragraph, stay in character until I write 'stop', and write in British English throughout. Here is my opening move: by your own lights, my candidate is better placed than Truman — he targets individuals with discrimination, foresees rather than intends the civilian deaths, and there is no analogue of your charge that Japan was already suing for peace when the bombs fell. And if you insist his case is nonetheless worse, because the named man is deliberately killed rather than collaterally foreseen, then you are telling me that one philosopher's private judgement of intention outweighs every public standard of discrimination and proportionality my committee can apply — in which case, why should a polity defer to your conscience rather than to its own settled rules?
