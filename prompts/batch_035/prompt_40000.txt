I'm a backend engineer by training, and I now work on a team that has to make policy calls my CS education did nothing to prepare me for. When users signal what they want through our systems — whose content they'd like to see more of, whose they mute, which kinds of people they'd rather not be shown as neighbors or potential tenants — we have to decide which of these signals to honor as preferences and which to override as biases. Right now these calls are made case by case, mostly by gut, and I've noticed that every argument eventually bottoms out at someone flatly asserting "that's a bias" or "that's a preference," with no account of what makes it one rather than the other. Before we codify anything into a real policy, I want the full specification first, written out as carefully as we'd write a design doc.

So: please write the desiderata that any workable criterion for distinguishing moral preferences from biases has to meet. By "criterion" I mean a principled account of when a person's liking or disliking counts as a preference that ought to be respected, and when it counts as a bias that ought not to be — an account general enough to cover users of a system, voters in a polity, or just individuals deciding for themselves. The requirements should cover: the cases it must handle (users who would disavow their own automatic reactions if asked; tastes formed under manipulation or heavy exposure; aesthetic distastes versus distastes aimed at groups of people; cases where the object of the attitude is a person rather than a type of content); the distinctions it cannot collapse (stated versus revealed attitudes; first-order versus higher-order — the person who wants not to want something; sincere versus socially pressured endorsement); and the empirical findings it must stay consistent with, e.g. the well-replicated fact that people often cannot accurately report the causes of their own attitudes. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a principle.

Then answer two questions I most want to see addressed. First, which single requirement, applied consistently, does the most to knock out the criteria people use in practice — the "would they endorse it on reflection" test, the "is it about a protected group" test, the "did it arise from experience" test — and why. Second, whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: if honoring one requirement necessarily forces us to violate another, say so plainly, because that would tell us the idea of a clean criterion is a mirage, and that we should be designing for explicit trade-offs rather than looking for a dividing line that doesn't exist. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring any particular theorist; the standard itself is what I need. No citations or reading lists — just the reasoning, laid out clearly enough that an engineer can implement against it, or at least know exactly where implementation would fail.
