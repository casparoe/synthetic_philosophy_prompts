i'm a phd student in political philosophy and our markets reading group basically imploded last thursday over kidney sales, and i'm still not sure who actually won. it was two other students arguing, i mostly watched and took notes lol. for context we'd been discussing whether a regulated market in kidneys could be defensible in a country like the US -- think fixed price, state as the only buyer, the way iran runs its system.

round 1: daniel said the market is inherently exploitative -- when the only people who'd ever sell are the desperate, saying they "consented" is a joke. maya said banning the sale doesn't fix anyone's desperation, it just confiscates the one asset poor people have in that situation. she also said we already let people take kidney-level risks for pay (logging, crab fishing in alaska), so if risk-for-money were the real problem those jobs would be banned too. daniel said those jobs don't involve giving up a body part permanently, so the line isn't arbitrary.

round 2: maya pushed the deeper point: if you're allowed to give a kidney away for free, blocking the sale just punishes the poor donor for being poor -- the gifted kidney is fine, the sold one is a crime. daniel said that principle obviously proves too much, you can throw your vote away by staying home but you can't sell it, some things genuinely change status when money enters, and body parts are plausibly one of them -- that's the whole commodification worry. maya shot back that the "meaning" of a transaction isn't fixed like that: life insurance used to be seen as betting on deaths and now it's just prudent, so meaning-arguments can't carry any real weight.

round 3: maya raised the crowding out worry herself and then dismissed it -- even if some altruistic donors get crowded out, paid donors more than make up the shortfall, and paid plasma in the US outproduces countries that ban compensation. daniel said crowding out isn't only about supply numbers, it's about what living in that kind of market does to how people see each other over decades, and you can't read that off a supply chart.

then they both kinda ran out of steam and people drifted off. can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, not just who wins overall? and is there something both of them are missing? (i keep having the feeling that the iran example is doing more work than either of them admitted -- like the fact that it's the one real-world data point cuts in some direction neither of them articulated -- but i cant put my finger on it.)
