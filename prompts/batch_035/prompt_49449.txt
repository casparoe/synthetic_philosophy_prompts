I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of an argument two friends of mine got into the other evening. I was a bystander, I have my own half-formed views, and I'd rather not tilt the field by sharing them — I just want to know who is right about what.

Some background first, so the argument makes sense. The Victorian philosopher Henry Sidgwick, author of The Methods of Ethics (1874), is also known for something that sounds odd next to that reputation: he was one of the founders, in 1882, of the Society for Psychical Research, its first president, and for roughly the rest of his life one of its leading figures, overseeing investigations into telepathy, apparitions, hypnotism, and mediums in search of credible evidence that consciousness survives bodily death. His motive, as he presented it, was philosophical. In the Methods he had argued that practical reason is split — the rational pursuit of one's own greatest happiness and the rational pursuit of the greatest general happiness can pull in opposite directions — and that the two fully coincide only if the universe is morally governed, with consequences for individuals that outlast this life. He refused to take that on faith, and refused to dismiss it; so he proposed to look for evidence, and the survival of death seemed to him the place to look.

At dinner, my friend Marcus said this chapter of Sidgwick's career was actually his finest. (1) Once you concede that the split in practical reason is a real problem and not a verbal one, following it up empirically — asking whether there is any evidence that the universe is morally governed — is exactly what a serious philosopher ought to do, and most philosophers who notice the problem simply change the subject. (2) The manner of the investigation refutes any suspicion of credulity: under Sidgwick the Society insisted on protocols, published negative results, and was instrumental in exposing celebrated mediums as frauds, which is more than can be said for the self-policing of a lot of respectable science in that era. (3) And the fact that his ethics gave him a motive to hope that survival is real cannot contaminate the inquiry, because the same is true of any research program: physicists search where their theories tell them to look, and calling that bias rather than having a theory would end empirical science.

Dana wasn't having any of it. (1) She said Marcus had the order of explanation backwards: Sidgwick did not investigate because the evidence was promising; he investigated because his system required the answer to come out a certain way, and inquiry undertaken because you need one particular result is precisely the condition in which you should trust yourself least. (2) The physics analogy fails, she said, for a specific reason: a theory's predictions can fail, and a long enough string of failures eventually counts against the theory — but nothing Sidgwick could have found would have counted against the split in practical reason itself. The search could confirm his hope, but never undermine the motive for searching, and that asymmetry, she said, is the tell that separates a research program from a consolation. (3) In any case, she added, the split itself is overrated as a scandal: it is a problem only if you insist from the start that morality and self-interest must converge, and the honest position may just be that they sometimes don't — in which case spending decades hunting ghosts in order to avoid saying so is philosophy refusing to look its own conclusion in the face.

Marcus replied that Dana was demanding a kind of motiveless neutrality that no inquiry has ever had — everyone investigates what matters to them — and that Sidgwick's lifelong public agnosticism tells against her charge of belief-protection: he never claimed the evidence added up, said so repeatedly, and one cannot be protecting a belief from refutation if one never forms the belief. Dana answered that this only relocates the problem: it wasn't the belief but the hope that did the steering, and thirty years of steering by hope is a failure of intellectual character even if every individual report along the way was scrupulously honest. Neither of them would budge after that.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? Specifically, please say who is right at each of the numbered points, assess the two unnumbered closing replies, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write your answer in American English.
