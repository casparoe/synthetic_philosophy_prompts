I'm comparing my own view with a colleague's and would like your number first, then the machinery behind it.

Consider long-lived agents — states, institutions, or hypothetically an AI system — that can make binding commitments visible in advance and can model each other well enough to know what those commitments are. An acausal threat, as I'll use the term, is a threat issued on the strength of such a precommitment alone: the threatener demands some concession and announces that if it is refused, it will carry out the threatened harm even though carrying it out is a pure loss in that encounter — because it committed, where the target could see, to doing so, and its commitment technology is taken to be reliable. The standard worry is that the target of such a threat rationally submits whenever the demanded concession is smaller than the threatened harm, and that this remains true however small the demand, since the threatener can calibrate the demand to just under the harm. The proposed escape is to adopt a surrogate goal: precommit, publicly and in advance, to respond to any threat against some broadened class of targets — things one does not in fact care about — exactly as one would respond to a threat against what one does care about. Threatening anything in the class then costs the threatener as much as threatening the real asset, so a threatener who could threaten almost anything now faces a deterrence wall rather than a menu.

My credence that adopting such a surrogate-goal policy is, in expectation, better for the adopting agent over its whole future than not adopting one — assuming throughout that the agent genuinely can make its precommitments binding and visible — is about 0.55. My colleague's is about 0.9. Give me your own number, then decompose it into at least the following terms, assigning each a rough probability with one line of reasoning:

1. That a threatener capable of choosing among many targets ever benefits from carrying through on a refused threat, given that carrying through is a pure loss in that single encounter — i.e., that the credibility of acausal threats rests on something real (reputation across encounters, selection dynamics favoring committed types) and is not exhausted by one-shot bluffing.
2. That broadening the commitment class deters rather than merely enlarges: that the threatener's menu of viable demands shrinks by more than the adopting agent's set of obligations to retaliate grows.
3. That the standing costs of the policy — retaliations triggered through surrogate targets the agent would otherwise have ignored, plus the reputational overhead of maintaining the facade — are in expectation smaller than the concessions the policy saves.
4. Whatever further terms you think belong in the decomposition.
