I'm a graduate student in ethics of technology applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need a two- to three-page research proposal built around a question I can't yet state crisply. My half-formed idea: the "responsibility gap" literature on autonomous systems — the thought that when a machine-learning system, trained by one firm, deployed by another, and making decisions no operator could have predicted, causes a serious harm, no one in the chain may be responsible in the way our ordinary practices require — may not be describing a new phenomenon at all. Organizational theorists have long discussed the "problem of many hands": committees, bureaucracies, and corporations diffuse responsibility across so many contributors that no individual seems properly blameworthy. My hunch is that much of what gets called a responsibility gap is just the many-hands problem with software in the middle, but that there is one genuinely novel element: the system's behavior at the time of harm is not merely the sum of many people's contributions but the output of a training process that no contributor intended or could survey, so the "hands" have become illegible even to themselves. I can't decide whether that difference is philosophically deep or merely an epistemic complication dressed up as a metaphysical one.
