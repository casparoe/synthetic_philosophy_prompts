David Hume traced the origins of religion to fear, ignorance, and the mind's habit of projecting humanlike agency onto hidden causes. Suppose he could be given a full briefing on today's cognitive science of religion: findings that humans are biased toward over-detecting agency; that concepts violating just a few intuitive expectations are more memorable and more transmissible; and that costly, collectively performed rituals function to bind groups together. Write a reasoned prediction of his reactions to three questions. First, would he see this research as vindicating, refining, or superseding the genealogy he gives in The Natural History of Religion — does the agency-detection bias support his emphasis on fear and ignorance as the seed of religion, or does it shift the explanatory weight onto cognitive mechanisms he never considered? Second, would he conclude that a belief's having a natural psychological explanation undermines its epistemic standing, or — given his own scruples about deriving an ought from an is — would he resist that debunking inference? Third, would he expect these cognitive biases to keep religion permanently widespread, or do his views on how religions change (his account of the rise of monotheism, and of enthusiasm versus superstition) contain the resources for predicting major transformations in religion's future? Ground each prediction in specific Humean doctrines from his published works, and clearly flag the points where you are extrapolating beyond anything he actually wrote.
