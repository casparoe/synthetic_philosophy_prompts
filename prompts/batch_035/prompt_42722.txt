I'm defending my thesis in about two weeks and need to get ready for the Q&A, and honestly the committee situation is a bit unusual so I want to be careful about who will come at me from which angle. The thesis: special duties to compatriots are real, but they don't come from shared culture, national identity, or anything sentimental like that. They come from the state's ongoing coercion of everyone inside its borders. Basically my claim is that the special claims compatriots have on each other are downstream of the coercive apparatus itself -- you and I are both subject to the same police, courts, tax authority, etc., and that shared subjection is what makes us owe each other things we don't owe strangers abroad. On this view a cosmopolitan who says "borders are morally arbitrary so duties can't stop at them" is answering the wrong question -- the arbitrariness of borders doesn't matter because the duties track the coercion, not the line on the map. And the communitarian who roots special duties in shared culture is wrong too, but for the opposite reason.

The room: a politics/philosophy workshop, maybe 20 people. The composition matters, so let me lay it out. There's one very senior Kantian who thinks duties have to be grounded in universal reason and will not like that coercion, of all things, generates anything positive. There are two effective-altruist types, grad students mostly, who are strong cosmopolitans and will do the drowning-child / distance-is-arithmetic argument with total sincerity. There's one old-school communitarian who is actually kind of on my side conclusion-wise (compatriots do have special claims) but will hate my grounds, and I expect him to be the most personally invested critic. A handful of undecideds who mostly want to see whether the position is just nationalism in a nicer jacket.

What I want from you: a prep sheet, not a defense of the view for its own sake. List the objections I'm actually going to get from this specific room, in rough order of how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it (the Kantian's version of an objection is not the EA's version of the same objection, even when it's basically the same worry). For each: a reply I can give in about two minutes, and, honestly, where the reply is soft -- I'd rather know that now than find out in the room. Please include the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, and tell me if you think it's actually as dangerous as I think it is or whether I'm overestimating it.
