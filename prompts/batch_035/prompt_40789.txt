I am a high school senior in a reading group that has just finished the self-reference-heavy chapters of Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I built an argument in my notes that I now suspect proves too much. Here it is:

(1) For any consistent formal system F strong enough for arithmetic, one can construct a sentence G_F that, through Gödel numbering, says of itself that it is not provable in F.
(2) If F is consistent, G_F is true but unprovable in F; so F is incomplete — there are truths of arithmetic it cannot derive.
(3) The engine of this incompleteness is self-reference: the trouble starts the moment a system's sentences manage to be about that very system.
(4) A mind is self-aware when its representational machinery takes itself as an object — when it contains representations of its own representing.
(5) A self-aware mind that can do arithmetic is therefore in the position of F: its representations loop back on themselves in just the way G_F does.
(6) So such a mind is incomplete about itself — either inconsistent, or there are truths about it that it can never in principle come to know.
(7) I am not inconsistent, and I am self-aware.
(8) Therefore I have a permanent Gödelian blind spot: self-awareness comes with truths about myself that I could never know, whether I am a brain or a program.

On its face, (8) is exactly the kind of thing the book made me want to believe. But two things make me suspect it proves too much. First, the blind spot in (2) appears for F even when F has no model of itself at all — Peano arithmetic does not represent itself, yet its Gödel sentence still exists and is still unprovable — so I cannot see what work self-awareness is doing, and if it is doing none, my conclusion does not follow. Second, a friend in the group mentioned having read that arithmetic with addition alone, without multiplication, is complete and decidable, which — if that is right — would make the blind spot a fact about the strength of a theory, not about minds looking at themselves. And if self-reference were really the engine, it seems a self-aware being could escape the blind spot just by declining to model itself, which would make (4) a choice rather than a fate.

Which step should I give up, and why did that step look fine to me when I wrote it down? If more than one step could take the fall, tell me which rejection is cheapest and what each costs. I would most like to keep (3), and the general idea that self-reference and self-awareness are deeply connected, but I want to know exactly what that loyalty buys me and what it hides. If there is a hidden equivocation or slide between two steps, name it precisely — that is the part I most want to see.
