My roommate works on ML evals and hit me with this argument at dinner, and it's been bugging me since. He swears its just Goodhart's law followed to its logical conclusion. Each step looked fine to me on its own, which is the annoying part, but the ending can't be right. Here it is:

(1) Every metric is a proxy. What you actually care about — learning, health, safety, whether software works — never shows up on a dashboard by itself; you only ever measure things correlated with it.

(2) Once people start optimizing against a proxy, they drift onto whatever raises the metric most cheaply, and the cheapest ways to raise a metric are the ones that skip the goal entirely — teaching to the test, padding airline schedules, whatever.

(3) Nothing gets measured at scale without getting optimized against. Nobody builds KPIs for fun; if a number matters to anyone, someone's incentives are already shaped around it.

(4) So any metric that's load-bearing enough to act on is already being gamed, and the harder it's leaned on, the less it measures.

(5) Since we can't observe goals directly, correction has to come through metrics too — the whole point of feedback is to put optimization pressure on a number, whether it's a grade, a benchmark score, or a market price.

(6) But by (4), pressure is exactly the thing that hollows out the signal. So every feedback system is busy wrecking its own instruments: the harder you lean on a measure, the less it tells you.

(7) Therefore feedback never transmits real information. A coach's stopwatch is useless to the swimmer, a price is useless to a buyer, tasting the soup teaches the cook nothing, and evaluation in general is theater.

I don't buy (7) — I've definitely gotten better at things because someone kept score — but when I go back through the steps they all still look plausible individually, and I can't find the seam. Which premise should I be rejecting, and what does rejecting it cost? If the honest answer is that several steps are only sorta true, tell me which one is doing the real work, and what the argument had to quietly assume to make it look as solid as the rest.
