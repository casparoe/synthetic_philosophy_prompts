I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on formal decision theory, and we've just finished three weeks on counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail that I found genuinely unsettled. In the blackmail case, my classmates were unanimous: the letter arrives whether or not you have the embarrassing condition, so paying is throwing money at a correlation you didn't cause. But in counterfactual mugging — Omega flips a fair coin before you existed and simulates you: on heads it shows up with $10,000, on tails it asks you for $100 — the class split down the middle and never recombined. The "pay" side says you should be the kind of agent who pays, because in expectation over the coin, that policy nets you $4,950; the "refuse" side says that once the coin has landed tails and you've seen it, the heads branch is causally closed, and handing over $100 is just lighting it on fire. What struck me is that each side fully understands the other's arithmetic — nobody disputes the expected values — and yet three weeks of argument produced literally no movement on either side. When the TA tried to defuse it by suggesting that "rational" is simply ambiguous, both camps objected that this makes rationality toothless: a decision theory that can't tell you what to do when the coin has already landed isn't doing its job.
