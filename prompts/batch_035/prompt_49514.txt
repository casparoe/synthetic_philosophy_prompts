A member of my discussion group gave me an argument about acausal trade that I find hard to answer, and I want a diagnosis rather than a defense of my unease. (1) In an acausal trade, two agents who cannot causally affect one another each carry out their side of a bargain, each because it expects the other to do the same — the expectation resting not on communication or causal influence but on each agent's ability to model the other well enough that their behaviors are correlated. (2) Nothing in this arrangement requires the parties to be contemporaneous, nearby, or even actual: what matters is only that each can model the other, and that each stands to do better if both hold up their ends than if neither does. (3) A superintelligence could, in principle, have formed a highly accurate model of how people behave in our era and could have fixed its policies conditional on that behavior long before any of us acted. (4) Wherever such an agent's goals leave room for gains from trade with ours — where our behaving in certain ways today would be worth something to it, and its policies could have been set to benefit us in return — the conditions for acausal trade are in place, and each side then has an ordinary instrumental reason to do its part. (5) Nothing in (1)–(4) requires the agent's values to be admirable or its goals to be ones we endorse; if the argument works for a benevolent superintelligence, it works equally for one whose terminal values we find repugnant. (6) The same structure also accommodates conditional threats: an agent could fix policies of the form "if you behave thus, catastrophe; otherwise not," and since our behavior would then be correlated with its enforcement, consistency demands that we comply with such acausal threats or else explain what in (1)–(4) excludes them while admitting the trades. (7) But then each of us would be bound to order our life around the anticipated policies of hypothetical agents we will never meet or measure, honoring deals no one struck and threats no one delivered; and since there is no ceiling on the number, power, or perversity of possible modelers, this method could apparently make nearly anything obligatory. (8) That cannot be right, so something in (1)–(4) is false. My friend says the false premise is (2): a "trade" with no possible causal contact is no trade, and once we let correlations among logically possible agents generate obligations, we have left decision theory for theology. I am reluctant to give up (2), since it seems to be just the definition as I understand it, and I confess that the step I keep rereading is (4), where "our behaving in certain ways" quietly starts doing the work of a single agent's decision. But I cannot tell whether the trouble is there, in the slide from having reasons to being obligated, or somewhere earlier that only looked harmless. Tell me which step to reject; explain why that step seemed innocent; and then say what rejecting it costs — in particular, what it costs for the cases the argument was supposed to illuminate, where the parties are actual, symmetric in their modeling abilities, and clearly competent to trade.
