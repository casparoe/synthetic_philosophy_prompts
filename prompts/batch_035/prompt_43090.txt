In my philosophy of mind seminar we spent three weeks on the extended mind thesis, and my study group has since produced an argument our TA says cannot possibly be right but refuses to tell us where it breaks. The argument runs as follows. (1) Parity: whenever an external resource is as reliably available, as fluently deployed, and as deeply integrated into a subject's problem-solving as the corresponding internal resource would be, it counts as part of that subject's mind -- this is just the principle Clark and Chalmers use to fold Otto's notebook into Otto's memory. (2) Consider my colleague Dmitri: over years of joint work I have offloaded so much to him -- he remembers my deadlines, corrects my arithmetic, anticipates my objections before I voice them -- that his contributions to my reasoning are now as reliable and as automatic as anything my own brain does, and the integration is mutual, since I do the same for him. (3) By parity, Dmitri is part of my mind, and I am part of his. (4) Parthood is transitive and symmetric in the way needed here: if my mind is partly constituted by Dmitri and his by me, then whatever else constitutes his mind -- including his other collaborators, integrated into his problem-solving exactly as I am -- stands in the same constitutive relation to my mind, and their collaborators likewise. (5) Chaining through co-authorships, shared lab practices, referee reports, and citation networks, the entire international research community collapses into a single mind that has been believing, doubting, and discovering things for centuries. (6) That conclusion is absurd -- nobody thinks a tenure committee is literally a ganglion of one planetary intellect -- so somewhere in the chain something has gone rotten.

My own suspicion is that the trouble lies in step (4), in the slide from "Dmitri is partly constitutive of my mind" to "whatever is partly constitutive of his is thereby constitutive of mine." But when I try to defend that suspicion I find myself merely asserting that parthood of cognitive systems must be transitive the way parthood of chairs is, which our TA has already told me is question-begging, since the whole dispute is about what kind of parthood cognitive integration delivers. I would also, if forced, prefer to keep (1) and (2) and pay almost anything else -- I've become genuinely attached to the idea that Otto's notebook is part of Otto, and I don't want to be driven back to saying the mind just is the brain.

So: diagnose the argument for me. Respond in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion -- between them, your five points should name the precise step you reject, explain why that step looked fine on first inspection, and say what the defenders of the extended mind must concede in order to block the slide from Otto's notebook to the world-spanning group mind.
