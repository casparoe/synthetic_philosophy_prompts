At a recent meeting of our local astronomy society, two longtime members, Renata and Sam, got into a sustained argument about what, if anything, the silence in the sky tells us. I took notes, and neither of them has a clear sense of where things stand, so I'd like an adjudication. The exchange went as follows.

(1) Renata opened with a Bayesian point: we've been listening for radio signals, probing nearby stars, and scanning for megastructures and anomalous waste heat for decades, and we've found nothing. Where you would expect to see traces if technological life were common, the absence of traces is genuine evidence of absence. The universe therefore has to be mostly empty of technological civilizations, or at least the evidence points that way.

(2) Sam responded that the search has covered a minuscule fraction of the relevant parameter space — a thin slice of frequencies, a tiny sample of stars, sensitivity thresholds that would miss civilizations modestly weaker than our own. A failed search of a thimbleful of a haystack tells you almost nothing about the haystack, and a civilization even slightly more advanced than ours might communicate in ways we simply don't know how to look for, so the silence is extremely weak evidence of anything.

(3) Renata said this all misses the stronger argument, which doesn't depend on detection at all: expansion. A single expansionist civilization with self-replicating probes could colonize the galaxy in a few million years, and the galaxy has had billions of years to produce one. If that had happened, their artifacts would be in our solar system and their handiwork visible everywhere, regardless of whether they broadcast anything we can decode. So even granting everything Sam said about radio searches, the failure of anyone to be here is strong evidence that expansionist technological life is rare or short-lived.

(4) Sam made an anthropic reply: observers can only ask this question on planets that evolved them. If colonization were common, no one would be around on a quiet, untouched planet to wonder about the silence — anyone there would be replaced by or assimilated into the colonizers, or would themselves be one of them. So our vantage point is not a random sample of the galaxy; it is pre-conditioned on nobody having colonized us, and you cannot treat a pre-conditioned observation as an unbiased test of the hypothesis that colonizers are rare. At most, the silence tells us about the conditions under which observers like us can arise.

(5) Renata closed with two rejoinders. First, that move proves too much: if you're allowed to say our observation is pre-conditioned on the outcome, you could shield the hypothesis of a galaxy crammed with colonizers from any observation anyone could ever make, which is a recipe for unfalsifiability, not for reasoning. Second, the anthropic point cuts the other way: if colonization were common, the overwhelming majority of observers would find themselves inside the colonized state, so the fact that we find ourselves in an apparently untouched corner is itself the relevant data, and it counts against common colonization just as much as the radio silence does.

Both of them have decent instincts, and I genuinely can't sort out which of the five moves is doing what. Please adjudicate step by step: for each of the five moves, say whether it is right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or plainly mistaken, and justify your rating. Then say who, if anyone, is more right overall, and identify anything both of them are missing — particularly on whether the anthropic reasoning in moves (4) and (5) is legitimate, and on how it interacts with the expansion argument in move (3). Write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a careful letter.
