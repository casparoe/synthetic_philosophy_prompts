I've been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about what counts as a disease, and I want to know whether they add up to a coherent position in the naturalism/normativism debate, or whether they force me to give something up. Here are the verdicts.

Gingivitis and dental caries are diseases, even though mild gum inflammation is close to universal — statistical commonness doesn't matter. Myopia is a disease; presbyopia in an otherwise healthy sixty-five-year-old is not, it's just aging. Congenital deafness I'm inclined to call a disability but not a disease, though I waver. Homosexuality was never a disease: the 1973 removal from the DSM was the correction of an error, not a reclassification of something that had legitimately been a disease. Grief likewise did not become a disease when prolonged grief disorder was added to the DSM. But a psychopath — callous, fearless, feeling no distress, thriving by his own lights — does have a disorder, even though nothing about him is failing from his point of view. And a person with an indolent tumor found incidentally on a scan has a disease, full stop, even though she feels no harm and may never suffer from it. One more: ordinary sadness after a job loss is not a disease, even though it impairs sleep, concentration, and daily functioning for weeks — which is roughly the checklist depression is diagnosed with.

The tensions I can already see: the homosexuality and psychopath verdicts seem to make disease depend on harm or on some evaluative judgment (normativism), but the indolent-tumor verdict seems to make it independent of harm — the tumor is just machinery gone wrong (naturalism). And the presbyopia verdict seems to turn on what's statistically typical for the species at a given age, which can't be what makes homosexuality not a disease, since same-sex attraction is at least as statistically atypical as presbyopia yet was never even a candidate for disease.

So: is there one principle — or a small set of explicitly conjoined criteria — that captures all of these? State it precisely enough to be applied mechanically, tell me which clause each of my cases is buying, and be honest about which of my verdicts you would overturn to keep the principle simple, rather than tacking on epicycles to save them. Then apply it to three cases I haven't decided: male-pattern baldness in a man who minds it greatly and one who doesn't; prediabetes in an asymptomatic person; and the bone-density loss astronauts suffer in orbit — if the answer depends on whether the reference class is humans in Earth gravity, doesn't that make disease relative to environment in a way that should worry the naturalist?
