retired solid-state physicist here — spent forty years on ferromagnets and phase transitions, now reading philosophy in retirement and getting owned by it regularly. i've been chewing on Black's two spheres, the thought experiment where the universe contains exactly two qualitatively identical spheres in a perfectly symmetric arrangement, and the question is whether "there being two" even means anything when nothing distinguishes them. here's my angle, straight from my old job: in physics we're completely comfortable with symmetric laws producing an asymmetric state. the magnet points somewhere even tho nothing in the hamiltonian prefers north — spontaneous symmetry breaking. so: write me a poem, maybe 14-18 lines, in two stanzas that are mirror images of each other — the second repeats the first's lines in reverse order, or the rhyme scheme is a palindrome (abba abba, your call) — so the form has exactly the symetry Black gives his universe, and no line should tell you which stanza is "first" except by its position on the page. then a short final section that breaks the symmetry, and the breaking should BE the argument: the moment someone asks "which sphere is which," they've picked a direction, the way the magnet does, and done something the universe itself never did. don't settle whether there are two spheres or one — leave that standing like the magnet's field, arbitrary but real. someone who knows the identity of indiscernibles should be able to read the argument off the form, and someone who doesn't should just get a good poem about a very empty universe.
