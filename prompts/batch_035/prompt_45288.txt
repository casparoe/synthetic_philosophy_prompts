I am a health economist by training: my daily vocabulary consists of QALYs, DALYs, incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, willingness-to-pay thresholds, equity weights, and social welfare functions. My closest friend is a moral philosopher, and we spent most of last weekend arguing about Scanlon's contractualism, which he defends with real conviction. He walked me through the core idea — that an act or policy is wrong if it could be reasonably rejected by someone in the position of being burdened by it, where the relevant comparisons are personal, complaint by complaint — and then through what he called the aggregation problem: Scanlon's insistence that the strength of a single person's claim is not automatically outweighed by adding up many smaller claims, illustrated by the case where one person's death is at stake on one side and, on the other, a large number of people each facing some minor burden. I told him this is exactly the logic my field had to reject when it started summing QALYs across a population and ranking interventions by total health gain, and that his position amounts to an insistence on lexicographic or threshold-based priority that would make most cost-effectiveness analysis incoherent. He countered that I had smuggled in a commensurability assumption that his view was precisely designed to deny, and neither of us managed to land the point.

Restate his position in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Translate the idea of reasonable rejection into the language of claims on a fixed health budget, the individualist restriction on comparing complaints into the language of equity weights and social welfare functions, and the anti-aggregation claim into the language of thresholds, discontinuities, and separability across persons. Then give me the residue report: which parts of his view translate cleanly into something an HTA body could in principle implement, which parts require distorting either his view or my framework to force the fit, and which parts have no expression in cost-effectiveness analysis at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue reflects a genuine gap in my framework — something health economics cannot say but should — or whether his position, once forced into precise form, dissolves into something my field already handles with equity weights and thresholds, in which case our argument was partly verbal.
