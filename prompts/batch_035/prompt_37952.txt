I've been defending two-dimensional semantics in my reading group, and someone finally cornered me with an argument that the framework cannot be doing the job it advertises. Some background first. Two-dimensional semantics assigns each expression two intensions. The secondary intension is the ordinary possible-worlds function, evaluated with our world fixed as actual. The primary intension is evaluated with each scenario considered as actual — it captures how the extension would fall out if that scenario were the way things really are. The framework's great advertisement is that it accommodates Kripke's necessary a posteriori truths: water is H2O is necessary (constant secondary intension) yet a posteriori (contingent primary intension — there are epistemically possible scenarios in which the watery stuff turns out to be something else). Here is the argument I can't answer.

(1) A truth is a priori iff its primary intension is true at every epistemically possible scenario; this biconditional is not an optional extra but the load-bearing wall of the framework.
(2) Water is H2O is a posteriori. So by (1) there is an epistemically possible scenario — Twin Earth — in which, considered as actual, that sentence comes out false.
(3) On Twin Earth considered as actual, the clear, drinkable, ocean-filling liquid is XYZ, and the inhabitants' word for it refers to XYZ.
(4) The two-dimensionalist's explanation of why Twin Earth's word refers to XYZ rather than to H2O cannot appeal to the actual reference of our term, since a scenario considered as actual is by hypothesis being taken as the real world; whatever settles the extension must be internal to the scenario.
(5) What is internal to the scenario, as the Twin Earth case is always presented, is just the qualitative profile — the watery stuff, the patterns of usage, the environments — together with the stipulated underlying chemistry. So the extension of the word at any scenario considered as actual is settled by that scenario's own facts, without any input from our world.
(6) The actual world is simply one more scenario considered as actual. So the extension of our word — its reference — is settled by our world's qualitative and chemical facts alone, without input from anywhere else.
(7) Reference settled by qualitative facts alone is descriptive reference-fixing under another name.
(8) If the word gets its reference descriptively, then the claim that water is H2O is true in virtue of our world satisfying the description with H2O — which makes it exactly as contingent as the claim that the stuff that actually fills the oceans is H2O, a paradigm of a contingent a posteriori truth.
(9) So two-dimensional semantics cannot hold onto the necessity of water being H2O. Either the secondary intension inherits the descriptive fixation, in which case there are no necessary a posteriori truths at all and Kripke's cases were misdescribed from the start, or the descriptive story fails for the actual world, in which case (5) is not a general account of primary intensions and the framework loses its explanation of the very cases it was built for.

My friend says this shows the framework was descriptivism wearing Kripke's coat: it doesn't accommodate the necessary a posteriori, it quietly eliminates it, which proves too much for a view whose selling point was reconciliation. I can see that the argument is either invalid somewhere or valid and devastating, but I can't tell which, and I can't locate the weak step. I would most like to keep (1) and the necessity of water being H2O. Walk me through which premise or inference to give up, why it looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs — including what the old-fashioned descriptivist and the Millian would each say the two-dimensionalist is really paying.
