I run a student debate society — about 18 people, weekly 90-minute sessions, and we're very proud of our open-floor policy: anyone can speak on any topic, no view is off-limits. Recently we've had a member (call him D.) who never breaks a single written rule, but uses the open floor to dominate: he signs up for every slot, uses his time to "question" whether other people should've been allowed to speak at all, and last week spent his slot arguing the society shouldn't exist. Half the club wants him gone; the other half says we'd be total hypocrites, since tolerating views we hate is literally our founding thing. Rather than me deciding from the chair, I want to turn it into a session where the club feels its way through the problem itself.

Design a structured deliberation for that session: rules of procedure, roles (D. will actually be there, cooperating, playing himself), some decision the group has to reach by the end of the 90 minutes, and a debrief. I want them to run into the paradox on their own — the moment where kicking him out requires naming a principle that seems to condemn the kicking-out itself, but keeping him is visibly destroying the thing the principle was for — before I mention Popper or the phrase "paradox of tolerance" at the very end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess is it collapses into a personal referendum on D. instead of a fight about rules — how do I steer away from that?), and be honest about which part of the real philosophical issue an activity like this just can't reproduce no matter how I set it up.
