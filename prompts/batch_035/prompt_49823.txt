The question:

"Question 4 (25 points). State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, being careful to list all of its hypotheses. Explain what strategy-proofness means for a social choice function, and why the theorem is sometimes summarized as saying that every reasonable voting method with three or more alternatives is manipulable. Finally, explain how restricting the domain of preferences provides an escape route, and describe a rule that is strategy-proof on that restricted domain."

The student's answer, verbatim:

"The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that when there are three or more alternatives, any social choice function satisfying non-dictatorship, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and unrestricted domain must be manipulable. Strategy-proofness means that no voter can obtain an outcome they prefer by misreporting their preferences, so sincere voting is a dominant strategy for everyone. So every voting system that is reasonable — meaning it doesn't concentrate all power in a single voter, treats all preference profiles as admissible, and doesn't let rankings over irrelevant alternatives distort the outcome — is vulnerable to strategic misreporting.

The proof works by first showing that any strategy-proof rule must be monotonic, meaning that raising an alternative in your ballot can never cause that alternative to lose. Monotonicity together with the other conditions forces the rule to be a dictatorship, where a single voter's entire ranking becomes society's ranking. A useful byproduct is that the implication runs both ways: any monotonic rule is strategy-proof, so checking monotonicity gives us an easy test for whether a voting method can be manipulated.

The escape route is domain restriction. If preferences are single-peaked — each voter has an ideal point on a left–right axis, and their preference for alternatives falls off monotonically with distance in either direction from that point — then majority rule selects the median voter's ideal point, and this median rule is strategy-proof. Since single-peaked preferences describe most real electorates, where politics is largely one-dimensional, the practical bite of the theorem is limited: it applies to the unrestricted domain, and real domains are restricted enough that the escape is usually available."

Where the student is right, I'd still like a sentence confirming it, so my feedback doesn't leave the student guessing which parts of their answer survived scrutiny.
