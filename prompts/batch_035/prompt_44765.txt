I serve on the grants and oversight committee of a small development foundation. For six years we have funded a community health program run by a local partner organization in a rural region where the distribution of nearly all outside assistance — seed stock, medical referrals, food aid — is brokered by village patrons who act as customary intermediaries between communities and any external agency. Our partner pays these patrons a documented share of program resources, roughly eight percent, in exchange for their facilitation, and last year its director accepted a ceremonial gift of livestock from a council of village elders at a public ceremony marking the program's anniversary. A newly commissioned external audit has flagged both practices: the eight percent share as undisclosed kickbacks, and the livestock as an impermissible personal benefit. Our country director responds that the audit applies a template of corruption that presupposes an impartial state and functioning formal institutions — that in this region the patronage network is not a deviation from the rules but is itself how anything gets delivered, that bypassing it would mean the poorest households never hear of the program at all, and that the gift was accepted on behalf of the organization and slaughtered at a community feast. The committee is split between members who hold that anything short of direct, unmediated distribution is corrupt by definition, and members who argue that condemning the local way of doing things is a kind of institutional imperialism — particularly awkward for us, since our own board includes a consultant whose firm lobbies governments on behalf of pharmaceutical clients. At our next meeting we vote on whether to renew the grant, and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. Please draft that memo. It should take a clear position on renewal; propose concrete criteria for distinguishing acceptable brokerage and gift exchange from corruption in settings like this one — criteria our future audits could actually apply, not vague appeals to context; and give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side the recommendation comes down against, including the uncomfortable comparison with lobbying practices at home.
