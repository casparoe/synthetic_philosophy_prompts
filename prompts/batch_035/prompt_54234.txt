I'm a novelist, three years into a book whose formal conceit is this: it is narrated in strict alternation by two voices that the reader only gradually realizes belong to one person — a woman in her sixties who, decades earlier, underwent a commissurotomy for intractable epilepsy. I've read enough of the popular and semi-popular literature to be suspicious of it. My editor wants a late twist in which the two narrators flatly contradict each other about a memory they shared, and I can't decide whether that's licensed by the actual clinical picture or just a convenient fiction dressed up in neuroscience. Before I commit the book to a stance, I want to know which of the genuinely open questions in this area matter most for representing such a patient truthfully — and which are overrated for that purpose, however live they may be in the journals.

The candidates I can see, though I may be missing some: whether the separated hemispheres support two subjects of experience or rather one subject with impaired interhemispheric access; how much of the classic experimental behavior could be explained by cross-cuing between the hemispheres or by the left hemisphere's confabulating tendencies, rather than by real disunity of consciousness; whether the right hemisphere has an inner life of the morally relevant sort — the sort that ought to matter for consent, for how her family speaks to her, for whether either "voice" can be held responsible for anything; and how much the famous results generalize at all, given how few patients had complete rather than partial callosotomies. Rank these for me, and defend the ordering. I want to know your criteria — what makes a question central for a novel trying to be honest about this condition versus peripheral, what makes one intractable for a writer who can only consult the literature and clinicians rather than run experiments — and specifically what would have to be true for your ranking to change.

Two things I'd like you to rule on explicitly. First, which implications that people commonly draw from the split-brain cases don't follow at all — I have in mind the pop-psychological habit of concluding that everyone is "really two people," or that the left hemisphere is the seat of the self, and I'd like to know whether these are distortions of the cases or exaggerations of something genuinely there. Second, and this is the question that keeps me up at night: if I write the two narrators as fully distinct centers of experience, am I giving the reader a useful analogy for the self, or am I falsifying a condition I have a duty to depict accurately? I would rather restructure the book than publish a metaphor masquerading as medicine, so tell me where, in your judgment, the honest novelistic representation sits.
