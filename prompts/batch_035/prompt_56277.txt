So I'm on the design review board at a mid-sized studio that makes open-world RPGs (I'd rather not say which one, and please don't guess in the memo). We have to decide next week whether to ship a system the narrative team has been building, and I want you to draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the board before the meeting. It needs a real bottom line, not a fence-sit.

The system, roughly: most NPCs are generic, but ones tagged as kin to other NPCs have memory. If the player kills someone's family member (or lets them die in a quest), the survivors grieve for about thirty in-game days. They weep at the gravesite in the morning, refuse to trade with you, snap at you in dialogue, and a few of them can be slowly consoled back over weeks if the player puts in effort. The writers are proud of it. Playtesters mostly found it haunting; one said it was the first time a game made him feel like a perpetrator instead of a hero, which he meant as praise.

The fight on the board is roughly this. One faction, led by our systems lead, says the only morally relevant thing here is the players: the system either trains empathy or feeds cruelty — we already saw a streamer wipe out a village and then follow the mourning mother around for content, laughing. On that view this is just an ordinary content-design question like violence ratings, and we can settle it with playtest data about player behavior, no philosophy required. The other faction, mostly narrative folks, says that can't be the whole question: if the NPCs' distress were ever real in a morally relevant sense, the effects on players wouldn't be the thing that matters most, and they want the memo to say where the studio stands on that, even if the answer ends up being "nowhere, it's a lump of code." There's also a wrinkle nobody's framework predicted: our QA lead reported that testers didn't feel guilty toward the NPCs, they felt guilty about their own character, and a couple said it felt wierd to be consoled by NPCs they'd wronged. And attached to the whole thing is a conditional-shipping proposal: an option to toggle the system off, content notes for QA, and a short design stance paragraph for our design bible about whether NPC suffering counts for anything.

So please draft the memo. It should recommend one concrete course — ship, don't ship, or ship with conditions — with reasons the losing faction could actually respect. Whatever side you come down on, take the other side's strongest point seriously: if you go with the player-effects view, be honest about what would have to be true for the narrative faction to be right and whether our NPCs are anywhere near that; if you go the other way, explain why that's not absurd when we're talking about scripted code. And please say something about the QA finding, since it seems to cut across both factions' stories. Keep it under two pages, circulateable, no bibliography or citations — just the reasoning. And don't name-drop philosophers unless the name is genuinely doing work; half the board glazes over at that stuff, myself included if I'm honest.
