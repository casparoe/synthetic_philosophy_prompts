Last term I took a graduate seminar on intergenerational justice, and it left me with a notebook full of verdicts about discounting the future that I cannot make consistent with one another, so I want help extracting whatever principle, if any, underlies them. Here is what I actually believe, case by case. When my grandfather spends money on a trip he can enjoy now rather than saving it for a somewhat better trip in five years, that seems permissible — even sensible — even though the later trip would be better in expectation. When I catch myself preferring a slightly more painful dentist appointment in six months to a slightly less painful one next week, and then, once next week arrives, still wanting to push it off, I think something has genuinely gone wrong — the preference reversal feels like a rational failure and not just a taste. When a government discounts the welfare of people alive in 2200 simply because they live later — the kind of pure time preference that made the Stern Review fight so bitter — I think that is a moral error and not merely a policy choice: a drowning stranger two centuries from now matters no less than a drowning stranger today. Yet I also think it is perfectly legitimate for that same government to discount future benefits because the future is uncertain, and because people in 2200 will probably be richer, so that the same dollar does them less good.

Then there are the verdicts that bother me most. I hold that past suffering is morally closed: no good done now can offset a pain endured last year, and we cannot compensate the dead for what they went through. But if temporal position as such is no reason to count future people less, it is hard to see why it is a reason to count past people less, and I cannot explain why I am entitled to the asymmetry. I also believe my own future self is fair game for discounting in a way that strangers' futures are not: it seems fine for me to enjoy resources now at the expense of my eighty-year-old self in a way it would be monstrous to enjoy them at the expense of a stranger — though I notice I would not accept the mirror-image claim that my past self was entitled to discount me. Finally, a case I genuinely cannot decide: my grandmother is dying, and time with her this month seems to matter more — not just to me, but somehow impersonally — than the identical amount of time spread across her earlier years, as though the exhaustibility of her remaining time changes the arithmetic rather than just our feelings about it.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all of this? Two half-formed candidates I keep circling are that discounting is legitimate only as a response to uncertainty and never to temporal position as such, and that agent-relative discounting of one's own future is permissible while impersonal discounting is not — but I cannot tell whether either delivers my verdict on the reversal case, the past-suffering asymmetry, or the grandmother. Please state whatever principle you recommend precisely enough to generate verdicts on cases I have not listed — for instance, whether an immortal with no uncertainty about her future should discount it at all, or whether I should care less about a risk of pain that will materialize after my death — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts cannot be saved, since I suspect at least one has to go.
