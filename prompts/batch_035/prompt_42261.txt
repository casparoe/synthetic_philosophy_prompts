I run a small philosophy reading group — eight of us, meeting fortnightly in a pub's back room; the members are sharp but non-specialist: a retired engineer, two GPs, a primary-school teacher, a solicitor, and a couple of others with no particular background. Over the past year we've worked through the knowledge argument, the hard problem of consciousness, and a session on Mary's room that went unusually well, so the group now has a confident but shaky grip on the idea that physical explanation seems to leave something out. Next month I want to tackle panpsychism and its combination problem, and I've decided — possibly against my better judgement — not to assign any reading, so the whole thing lives or dies on my ninety minutes at the whiteboard.

What I want from you is not an explanation of panpsychism but a teaching plan for the evening. Specifically:

1. Order of operations. There's an obvious dilemma I keep going back and forth on. Option A: present the hard-problem-shaped motivation first (physical science tells us about structure and relation, not intrinsic nature; so maybe experience is what fills in the intrinsic nature of matter), let the group feel the pull of the view, and only then spring the combination problem on them. Option B: lead with the combination problem as a freestanding puzzle — how could any number of tiny experiences, or experiential subjects, add up to one unified subject of experience at all? — and only afterwards reveal that this puzzle is what anyone buys into when they buy panpsychism. Argue for one order, and tell me what the wrong order costs the evening.

2. The carrying example. I want one concrete scenario that carries the whole session. My instinct is something like the transition from two streams to one in split-brain or in the binding of distributed neural processes into a single visual experience, but I suspect these smuggle in too much neuroscience and not enough metaphysics. Propose the example (it can be ordinary or slightly artificial, but it must be one I can hold on the whiteboard), say what it must have and what it must lack, and say why the naive example — two micro-experiences of red and blue somehow mixing into a macro-experience of purple, as if combination were colour mixing — is actively dangerous, and where in the sequence I should dismantle it.

3. Disentangling the sub-problems. The combination problem is really several problems wearing one hat, and I want the group to leave able to tell them apart: at minimum the subject-summing problem (how do many subjects become one subject?), the problem of how qualities combine, and the structural problem of how a subject with anything like our sensory architecture could be constituted by things that individually have nothing resembling a sensory structure. Decide which of these the group should meet first, which is the one they'll genuinely engage with, and which I should wave at and defer.

4. The misconception map. Predict the misconceptions this exact audience will form, in the order they'll form them. My guesses: that panpsychism claims rocks are conscious (conflating panpsychism with animism); that micropsychism is just vitalism come back; that the combination problem is simply the hard problem with smaller pieces and so nothing new; and — from the retired engineer, I'd bet — that experience at the fundamental level is a hypothesis with empirical content that physics could in principle test. Tell me where in the sequence each of these should be headed off, and whether any of them is better left alive until the end as fuel for discussion.

5. The check. One question I can ask in the last ten minutes that would show me whether they've understood the combination problem as a problem about subjects, rather than just about getting the right qualitative contents into one place.

6. What to refuse to explain. Say openly which parts of the terrain I should refuse to go into at this level — I'm thinking constitutive versus emergent varieties, the palette problem, the question of whether the micro-level has any integrated subjects of its own at all — and how to mark them as deferred rather than settled.

Please write the plan in British English. It should be disciplined about what depends on what; if a later piece of the evening only works because of an earlier one, say so.
