I have been keeping a notebook of my own reactions in emotionally loaded situations, and I would like to know what theory of emotion, if any, my verdicts add up to. For orientation, I understand the main candidates as three families: judgment theories, on which an emotion essentially includes an evaluative judgment (fear involves judging that there is danger); perceptual theories, on which emotions are perceptions of values or formal objects, akin to sense perception and capable of misrepresenting; and feeling theories, on which emotions are felt bodily states with no essential representational content.

My verdicts, case by case:

1. Hiking, I mistook a curved branch for a snake and recoiled before recognizing it; for a second or two after recognition, the fear persisted. Verdict: genuine emotion, and irrational.
2. Reading a novel, I felt dread on behalf of a character I know full well does not exist. Verdict: genuine emotion, and — oddly, since I believe nothing dangerous is happening — not irrational. I do not consider myself irrational for being moved by fiction.
3. A friend with a spider phobia sincerely asserts the spider is harmless, could pass a quiz on its harmlessness, and still panics on seeing one. Verdict: genuine emotion, irrational.
4. Watching a stranger's conference talk collapse into a slideshow failure while the stranger remained cheerful, I felt vicarious embarrassment on the stranger's behalf. Verdict: genuine emotion, and fitting.
5. At my father's grave, decades after his death, fully accepting that nothing bad is happening now, I wept. Verdict: genuine grief, and I would resent calling it irrational.
6. A chess-playing acquaintance describes a wordless surge of something like exultation the instant she spots a winning combination, before she can articulate anything at all. Verdict: I am inclined to count that as an emotion, despite the absence of anything articulated.
7. The stomach-drop in a fast elevator. Verdict: not an emotion, merely a bodily sensation.
8. Nostalgia triggered by a song. Verdict: an emotion, though I cannot say what it is about; it seems directed at the past in general rather than at any fact or object.

I am aware of at least two tensions in this set. Between cases 1 and 3 on the one hand and 2 and 5 on the other: in the former pair, the absence of the corresponding belief makes me call the emotion irrational, while in the latter pair the same absence does not. And between 6 and 7: a wordless state I count as an emotion, versus a wordless state I refuse to.

My questions are these. Is there a single account — one of the three families, or a principled hybrid — that delivers all eight verdicts as I stated them? If not, which verdicts would you overturn, and on what grounds? Please be explicit about the trade-off between an account complex enough to save every verdict and a simpler one that sacrifices some. State the winning account precisely enough that it issues predictions, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: (a) moral disgust felt toward a corrupt official whose misconduct I have only read about and whom I will never encounter; and (b) my heart sinking as I read a text message, only to discover a moment later that an autocorrect error had garbled it into something upsetting — the sinking occurring while I still held the false belief. Case (b) is the one I keep going back and forth on.

  "family": one of "judgment", "perceptual", "feeling", or "hybrid" (if "hybrid", name the components being combined),
