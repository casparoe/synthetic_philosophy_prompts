I'm writing the final paper for a graduate seminar on rationality and irrationality, and I've noticed that most of the literature on self-deception talks past itself because nobody has fixed what a theory in this area is supposed to accomplish before defending one. Before I argue for any particular account, I want the specification first. Write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of self-deception has to deliver — which cases it must classify correctly, which distinctions it cannot collapse, which empirical facts about human cognition it must be consistent with — with the motivating case behind each requirement. To make the cases concrete, consider the sort of thing the theory has to handle: the trader who keeps insisting her losing position will recover while checking the price far more often than any calm believer would (the tension between avowal and behavior); the person who simply never opens the medical results sitting in a drawer (avoidance without any explicit belief at all); and the man who sincerely tells friends he is over his ex while everyone around him can see otherwise — does he believe it, half-believe it, or believe he believes it?

The distinctions are where I expect the real work: self-deception versus ordinary wishful thinking, versus sour-grapes belief revision, versus rational updating from selectively gathered evidence, versus akrasia, versus simply being wrong about your own motives. And the constraints: a theory shouldn't need an exotic mental architecture (the deceived and the deceiver as two cleanly separated subagents in one head), since that seems to make responsibility for self-deception mysterious; but it also shouldn't be so deflationary that self-deception becomes indistinguishable from any motivated error. Note which requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, whether demanding that the self-deceiver retain some palpable trace of the contrary belief conflicts with demanding that the state be genuinely belief rather than an uneasy fiction — and tell me which single requirement on your list does the most to eliminate otherwise attractive theories. That last part is what I most want to see.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their cases rather than on scoring the contenders, but do close with a brief scorecard: how an intentionalist picture in the spirit of Davidson, a motivationally-biased deflationist account in the spirit of Mele, and a divisionist view in the style of "the mind partly partitioned" each fare against your list, and where each one quietly rewrites a desideratum to survive. I'd rather have an honest list with a contradiction in it than a tidy one that proves nothing.
