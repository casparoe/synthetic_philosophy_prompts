My best friend and I have been having the same argument on and off for two years, and I've started writing down her verdicts because I think there's a hidden rule in them that even she can't state. The argument is about when you have a reason to feel or believe something because of what holding that attitude does for you. Her rulings so far: a demon will torture you forever unless you believe he is benevolent, and he isn't — she says you have no reason at all to believe it, none. A billionaire offers you a fortune tonight if you form the intention to drink a vile but harmless potion tomorrow — she says you have no reason to form the intention, even though you could take the money and renege. A tyrant arranges that you'll be rewarded eternally if you admire him — no reason to admire him, she says, however extreme the reward. But then she flips. A patient whose belief that she will recover actually makes recovery more likely: she says the patient has some real reason to believe she'll recover. A business partner who has given you no strong evidence either way, but where your trusting him makes him behave better and the venture succeeds for you both: some reason to trust him, she says. Threats, bribes, and rewards never count, but attitudes that help make themselves true apparently do — at least sometimes.

We're meeting again next week and I want to walk in prepared, so I've picked three new cases to put to her. First: a pill that would make you genuinely fall in love with your devoted but honestly rather dull partner, where the love would visibly transform both your lives for the better — does she say you have a reason to take it, or even a reason to love him once it's taken effect? Second: you believe your closest friend's first novel, which you privately find mediocre, is brilliant, and holding that belief spares him a breakdown and preserves a friendship you both treasure — but nothing about your believing it makes the novel better, so it's not self-fulfilling. Third: forming the intention to stay calm before your driving test, where having the intention is itself what steadies your hands — so the intention, unlike the potion case, actually brings about what it's about.

What I want from you is this: predict her verdict on each of the three, and more importantly, work out the rules she might be running. At least two fit everything she's said so far — maybe the line is self-fulfillment (attitudes get practical reasons only when holding them helps make them true), or maybe it's something about whether the benefit comes from an agent offering a bribe versus from the ordinary causal workings of the world, or something else you can see that I can't. Say what each candidate rule predicts for my three cases, where the rules diverge, give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and suggest one further case — the sharpest one you can think of — that would force her to reveal which rule she's actually been following all along.
