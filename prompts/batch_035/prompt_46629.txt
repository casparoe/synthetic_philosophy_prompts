been reading population ethics threads on lesswrong-adjacent blogs for like a decade now, and the thing that finally got on my nerves is that half the arguments in the comments are people with different axiologies talking past each other — one person is quietly an average-ist, the other is a totalist who thinks that's obvious, nobody notices. so I want to run a "which population axiology do you actually hold?" decision tree on my blog, and I want you to write it.

You are a professional philosopher who specialises in population ethics and also writes well for smart non-specialists. Think carefully before you write anything — start with a brainstorm of the candidate branch questions and what separates them, then commit to the tree. Don't just regurgitate the standard "here are the positions, here's the repugnant conclusion" survey; I want an actual sorting instrument.

Rules for the tree:

1. Build it from the logical space, not from the list of famous isms. First the questions people can differ on, then the positions located in that space. The repugnant conclusion is an output of the tree, not an input.

2. Every node must be a concrete case — two worlds described in terms of population sizes and welfare levels, "which world is better?" — that a reader can answer without knowing which label the answer earns them. No question may just ask "do you accept the repugnant conclusion?", that's asking which team you're on and tells me nothing.

3. These cases have to show up somewhere in the tree: mere addition (adding lives worth living but below the existing average, with no one else affected), the repugnant conclusion proper, the sadistic conclusion (adding some lives not worth living versus adding a huge number of barely-positive lives), and one case I care about personally: a datacentre running trillions of copyable minds whose lives are each just barely worth living, versus a small world of genuinely flourishing people. I want a node asking whether the medium — biological versus digital, copies versus originals — does any work in the reader's judgement, or whether it's just the repugnant conclusion with a sci-fi paint job.

4. The leaves should include at least total utilitarianism, a critical-level view, variable value, average utilitarianism, a person-affecting view, and some lexical/perfectionist option, plus whatever else falls out of your tree. Each leaf gets a name (or "unnamed, and here's why nobody lives there") and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

5. For the unoccupied combinations of answers: note them and diagnose each — is nobody there because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because it's simply unexplored?

6. Flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree can't seperate. E.g. if a variable value view with certain parameters and a critical level view with certain parameters will take every branch identically, say so instead of pretending the tree sorted them.

Audience: fluent in expected utility theory, has maybe read one pop-philosophy summary of the repugnant conclusion, allergic to jargon in the questions themselves (labels may appear at the leaves with one-line glosses). Size: 6 to 9 branch questions, at most 12 leaves. No citations, no reading lists, no "see Arrhenius 2000" — the tree has to stand on its own.

End with a short section for me as the editor: which single branch does the most work (the answer that, if you flipped it, would drag the reader to a totally different leaf), and which branch you expect readers to answer differently in the abstract versus when the case gets concrete. I suspect it's the mere addition one but I want your read, not my hunch.
