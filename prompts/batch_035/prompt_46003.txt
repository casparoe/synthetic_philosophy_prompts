I've noticed something about my own decision-making that I can't find a word for, and I suspect it's less a new phenomenon than a known one run in reverse. When I face a choice, I don't try to find the best option — I decide in advance what would be good enough and stop looking once something clears the bar. That part, I take it, is just Simon's satisficing, and it's not what I'm asking about. What I'm asking about is that I sometimes deliberately choose something *below* the bar I've already set, because I've learned that my bar doesn't stay put. For a few years I lived in an unusually nice apartment, and when I later had to move to a merely adequate one, the adequate one felt intolerable for the better part of a year: the earlier, better choice had permanently raised the level I could comfortably settle for. Since then I've started treating my own "good enough" threshold as a scarce resource — something any indulgence spends down — so, for instance, I've stopped buying the genuinely good olive oil, because I know that once the ordinary stuff starts tasting inadequate to me, there's no going back, and I'd rather keep a low threshold that most future options can meet than a high one that most can't.

Is there an established name for this — deliberately managing one's own aspiration level to keep it from ratcheting upward, as a forward-looking strategy rather than a limitation? I've come across the hedonic treadmill, but that seems to be a descriptive claim about how pleasure fades, not advice about how to exploit the fading. I've also come across adaptive preferences in the sour-grapes sense, but those look like rationalizations formed after the fact, when a desired thing turns out to be unavailable, whereas what I'm doing is planned: I decline an available and affordable option precisely so that I won't want things like it. And I gather Simon had something about aspiration levels adjusting with experience, but that seemed to be about when to stop searching, not about strategically avoiding raising the bar in the first place. I won't be satisfied with a bare label: I'd like to know the nearest concepts in decision theory, psychology, or philosophy of mind, and exactly how this differs from each of them — whether it's a species of self-command or precommitment, whether it counts as a rational form of preference-shaping or as something closer to self-deprivation, and where its edges are (for instance, is it the same phenomenon when someone lowers their standards out of fear of disappointment rather than to keep future options open?). If nothing established fits, coin a term for it and give a definition precise enough to separate it from ordinary thrift and from adaptive preference formation, along with the boundary cases where you yourself would be unsure which side it falls on.
