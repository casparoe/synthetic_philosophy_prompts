A colleague of mine, Diya, has unusually firm views about when it is proper to assert something, and after years of arguments over coffee I can usually predict her verdicts. Her track record, as I have recorded it:

- Reading the time off a watch she has worn for years and telling a passer-by it is twenty past three: proper.
- Telling a visitor that the station is a ten-minute walk, when she honestly believes this and it is in fact fifteen: improper — the speaker was entirely blameless, but she should not have asserted it.
- A numerologist announcing, on the basis of a dream, that the eclipse would fall on the ninth of March, and turning out to be right: improper.
- Reading the time off a public clock that stopped exactly twelve hours earlier and telling a passer-by it is twenty past three: improper — right by luck, and luck is not good enough.
- A schoolteacher who personally rejects evolutionary theory but knows her state-approved textbook has been rigorously vetted telling her class that whales descend from land mammals: proper.

Last week a new case came up, on which she went uncharacteristically quiet. A mathematician announces a theorem on the strength of a proof containing a subtle gap which two of her later slips happen to cancel out; the theorem is in fact true, necessarily so, and she will never learn of the flaw. Was her assertion proper?

Give me your prediction of Diya's verdict first, then the reasoning. The obvious norms each die on at least one of her five cases, and I would like you to show which dies where; the interesting work is with the survivors. As I see it, the knowledge norm fits the first four but needs surgery to accommodate the fifth, and some anti-luck principle about not easily being wrong fits all five — tell me whether those really are the live candidates, what each predicts about the mathematician (including whether an anti-luck principle should be read as tracking the proposition, which here could not have been false, or the method, which could easily have delivered a falsehood), which rule Diya is most likely running and why, and what further case would best discriminate between the survivors. Begin with the verdict itself, no preamble, and no restatement of my question.
