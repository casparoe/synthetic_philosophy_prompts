I am a philosophy PhD student working on intergenerational justice, and I serve as the graduate representative on my university's Carbon Neutrality Steering Committee. At next month's meeting we must decide whether to recommend an accelerated decarbonization plan: roughly $40 million in spending over fifteen years — deferred lab renovations, higher operating budgets, an end to the phased gas-boiler replacement — whose principal benefits, in avoided climate damages, accrue mostly after 2090 and largely to people not yet born. Two objections stand in the way, and I want our memo to confront both rather than route around them.

The first comes from our CFO, who applies the university's standard 5% real discount rate and notes that under it, essentially any benefit delayed by seventy years counts for almost nothing. Several committee members treat this as simply "the math," and I do not think it helps to accuse them of not caring about the future; the memo needs to engage the rate on its merits, distinguishing discounting for uncertainty and opportunity cost from pure time preference, and saying plainly whether a 5% rate is defensible for this kind of decision or whether a lower, declining, or zero rate for far-future benefits is what the reasoning actually supports.

The second objection comes from an engineering professor on the committee who has been reading about Parfit's non-identity problem. He argues that because different budget decisions will indirectly alter who meets whom and when, the people who would benefit from the accelerated plan would not have existed without the plan, so the plan does not benefit anyone in the relevant sense, and the moral case collapses. He is not using this as a rhetorical dodge — he has genuinely convinced himself, and he has challenged the rest of us to show him where the reasoning goes wrong.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate in advance of the meeting. It should (1) give a clear recommendation — endorse the accelerated plan, reject it, or endorse a modified version with explicit conditions — that the full committee could act on, with a concrete time frame; (2) address the discounting argument as asked above, in terms a non-philosopher CFO would have to take seriously; (3) address the non-identity objection on its merits, saying how, if at all, it can be answered and what view about the moral status of future people the answer presupposes (for instance, whether it requires caring about making people exist with better lives rather than making existing-people-to-be better off); and (4) treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than burying it. I personally lean toward endorsing the plan, but I am not certain the philosophy I would invoke can carry the practical weight, and the committee will notice if the memo overreaches. Please close with a short section stating where the memo's reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration or argument, if it were established, would most plausibly reverse the recommendation.
