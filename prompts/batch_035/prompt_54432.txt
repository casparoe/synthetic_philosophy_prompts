I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and we're preparing our next interactive feature — the sort where readers answer a series of questions in the browser and are told at the end which camp they fall into. The subject is the old economists' fight over interpersonal utility comparisons: whether, and in what sense, one person's wellbeing can be weighed against another's. Our readers are educated laypeople, so no question may presuppose any economics beyond the ordinary idea that people have preferences and that some lives go better than others.

What I need from you is the full classification instrument. Please build it as follows:

1. First, construct the underlying logical space: identify the genuinely independent questions about interpersonal comparability — whether there is any fact of the matter about whose situation is better, whether comparisons can be merely ordinal (this person is worse off than that one) or must be cardinal (this person's loss is three times that person's gain), whether self-reports are authoritative evidence or merely one input among facial expressions, life circumstances and behaviour, and whether comparability survives large differences in temperament or life history. Explain briefly how these axes combine before you name any position.

2. Then write the quiz itself: eight questions, each a concrete case a reader can answer without ever having heard the jargon. Aim for cases like the following (adapt or replace as you see fit, but keep them concrete): a planner deciding between a modest benefit to a well-off person and a larger one to a poor person; a patient who insists her mild complaint is unbearable versus another who cheerfully minimises a broken leg; a case where one person's pleasure is produced by another's suffering; a choice between extending a contented life for many years and a short, ecstatic one; and a case involving two people so different in temperament and circumstance that a reader may baulk at the comparison itself. Each question should offer two or three options, and no option should be labelled with a position's name or be recognisably 'the correct answer' in tone.

3. Finally, give me the scoring key. Map answer patterns onto the positions in the space: the strict incomparability view that treats comparisons as value judgements rather than facts; the view that comparisons are possible but only ordinally, via imagined trading of places between yourself and another person; full cardinal comparability of the kind a utilitarian planner needs; the Rawlsian move of abandoning comparisons altogether and ranking by an objective list of goods that anyone would want whatever their conception of the good; the prioritarian view that comparisons are cardinal but that benefits to the worse-off count for more than the numbers alone imply; and any others you think the space genuinely contains. For each result, include two or three sentences written for the reader who lands there: what they believe, and the most awkward commitment that comes with it.

4. Be explicit about the instrument's limits. Flag any pair of positions the questions cannot reliably separate (I suspect ordinal comparability through imagined place-switching and the objective-goods view may collapse together on some answers — tell me whether they do and why). Note any combination of answers that corresponds to no defensible position, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent or merely unexplored. If separating any two positions would require a question too technical for our readership, say so rather than fudging it.

Please write the whole thing in British English, at the register of a serious magazine feature — formal and polished, but never pedantic, and with no untranslated technical vocabulary anywhere a reader would meet it.
