I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, which mostly means I write about physics for people who don't do physics, and it also means I listen to a lot of podcasts where people who are also not physicists explain quantum mechanics to each other. Usually I let it wash over me but a recent episode about Schrödinger got me tangled up, because it's close enough to my own patch that I can't tell how much of it was fair simplification and how much was just wrong.

The host's summary, as close as I can reconstruct it: Schrödinger was one of the founders of quantum mechanics, but unlike Einstein he was also a serious reader of the Upanishads — he got to them partly through Schopenhauer and through a nineteenth-century German scholar of the Vedas — and he took from them the doctrine that Atman is Brahman, meaning the self in you is the same one that's in me and in everything, and the apparent plurality of minds is an illusion, there's really just one consciousness wearing a lot of faces. So far so good, I think. But then the host said that Schrödinger's physics pushed him toward this: once you accept that the observer can't be kept out of the description, and that looking at the system is what collapses the wave function, the unity of all minds follows pretty naturally — the observer in you and the observer in me are the same observer, so to speak. He used "mind" and "consciousness" interchangebly the whole episode, did the inevitable cat-in-the-box bit saying Schrödinger invented it to show what happens when consciousness peeks in the box, and near the end he said Schrödinger "basically thought the mystical view had been confirmed by physics," which is supposedly why modern physics keeps drifting East — quantum computing, entanglement, all of it.

Something felt off to me. My recollection is that Schrödinger was pretty careful to keep the metaphysics out of the physics papers, and that he had a specific reason to distinguish mind — which as I remember it he treated as an ordinary physical, brainy process — from consciousness, which is where he said the plurality somehow isn't real. But I also genuinely remember that he did believe the unity-of-consciousness thing, said it in print more or less bluntly, and wasn't embarassed about it. So: which parts of the host's picture would a historian of physics nod along to, which are simplifications a careful person might allow, and which are conflations Schrödinger himself would have objected to? And does the big finish — that physics has confirmed, or for Schrödinger quietly confirmed, the one-mind view — survive once you fix the sloppy parts, or was it resting on them the whole time? I'd email the host but he'd just read my question on air and riff for twenty minutes.
