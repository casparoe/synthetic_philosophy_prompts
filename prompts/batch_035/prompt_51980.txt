I'm a third-year history student taking a module on memory and public commemoration, and I keep noticing a pattern in debates about how communities should relate to their past that I can't find a name for. In arguments over statues, anniversaries, and school curricula, people seem to have not one attitude to the past but several, and they switch between them depending on what the past is being used for at that moment. Sometimes the past is treated as a quarry of exemplary figures to emulate; sometimes as something to be preserved in full local detail, whatever it was; sometimes as a defendant to be arraigned and condemned. What strikes me is that the same participant in the same debate will move between these attitudes without acknowledging the shift: a hero invoked as a model turns out to be exempt from scrutiny, or a tradition defended as harmless local colour is suddenly deployed as a binding norm. Is there an established concept or literature for this tactical switching between modes of relating to the past — as distinct from simply disagreeing about what happened or how to evaluate it? If there is no established concept, could you coin one and define it, with attention to how it differs from ordinary selective use of evidence and to its boundary cases? I should say that I'm less interested in a label than in how exactly the phenomenon should be carved up. Please answer in British English.
