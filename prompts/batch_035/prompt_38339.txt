I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and next month I've been asked to give a guest lecture in our department's graduate survey course on philosophy of science — a room full of ML researchers and HCI students with no philosophy background. The assigned topic is Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, and I want to do something a bit unorthodox: build the whole session around the question of whether deep learning itself, as it has actually been practiced since 2012, counts as a progressive or a degenerating research programme. My instinct is that this case study will make the framework feel like it's about *them* rather than about dead physicists, but I'm worried about the opposite failure — that the audience will treat the whole apparatus as a set of handy debate terms for dunking on research directions they dislike ("that's just a degenerating programme" as a fancy way of saying "I don't like it").

Please don't write me the lecture; plan it. I want you to think carefully, and start with some rough brainstorming before committing to a sequence. Specifically: what has to be established before Lakatos even makes sense — I assume naive Popperian falsificationism has to be set up sincerely and then shown to fail on examples the audience already believes in, but tell me how to do that without strawmanning Popper and losing the philosophers' goodwill; then the dependency structure of the framework itself (hard core, protective belt, the two heuristics, progressive versus degenerating problem shifts — what must come before what, and where the hindsight problem — that we only know ex post — has to be acknowledged). Then the case study: which concrete episodes from the deep learning literature would carry the argument best — I'm torn between the scaling-law story as a candidate hard core with a positive heuristic, and the history of patchwork fixes to failure modes as belt-building — and what the example must include and what it must omit to stay honest. Then the misconception map: the ones I anticipate are (a) research programmes as excuses for never giving anything up, (b) collapsing Lakatos into Kuhn's paradigms and normal science, (c) reading "degenerating" as a synonym for "wrong" or "unfashionable," and (d) the ML-specific one, that benchmark improvements automatically equal theoretical progress — but tell me which ones I've missed and, crucially, at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than left for Q&A to mop up. Finally, one question I can pose at the end to check whether they've internalized the apparatus as opposed to just acquiring new vocabulary for their priors, and one thing I should openly refuse to adjudicate in a single lecture and say so.

For format: please give me the plan in whatever structure serves it (a sequenced outline is fine), but end your response with a line of the form \answer{...} containing, in a short phrase, the single misconception from your map that you judge most likely to survive my lecture no matter what I do, so I know where to spend my remaining prep time.
