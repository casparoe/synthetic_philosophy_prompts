I'm a third-year politics student, and this semester I'm taking a seminar on democratic theory that spends its back half on institutional alternatives to majority rule. The unit we just finished covered the proposal, sometimes called futarchy, under which the electorate would vote only on values, with the winning values compressed into a measurable national welfare metric, while markets in policy futures would determine which policies are expected to raise that metric, the government being bound to implement whichever policy the market prices highest. I found the idea stranger and more interesting than I expected, and for the course's creative assignment I have chosen to satirize it — the design itself, that is, not the people who proposed it. My first draft kept drifting into mocking a persona, and my instructor pushed me back toward making the machinery supply the comedy.

Could you write the piece for me? The form I have in mind is a government press release, around 700 words, issued by the Ministry of Welfare Optimization of a mid-sized country that has been governed this way for two decades. The occasion is the launch of version 4.0 of the National Welfare Metric, the number the policy markets are obliged to maximize, and the release doubles as an orientation insert for newly arrived immigrants. I would like it to contain, roughly in order: a short institutional history in which each revision of the metric is candidly dated to the moment the markets became suspiciously good at raising the old number without improving anyone's life, with v4.0 said to add such terms as measured life satisfaction net of hospital waiting-list anxiety and of the despair index; a proud paragraph explaining that although the wealthy hold more votes than the poor, this is entirely in order, because their additional votes are cast only on beliefs and never on values, and the values remain sovereign in the hands of the people, who express them once a decade at the Values Census; a defense of the system's record on long-lag policies, noting in passing that the average trading position expires long before a river gets clean, so it would have been irrational to expect traders to price the river at all; and a closing question-and-answer section of two or three entries, one of which answers a citizen who complains that she voted for the river to be cleaned and the river is still dirty — the ministry explaining that she voted on the value, the market weighed all available information, and the river-cleanup futures were simply found to be overpriced.

The tone throughout should be earnest, bureaucratic, and entirely sincere: the ministry never doubts itself, never winks, and treats every objection as though it had already been answered somewhere in the system's own vocabulary. The jokes must come from the actual mechanics rather than invented failures, so that a reader who knows the literature on futarchy recognizes each gag as one of the standing objections — the metric being gamed installment by installment, deep pockets moving prices on beliefs while the values facade stays democratic, the mismatch between trading horizons and decade-long payoffs — and ideally finds the satire fair; while a reader who has never heard of any of it should still be able to laugh at the voice of an institution that has quietly replaced accountability with liquidity.
