I'm a junior on my high school's debate team, and our current resolution concerns moral and legal responsibility for failures to act — a landlord who never replaces the battery in a smoke detector, a bystander who stands by while someone drowns, a doctor who simply doesn't prescribe an available treatment. In our practice round this week, my opponent dismissed my entire case with the line "nothing comes from nothing." His argument, as far as I could reconstruct it, was that an omission is not an event, that an absence cannot do anything, and therefore that a person who fails to act cannot cause the bad outcome at all — so my landlord, for instance, never caused the fire deaths, and at most is guilty of some separate offense like negligence.

Afterward I looked up the slogan and found that it has a long history in a completely different context — I gather it traces back to Parmenides and gets invoked in physics and cosmology discussions about whether the universe could have arisen from nothing. That made me suspect it is being stretched when applied to omissions, but I can't quite pin down where the stretch happens.

What are the different precise claims this one-liner could actually be making — from readings that are trivially true to ones that are substantive and defensible to ones that are flat-out false? For each reading, I'd like to know whether it is plausible, and whether accepting it would actually entitle my opponent to conclude that omissions can never cause anything (as opposed to merely concluding something weaker or something about a totally different subject, like the beginning of the universe). Please lay the readings out as a menu I can bring to my coach, and end by telling me which reading he was most likely invoking and what I should say in the next round if he repeats it.
