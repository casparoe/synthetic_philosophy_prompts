Write a villanelle in the voice of the diarist from Wittgenstein's private language argument: the man who, having a recurring inner sensation that no one else could in principle observe, resolves to keep a private diary of it — attending to the sensation on its first appearance and writing a sign for it, confident that he will recognize the same sensation when it returns and enter the sign again.

The two refrains should carry the two moments of the thought experiment. The first refrain is the private ostension: the inward act of pointing, the thought that this, whatever it is, is what the sign is to mean. The second refrain is the later recognition: the claim, made with complete sincerity, that this new sensation is the same one, and so the sign applies again. Leave the actual wording of both lines to yourself, but each should be recognizable as its moment — one an establishing gesture, the other an application.

Through the middle tercets the surrounding lines should quietly withdraw the ground from under the second refrain, without ever disputing that the diarist feels confident. What they should make vivid is that nothing in his situation distinguishes genuine recognition from its mere seeming: the check he appeals to is itself another impression, one that would need the same kind of check, and so on down. The poem should not state this; the tercets should enact it, perhaps by letting the second refrain recur each time with a little less around it to support it. The final couplet, where the two refrains at last stand side by side as villanelles require, should read not as the form's usual resolution but as the argument's conclusion: two acts of assurance with nothing between them — so that the reader sees that when whatever is going to seem right to him counts as right, the diarist's claim to be right has stopped meaning anything, and the pointing and the recognizing were one gesture performed twice.

A philosopher who knows the argument should be able to say which refrain is which and to locate the exact tercet where the collapse is being enacted; a reader who has never heard of it should still get a good poem about a man watching his own certainty circle with nothing for it to catch on. Avoid philosophical jargon beyond what the diarist himself might think in — no talk of criteria, grammar, or language-games — and do not name Wittgenstein anywhere in the poem.
