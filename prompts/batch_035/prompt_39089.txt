I teach intro epistemology at a mid-sized state school, and this semester I've had a bit of a come-to-jesus moment: I was grading papers and realized I'd gotten lazy about checking the AI's summaries of articles I hadn't reread in years — I was grading students partly on the authority of a chatbot's account of Frank Jackson's knowledge argument, which, if I'm honest, I could no longer reconstruct from scratch myself. And then a student came to office hours and said she's stopped doing the reading because she "understands it better" from the AI's explanations, and I genuinely didn't know what to tell her, because what's the difference between what she does and what I do? So I want to put epistemic dependence on AI on the final, and I want it to be a question that actually has some bite.

Here's the thing I keep running into and want the question to force students through: the obvious move is "outsourcing to AI = bad, you lose understanding," but that can't be the whole story, because we outsource tons of stuff without it being epistemically corrosive. I offloaded arithmetic to a calculator decades ago and nobody thinks that destroyed my understanding of math (or at least not in a way that matters). I also can't fix my own car, or grow my own food, and the standard division of cognitive labor handles all that fine through testimony and trust norms. So the interesting question is what, if anything, is different about AI. My own hunch is it's something like: the calculator gives me answers to a kind of question I can still independently verify and whose failure modes I understand, whereas the AI gives me *explanations* — the very thing that was supposed to be my job — and I can't tell good ones from fluent garbage without already having the understanding I'm outsourcing. But maybe that hunch is wrong, or maybe it proves too much (the same thing was said about books, and about Wikipedia, and those seem fine?). Also I've been reading a bit about extended mind stuff and I can't decide if "the AI is part of my mind now" is a serious answer or a dodge that makes the problem worse by definition.

So: write me ONE essay question for a 45-minute final exam slot, plus a model answer of around 500-600 words, plus a marking scheme out of 20 that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each contains. The question must NOT be answerable by reciting "AI causes skill atrophy" or "critical thinking is important" platitudes — I want it to force the student to actually produce some criterion or test that sorts benign offloading from corrosive offloading, and then stress-test that criterion against at least one case where it gives the wrong verdict (calculator, books, expert testimony, whatever works). The question should give them a concrete scenario to chew on rather than being fully abstract — maybe something like a student who uses the AI to generate practice problems vs. a student who uses it to explain the readings, or feel free to invent something better, I'm not attached to that. One more constraint: since this is a final and I have limited space on the exam, keep the question itself to under 120 words.
