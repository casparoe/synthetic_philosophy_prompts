So I've been chewing on open-source game theory stuff for a while now, ever since the prisoner's dilemma tournaments where the bots get to read each other's source code (the modal combat stuff, PrudentBot and friends), and there's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a clean word for.

The setup: a bot can make its action conditional on properties of the other bot's code, and the condition can end up *bringing about* its own satisfaction. The nice version is the Löbian handshake, where two bots each cooperate iff they can prove the other cooperates, and the fixed point is mutual cooperation, everyone goes home happy. But the exact same machinery runs in reverse. I cooperate iff you accept an 80/20 split in my favor. I cooperate iff you transfer your resources to me first. If the other agent is risk-averse, or satisficing, or just slightly worse at proofs, the extortionate condition gets satisfied too — and the aggressor isn't leaning on superior resources or outside bargaining power at all. The leverage is manufactured entirely out of the self-referential condition. It's *sort of* commitment, but not the burn-your-boats kind where the other guy best-responds to your sunk cost; here the condition is only binding because the other agent's reasoning about the condition is what makes it binding on both of you. The mechanism is perfectly symmetric but the outcomes sure aren't.

Is there an established name for this family of moves — conditions whose satisfaction is constituted by the other agent reasoning about the condition, deployed to shift a bargaining point? I know commitment races got discussed a bunch on LW, but that framing is about racing to commit *first*, and what I'm pointing at is more like the fixed-point structure itself being up for grabs, with no obvious temporal ordering. Please structure your answer as: "Closest existing concepts" (~150 words, the nearest things in the literature and how near they are), "Why none of them is exactly this" (~250 words, working through commitment proper, focal points, and ordinary bargaining leverage as competitors), "A name, if one is needed" (~100 words, with a definition), and "Edge cases" (~150 words — e.g., does the plain symmetric handshake count, and what about two extortion bots pointed at each other? I genuinely don't know whether that's a stable anything).
