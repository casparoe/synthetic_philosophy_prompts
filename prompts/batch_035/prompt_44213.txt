I write a column on cognitive science and AI for a general readership, and I'm preparing a long-form piece on what large language models have and haven't settled about how minds work. Before I commit my own views to print, I want a consistency audit of the position I've drifted into, because I suspect it doesn't all fit together, and I can't see where the seams are. Here is the position, stated as plainly as I can.

(1) I believe something like the language of thought hypothesis: thinking consists in computations over mental representations that have language-like structure, and the causal transitions between thoughts are driven by the syntactic form of those representations.

(2) I think the strongest evidence for (1) is the systematicity and productivity of thought — the fact that anyone who can think "John loves Mary" can think "Mary loves John," and that each of us can entertain an unbounded number of novel thoughts. A system with that profile looks like it has to be compositional in something like the way a language is.

(3) At the same time, covering deep learning has left me convinced that connectionist systems display genuinely systematic and productive behavior — they generalize, they compose, they follow instructions about things they've never seen — without anything in them that looks like explicitly structured symbols, and I believe this is compatible with (1) and (2): the networks are presumably implementing symbolic computation at some level of description.

(4) I'm a semantic externalist: what my thoughts mean depends partly on facts about my environment and causal history, not just what's in my head. I also believe that content — what a thought means — must do genuine causal-explanatory work in psychology, otherwise talk of beliefs and desires would be idle decoration on the real story about the brain.

(5) I find Searle's Chinese Room argument persuasive: running a symbol-manipulating program, however sophisticated, is never by itself sufficient for genuine understanding.

(6) I believe most of our concepts are learned through experience, education, and culture. I accept that some conceptual apparatus must be innate, but the idea that we come equipped with a large innate stock of specific concepts has always struck me as incredible.

What I'd like from you: go through these six commitments pairwise and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is. For each genuine one, say what would have to give and what abandoning it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd rather have your severity rankings than a flat list where everything is flagged as equally problematic — I want to know which of these is load-bearing.

I'd also ask two things about your own answer. First, since my framing of the commitments may itself be steering the audit: tell me where my statement of the position is most likely misleading — where a commitment as I've phrased it is ambiguous between a defensible and an indefensible reading, and whether the tension survives the defensible reading. Second, state explicitly what would change your verdicts: which argument, consideration, or distinction — if it failed or if it were exposed as mistaken — would lead you to a different ranking of the tensions, and where in your reasoning you think the audit is most likely to have gone wrong. I'd rather publish a corrected version of this position than a consistent elaboration of a mistake.
