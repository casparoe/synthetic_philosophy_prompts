I'm reading a popular book on the epistemology of testimony — a trade-press volume aimed at general readers, no philosophical background assumed — and near the end it sums up the whole field like this: "For most of modern philosophy, testimony got no epistemological respect. Hume and Locke treated other people's say-so as a second-rate source of belief: you were entitled to accept a report only insofar as you could check it against your own observation. In the twentieth century Coady, drawing on the eighteenth-century Scotsman Thomas Reid, showed that this stance is self-defeating — if every testimonial belief had to be verified against what we've observed ourselves, we couldn't so much as learn our native language or our own country's history, since nearly everything we know about both came to us second-hand. Testimony therefore had to be recognized as a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory, and today essentially everyone in the field accepts that we should take people's word at face value unless there's positive reason to doubt it."

The overall arc sounds right to me — Enlightenment suspicion, Reid's rehabilitation, Coady's revival — but a few things nag. The framing of Hume and Locke seems to require first-hand verification of each particular report, whereas I dimly recall Hume's actual point being about how testimony has conformed to experience in the past, not about personally checking everything one is told. The closing sentence also seems to slide from "testimony is a basic source of justification" to "take people's word at face value by default," and I'm not confident those are the same claim. And "essentially everyone accepts" makes the field sound more settled than I'd expect of a debate that, from what I've gathered, is still very much open.

Could you audit the passage for me? Go claim by claim and tell me what a working epistemologist would accept, what they would wince at, and what is flatly wrong. In particular: (a) is the reductionist position the book attacks the one reductionists actually hold? I've heard there's a distinction between verifying each individual report and establishing the general reliability of kinds of testifiers, though I can't remember where it comes from or whether the book's self-defeat point touches only the first; (b) does the infeasibility of verification establish the positive conclusion the book draws — that testimony is a basic source and that reports deserve acceptance by default — or only something weaker, with the stronger claim resting on further argument?; and (c) how much genuine consensus is there? If parts of the book's summary survive correction, I'd like to hear that too — I'd rather not swing from trusting the book wholesale to dismissing it wholesale.
