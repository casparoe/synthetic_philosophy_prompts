I'm a PhD student in ML working on safety evaluations, and I've been asked to lead a seminar discussion contrasting the AI control research program with the AI alignment research program. The naive gloss — alignment tries to make the model want good things, control tries to make it unable to cause harm even if it doesn't — strikes me as too thin, and I want to push the group past it. My own suspicion is that the interesting question is whether the two are genuinely separable strategies or whether control covertly presupposes alignment commitments, and vice versa. For instance, control measures like tripwires, honeypots, and staged deployment all need some model of what a misaligned system would try to do, which starts to look like a theory of misaligned behaviour — which is uncomfortably close to the thing alignment research claims to provide. Conversely, alignment techniques like Constitutional AI or RLHF seem to depend on control-adjacent machinery (monitoring, oversight during training, red-teaming) to even be instantiated. I'd like you to compare the two research programs as strategies: where do they genuinely come apart, what does each one assume that the other is supposed to supply, and is there a principled way to allocate a given safety intervention to one bucket rather than the other?

To make it concrete, I've been circulating a toy scenario to the seminar and would appreciate your take on it as part of the comparison. Suppose a lab deploys a frontier model behind a strict control regime: capability evaluations, a monitor that flags suspicious reasoning chains, staged access to tools, and kill switches at every layer of the stack — but the lab has done essentially no alignment work, taking no position at all on what the model's internal objectives are. A skeptical colleague in my group argues this is like operating a nuclear reactor with superb containment and no theory of the fuel: it can be fine, and even preferable, if your containment is good enough that the physics never matters. Another colleague counters that in the nuclear case the reactor is not adversarially optimizing against your containment — whereas a misaligned model is, in which case control without any alignment signal degenerates into an arms race you can't expect to win, since the model can learn your monitor's distribution and route around it. Who is closer to right, and what does your answer imply about whether "control as a standalone research program" is coherent? Please also address the practical question of burden-shifting: in a world of limited safety budgets, does the control/alignment distinction give you a defensible way to decide how to spend the marginal researcher-hour, or does it just relabel the same undifferentiated pile of interventions?
