Got into an argument with my housemate (econ grad student) about whether a soda tax can ever make anyone better off, and he laid out an argument I can't refute but also can't believe:

(1) For policy purposes we should only rely on evidence about people's welfare that's actually observable — you can't look into anyone's head, and when you ask people directly they tell you what they think they should say.
(2) The only observable evidence of what someone values is what they choose when the alternatives are genuinely available and affordable.
(3) So the measure of a person's welfare is their revealed preference: if they pick A over B, A is better for them than B. That's all "better for them" can even mean to anyone outside their skull.
(4) It follows that every voluntary choice is welfare-improving, at least relative to what was actually on the menu at the time.
(5) Therefore any policy that steers people away from what they would have picked voluntarily — taxes, default rules, waiting periods, whatever — makes them worse off by construction. Policy can still help by making people richer, but never by overriding choices.
(6) Regret is not counterevidence. If I keep buying scratch cards while telling everyone I regret it, the buying is the data and the talking is noise.

My uncle smoked for thirty years, begged people to stop selling him cigarettes, was furious at himself every time he "chose" to buy a pack at 2am, and now says quitting was the best thing that ever happened to him and that his past self was an idiot. My housemate just shrugs: the past self's choices were still better for him than the alternatives available at the time. Which sounds tough-minded until you notice it makes the whole thing unfalsifiable — there is literally nothing anyone could ever do or say that would count against it. And it seems to prove both too much and too little: too much, because a theory of welfare on which the addict begging to be tied down is "doing fine" isn't doing any work anymore; too little, because when I said "but a tax just changes the menu, so by your own (3) it can't make anyone worse off either," he said great, he accepts that — and seemed happy to have bought it.

Everyone points at step (3), but I don't see the actual counterargument. It's not enough to say "sometimes people choose badly," because by his lights that's just helping yourself to a second, unobservable standard of welfare, which is exactly what he's refusing to do. I'd rather keep (1) and (2) if at all possible — the skepticism about mind-reading seems right to me. So: which step do I give up, what does giving it up cost, and is there a version of his view that survives without swallowing the 2am cigarette thing?
