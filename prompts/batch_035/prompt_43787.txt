Quick background: my grandfather was in a Japanese POW camp in 1945, and the family story has always been that Hiroshima is the only reason he made it home -- supposedly the camp had orders to execute the prisoners if the mainland was invaded. I mention this because ever since I read Anscombe's "Mr. Truman's Degree" pamphlet for a college class, I've been the person defending her position at family dinners, and it's getting awkward. She tried to stop Oxford from giving Truman an honorary degree in 1956, arguing that since "For men to choose to kill the innocent as a means to their ends is always murder," Truman was a murderer on a massive scale and honoring him was shameful. And the annoying thing is I think she's right, including about the degree.

The problem is I can't figure out what would actually make me give the view up, which is itself a bad sign, right? So play it straight and map out what counts against her position. I'm thinking of stuff like: her "innocent" just means non-combatant, not morally innocent -- which means my relatives' "Japanese civilians supported the war effort" reply misses her point -- but then the whole load-bearing wall is the combatant/non-combatant line, and is that defensible with conscript armies, munitions workers, civilians who voted for the government? Also, she says elsewhere in the same pamphlet that killing the innocent isn't necessarily murder when the deaths are foreseen but not intended, and that "unscrupulousness in considering the possibilities" is what turns it into murder -- doesn't that smuggle consequences straight back into a supposedly absolute prohibition? And is her own trajectory relevant -- I gather she was a pacifist at the start of WWII and publicly changed her mind, so what happened to her there? Plus the obvious one: my family's straight consequentialist reply about grandpa.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which a serious Anscombian would concede as counting against her versus dismiss as missing the point, and be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would even be a problem for a view of this kind. Two other things: keep clear what's settled versus genuinely contested among people who know this stuff -- both the philosophy and the history, e.g. whether Japan was really trying to negotiate a surrender and whether anything like the POW execution order is solid -- and don't sell me some contested reading as the consensus. And whatever you do, don't soften it just because of the grandpa angle; he'd have hated that.
