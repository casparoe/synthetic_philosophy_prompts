I write fiction and each spring run a two-day craft workshop for ten adult novelists. This year I want to stage Frege's puzzle about identity statements as a problem in narrative craft rather than lecture on it. Design a ninety-minute structured exercise for that setting — no philosophy up front — in which participants encounter co-referential names inside an invented story world of your devising: two names for a character, place, or object that the group gradually discovers refer to the same thing. The mechanics should make them act before the distinction is named — for example, having them rank pairs of sentences from the story by how much a reader learns from each, or wager fictional workshop points on whether a given reveal will land as news. I want them to feel, in their own choices, why a statement of the form that a is b can be a revelation while a statement that a is a is empty, and where a sentence's informativeness seems to outrun what it is actually about. Close with a debrief that maps particular moments of the exercise onto the sense/reference distinction, separates the purely narrative kind of surprise from the kind of cognitive significance Frege was after, and is honest about which part of the puzzle a fiction workshop cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, participants who think the whole thing is just a reader's missing information about the plot — and how to handle that without slipping into a lecture.
