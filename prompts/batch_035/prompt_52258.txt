Here it is, as he tells it. (1) Kripke's point about the table: it plainly could have been made from this very hunk of wood, but surely could not have been made from a completely different hunk -- then it would just have been a different table. He takes this as pretty obvious. (2) Now, could the table have been made from a hunk of wood differing slightly from the actual one, say one molecule swapped for a matching one? Saying no seems insane, the difference is negligible. (3) But if the table could have come from a slightly different hunk, then by the same reasoning that slightly-different hunk could have come from a hunk slightly different from *it*, and so on down a chain, each step trivially unproblematic, ending at a hunk sharing not a single molecule with the actual one. (4) Possibility of origin doesn't obviously chain like that -- but my flatmate says that unless you can name which link in the chain breaks, and say why, denying (2) looks like dogma. (5) So he accepts (2) is false and the table's origin is fixed in every detail. (6) Then he applies it to people: since you came from a particular sperm and egg, you could not have come from a different pair -- even a genetically identical pair, even the same pair at a different moment. (7) Which means: if your parents had been interrupted that night, you would never have existed at all -- not "you with a slightly different life," but no you, and no one in your place. He insists this follows, and honestly I can't find the flaw, but the conclusion that virtually all the nearest possible worlds are empty of me feels like it must be wrong somewhere. Where does it go wrong -- is it the chaining step, the leap from tables to people, or is he right and I just have to bite the bullet? And if I deny (2), what am I actually paying for it?
