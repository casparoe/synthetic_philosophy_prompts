A friend of mine who took an early modern philosophy elective was explaining Pascal to me the other day, and I want to check whether he's being fair. He said Pascal's famous point in the Pensées about justice and force goes roughly like this: justice without force is impotent, force without justice is tyranny, so the two ought to be joined; but since we can't make what is just strong, the world settles for making what is strong just. So far so plausible-sounding. But then he took it further: he said this makes Pascal an out-and-out legal positivist — no natural justice at all, just custom, so justice is whatever the established power can enforce and maintain — and that Pascal's practical advice is therefore to obey whoever happens to be in charge, period, since that's all justice ever amounts to for him. He capped it off by calling Pascal basically Hobbes in a cassock, and said Pascal's real insight was just seeing that might makes right is how politics necessarily works.

I've only read chunks of the Pensées, but the bits I remember — the passage joking that it's a river or a mountain range that decides where justice ends and error begins, and something about custom being accepted as the whole of equity — felt more layered than this. It also seems suspicious that my friend left out God entirely, which for Pascal feels like leaving out the main character. So: is this reading fair? Which parts would a Pascal scholar accept, which parts flatten or conflate distinctions the fragments actually draw, and does the Hobbes comparison do any real work or is it just vibes? Happy to be told I'm the one who's garbling the fragments, or that the friend is basically right and I'm just being squeamish about how cynical Pascal is — just tell me straight.
