Grading season, so forgive the typos and the slightly cranky tone. I've taught philosophy of biology for a while now, and every spring we hit the "what is life" unit, and every spring I die a little inside going through the same borderline casses — viruses, prions, mules, brain-dead patients, that immortal cancer cell line. I want something fresh, built to order, because honestly the specs are where the philosophy lives. What the case is ultimately for: I want to show students that the two big definitional families don't just disagree about where to draw one line, they disagree about which axis the line should even be drawn on.

Setup. On one side, the self-maintenance family: something is alive if it's a self-producing, boundary-maintaining system that actively works to keep itself going (metabolism, membrane, homeostasis — you know the genre). On the other, the evolution family: something is alive if it belongs to a lineage undergoing natural selection, i.e. replicators with heritable variation. These two mostly agree. Build me where they don't, under these constraints:

1. Realistic. No von Neumann machines, no synthetic life in a vat, no "imagine an entity that...". Built out of ordinary biology — farm, zoo, hospital, lab bench, greenhouse, your pick.

2. Not a stock case, or at most a cousin of one. If it's a variant of a standard example, the difference has to do actual argumentative work, not costume changes.

3. The split has to be generated, not stipulated. Don't write "and clearly this thing is alive" and let the definitions trail along — show me what each definition actually computes when you run it on the case. And make it a case where a competent defender of the losing side can still put up a fight; a contested split is worth more than a slam dunk.

4. This is the part I care about most. Don't give me one case, give me a pair: the main case splits them self-maintenance yes / evolution no; then a nearby variant, changed in exactly one feature, that splits them the other way — self-maintenance no / evolution yes. The feature you toggle has to be the same one doing the work in both splits, so the pair jointly isolates a single variable. I have a hunch the variable is something like where the persistence comes from — work the system does on itself versus conditions it merely happens to sit in — but I'd like to be suprised.

5. After the pair: name the load-bearing feature; say what happens if you neutralize it, whether both cases collapse into boring territory where the definitions agree; and give the best response available to whichever definition loses in each case — bullet-biting, definitional surgery, or attacking the case itself. And confront the token/type problem head on: does the evolution view even classify organisms, or only lineages, and does your case make the lineage-level move look principled or like a dodge? If the self-maintenance view has an analogous escape hatch, name it and tell me whether it's better or worse.

Bonus gratitude if a room of non-science undergrads can grasp both cases in about two minutes.
