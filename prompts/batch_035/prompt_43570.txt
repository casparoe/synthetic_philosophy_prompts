I'm on the claims-causation subcommittee at a mid-size property insurer. Whenever a loss could plausibly be pinned on more than one thing, we have to rule on what "caused the loss," becuase the policy wordings hang coverage on that exact phrase. From the case files I could pull, our rulings so far:

1. Arson, straightforward: warehouse torched, total loss. Ruled: the arson caused the loss.
2. Two seperate fires, each on its own big enough to destroy the warehouse — one from a tenant's space heater, one that spread from the building next door — merged on the way in. Ruled: both fires caused the loss, liability shared between the two policies.
3. An electrical fault burned the place down on the Tuesday. It later came out that a developer had already paid an arsonist to torch it that Wednesday, and the arsonist would have gone through with it. Ruled: the fault caused the loss; the never-executed arson isn't a cause of anything, and the fact that it was planned doesn't weaken the fault's causal status.
4. Kitchen fire, the kind a working sprinkler system would have snuffed out. The maintenance contractor had let the suppression system sit dead for months, in breach of contract. Ruled: the lapse caused the loss, and so did the fire.
5. Same kind of kitchen fire, but this warehouse predated the sprinkler code and the owner had simply never installed any — no duty to. Ruled: the absence of sprinklers didn't cause the loss; the fire is the sole cause.

Now before us: lightning hits the roof. Everyone on the panel agrees the fire itself was small and ordinary — on its own it would have scorched some beams and stopped. But this facility is licensed to store oxidizing chemicals, which is legal and standard for it, and it was storing them. Without the goods on site, roof damage; with them, total loss. The policy pays fire in full, about $14M, but caps payout at $3M if the loss is "caused by the storage of oxidizing materials." So: is the storage a cause of the loss?

I'll be honest, I can see it going either way. One panel member keeps invoking Hart and Honoré on deviations from the normal course of things, which cuts one way. Another colleague says ruling 3 only makes sense if you hold the never-executed arson fixed when you ask what the fault did, which sounds like the interventionist move and cuts some other way. I've genuinely lost the thread of which rule we're even running.

- "The rules still in play" (around 200 words): the candidate rules that fit our record, which ones the record has already killed (my suspicion is a plain counterfactual rule dies somewhere in rulings 2 and 3 — tell me if I'm wrong), and what each survivor has to be amended with to get past ruling 5.
- "The new case under each rule" (around 350 words): what each surviving rule says about the storage, together with the payout each verdict implies.
- "Closest analog and my call" (around 150 words): the precedent we'd be leaning on, the ruling that best coheres with our own record, and — since the record may genuinely underdetermine it — what principle we'd be adopting with this ruling, and what we'd be adopting if we went the other way.
- "The case that would settle it" (around 120 words): one further case, described concretely, whose ruling would split the surviving rules apart.

No citations or reading lists please — I'm working from case files, not a library.
