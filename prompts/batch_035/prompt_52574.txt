I need your help turning an argument I got into last Saturday into something my friends and coworkers can actually take. You are, for these purposes, an expert in measurement theory and the philosophy of social science — someone who has thought hard about quantitative indicators, selection effects, incentive design, and the replication crisis, and who also has practical experience building workplace metrics that don't rot. Please think carefully and do some genuine brainstorming before drafting; the reasoning behind the quiz's structure matters to me as much as the quiz itself.

Here is the background you need. My friend Ravi's company just rolled out a new performance dashboard, and when he described it to me I made the predictable observation that a measure tends to stop being a good measure once you start targeting it. Ravi pushed back hard. For him, Goodhart's law is fundamentally an incentive problem: agents game whatever the principal rewards, and the cure is contract design, monitoring, rotating the metrics before anyone can learn to exploit them, and occasionally firing cheaters. I found myself arguing the opposite: in a huge fraction of Goodhart cases, nobody is gaming anything. If you promote the salesperson with the best quarter, or invest with the fund that had the best five-year track record, you are largely selecting on luck and measurement noise; the disappointment arrives even if every single person involved is a saint, and no amount of monitoring or better contracts would change it. As the evening went on we discovered a third disagreement lurking under the first two: whether the deep problem is that proxies get gamed, that proxies are noisy, or that optimizing a proxy actively destroys the correlation that made it a proxy in the first place — as when test-prep decouples test scores from whatever the test was originally a decent sample of. And Ravi's wife, who does program evaluation for a living, kept insisting that half of this is just curable measurement error: stratify, normalize, re-measure, and your indicator improves on unaided judgment. We eventually realized we weren't disagreeing about whether Goodhart's law is true but about what kind of failure it names — and that our answers implied different and mostly non-overlapping fixes.

The assignment: write a diagnostic quiz, roughly ten questions, with the working title "What kind of Goodhart's law do you believe in?" It is for a general audience of educated laypeople (I want to send it to Ravi, but also to publish it on the small blog I keep about measurement in organizational life), and its purpose is to sort readers by what they actually believe about cases, not by which labels they like or have heard of. Concretely:

1. Every question must be a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, phrased so a reader can answer it without knowing any of the position names. No question may simply ask about beliefs ("Do you think incentives matter more than measurement?"). Suggested raw material, which you should extend rather than merely consume: a promotion or hiring decision where nobody involved can game the proxy at all; a classic adversarial case where gaming is obviously present (bounties on pests, factories graded on the weight of nails produced, universities gaming rankings); a case where honest optimization visibly decouples the metric from the underlying good (test scores and learning, citation counts and scientific contribution); a case where the boring measurement-engineer fix — better instruments, normalization, shrinkage toward the mean — genuinely seems to work; and at least one case where the intuitively right answer cuts against the position the reader is drifting toward, so the quiz cannot be gamed by someone who knows what it is sorting for.

2. The scoring key must map answer patterns onto at least five positions: (a) the incentive engineer, who reads Goodhart as a principal-agent problem with a monitoring and contract-design cure; (b) the selection-effects skeptic, who reads it as regression to the mean and off-distribution degradation, untouched by anyone's honesty; (c) the proxy ontologist, who thinks the real failure is causal decoupling — the measure was only ever a correlate of an unmeasurable thing, and optimization pressure breaks the correlation; (d) the anti-optimizer, who concludes the culprit is full-throttle optimization itself and prescribes satisficing, bounded targets, and dashboards of deliberately unranked metrics; and (e) the measured optimist, who holds that with proper adjustment metrics beat unaided judgment and that Goodhart is a curable artifact rather than a law.

3. One piece of intellectual scaffolding you may or may not want to expose to readers, but which should organize your thinking: some researchers in the AI safety and forecasting communities distinguish regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial varieties of Goodhart's law — roughly, disappointment from selection on noisy proxies, disappointment from operating at the edge of where the proxy was validated, outright decoupling of proxy from target, and deliberate gaming by strategic agents. You do not need to attribute or cite any of this; treat it as raw conceptual material and use the distinction however it helps, including noting where the five positions above cut across it.

4. Before writing the questions, please brainstorm the logical space: what questions jointly determine which position a reader holds, which combinations of answers are actually possible, and where the hybrid profiles live (for example, the reader who thinks Goodhart is really two unrelated laws sharing one famous name — the selection-effects one and the adversarial one — and that the other candidates are confusions). The scoring key should include at least one such hybrid profile and at least one combination of answers you judge to be unoccupied, with a sentence on whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

5. Finally, flag honestly at least one pair of positions your quiz cannot cleanly separate, and explain the structural reason why (e.g., both positions predict the same verdict in every case you could devise), rather than papering over it with a fake diagnostic question.

Target roughly 900 to 1300 words for the quiz and scoring key combined. The tone should be sharp but fair — I want Ravi to feel diagnosed, not strawmanned, and I want his wife's position to come out as genuinely defensible rather than as the quiz's designated loser. If, while brainstorming, you find the five-leaf structure I've imposed is genuinely inadequate to the logical space, say so and adjust, but tell me what you did and why.
