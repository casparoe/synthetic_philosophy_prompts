I'm a novelist who has been asked to write a short essay for a literary magazine on what I actually believe about privacy — not a survey of other people's thinking, but my working convictions, such as they are after twenty years of making books out of other people's lives. Before I write it, I'd like a rigorous audit of whether my views hang together. Friends have told me for years that they don't, and I've never been able to see the problem clearly myself. Here is the set:

(1) The heart of privacy is the audience. What makes an intrusion an intrusion is that a human being is observing and judging you. Data collected by systems and sifted by algorithms, where no person ever actually looks at it, does not wrong you; the wrong begins only when it surfaces to someone's eyes.

(2) The deepest reason privacy matters is that people need a backstage — a space of drafts, experiments, and contradictory half-selves that would be falsified the moment they were performed for anyone. Selves are written in private the way novels are.

(3) I keep a diary containing unguarded things about friends and lovers, and I consider keeping it no wrong at all, because its entire value lies in its never being read. The practice is for the writing, not for an audience.

(4) I have instructed my literary executor to burn those diaries when I die.

(5) I believe the dead have no privacy interests of their own — no claim over reputations, confidences, or the body. Whatever duties we have regarding the dead are owed to the living.

(6) When friends recognize themselves in my fiction, I take the complaint to have been dissolved by the transformation: a character shaped into art is not the person. Last year a friend stopped speaking to me over a story, and while I regret her pain, I still think she had no real grievance — though I notice that I would be unforgiving if a friend published things I had told her in confidence.

(7) On state surveillance I am relaxed, at least as regards myself: I have nothing to hide, my interior life having been on public sale in bookshops for two decades.

Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and what the least costly repair would be and what it would cost. Rank the tensions by severity rather than handing me a flat list in which everything is equally bad, and tell me which single change would resolve the most. I would rather not know in advance which commitment you think has to go — I want the honest audit, not the comfortable one.
