I've been going around with a friend on Wittgenstein's diary case, and he's produced an argument that uses it to dismantle public language along with the private sign. Each step looks defensible to me, the conclusion is absurd, and I want help locating exactly where it goes wrong -- and what rejecting that step costs.

(1) A word is meaningful only if there is a real difference between applying it correctly and applying it incorrectly.

(2) In Wittgenstein's diary case (Philosophical Investigations, §258), the diarist associates a recurrent sensation with a sign and writes it in a calendar on every day the sensation returns. When she later wants to know whether today's sensation is the same one, the only thing she can consult is her memory of what the sign was meant to mark. But memory checking itself is, in Wittgenstein's image, like buying several copies of the same morning paper to confirm what it prints. So whatever is going to seem right to her is right -- and if that is so, we can't talk about right at all here. So by (1) the sign is meaningless.

(3) Now take an ordinary speaker of a public language making any past-tense claim: that the meeting was on Tuesday, that she locked the door, that the color word she is using now is the one she has always used.

(4) When such claims are challenged, the ultimate evidence is always memory -- the speaker's own further recollections, or other people's. And every one of those memories is in turn checkable only by still more memories. So the whole edifice of past-tense public talk rests on what seems right to rememberers, with no check that comes from outside memory.

(5) The public rememberer is therefore in exactly the diarist's position: for any word whose application over time depends on memory, whatever seems right is right, and so by (1) and (2) those words are meaningless.

(6) Hence past-tense words, and every word whose continued use depends on remembering, are meaningless; most of ordinary language collapses along with the diary. That is plainly absurd.

(7) So either Wittgenstein's diary argument proves too much -- if it bans the private sign, it should equally ban "yesterday" -- or something distinguishes the diarist from the public rememberer that the diary argument never states.

Where I've gotten stuck is trying to say what that something in (7) could be. My instinct is that (4) contains an equivocation between a check that must come from outside memory altogether and a check that is merely independent in some weaker sense -- other people's memories of the same event are, after all, distinct traces and can conflict, which the diarist's single re-remembering cannot. But when I press on this, it either collapses back into corroboration by more memory, which looks like the newspaper trick again, or it smuggles in something public and rule-governed, in which case I have to explain why a solitary diarist couldn't have internal analogues of it -- and if she could, the diary argument never banned her sign in the first place.

Please tell me which step to reject and why that step looked fine when it didn't bear that weight. Then price the alternatives: if (1) goes, have I made correctness of application too cheap to do any work? If the Wittgensteinian instead rejects (2)'s verificationist framing -- holding that public words aren't answerable to checks at bottom but to trained agreement in judgment -- does that rescue the diary argument from my friend's extension of it, or does it concede that the argument was never really about memory? And if you think the argument doesn't rest on a confusion at all but exposes a genuine tension in the standard reading of §258, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a flaw to diagnose; but equally, if you think I've misread Wittgenstein somewhere -- for instance, about what the diary case is supposed to show -- tell me that directly instead of working within my framing.
