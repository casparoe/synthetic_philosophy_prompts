I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level course on rational choice and the philosophy of economics, and I'm working through the final exams. The exam question read as follows:

> Herbert Simon claimed that real decision makers satisfice rather than optimize. (a) Explain what satisficing amounts to, making clear what an aspiration level is and how aspiration levels change with experience. (b) A critic objects: satisficing may describe how cognitively limited agents in fact behave, but it cannot be normatively defensible, since a rational agent should still choose the best available option. Assess this objection. (c) Should Simon's program be understood as continuous with the heuristics-and-biases program of Kahneman and Tversky? Defend your answer briefly.

One student wrote the following (transcribed verbatim from the script):

> Satisficing means that instead of searching for the best option, an agent examines options one at a time and stops as soon as one is good enough by the agent's own standard. That standard is the aspiration level, and it is not fixed: Simon says it adjusts with experience, rising when good options are easy to find and falling when they are scarce, so over time an agent can come to expect more or settle for less. This is what bounded rationality means: because human computational capacity is limited, we cannot optimize, so we fall back on this shortcut.
>
> The critic's objection fails. Simon's deeper point is that the notion of the best available option is not well defined until you account for the costs of search itself. Finding the optimum is itself an action that takes time and effort, so for a satisficer with a correctly tuned aspiration level, stopping is always what a full optimizer would recommend. Satisficing is just optimization with search costs folded in, and that makes it normatively defensible by definition.
>
> On (c), yes. Simon's bounded rationality is the direct ancestor of Kahneman and Tversky's program: both show that actual human reasoning deviates from the norms of rational choice, and both treat heuristics as systematic biases that produce errors. Simon, writing in the 1970s in response to their work, would agree that the availability and representativeness heuristics are cases of satisficing: shortcuts that serve us well enough in everyday life but go wrong in the laboratory.
