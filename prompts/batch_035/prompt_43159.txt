The question was:

"A risk score used in lending is calibrated for a group if, among everyone in that group who received a given score, the fraction who actually go on to default equals the score. Equalized odds requires that a decision rule built on the score has the same false positive rate and the same false negative rate for every group. (In its score version: the average score among actual defaulters is the same across groups, and likewise for non-defaulters.) Explain, intuitively, why a score can't in general be calibrated for each of two groups with different base rates of default while also satisfying equalized odds. Under what conditions do the two become compatible? Does excluding group membership from the model's inputs make any difference?"

And the student wrote:

"Calibration means the score means what it says: if the people in a group who got a score of 0.7 defaulted 70% of the time, the score is calibrated for that group. Equalized odds says the errors should come out the same across groups, i.e. the same FPR and the same FNR.

Here is why you can't have both when base rates differ. Say group A defaults at 40% and group B at 10%, and the score is calibrated within each group. Then the average score within a group has to equal that group's base rate, since averaging the observed default rate over the whole group just gives you the base rate back. Now suppose equalized odds also holds: among actual defaulters the average score is the same in both groups (call it m+), and among non-defaulters it's also the same (call it m−). Each group's average score is then a base-rate-weighted mix of m+ and m−. For group A, 0.4m+ + 0.6m−, which must equal 0.4; for group B, 0.1m+ + 0.9m−, which must equal 0.1. Subtracting gives 0.3(m+ − m−) = 0.3, so m+ − m− = 1, and since scores live in [0,1] this forces m+ = 1 and m− = 0, i.e. every defaulter scored 1 and every non-defaulter scored 0. So apart from degenerate cases the two conditions clash. This is the substance of the impossibility results (Kleinberg et al. 2016, Chouldechova 2017).

The two become compatible if (a) the base rates happen to be equal, or (b) the predictor is perfect — a perfect predictor is trivially calibrated and has no errors to distribute unequally. Note as well that calibration within each group is only achievable in the first place if you train with group membership as a feature or fit a seperate model per group; otherwise the model has no way of knowing it's over- or under-shooting for a particular group.

On excluding the group attribute: yes, this dissolves the incompatibility. The impossibility is about comparisons between groups, and a model that never sees group membership cannot treat the groups differently, so the error rates come out the same by construction. This is fairness through unawareness. It at least guarantees equal selection rates — the same fraction of each group gets flagged as risky — though there are well-known problems with proxies (e.g. postcode standing in for race), so the guarantee is weaker than it looks. But the specific clash between calibration and equalized odds doesn't apply, because those are conditions on group-wise comparisons the model isn't making."
