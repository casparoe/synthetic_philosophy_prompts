I'm a second-year PhD student in linguistics, and our department has been running a standing argument about linguistic relativity since before I arrived, and I've started to suspect that almost nobody involved is disagreeing about anything empirical anymore. The history as everyone tells it is that the strong version was debunked decades ago — the famous case being the claims about speakers of a language lacking tense-based time expressions being unable to think about time in a certain way, which didn't survive scrutiny — and then universals were supposed to have finished the job, and then the experimental program came back: cross-linguistic differences in color discrimination tracking color-term inventories, speakers of languages that use absolute rather than relative spatial frames keeping cardinally-oriented track of where things are in ways that seem genuinely alien to English speakers, grammatical gender nudging how people remember and personify objects. The skeptics in my department concede each of these effects and then call them shallow: milliseconds of priming, online tasks in the lab, effects on attention and habit rather than on thought proper. The empiricists reply that lots of shallow effects can compose into deep ones, and that dismissing something as 'mere attention' presupposes exactly the modular picture of mind that the debate was supposed to be about. Both sides cite the same papers at each other, and nobody has budged in years.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than just accept, is that the real disagreement sits upstream of the data, in the criterion for what even counts as language influencing thought — the sides have quietly fixed on different thresholds, one demanding effects of a strength or domain-generality that would approach conceptual impossibility without the language, the other satisfied with stable, automatic, inferentially consequential differences in attention. But there are rival diagnoses worth taking seriously: that one camp is answering a cognitive-psychological question about processing while the other is answering an anthropological or interpretive question about forms of life, and the two have been fused under one heading since Sapir and Whorf were first anthologized; or that the entire framing presupposes a boundary between language and thought that is itself the substantive thing in dispute, so that asking whether language influences thought is like asking whether the left half of a circle influences the right half. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — substantive but hard, verbal over what deserves the word 'significant', a dispute about standards of evidence, or different questions sharing a name — using what the two sides actually claim, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: some class of experiments, a conceptual distinction the field lacks, a reframing of the question, or an argument that the impasse is the rational terminus of the whole research program. If your diagnosis is that part of the dispute is merely verbal, be precise about which part dissolves under disambiguation and what, if anything, genuinely remains live once the verbal residue is cleared away.
