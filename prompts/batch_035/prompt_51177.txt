I'm taking a comparative politics course, and last week's seminar was meant to debate whether the category 'corruption' travels across political cultures or is quietly parochial. The discussion never really got going: a classmate closed it down in about a minute by reciting Transparency International's definition — that corruption is "the abuse of entrusted power for private gain" — and then applying it, almost mechanically, to three cases the tutor had set out. First, the Kenyan member of parliament who distributes constituency bursaries through his own office, quite openly, in a manner calibrated to reward loyal wards — branded corruption, apparently, because power entrusted by constituents is being used to benefit his re-election prospects. Second, the Egyptian wasta intermediary who, holding no public office whatsoever, extracts favours from officials by calling in obligations of friendship and kinship — branded corruption on the grounds that 'entrusted power' can mean social influence, not just formal office. Third, the Japanese civil servant who accepts seasonal gifts from business contacts, carefully keeping their value just below the reporting threshold — branded corruption, my classmate said, because legality is beside the point; abuse is abuse even when technically lawful. On this basis she concluded that the question of whether Western standards are universal simply dissolves: the definition is universal, so the cases are settled.

I came away dissatisfied, though I couldn't articulate why at the time. My sense is that the one-liner is doing several jobs at once, and that my classmate's argument only works if you take the most contentious reading of each of its four terms — and moreover that the verdicts on the three cases pull in different directions depending on which reading you pick. The wasta case, for instance, seems to require 'entrusted' to cover influence with no office at all, while the bursary case seems to require that 'abuse' be measured against something other than the expectations the local public actually holds about what an MP's office is for; and if 'abuse' is instead measured against those local expectations, or against formal rules, some of the three cases fall out of the category again. I've also heard of a distinction, associated with Heidenheimer, between black, grey and white corruption — practices a given public condemns, tolerates, or approves of — and I can't work out whether the TI definition is meant to override that distinction or is parasitic on it.

Could you take the definition apart for me? I'd like the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making — a stipulation we're free to accept or reject, a report of the word's ordinary meaning, a normative principle about what ought to be condemned, a legal-style test — and then, for each of the terms 'entrusted', 'abuse', 'private gain', the different things it could be picking out: whose trust counts, and whether influence without office qualifies; whether abuse means violating formal rules, betraying the office's proper purpose (and whose account of that purpose), or merely acting in ways a relevant public would condemn; and whether gain to oneself, or also to one's family, clan, co-ethnics, or political faction, counts as 'private'. Lay these out as a menu of readings running from the trivial to the radical, flag which are plausible, which are contentious, and which look flatly arbitrary, and then tell me which readings my classmate actually needs for each of her three verdicts to go through — and whether any consistent package of readings gets her all three. If some of her conclusions turn out to depend on readings that render the definition either vacuous or question-begging (settling by fiat the very cases the universality question was supposed to turn on), I'd like that made explicit, since I may raise it at the next seminar.
