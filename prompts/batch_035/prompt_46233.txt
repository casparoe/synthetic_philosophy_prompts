I've been going down a rabbit hole on pain asymbolia -- the thing where some stroke patients say they still feel pain but it just doesn't bother them at all, like they notice it the way you'd notice a sound in another room. Then I found out that back in the late 1940s and 50s, surgeons were treating unbearable chronic pain (mostly cancer patients, people the morphine of the time couldn't help anymore) with lobotomies and related cuts into the frontal lobes, and the strange outcome a lot of patients reported was: the pain is still there, same intensity even, but it doesn't matter anymore -- it registers like background noise. I gather surgeons still occasionally burn a small spot in the cingulate cortex for pain today and get similar reports. So here's what I want: analyze that whole surgical episode as a case of pain asymbolia, kind of asymbolia on demand. Map it carefully -- what was actually being cut, what the patients said before and after, whether the distress was really separated from the feeling or whether the feeling itself changed and patients just couldn't tell -- and then run it both ways: what does the episode suggest about whether the badness of pain is built into the sensation itself versus coming from a separate suffering component that can be sliced off? And be honest about where the case is dirty -- these were lobotomies, with all the flattening of emotion and personality that came with them, so maybe what got removed wasn't pain-specific distress but the capacity to be bothered by anything at all. Does that ruin it as evidence for the two-component picture of pain, or does it just need a caveat?
