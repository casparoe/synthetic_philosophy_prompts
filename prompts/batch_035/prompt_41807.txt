For twenty years I sent every manuscript to the same editor — call her Vera — and since she retired last spring I have kept the habit of imagining her verdicts before I send my work anywhere. I now intend to put that habit to use, because I have a decision to make and find I cannot predict her. Her record on the question of whether a book's moral life can touch its aesthetic rank is long and, I thought, consistent. She never wavered about a long-dead painter who beat his models and killed a man: the canvases are great, full stop, and they hang in her study. When a living singer-songwriter's cruelty toward the women in his life came out, she stopped playing him — but told me the songs remained great songs, she simply wasn't going to be the one playing them; note that his finest songs are about those very women. She pronounced a certain dead novelist's books brilliant yet permanently barred from the rank of masterpiece, because their whole imaginative world runs on contempt for their female characters. When letters revealed that a dead essayist had written his most humane book as a calculated charm offensive, she was unmoved: the achievement is on the page, and no motive ever entered the page. But when a living poet was caught plagiarizing, she declared the poems no longer poems at all — stolen goods with varnish on them.

Now my own case. I have finished a novel about the collapse of a marriage — a marriage I watched from inside the friendship, having been told everything in confidence. My friend would recognize herself within pages; publishing will cost me her friendship and do her real harm. I also believe it is the most morally perceptive thing I have written, and I cannot see how to write it any other way. Predict Vera's verdict — on the novel's aesthetic rank, and on whether it should be published — and tell me what rule she has actually been running, because I can see at least two that fit her record equally well: one that locates the moral flaw only in what a work imagines and endorses, treating everything outside the page as a matter for boycotts and nothing more; another that holds a work made of a wrong against a person to be diminished as a work, the plagiarized poems being the clearest instance. Say which reading of her track record you find stronger and why, and then tell me what further case would tell the two rules apart, since I may yet be tempted to write to her.
