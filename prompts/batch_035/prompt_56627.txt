I'm a second-year law student in a seminar on criminal theory, and this week we discussed a pair of cases that has stuck with me: two equally reckless drivers run the same red light at the same speed, and a child runs into the street in front of one of them but not the other. Same intentions, same choices, same culpable disregard; one walks away with a ticket, the other faces years in prison for homicide. In the discussion, one of my classmates kept returning to the old proverb that the road to hell is paved with good intentions, deploying it to insist that the unlucky driver deserves everything the statute can give him: whatever the killer intended, she said, we judge people by what they actually do to the world, and the child is just as dead. Another classmate, a philosophy major, pushed back that the proverb, taken seriously, would collapse the distinction we otherwise insist on between murder and attempted murder, and that punishing people for outcomes beyond their control is exactly what the literature on moral luck identifies as the problem of resultant luck.

I want to walk into next week's session with something better than a vibe. Could you take that proverb apart for me? What distinct, precise claims might it actually be expressing — from the nearly trivial (good intentions alone don't make an action right) through intermediate readings (intentions don't mitigate outcomes; agents own the foreseeable risks they run) to genuinely radical ones (moral worth attaches to consequences alone, or even that well-intentioned people are somehow more culpable when things go wrong, as some readings of the proverb seem to suggest)? For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible on its own terms, whether it's consistent with the control principle that responsibility should track what's within an agent's control, and — most importantly — whether it actually supports my classmate's use of it, since almost any reading strong enough to justify the full homicide sentence against the unlucky driver would also seem to justify something she clearly doesn't want, like blaming the lucky driver equally. Lay the readings out as an ordered list with a verdict on each, note where two readings might get run together by someone quoting the proverb quickly, and tell me which reading, if any, could be held both by someone who accepts that luck pervades morality and by someone who insists our criminal law's differential treatment is justified. If some readings turn on an ambiguity between intentions, foresight, and negligence, flag that too, since our two drivers may not have intended anything at all.
