Until last year I was an experimental physicist, and I have recently joined a philosophy discussion group for retired academics. Last week two members argued about the justification of our beliefs in a way I could not referee, even though it felt uncomfortably close to the question I dealt with at work for forty years: how do you know an instrument is telling the truth? The exchange ran as follows.

(1) Marcus said every justification is a reason given for a belief, and reasons must bottom out: a chain of reasons either terminates in something that needs no further reason — something directly experienced or self-evident — or it circles, or it runs forever; and in the last two cases, he claimed, nothing is ever justified.

(2) Elena replied that real inquiry never works that way: no measurement is self-certifying, and we validate instruments against one another, so a reading counts as evidence only because it coheres with a mass of other readings and background assumptions. Justification, she said, is mutual constraint within a web, not a chain that must touch ground.

(3) Marcus objected that mutual support is circularity with better manners: if A vouches for B only because B vouches for A, no warrant has been created — as with a second witness who got his story from the first witness.

(4) Elena answered that the witness analogy assumes support can only travel down a single channel: when many seemingly independent checks all agree, that agreement is informative, which is precisely how we detect that one instrument has gone bad; a large system of joint constraints can be over-determined even though no single member of it stands on its own.

(5) Marcus said the independent checks are independent only relative to further background beliefs, all inside the same web, so the entire web can be wrong in a way nothing within it could reveal — a community of witnesses quietly sharing one delusion will be perfectly consistent.

(6) Elena replied that foundations suffer the same defect: a direct experience functions as evidence only given interpretive assumptions, so Marcus's stopping points are simply places in the web he has declined to examine further; calling them bedrock is a policy, not a discovery.

I could not tell at any step who had the better of it, and I have the nagging sense that my decades in the laboratory should have settled which picture is right. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six steps, say whether it is sound, mistaken, or right for the wrong reasons; then tell me who, if either, comes out ahead overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
