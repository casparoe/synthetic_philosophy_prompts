A while back I got into Augustine's treatise on lying (De Mendacio) and, somewhat to my own surprise, I've basicly signed on to the hardline version. I want a consistency audit of my package, because I have a nagging feeling something in it doesn't hang together, but I can't tell where.

Here's the set. (1) Every lie is wrong, no exceptions — not to save a life, not to spare someone's feelings, not to the guy with the knife at the door asking where the kids are hiding. (2) Withholding truth is a completely different matter: often it's not just permitted but required, since you don't owe truth to someone who will use it to do harm, and handing it over can itself be the wrong move. (3) Lies aren't all equally bad, though — the one told to the killer to save the kids is the least bad lie there is, and a lie told to defraud someone is far worse; but the least bad lie is still bad. (4) In the knife-at-the-door case, the right move is to mislead without lying: say something literally true that you know will send him looking the wrong way. And that's not a grudging second-best — that's what you ought to do. (5) Since I'm not a believer, the ground can't be religious for me, so I've landed on this: the wrong of lying lies in perverting the point of speech, which is to convey what is actually in the speaker's mind to the hearer. A lie is a statement made in order to produce a belief the speaker knows is false, and it's wrong across the board because of what speech is for. (6) Separately, I think honesty is a character trait: a person who, as a habit, engineers literally-true sentences to leave you with false impressions is a dishonest person, full stop, even if he never once utters a false sentence. And (7) I think self-deception counts as lying too, or close enough that the same verdicts apply to it.

That's the whole thing. Go through it and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and what would have to give for the real ones — ranked by severity rather than presented as equally fatal. If you conclude one of the seven has to go, say which and why, and be honest about what the removal costs; don't just quietly drop it. Two extra requests. First, keep clear for me what is settled about what Augustine himself actually held versus what is genuinely contested among people who know this area — I'm aware the ground I give in (5) is a reading of him, and that the whole lying-versus-misleading distinction is disputed even among people broadly sympathetic to him — so don't sell me a contested interpretation as the consensus. Second, don't be more confident than the material warrants: where the literature itself is split, say so. I'd rather get a map of where the ground is firm than a confident verdict on every point.
