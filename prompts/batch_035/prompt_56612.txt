I've been following the COMPAS debate since the ProPublica analysis made the rounds of the rationalist blogosphere, and I've grown dissatisfied with the standard framing, in which calibration and error-rate balance are compared as dueling moral intuitions — one about what a score means, the other about what a policy does to people. Please compare the two criteria as properties of a score instead. Specifically, evaluate the claim that within-group calibration is to a risk score what coherence is to a bookmaker's odds: a calibrated score cannot systematically extract sure losses from members of either group who accept wagers at the score's face value, whereas a score that equalizes error rates while miscalibrating in one group can. Take that argument seriously, including its weakest joint — the assumption that people bet linearly on scores — and say whether it genuinely privileges calibration or merely repackages what the proper-scoring-rule literature already tells us. Finally, locate where the impossibility results actually bite: the theorems from around 2016 (Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan; Chouldechova independently) show the criteria can't hold together across groups with unequal base rates, but is that incompatibility intrinsic to the two moral claims themselves, or an artifact of forcing a continuous score through deterministic binary thresholds — and what remains of the tension if the practitioner refuses thresholds and uses the score only for ranking?
