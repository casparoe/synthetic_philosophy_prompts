I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I'm trying to get a small semester grant from my department for a pilot project before I commit to it as a dissertation chapter. My half-formed idea, take it and make it into a two-page proposal:

The debates about how consciousness is distributed across animals — whether fish feel pain, the whole insect sentience thing, whether crabs and octopuses deserve welfare protections — all seem to quietly lean on an inference I've started calling "same mechanism, same feel": the idea that structural or functional similarity between our neural machinery and theirs supports attributing similar experience. Nobody ever states this principle explicitly, and I suspect that a lot of the disagreement between the attributors and the skeptics is actually disagreement about the principle, not about the data. On top of that there's an asymmetry I can't shake: the skeptics argue from *absence* of human-like structures ("no cortex, no thank you") while the attributors argue from *presence* of flexible behavior, and these two inferential routes aren't mirror images of each other — one is a claim about what experience requires, the other about what it reveals. Or maybe they are mirror images and I'm confusing myself.

What I need in the proposal: the question stated precisely enough that it could in principle be answered, why the existing standard positions (the analogy-based ones, the theory-based or "neurobiological marker" ones) leave it open, what I'd actually do week by week in one semester, what a deliverable looks like given there's no new empirical data, and an honest account of what would make this too big or just fail. And please don't add citations or a bibliography, I'll handle references myself — just refer to positions and argument types in plain terms. Also if you think my "same mechanism, same feel" framing collapses into something already standard, tell me in the proposal's limitations section, don't just quietly absorb it.
