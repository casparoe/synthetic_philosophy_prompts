I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and we just covered Aumann's agreement theorem (the 1976 "agreeing to disagree" result). My roommate, an economics PhD student, has taken to deploying it at dinner, and the argument he's built seems to prove far more than the theorem could possibly support. I'd like you to act as an expert in Bayesian epistemology and decision theory, brainstorm the possible failure points before committing, and then give me a careful diagnosis.

Here is his argument. (1) Aumann's theorem says that if two agents are rational Bayesians with a common prior, and their posteriors on some proposition are common knowledge between them, then their posteriors must be equal. (2) The common prior assumption is trivially satisfied for ordinary factual questions: there's no antecedent reason my prior on whether the minimum wage significantly reduces teen employment should differ from that of another economist. (3) When sincere experts publicly state their credences — which is what review articles and policy debates amount to — those credences become common knowledge. (4) So two rational, sincere experts who have publicly announced their positions on a factual question cannot coherently disagree; persistent public disagreement is proof that at least one party is irrational or insincere. (5) Since economists and other academics disagree publicly for decades on end, entire fields are pockets of irrationality. He has concluded that he is entitled to dismiss any disagreement he encounters, and he now ends arguments with "we can't agree to disagree" — including one about whether my joke at his department party was funny, on the grounds that we have roughly the same sense organs, hence a common prior about humor.

Something in this proves too much, but I can't pin down which step fails, and I suspect several distinct things are being smuggled across the steps at once — something about what "common knowledge" means in the theorem versus how he's using it in (3), and something about what "common prior" quietly assumes in (2). Please identify exactly which premise or inference to reject, explain why it looked fine, and then price the alternatives: what does my roommate have to give up to keep each premise he wants, and is his conclusion about fields of inquiry salvageable in some weakened form?
