Eight months ago I was diagnosed with chronic migraine, and since then I have been trying to settle on a long-term management plan. There are three live options. Option A is a highly effective daily preventive with a substantial side-effect burden — persistent cognitive fog. Option B is a monthly injection, somewhat less effective, with two or three flare days after each dose. Option C is minimal medication plus a strict lifestyle regimen — sleep discipline, trigger management, the rest of it. Here is the thing: comparing them pairwise, I get a cycle. Offered only A or B, I choose B — the milder, more spread-out burden seems clearly preferable to A's extra efficacy. Offered only B or C, I choose C — the flare days and the incremental efficacy do not seem worth it against C's freedom. Offered only C or A, I choose A — faced with so stark a comparison, I would take the effective drug and live with the fog. I have retried these comparisons many times; each verdict is stable, and each one feels like a judgment, not a mood.

I also happen to have views about betterness, and I want an audit of whether my whole package hangs together. Here it is:

(1) 'At least as good as' is transitive. If A is at least as good as B, and B at least as good as C, then A is at least as good as C. I cannot see what could make that fail for a coherent evaluator.

(2) Betterness is complete, at least for options like mine: of any two, either one is better than the other or they are equally good. I am not tempted by talk of parity or incomparability here — these options are comparable; that is exactly the problem.

(3) The spectrum arguments are right. Ever since I first worked through the case of a year of headaches versus a longer period of milder suffering, and the longer spectra built on that pattern, I have been convinced that 'better than' can genuinely fail to be transitive — as a fact about the relation itself, not as an artifact of our ignorance or of the limits of introspection.

(4) Pairwise verdicts are verdicts of betterness, and pairwise betterness governs pairwise choice: when the choice is between exactly two options and one of them is better, I ought to choose it.

(5) My cycle is not a mistake. Each of the three pairwise preferences above is, on reflection, a judgment about which option is better in that comparison.

(6) Whatever else is true of me, I am not exploitable. A rational agent cannot be led, by a sequence of individually acceptable trades, to pay for the privilege of ending up exactly where she started.

Two further things. First, I do believe there is a fact about which of the three plans I most ought to adopt — that one of them is the right plan, and choosing either of the others would be a substantive mistake rather than an arbitrary picking. Second, I have to decide within the month.

Please audit the set rather than survey the literature. Which of the six commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? For each genuine one, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. And please say whether the belief I report just above — that there is a fact about which plan I ought to choose — is itself part of the problem.
