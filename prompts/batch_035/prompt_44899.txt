My vegan friend and my cousin who's been getting more religious had a blowup at dinner last week, and I somehow ended up in the middle of it. The topic was animal sacrifice — my cousin was saying how when the Temple gets rebuilt the sacrifices come back, and that's part of what he's looking forward to, and my friend basically said that worshipping a God who demands you slit animals' throats to please Him is worse than the pagans, since at least they thought they needed to feed thier gods. Later I asked my cousin whether any of this bothers him at all, and he said some big medieval rabbi had an answer to exactly this — I think he said it was Maimonides? — the idea being that the sacrifices were never what God actually wanted. People back then couldn't imagine religion without offering animals, everybody around them was doing it for idols, so God gave them a version pointed at Him instead, like methadone for idol worshippers, with the plan being they'd eventually be weaned off it onto prayer and study.

I find this weirdly compelling and weirdly unsatisfying at the same time and I can't put my finger on why. Like — if the sacrifices were a workaround for an ancient audience that couldn't handle anything else, why does my cousin's tradition still pray every single day for the sacrificial service to come back? And does the concession thing actually answer my friend, or does it just relocate the problem? A God who's willing to command something wrong, or at least pointless, for centuries because people are attached to it seems maybe scarier than one who simply wants the sacrifices. Is the medieval rabbi's move a real solution, a dodge, or does it only work if you buy some bigger picture he had about what religion is even for? Also wondering whether my friend's "worse than the pagans" line is actually fair. Curious how someone who knows this stuff would sort it out, and whether anyone back then already made my objections to this view — seems like the kind of thing people would have fought about.
