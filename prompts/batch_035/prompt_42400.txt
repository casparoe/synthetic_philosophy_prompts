My great-aunt left me a watercolor that's been "the good one" in our family for sixty years, and last month an appraiser took one look and said it's a mid-century student copy, worth maybe a hundred bucks. It looks exactly the same on my wall as it did before, which has sent me down a wierd rabbit hole about why originals matter, and I've been testing my gut on a bunch of cases. Here's roughly where I've landed, see if you can find the principle behind it:

- Van Meegeren's fake Vermeers fooled everyone and were adored until the exposure. My gut: what he did was genuinely wrong, not just commercially wrong, and the paintings got *worse as art* when the deception came out, even though nothing on the canvas changed.
- An authorized museum print of a famous painting: totally fine, nobody's deceived, it's just worth less. No whiff of wrongdoing at all.
- An honest exact copy: some painter with mad skills hand-copies a Monet stroke for stroke and sells it clearly labeled "exact copy, artist unknown." Fine. Worth much less than the Monet, but nothing wrong with it.
- A "genuine" old master that's been restored so heavily over the centuries that maybe 70% of the surface is conservators' repaint: still feels like the real thing to me, more real than the honest copy does. But the thing I apparently believe is that 30% original pigment counts as the real painting and 0% doesn't, and put that way it feels completely arbitrary.
- A Renaissance workshop piece where the master designed it and assistants executed most of it under his eye, sold under his name then and now: feels fully legit to me. And I can't square that with the forgery verdict, because the physical relationship between the famous name and the brushwork is actually pretty similar in the two cases.
- Cover song: someone rerecords a song note for note, everyone knows it's a cover, zero sense of fraud, and I don't even feel much of a value gap beyond habit. But with the structurally identical painting case (exact copy, honestly labeled) I do feel a real gap. This might just be an inconsistency in me, but I'd rather know which way to resolve it.
- Wine: someone quietly tops up a magnum of a great vintage with identical-tasting wine from the next year over. Feels like fraud, full stop, even though blind tasters can't tell. I'm firm on this one, and I notice it has the same "identical token, wrong history" structure as the forgery, but without any of the art talk about creativity to lean on, which makes me think the art talk isn't doing the real work.

The case I keep going back and forth on: van Meegeren's fakes are now enjoyed in their own right, as "the famous forgeries," with the deception totally out in the open and kind of part of the point. Once everyone knows the story and the fakery is baked into the object's identity, does my "it got worse when exposed" verdict have to go? I half think it does.

So that's my assignment: is there one principle that gets all or most of these verdicts -- something like the value riding on causal history rather than on how the thing strikes you when you look at it? If there is, state it precisely enough that I can actually apply it, tell me what it says about two cases I haven't listed (a digital artwork where the artist's own file and a bit-identical copy exist side by side and everyone knows which is which; and a living artist who paints something in the style of a dead master and honestly sells it as "painted last year, in the style of X" that nobody can distinguish from the real thing), and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle just cannot save. If you need two principles instead of one, fine, but then tell me which verdicts belong to which, and why my gut runs them together. Also feel free to tell me if one of my verdicts is just wrong and I should give it up -- I'd rather lose a verdict than carry a principle nobody should believe. And if it matters, I'm keeping the watercolor either way.
