I run a philosophy discussion section for second-year undergraduates (15 students, a single two-hour block) attached to an ethics course that just finished a unit on moral status. I want the session to be an activity, not a lecture, built around whether video-game characters could matter morally — and, more specifically, around two puzzles I've been chewing on that I want the students to hit experientially rather than be told about. The first is what I've been privately calling the "script versus someone" problem: in games, some NPCs behave in ways that look exactly like suffering, but we know some of them are governed by a simple lookup table while others are governed by something more complicated, and we can't tell which is which from behavior alone. The second is the "respawn" problem: in most games, no character can be permanently destroyed — kill one and it reloads — yet something in the system may still be changed by what we did, even if the character itself, on re-entering the world, remembers nothing. I've come to suspect that what these two puzzles jointly show is that our intuitions about moral status quietly trade on a distinction between the *character* (the continuous person-like entity we're interacting with) and the *process* underneath it (whatever generates the character's behavior and may accumulate effects the character never experiences), and I want the activity to force students to notice that distinction themselves before anyone names it.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, though I'd like you to redesign it properly. Split the students into two groups: five play "villagers" and ten play "harvesters" in two competing teams. Villagers are each given a sealed instruction card — half the cards tell the student to follow a fixed decision tree (if threatened, beg; if threatened twice, offer supplies; and so on), half tell them to respond however they think a frightened person actually would. Harvesters can only win points by taking things from villages, and taking by force costs the villagers something (each villager decides how to show this). Then, mid-game, the facilitator introduces the respawn rule: any villager "killed" rejoins after ten minutes as a new character with no memory of anything — but secretly, each subsequent death requires that villager's student to play their distress more intensely than the last, so the pressure accumulates in the process even though no character carries it. At the end, before the debrief, reveal which villagers were scripted and which were improvised, and have the harvesters vote on whether the session they just enthusiastically participated in was something they'd be prepared to defend. My hope is that the vote goes badly wrong in an instructive way — that students who were fine slaughtering villagers all game suddenly feel uncomfortable once they learn which ones were "really" responding, and then can't articulate why that should matter.

Please act as an experienced designer of philosophy workshops and think through this carefully before writing it up — maybe start with a little brainstorming about what the mechanics are actually doing to the participants at each stage. Deliver: complete rules and materials (including the actual text I should put on the villager instruction cards and any point tables), a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, and a structured debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the specific philosophical questions — the behavioral evidence problem, whether the character/process distinction can do real moral work, whether harm can accrue to something that experiences each episode as isolated, and whether the vote at the end is tracking guilt, evidence, or something else. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm particularly worried the harvester teams will just treat it as a competitive game and not feel anything at all until it's too late for the debrief to land) and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the underlying issue no classroom exercise of this kind can reproduce, and what I'll have to supply by fiat in the debrief.
