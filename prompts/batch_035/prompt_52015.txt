So I'm on my city's commemorative review panel — the committee that gets petitions about statues, murals, street names, building names, that kind of thing. We've been running for a few years now and I've been going back over our record trying to figure out what our actual rule is, because we have a new case coming up next month and honestly I'm not sure our past decisions point one way.

Here's the track record, in rough order:

1. The bronze of a Confederate general in Market Square, put up in the 1920s by a heritage association (paper trail shows they chose that spot to intimidate). Descendants of enslaved people in town petitioned for removal. We removed it, relocated to a cemetery with an interpretive plaque.

2. A street named for one of the town founders, who owned slaves but is commemorated for literally founding the place and endowing its first school. Nobody petitioned. We kept the name and added a plaque about the slavery.

3. An auditorium named for a local doctor celebrated for a famous post-war medical breakthrough. Files came out showing the breakthrough's data came from experiments on poor patients without consent, some of whom were still alive or had children in town. Survivors' families petitioned. Name came off within the year.

4. A statue of a colonial-era militia leader who led a massacre of the region's indigenous people, in a park nobody especially loved. The indigenous community petitioned. We removed it to storage pending a museum placement.

5. A mural of a labor organizer who decades later was shown to have belonged to a segregationist club early in life, before his organizing career. No petition. We kept it with an added interpretive panel.

Now the new case. A bronze statue of a 19th-century industrialist who built the mills that made the town — the library and the park are his too, and his descendants still live here and fund a lot of things. But the mills ran on child labor under conditions that killed people, and a group of descendants of millworker families has petitioned for removal. Complicating things: the square is basically named after him informally, everyone gives directions by his statue, and I'd guess most residents who use his name daily couldn't tell you the first thing about the man. The family is offering to fund a "contextual reinterpretation" of the statue rather than lose it.

So, what do we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Walk me through what you think is going on: what rule or rules actually fit our past decisions (there's more than one that fits, I'm fairly sure — e.g. one that keys off what the person is mainly honored FOR versus one that keys off whether there's an affected group actively carrying the harm), which precedent is the closest analog, and what each available choice would commit us to going forward — like, if we remove him, what happens to the founder street, and if we keep him with reinterpretation, what did we think we were doing in the doctor case? Also tell me what hypothetical next case would most cleanly expose whether our real rule is the one about the honoree's primary legacy or the one about ongoing harm to a living community, since I suspect those two come apart somewhere and I'd like to know where before we vote.
