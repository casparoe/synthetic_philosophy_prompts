the case: a low-level manager at a firm owned by a notorious polluter learns that installing scrubbers would marginally hurt quarterly profits but would meaningfully clean up the town's air. the firm's owner has an explicit standing rule -- never spend a dime on emissions controls -- and everyone knows he'll be furious. the manager installs the scrubbers anyway, saying "i don't care about the owner's rule, i just want the plant to pass the town's inspection." question asked to subjects: did the manager intentionally help the environment? we actually ran this informally on the class last week and got a genuinely messy split, which is exactly the kind of data that makes the two main explanations disagree: the view that moral judgment itself drives intentionality attributions, versus the view that what matters is whether the outcome is abnormal relative to the operative norm, regardless of its moral valence. helping here is morally good but norm-violating in the local context, so the explanations make different predictions, or at least the students have to figure out whether they do.

so: please draft the essay question (something they could answer in 60 minutes, aimed at whether they can operationalize each explanation and say what it predicts about the scrubber case), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, with a note on the difference between an A answer and a B answer. one formatting thing, and im serious about this: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. write everything as continuous prose like an email, even the marking scheme -- ill restructure it myself when i build the rubric doc. and keep the model answer honest about where the case is underdetermined, i dont want students rewarded for bluffing confidence that the data cleanly favors one side.
