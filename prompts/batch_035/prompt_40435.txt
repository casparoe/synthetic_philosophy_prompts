I'm a first-year graduate student in public policy, and I keep running into an inconsistency in the way my coursework justifies paternalistic taxes. When we cover cigarette taxes, sugary drink levies, or mandatory pension contributions, the textbook rationale is no longer just externalities (secondhand smoke, public healthcare costs); it's what the behavioral economics literature calls internalities — the idea that the smoker or the undersaver is imposing a cost on their own future self, because they discount hyperbolically rather than exponentially, and the tax or the mandate functions as a commitment device that the present-biased self would have chosen for itself if it could. The same logic shows up in defenses of auto-enrollment defaults and cooling-off periods for high-interest loans. Fine so far. But the very same governments that tax cigarettes to correct my present bias also run their own lotteries, design scratch tickets with vivid near-miss imagery, and market them most heavily in precisely the neighborhoods where present bias is most pronounced; my own state's budget briefing apparently counts on lottery revenue and celebrates when it grows. So the state corrects my akrasia when it costs it money and monetizes my akrasia when it pays.

Here's what I want, as a philosopher would give it to me: What theory of weakness of will does internality-correcting taxation actually presuppose? Does the policy need the full hyperbolic-discounting machinery — literally a person whose preferences reverse predictably over time, so that the tax just re-weights the intertemporal choice in favor of the long-run self? Or would older and quite different accounts of akrasia license the same policy: the Socratic view that nobody knowingly does wrong (so the smoker doesn't really prefer smoking, and the tax just removes confusion), the Aristotelian view of acting against one's settled judgment, or the Davidsonian picture of acting against one own better judgment while holding the evaluative belief in place? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the person and of welfare that internality rhetoric presupposes — whose preferences count, over what time horizon, and whether the present self's actual choice has any standing to be overridden. Then do the residue analysis: which parts of the tax policy are strictly implied by that implicit theory, which merely fit comfortably with it, and where the practice is underdetermined so that several rival accounts of akrasia would bless the same tax.

Finally, tell me whether the lottery contradiction is real or only apparent. Is there a coherent theory of rationality and welfare under which a government may simultaneously correct present bias in savings and smoking while promoting it in gambling — for instance, a view on which lottery purchases express genuine, well-informed demand for hope and are not failures of self-control at all, or a view on which the two policies answer to different constituencies' considered preferences? Or does the practice, taken whole, presuppose flatly inconsistent things about whether the present-biased citizen's choices reveal her welfare, and what would have to change in either the taxes or the lotteries to make it consistent? I don't need citations or a literature review; I need the reconstruction argued out.
