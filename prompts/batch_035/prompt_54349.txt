Quick background: I help manage a midsize farm co-op, so I deal with the actual apparatus of pest control every season, and the more I look at it the less sure I am what our whole system thinks insects are.

So: for field crops, extension entomology runs on thresholds. You don't spray when you see aphids, you scout, count, and spray only when the projected yield loss exceeds the cost of spraying — the "economic injury level." Pure cost-benefit arithmetic, the insect is basically a debit. But then at the same time the pesticide label tells me not to spray when bees are foraging, spray at dusk instead — and that's framed as protecting the pollinators, which sounds like the bees matter somehow, except obviously it's about my almond yield, so is it welfare or just economics wearing a costume? And organic certification bans synthetic insecticides but happily lets me use pyrethrin or Bt, which kill insects just as dead — so the line being drawn there isn't about the insect's experience at all.

Then the weirdest part: the same regulatory world that doesn't care how my crickets die has actual opinions about how rats die — glue trap bans, rules about dispatching vertebrate pests quickly. One phylum gets welfare rules, the other gets none.

So my question: what theory of the moral status of insects does this whole apparatus presuppose, if any? Is it consistent across its parts, or is the threshold system presupposing zero status while the bee-label stuff quietly presupposes something more? And practically — if we swapped in a genuine insect-welfare presupposition, what would actually have to change? Would thresholds even survive, or would the way the threshold is met (fast-acting vs slow-acting agent, bait vs broadcast) become the whole game? I'd also like to know whether the current practice is actually committed to zero status or is compatible with several views, cause that's the part I can't get straight myself.
