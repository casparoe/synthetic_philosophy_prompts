i'm a phd student in social policy, working on benefit conditionality and sanctions, and there's this one analogy that does a ridiculous amount of work in our seminars (and honestly in the whole debate about the "undeserving poor"): the safety net. you know the family of metaphors -- the welfare state is a net strung under the highwire of the labour market, there to catch people who fall. then critics on the right say the net has become a hammock (dependency, people settling in), and the workfare people say what we really need is a trampoline -- something that bounces you back up instead of just holding you. my supervisor basically thinks in this imagery, and it's not just rhetoric either, people derive actual policy conclusions from it: if it's a net, benefits should look temporary and emergency-ish, and if it's become a hammock, then sanctions and work requirements are justified to keep it net-shaped.

i want a proper audit of the thing. first map it out for me: what plays the role of the highwire, what's the fall supposed to be, what's the catching, who strung the net, and so on. then tell me where the mapping breaks. the disanalogies i keep running into are (a) a net catches you after a discrete fall, but alot of poverty isn't a fall at all -- people are born near the ground and never get up on the wire in the first place; (b) in the picture the person climbs back up onto the wire by themselves, but getting back into work isn't really like that; (c) nobody in the image built the highwire or decided how high it hangs or who's allowed on it. are there others i'm missing? and which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions people draw, versus which are just pedantic?

then the two derived metaphors. does "hammock" track anything real that "net" got wrong, or is it just a sneer? and "trampoline" -- my instinct is the trampoline people (the mead-style workfare / new paternalism crowd) use that word to sound pro-poor while justifying conditionality, but does the trampoline image itself carry any of that argument, or would the conclusion need independant grounds?

last thing: is there a better analogy in the neighbourhood? i've heard people in my field argue we should talk about a floor rather than a net, or scaffolding rather than a net, and i wonder whether switching the image changes which policies come out justified. basically what i want to know is how much of the argument about sanctions and "deservingness" is the metaphor doing the work, and how much is doing it on its own.
