I've come to hold a refurbished version of Pascal's wager, and I want a serious map of its exposure: not the familiar dismissals, but the observations, arguments, and formal results that should move someone who has already accepted the decision-theoretic framework the wager lives in.

Here is the position, patches included. Strip the wager of its specifically Christian content and read it as an argument in expected-utility theory with payoffs treated as infinite only in the limit — do the comparisons at finite reward R, then let R grow without bound, so the ordering of acts is fixed before any actual infinity appears. Handle the many-gods objection with a mixed strategy: since mutually exclusive salvific hypotheses cannot all be bet on, distribute practical commitment across them in proportion to their evidential support, the way one would randomize over actions when no pure option is optimal. And my own further patch, to block the arbitrary-deity objection (the god who rewards only heretics, or only skeptics, or only people who bet against him): count as live infinite-payoff options only those hypotheses with substantial historical uptake — traditions that a serious number of reflective people have actually staked their lives on. The upshot I claim is that if any live option has nonzero probability of infinite reward, some bet among the live options strictly dominates betting on none, and the patches let me say which.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness, with at least these on the table, though I'd welcome others: the worry that once infinite utilities are in play, any act mixed with the wager inherits the same infinite expected value, so the argument proves too much and cannot discriminate between betting, half-betting, and betting on almost nothing; the worry that exclusivity comes back at the level of the mixed strategy itself, since some traditions punish false worship more severely than indifference, making the mixed strategy strictly worse than a single well-chosen pure bet or none; the worry about whether belief is even the kind of thing that can be adopted on the basis of a payoff table, and whether the wager's replies (acting as if one believes, cultivating the habits) preserve the argument or quietly change its conclusion; and the question of whether my historical-uptake patch is doing anything principled at all, or whether it just makes the wager hostage to sociological facts I'd be embarrassed to cite in any other philosophical argument.

For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of my refurbished wager would concede it as counting against the position, or would dismiss it, and what the best available reply costs — I care about replies that save the conclusion only by draining the argument of content. I also want an honest verdict on whether the successive patches (limit reading, mixed strategy, historical-uptake filter) have insulated the view into something that cannot lose, and whether that should bother someone whose position is a decision-theoretic one, where you'd think the possibility of losing was the whole point of the exercise.
