My roommate's in law school and she's got this argument that there's no real moral difference between lying and misleading someone — the difference is legal at most. I want to say she's wrong but honestly I can't find the broken step. Here's the argument as she gave it to me:

(1) Whether an utterance is a lie can't depend on the words themselves, since "the money is in the account" is a lie when I say it believing it's not, and innocent when I say it believing it is.
(2) So whatever makes lying wrong has to live in the speaker's effect on the hearer: getting someone who trusts you to end up believing something false.
(3) When I mislead without lying, I have exactly that same effect and the exact same intention — asked "did you cheat on me?" I answer "I've been completely honest with you about everything that matters," strictly true, said precisely so you'll conclude no. Same target, same trap.
(4) The only remaining difference is that in the lie I hand you the false belief finished, while in the misleading case you assemble it yourself out of true parts I supplied. Whether the victim completes the con themselves can't change how bad the con is — the mark putting the last signature on the contract himself doesn't make the con man any more innocent.
(5) So lying and misleading are morally on a par, and treating liars as worse is just a hangup about literal truth.

Here's my problem. (1) looks undeniable, (3) is just a description of how misleading works, and (4) sounds right until I notice it's the same reasoning that would erase the difference between pulling the trigger yourself and rigging the door so the guy fires the gun into himself on the way in. And her conclusion makes the perjury rule — false statements under oath get prosecuted, "merely misleading" testimony mostly doesn't — come out as pure arbitrariness, which even she doesn't quite beleive, because she keeps saying the law is tracking something real there. My best guess so far is that the trick is in the slide from (1) to (2): she talks as if, once it's not the words and it's not just the hearer's final mental state, there's nothing left for the wrong to consist in. But when I try to name a third thing — something about the act you're performing rather than the string you're producing or the belief you're causing — I can't make it precise without it collapsing back into "the words matter," which (1) was supposed to have killed. Other days I think the answer is (4) and the cost is that people who let others fool themselves get partly excused, which I also don't want to sign.

So: which premise do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If you think more than one is available, say which rejection is cheaper and why. And since you're working this out off the top of your head, no looking things up — tell me how confident you are in each claim you make along the way, and flag the single point you're least sure of.
