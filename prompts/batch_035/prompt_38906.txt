So I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on decision theory, about 6000 words over one semester, and I want to build it around the Pasadena game. Quick setup so we're on the same page: like the St. Petersburg game, you flip a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time, and if heads first shows up on flip n your payoff is 2^n/n, except the sign alternates — you *gain* that amount if n is odd and *lose* it if n is even. So the "expected value" is the series 2 - 4/2 + 8/3 - 16/4 + ..., which sums to about ln 2 if you add terms in the natural order, but by the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reorder the same terms to get any real number you like, or make it diverge. Hence the game "has no expected value," and everyone from Hájek and Nover onward has been fighting about what a rational agent should pay for it.

Here's my half-formed idea that I want turned into a real proposal. People keep saying the expectation is "undefined" for both St. Petersburg and Pasadena, but these look like really different *kinds* of undefinedness to me. St. Petersburg is more like divergence: the partial sums run off to infinity and no reordering changes that — the failure is order-invariant and kind of robust. Pasadena is more like order-dependence: the value isn't missing so much as it's hostage to an arbitrary choice of summation order, and once you fix an order (the natural one) you get a perfectly ordinary number. My hunch is that the standard responses to Pasadena — sum in the natural order and get ln 2, or constrain the value of a single play by what the average payout converges to over many repeated plays, or just say any valuation is as consistent as any other (I've heard there's a result along those lines showing arbitrary value assignments are compatible with the usual axioms, but I only know it secondhand, so just present it as a position in the literature, no attribution needed) — are all really responses to the second kind of failure, order-dependence, and whether any of them works turns on a question nobody states outright: is the ordering of outcomes in the expected-value summation an actual part of the description of the gamble, or just an artifact of how we happen to write it down? If it's an artifact, ordering the terms to get ln 2 looks like smuggling a conclusion in; if it's real, then the gamble and a rearranged cousin are genuinely different objects and the paradox mostly dissolves. I want the proposal to make *that* the central question, not the usual "what should you pay" framing.
