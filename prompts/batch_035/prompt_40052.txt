I'm a high school senior taking an elective on modern physics, and a few weeks ago our teacher mentioned in passing that Einstein did not believe in free will — that he regarded our choices as fixed by natural law just as thoroughly as the orbits of planets, and that he once said, in a paraphrase I trust more than my memory of his exact words, that everything is determined from start to finish, and that although we imagine we choose, we are like the moon believing it steers itself. My friend from the debate club seized on this and has been building an argument out of it ever since, and I can't find where it slips.

His argument runs like this. (1) On Einstein's picture, everything that happens, including every act of thinking, is fixed by law; nothing we do could have been otherwise. (2) If my future choice is already fixed, then my deliberating about it cannot make any difference to which option I end up taking. (3) A process that makes no difference to any outcome is idle — it is something that happens in us, not something we do. (4) Nothing idle can be rational, since being rational requires that there be a better and a worse way to conduct it, and that conducting it better would change something. (5) So deliberate reasoning is never rational, in us or in anyone. (6) But theoretical physics is deliberate reasoning par excellence — years of weighing, trying, discarding. (7) Therefore, if Einstein's denial of free will is right, Einstein never reasoned his way to anything: the special theory of relativity happened to him the way a sneeze does, and his claim that it was the product of patient thought was an illusion he was fated to suffer. My friend insists this is a genuine refutation of Einstein's whole outlook, since the outlook ends up denying the possibility of the very science it was built from. It seems to me the conclusion proves far too much — it would equally show that no one has ever so much as deliberated about what to eat — and I suspect the trouble is around (3) or (4), where the argument seems to trade on treating "making a difference" as if it had only one meaning, but I can't pin down exactly how.

Please tell me which step the argument turns on, why that step nevertheless looked innocent, and what it costs to reject it — in particular, whether there is any coherent way left to describe how deliberation relates to a choice that was, on Einstein's view, fixed before I sat down to think. I'd also ask you to be explicit about your confidence in each part of your answer and to single out the point you are least sure of, since I may present this to my class and I would rather qualify one claim carefully than have the whole analysis rest on it unnoticed.
