Draft: "Cutting your losses"

Everyone knows the sunk cost fallacy. You keep watching the terrible movie because you paid for the ticket, Britain and France keep funding the Concorde, you finish the PhD you already know is a mistake because you're five years in. The standard diagnosis is clean: rational choice is forward-looking only. Costs already paid are gone no matter what you do, so they should drop out of the comparison between your options entirely, and to the extent they don't you're being irrational, full stop. I used to believe this without reservation. Now I think it's too fast, and I want to defend a limited, qualified version of honoring sunk costs. Three arguments.

First, the disposition argument. Even if honoring sunk costs in any particular case makes you worse off, being the kind of person who honors sunk costs might be good for you. Someone who abandons projects the moment they turn unpleasant never builds up the track record of sticking with things that hard projects require. So the disposition is rationally required even though each exercise of it is irrational. It's like being honest: maybe some individual lie would help you, but you should still cultivate honesty as a trait.

Second, the self-promise argument. Deciding to do something is a kind of promise to your future self, and going back on it, just because the going got hard, is a kind of betrayal. If you've told yourself you'll finish the renovation, then quitting halfway because it's unpleasant is not merely a fresh decision -- it's breaking a commitment. And we don't think breaking promises becomes fine as soon as keeping them gets costly.

Third, the evidence argument. The pang you feel at the thought of wasted years is not just noise. It's data about what you actually valued. If abandoning the PhD feels like losing something rather than being set free, that's evidence that the project mattered to you in a way you couldn't have articulated by direct introspection. So the feeling of sunk-cost attachment is informative and shouldn't just be dismissed as bias.

I think the third argument is the strongest and the first is the weakest, but I'll admit I haven't fully worked out how they fit together.

---

So here's what I want from you. Comments of a few kinds:

1. Do any of these three arguments actually do the work I want? My worry, which I can't quite shake, is that they all collapse into ordinary forward-looking decision theory with extra steps: the disposition argument works only because of the future effects of having the disposition, the evidence argument just says the feeling is information about future payoffs, and even the self-promise argument could be recast as "keeping your self-promises has good future consequences." If that's right, then maybe the "defense of honoring sunk costs" is really just a defense of honoring *apparent* sunk costs, and the genuinely sunk part never does any work. Is that fatal for the essay, or is "the costs were never really sunk" itself an interesting enough conclusion?

2. The self-promise argument specifically -- does the analogy between deciding and promising hold up at all? There's no distinct promisee, and it seems like I can just release myself. Also, unlike a real promise, no one else can rely on it. Should I cut this argument, fix it, or lean into it?

3. Am I anywhere conflating the rationality of an act with the rationality of a disposition or trait? I suspect I am in the first argument, but I honestly can't see where.

4. Anything I'm getting just plain wrong, or any obvious objection I'm missing that would embarrass me if this got posted somewhere public?

Be honest about whether this is worth developing or whether it's just reinventing the wheel badly. It feels like the disposition point especially has probably been made a hundred times, maybe under some name I don't know.
