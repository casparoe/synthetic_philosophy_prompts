Before I go back into the literature on the Pasadena paradox and its relatives, I want to work out what I should be demanding of a decision theory in that neighborhood in the first place. For my decision theory seminar, I'm supposed to write the specification first and only then score the proposals against it, and I'd like help constructing the list of desiderata.

The scope is decision-making when expected values are infinite or undefined rather than merely hard to compute. So the theory has to cover at least these cases: the St. Petersburg game, where every finite play is fine but the expectation is infinite, so naive theory says pay any finite price to play; the Pasadena game as Nover and Hájek present it, where the series of probability-weighted payoffs alternates in sign and converges conditionally, so the expectation is not merely infinite but undefined, and reordering the terms yields any value you like; the Altadena game, which pays a dollar more than Pasadena in every state, so it seems obviously at least as good; the super-Petersburg variant with even faster-growing payoffs; and the two-envelope paradox, where the same theory appears to recommend switching forever, or paying to switch.

What I want from you is the requirements list. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it -- e.g., whatever else a theory says, it presumably must prefer Altadena to Pasadena, and must not charge me to swap envelopes with myself -- and say whether the requirement is non-negotiable or a camp preference. Some candidates I have in mind but am not sure how to weigh: dominance and statewise dominance; consistency of preferences under refinements of the outcome space (splitting a payoff into two equiprobable payoffs shouldn't change anything); some relation to the limit of the truncated or capped games, or an explanation of why no such relation is required; and extendability to unbounded utility, or else a principled reason to ban unbounded utilities and accept that St. Petersburg becomes unchoosable rather than infinitely choosable.

The part I most want to see is where the desiderata conflict. In particular, I suspect that dominance plus any requirement that the theory's verdicts be insensitive to how we group and order the terms of an infinite series cannot be jointly satisfied, given what conditional convergence allows; if that's right, or if some other subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and say which requirement you'd drop. Also flag which single requirement does the most work in excluding the live proposals -- truncated-expectation views, relative or comparison-based accounts that rank games against each other rather than assigning values, and utility caps -- since that will tell me where the seminar debates actually turn.

Please keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on adjudicating between the proposals; I'll do the scorecard in class myself. And write the desiderata at a level of generality that would also cover finite-but-pathological cases where the theory's machinery must agree with ordinary expected utility theory, since I'd otherwise worry the list only fits the exotic cases.
