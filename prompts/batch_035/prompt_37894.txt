I'm a first-year PhD student in formal semantics, and at a department mixer a graduate student from comparative literature told me — citing Austin — that "all language is performative," and that this is why truth-conditional semantics is a research program built on sand: semantics treats language as describing the world when in fact it never merely describes anything. I didn't have a comeback, and now I want to know what I was actually being told before I decide whether I need one.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "all language is performative" could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest. Off the top of my head it might mean any of: that every utterance is an act of some kind, however trivially; that every utterance carries illocutionary force, not just the ones with "hereby" in them; that even assertions do things — committing the speaker, presupposing things, imposing norms — so that describing is just one thing language does among others; or the radical claim that language never represents the world at all, so assigning truth conditions is a category mistake. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or something false or confused, and — this is the part I most need — whether accepting it would actually undermine truth-conditional semantics in the way she wanted. Where the people who know this literature disagree among themselves (about whether Austin's own abandonment of the performative/constative distinction succeeded, about whether illocutionary force can be folded into semantics proper, and so on), say so explicitly rather than presenting one side as settled, and mark clearly which points are genuinely closed. Give me the readings as a numbered menu I can keep, and finish by telling me which reading she most likely had in mind, given the work the slogan was doing, and what one question I could ask her to find out.
