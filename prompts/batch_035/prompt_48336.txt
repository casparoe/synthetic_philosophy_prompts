Since I am quoting from memory and do not have the text in front of me, I will give you the passage in close paraphrase rather than in quotation marks — the structure and claims are faithful to the original as I recall it, but the wording is mine:

Peirce there distinguishes three kinds of reasoning — deduction, induction, and what he calls abduction, or hypothesis — and insists that each validates its conclusion in a different way. Deduction's conclusion follows necessarily; induction's conclusion follows with a certain probability, in the long run, from a sample; but the conclusion of an abduction, he says, follows in neither of those ways. It follows only with a kind of plausibility. On this basis he lays down what he calls his "cotary propositions" (a pun he explains: the word is meant to suggest shaving away, making philosophy close-cut), one of which states, roughly, that abduction is the only process by which anything new can ever enter into our knowledge. He then argues that pragmatism, properly understood, is nothing less than the logic of abduction — the doctrine telling us how we are entitled to accept an explanatory hypothesis tentatively, before induction has tested it. And in the course of this he says that when we choose among hypotheses, our choice should be governed by considerations of "economy," which he unpacks as considerations of cost (the expense of testing the hypothesis), of value (how much the explanation would matter if true), and of what he calls the "caution" and "hopefulness" of the inquiry — and he suggests that in the long run of inquiry, the human mind has a natural affinity, shaped by evolution, for guessing nature's laws correctly, so that plausible guesses are not mere stabs in the dark.
