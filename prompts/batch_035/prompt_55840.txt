I convene a reading group on nineteenth-century aesthetics, and for the past several months we have been stuck on one question about Schopenhauer's theory of music. In The World as Will and Representation, Schopenhauer claims that the other arts copy the Platonic Ideas — themselves objectifications of the will — whereas music, uniquely, dispenses with Ideas and copies the will directly: it does not depict the Idea of joy or of striving, but reproduces, in its own medium, the movements of willing itself, tension, satisfaction, and the swing between them.

The group has since split into two camps that have not moved an inch. Camp one holds that the metaphysics is detachable: strip away the will as thing-in-itself and what survives is a serious thesis — that music is intelligible without concepts, and that its play of tension and resolution is a grasp of the form of experience rather than a representation of any particular content. Camp two holds that once the metaphysics goes, nothing distinctive remains: the detached reading collapses into familiar, under-laborated talk about musical expressiveness, which the theory was meant to explain, not presuppose. Both camps can recite each other's arguments from memory; nobody converts.

My own hypothesis, offered as no more than that, is that the disagreement turns on the words "copy" and "about" — that camp one reads Schopenhauer's claim as one about experiential structure while camp two reads it as one about metaphysical reference, and neither side has noticed that they are using the same vocabulary for different jobs. But I am not confident of this; it may instead be a substantive dispute about whether structure without content can carry anything like intentionality at all.

A test case I have floated, without success: imagine a composer who builds a long work by strictly applying a rule for transforming intervals, with no felt tension in the writing and none intended — would either camp say the piece is still a copy of the will? Intuitively they should answer differently, and yet when pressed each camp starts borrowing the other's vocabulary, which is what made me suspect the dispute is partly verbal.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it: is it verbal, or a genuine dispute about whether the notion of aboutness applies to music at all, or two questions — one phenomenological, one metaphysical — sharing a single heading? Support the diagnosis with what each camp actually says rather than a survey of Schopenhauer's system, and make use of the test case if it helps. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a better test case, a restatement of the question — or argue that none should be expected and why. Finally, say explicitly how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
