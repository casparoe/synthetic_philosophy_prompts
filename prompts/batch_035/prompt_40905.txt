Each spring I sit on the jury of a small literary foundation's essay prize for writers under thirty, a duty I fell into some years ago when a novelist's name was needed to lend the enterprise a certain glamour. The prize is judged as much on prose as on argument, but the themes are always argumentative, and this year the foundation set the entrants the following question: "Populist movements across Europe and the Americas claim to speak for the will of the people more faithfully than parliaments, parties, and courts ever can. Either explain what defenders of constitutional democracy must concede if this claim is granted, or show precisely what the claim misunderstands about how democratic will is formed. Entrants may not sit on the fence." The finalists' essays have now reached us, and I must score each out of a hundred and circulate a page of written comments to the other jurors before we meet. My confidence in judging the prose is complete; my confidence in auditing the political philosophy is not, and I would rather check carefully than bluff. Here, in full, is one of the shortlisted entries:

"The constitutional state has an awkward relationship with its own founding fiction. It claims authority in the name of the people while surrounding the expression of that will with obstacles: unelected judges, supermajority rules, staggered terms, independent central banks. The populist thrives on this contradiction, promising to cut through the machinery and return power to its rightful owner. The constitutionalist who dismisses this as mere demagoguery has not answered it; he has only changed the subject. The philosophical difficulty is that the will of the people is not a natural object waiting to be discovered; it has to be produced. Rousseau saw this earliest. In the Social Contract he distinguishes the general will from the will of all: the former is simply what you get when you add up what each citizen wants on a given day, while the latter is directed at the common good and exists prior to any counting. A referendum, on this view, is not a discovery procedure but a construction, and the same holds for elections. There is no unmediated voice of the people for the populist to channel, and in this sense the populist claim is empty. Jan-Werner Müller's What Is Populism? makes the point from another direction. His distinction between the pragmatic and the redemptive faces of democracy explains why populism is a permanent shadow of democratic politics rather than a pathology imported from outside: democracy promises both workable institutions and a kind of salvation, and populists harvest the gap between the two promises. Müller adds that the populist's distinctive move is the claim to exclusive moral representation — only we truly speak for the people — which is why populism is anti-pluralist at its core. If the people's will is always mediated, the remedy is not less mediation but better mediation. Courts, electoral systems, and federal divisions do not thwart popular self-rule; they are what allow a plural public to govern itself over time rather than allowing one snapshot majority to govern everyone once. Opinion polls, being snapshots of dispersed and changeable preferences, are the weakest form of mediation and deserve the least authority. The populist is right that something is being represented in the name of the people; he is wrong only about the vehicle."

What I would like from you is a provisional mark out of a hundred, together with the point-by-point comments I can put before the other jurors. Please work through the essay paragraph by paragraph: identify where the argument is doing real work and where it merely gestures, and flag anything that is attributed to the wrong source, conflated with a neighbouring concept it had just been distinguished from, or asserted as following from a previous step when it does not. The jurors' notes are also sent to the entrants, so the comments should be specific and instructional rather than merely corrective — where you find a fault, say what a stronger treatment of that passage would have had to establish. A rough allocation of the mark between quality of argument, accuracy in the handling of the sources the entrant invokes, and quality of the prose would help me defend my score at the meeting.
