I'm a second-year history undergraduate taking a special subject on the French Revolution, and our seminars keep circling the question of how the Revolution of 1789 turned into the Terror of 1793–94: was it driven by social conflict, by the logic of revolutionary ideology, or by contingent events — the war, the invasion panic, the struggle between factions that nobody could have scripted in advance? In this week's seminar, when someone argued that the Terror owed most to contingency, another student shut the line down with the old proverb that revolutions devour their children, delivered as though it were a settled law of history. The implication seemed to be that once you have a revolution, the purge of its own leadership is baked into it, so no further explanation is needed and the contingencies are decorative: Danton and the rest were doomed the moment the Bastille fell, and the interesting question is only which phase of the meal we are watching. I found the move rhetorically powerful and intellectually suspicious in equal measure, and I'd like to understand it properly before the next seminar, where I expect the same proverb to make another appearance.

My suspicion is that the proverb is several claims wearing one sentence, and that only some of them, if any, actually support the use it was put to — that is, the dismissal of contingency as an explanation of the Terror specifically. Perhaps it is a universal generalisation about all revolutions (which the many revolutions that did not consume their leaders would falsify); perhaps it is a weaker statistical tendency about leadership survival; perhaps it is a causal-mechanism claim about how radicalisation escalates, on which the Terror would still need particular triggers; perhaps it is a normative maxim about the corrupting effect of delegitimising the old order, in which case it says nothing about the timing or shape of events in 1793; and perhaps it is merely a piece of ironic commentary after the fact. Could you lay these possible readings out as a menu, in a form I could bring to seminar, saying for each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and then — most importantly — saying which of the readings, if any, would genuinely license my classmate's inference from the proverb to the conclusion that contingency was irrelevant to the Terror? If none would, I'd like you to say that plainly, and also to note what the honest proponent of the proverb should concede before invoking it. Please write your answer in British English.
