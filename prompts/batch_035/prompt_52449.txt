so here's the situation. I'm a grad student who got drafted to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two faculty in our department who have been arguing about the same thing for two years now, and its gotten a bit stale, so the chair suggested this format.

One of them — call him the transitivist — holds that 'better than,' as it applies to welfare outcomes, behaves structurally like 'longer than': transitive as a matter of the logic of comparatives, so if A is better than B and B is better than C, it's just not an open question whether A is better than C. He also thinks the stakes are huge: if better-than isn't transitive, welfare can't be put on a scale at all, no measurement, no interpersonal comparisons, the whole edifice wobbles.

The other — the spectrum person — thinks the spectrum arguments sink transitivity, at least for welfare. Her favorite setup: line up outcomes where each differs from the next by a barely-perceptible increment (a headache one minute longer, or just barely more intense — each rung clearly better than the one below it), with endpoints as far apart as a five-minute mild headache versus weeks of severe pain. Every step better than the last, but compare the endpoints directly and any sane person says they're indifferent, or even orders them the other way around. She's also fond of a psychophysics analogy: indifference is a just-noticeable-difference relation, and JND relations are intransitive by construction.

Two scoping agreements they've already made, which your draft should reflect: no population ethics — everything is about one person's lifetime welfare, nobody gets added or subtracted; and the word 'indifferent' is on the table as part of the dispute, not treated as already understood.

Write the joint statement I'll circulate to both of them, in this shape:

1. A numbered section of claims both could actually sign — not vacuous ones, and not ones where the wording quietly favors one side. The hard one, they warn me, is anything touching the trichotomy assumption (that for any two outcomes exactly one of better / worse / indifferent holds), since each thinks the other's whole position leans on it. Try anyway. If a formulation only works by watering it down to nothing, say so instead of pretending it works.

2. A disagreements table: the precise points where they differ, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording. Should include at least: what 'better than' even is (a comparative like 'longer than' vs. something closer to perceptual comparison), what the spectrum cases actually demonstrate, and whether intransitive indifference is coherent or just noise smuggled into the word 'indifferent'.

3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — and for each, a concrete case both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. The headache ladder is the obvious test case; give me at least one more they haven't already flogged to death, ideally something where their predictions come apart in an observable way — what a careful judge says when comparing non-adjacent rungs, whether running the ladder downward instead of upward changes anything, that sort of thing.

4. If any part of their dispute strikes you as verbal — say, two senses of 'indifferent' talking past each other — flag which part and why. But only if you actually believe it; don't use 'verbal dispute' as a dodge that avoids drafting the real disagreement.

Then a short note at the end, clearly marked as the drafter's note and not part of the statement they'd sign: say how confident you are that each agreed formulation would really get both signatures, and single out the one point in the document you're least sure of. I'd much rather know where the draft is soft before they see it then find out when one of them refuses to sign.

No citations or reading lists, please — this circulates as-is and I can't have them quibbling over whether so-and-so really said that.
