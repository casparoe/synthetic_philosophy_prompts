I run a metaethics reading group that has spent the past several weeks on evolutionary debunking arguments: Street's Darwinian dilemma for realist theories of value, Kahane's distinction between debunking arguments and debiasing arguments, third-factor replies in the style of Enoch and Wielenberg, and the nagging suspicion that keeps resurfacing in our discussions — that genealogical debunking is rarely applied evenhandedly, but instead gets wheeled out selectively against the moral convictions we already wanted to drop. For our final session of the term, I'd like to hand out a piece of satire that compresses these issues into something the group can argue about, so I'm commissioning it rather than writing it myself.

Please write an FAQ page, roughly 600 words, in the form of numbered entries, for Genealogix Solutions, a fictional full-service belief-debunking company with a completely deadpan corporate voice. The services on offer should each exaggerate a real feature of how debunking arguments are actually run in the literature, so that someone who knows the metaethics laughs rather than winces. Among other things, the FAQ should include: a core service that traces the evolutionary etiology of any belief you dislike and certifies it as off-tracking (with the caveat that the certification is easiest to obtain when the belief conflicts with your client's existing commitments — the selectivity should be visible in the pricing structure without ever being named as a problem); a "third-factor guarantee" under which perceptual and mathematical beliefs are exempt from debunking on the grounds that for those domains, tracking the truth happened to be fitness-enhancing, with a customer-service entry patiently explaining why moral beliefs are mysterily excluded from this coverage; a two-tier service distinguishing full debunking from mere debiasing, priced differently and described in language that tracks the real distinction between showing that a belief-forming process couldn't have been sensitive to the facts in question and merely showing that it sometimes leads us astray; and a risk-disclosure clause raising something like the Darwinian dilemma — either the evolutionary forces shaping our evaluative beliefs were insensitive to independent evaluative truths, or those truths somehow rode along with fitness, and the company declines liability on either horn.

A few constraints. First, keep the corporate register absolutely straight: no winking asides, no italics screaming irony at the reader, no explicit statement of any objection — the piece should exhibit the standard worries (who gets debunked, why the exemptions fall where they do, whether the service could debunk beliefs about its own billing practices) rather than voice them, though you may include one or two customer testimonials and a "concerned" letter from a rival service that insists its third factors are just as good as anyone's. Second, the philosophical content has to be accurate enough that a specialist would recognize each gag as fair to the position or practice being satirized, not a caricature of it — the target is the selective deployment of genealogical debunking and the structure of the arguments themselves, not any named author, so attribute nothing to a real person beyond what the fictional company invents for itself. Third, somewhere in the piece there should be a moment where the reader feels the genuine pull of the debunker's point rather than only the joke — the satire should land on how debunking arguments are used, while conceding why they once seemed so threatening. Please end the FAQ with a closing disclaimer that quietly refuses to debunk itself, or that offers debunking services for beliefs about Genealogix Solutions at a price no rational agent would pay.
