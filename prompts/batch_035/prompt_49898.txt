I host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends the intellectualist view that knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that: to know how to ride a bicycle is to know a certain way of riding, grasped under a practical mode of presentation. Draft exactly five interview questions for him, numbered, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion and no generic questions about his career. The five questions should cover the following, in whatever order works best: Ryle's regress objection about the consideration of propositions preceding intelligent action; the standard insufficiency case of the person who has read every book on cycling but cannot stay upright; a harder variant I have been mulling over — a flamenco dancer who learned entirely by imitation, cannot produce a single true description of her technique, and yet performs flawlessly, which presses the necessity direction rather than the sufficiency one; whether a cat stalking prey, or even a paramecium pursuing nutrients, counts as knowing how, given that attributions of abilities come cheaper than attributions of knowledge; and finally, the linguistic argument that attributions of knowing how pattern syntactically with embedded questions like knowing where the keys are — pressed with the worry that even granting the semantics, nothing follows about abilities, and that the practical mode of presentation is functioning as a placeholder that makes the intellectualist view hard to falsify. Each question should build in a follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give, so that he cannot simply restate the view in answer to any of them.
