my friend Dev has gotten really into forecasting culture over the last year — Tetlock, calibration training, the whole thing — and last night at dinner he informed me that american primacy has maybe a couple decades left because the median lifespan of the empires he'd been reading about is a few centuries and the US is "past due." I pushed back and we argued for like three hours and now I've written up what I think is my best version of my side, except it feels like it proves way too much. here it is:

(1) The outside view says: when forecasting, the base rate of a reference class of similar cases should dominate your inside view, because the inside view is exactly the self-flattering storytelling that makes people bad at forecasting in the first place.
(2) But any actual thing belongs to a huge number of candidate reference classes, and the base rates diverge wildly. The US is an empire (those end fast, apparently), but it's also a nuclear-armed state (none have ever collapsed), a mature democracy, a country that issues the global reserve currency, a country that already survived one civil war, etc. Pick a different class, get a different forecast.
(3) The only way to choose the "right" class is to judge which similarities actually matter causally for the outcome — and that judgment is an inside-view judgment.
(4) So the outside view is either indeterminate (no class chosen, no forecast) or circular (class chosen by inside view, and the base rate just launders your prior opinion).
(5) If indeterminate, it's useless; if circular, it's worse than useless, because it dresses up your vibes as statistics and makes you MORE confident, not less.
(6) Therefore the outside view is basically a rhetorical trick, and Dev's empire forecast is just a doom opinion wearing a lab coat.

Here's my problem. When I got to (6), Dev said "congrats, you've just debunked insurance." As far as he can tell, my argument applies with zero changes to actuaries picking risk classes, doctors using base rates from studies, everything — the argument never mentions empires, so it should generalize. And obviously actuaries are not running a rhetorical trick. So either my argument has a flaw, or it proves something absurd about all statistical reasoning.

And when I tried to flip it on him, I got a symmetrical problem: if he takes the pure outside view seriously, it seems like he has to forecast the imminent collapse of every long-lived institution, since the base rate of "eventually ends" in the class of empires is 100% and inside-view reasoning about "but this one's different" is banned. Which is also crazy — you can't run a prediction market where every existing thing is shorted.

So: which step of mine actually fails, if any? I want to keep (1) in some form, because the bias-diagnosis part really does seem right, the inside view is where the motivated reasoning lives. And is Dev's "everything is about to collapse" horn actually as bad as it looks, or is there a way for him to bite it gracefully? I'd settle for knowing exactly what each of us is paying.
