I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading the book-length exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso, Just Deserts: Debating Free Will (Polity, 2021), and what fascinates me most isn't where they disagree but how much they agree on first: both naturalists, both dismissive of the traditional contra-causal free will, both rejecting the picture in which punishment is justified by an immaterial soul that could have done otherwise in an absolute sense. My amateur reading is that the real fracture is narrower and stranger than the usual free-will fight — whether anything worth calling desert survives once you strip away the contra-causal story, with Dennett holding that "deserve" can be rebuilt on forward-looking, protective grounds, and Caruso holding that it can't, and that we should replace desert-talk with something like the public-health quarantine model. I'd like a poem about that.

Please write a pantoum. As I understand the form, the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza circles back so that the poem's last line repeats its first. That structure seems almost suspiciously well suited to this debate: the same sentences keep coming back, but each time they land in a new context and mean something slightly different, which is roughly what it feels like to watch the two of them talk past each other while agreeing about nearly everything. Use the recurring lines for the shared ground (naturalism, the rejection of contra-causal free will, the shared distaste for harsh retributivism), and let the shifting context of each repetition do the work of the disagreement — Dennett's desert retained and rebuilt, Caruso's desert refused and replaced. The final stanza, where the opening line comes home changed by everything it has passed through, should carry the sense that they end where they began: still of one mind about free will, still divided about deserts.

After the poem, please add a brief prose note (a paragraph or so) that separates what is settled among people who actually know this debate from what is genuinely contested — in particular, flag whether my characterization of the two positions is a fair summary or a contested reading — and mark any place where the poem takes liberties with either philosopher's view for the sake of the form.
