I run a small emergency grant fund for grad students at a big public university — think a few hundred bucks to a couple thousand, when someone's car dies or they get a medical bill or their funding falls through mid-semester. We get maybe 40 applications a semester and we always have to turn people down, so the stakes of getting the criteria right are real. My department chair keeps saying we should fund people based on "need and fault," meaning roughly: did this happen to them, or did they do it to themselves? That's obviously luck egalitarianism territory — the brute luck / option luck distinction — but I've read enough to know the theory gets messy fast, and I don't want to hand my reviewers a slogan.

What I want from you is an actual decision procedure my review team (three grad students and me, ~15 minutes per application, no access to financial records beyond what applicants self-report, and we can't demand invasive documentation like medical records) can run on each application. Translate the theory into something usable: what questions do we ask in what order, what counts as a brute luck vs option luck case when we only have the applicant's own account of events, and where does the procedure have to just stop and hand it to a human because it's now a values question rather than a sorting question. Be honest about the known trouble spots — I'm thinking of things like people whose "choices" trace back to circumstances they never chose (grew up without money so never learned to keep a buffer, didn't buy the insurance because there was nothing to buy it with), and the expensive-tastes style problem where someone's chosen lifestyle makes the same blow hit them harder than it hits someone else. If the theory says those cases should come out a certain way and that way feels wrong, say so rather than hiding it.

Then run the procedure on three applications: (1) a student whose laptop was stolen from the library and who needs a new one to finish a thesis, (2) a student who blew their whole semester's funding on crypto trading and now can't pay rent, and (3) a student who missed a fellowship deadline because they were working two jobs to send money home, and the missed deadline is why they're broke. At least one of those should come out with an answer my reviewers will hate — I want to see whether the procedure owns that or fudges it. Also tell me what the procedure flatly can't do, e.g. anything it structurally can't catch about applicants who present their situations strategically, and where it will systematically mistreat a certain kind of applicant no matter how carefully we follow it.
