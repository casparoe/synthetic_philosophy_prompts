I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, currently serving as teaching assistant for our department's upper-level course on set theory and the foundations of mathematics (final-year undergraduates and first-year master's students). I've been asked to draft the final-exam question on the axiom of choice, and I keep running into the same problem: our students have memorized the standard trinity — choice, Zorn's lemma, the well-ordering theorem — and can recite that Banach–Tarski is a consequence, but they cannot say what the axiom actually does. Ask them whether a given proof requires choice and they answer by checking whether the word "choose" appears in it, rather than by examining the structure of the index set and of the sets being chosen from.

So I'd like you to write one essay-style exam question, suitable for a 90-minute answer, that is designed to be unanswerable by pattern-matching. The question should be built around the distinction between cases where a choice function is definable without any choice axiom (finite families; Russell's boots case, where a uniform rule — say, take the left boot — picks an element from each set; families indexed by a well-ordered set) and cases where it is not (Russell's socks; arbitrary families of pairs; arbitrary families of arbitrary nonempty sets). It should then require students to apply this distinction by diagnosing a list of concrete mathematical claims. Please include at least four such claims, at least two of which should be drawn from the following: a countable union of countable sets is countable; every infinite set has a countably infinite subset; every vector space has a basis; any two sets are comparable in cardinality; every surjection has a right inverse. For each item the student must say whether it is provable in ZF alone and, if not, which choice principle (countable choice, dependent choice, full AC, or something strictly weaker than full AC but stronger than ZF) is doing the work, and identify where in the natural proof it enters. Crucially, at least one item should be a trap in the opposite direction — a claim that sounds as though it needs choice but does not, for instance because a background linear order on the elements supplies a canonical least element of each set, making the choice function explicitly definable. Students who reflexively answer "choice!" to every item should lose marks.

Please provide three things:

1. The exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, self-contained, with any notation the students need defined on the spot.
2. A model answer of roughly 700–900 words, written to the standard a grader could hold scripts against.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, organized around the specific moves a top answer must make, with a one-line note on each saying how students who lose those marks typically go wrong. The scheme should be constructed so that an answer which merely lists the well-known equivalences of AC cannot pass.

One constraint on the model answer: where it relies on a standard metamathematical fact — for example, that some theorem is equivalent to full AC rather than merely implied by it — it should flag the reliance rather than try to argue it from scratch, since the examiners care about the diagnosis, not the proof of the equivalence.

Please end your response with a final line of the form \answer{...} giving, in at most fifteen words, the single conceptual distinction the exam question is designed to test.
