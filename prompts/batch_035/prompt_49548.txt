I am writing a paper on the metaphysics of dispositions, and the field's stock examples — glass fitted with a circuit that dissolves it, sugar cubes dipped in waterproof coating — strike me as engineered precisely to keep the fink/mask distinction clean. I want a case drawn from real medicine, where the distinction has to earn its keep. Construct a thought experiment to these specifications.

Setting: an epilepsy monitoring unit. The disposition in question is a patient's disposition to seize; the triggers are the ordinary ones — sleep deprivation, fever, flickering light. Realism constraints: no demons, no perfect devices. Any implanted device may be no more capable than the responsive neurostimulators actually in clinical use, which monitor brain activity continuously, detect abnormal electrical discharges at or near onset, and deliver stimulation to abort them, with detection latency and occasional failures. You may also draw on the documented clinical phenomenon of electrographic seizures without clinical correlate: seizure activity visible on EEG with no outward convulsion, as occurs in patients whose motor manifestation is suppressed by medication or paralysis.

Specifications: two patients, observationally identical — no clinical seizure ever occurs for either of them, in actuality or in any nearby world. Yet the case must be built so that, on the best analysis, one patient has the disposition to seize and the other lacks it, with the verdict generated by the theories rather than stipulated: design one patient's interferer so that the standard reply to fink-style counterexamples — that a mechanism standing ready to remove the disposition means the disposition was never there in the first place — genuinely applies, and the other patient's so that it cannot. Be careful about what the manifestation is: if an interferer suppresses only the motor component while the electrographic seizure proceeds, has the disposition been masked or merely re-described? Your design must face this, not dodge it.

After the scenario: walk through what the simple conditional analysis says of each patient, what the standing-ready rejoinder says, and what the fink/mask treatment says; identify which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the design if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the conditional analysis would attack the case. Finally, tell me honestly whether your two interferers differ in kind or only in degree — my suspicion is that aborting an episode and damping the excitability that produces it are ends of a continuum, and the glass cases hide this. The case is for a section arguing that when clinicians certify a patient seizure-free they are implicitly taking sides in this metaphysics, so a case that stipulates its verdicts instead of deriving them from the analyses fails.

Close with a final line of exactly this form and nothing after it: Verdict: Patient [A/B] has the disposition to seize; Patient [A/B] does not.
