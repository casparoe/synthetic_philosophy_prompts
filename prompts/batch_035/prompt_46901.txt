I'm a fourth-year PhD student in a philosophy-and-cognition program, and I've been given the job of writing one question for the in-class final of our upper-level seminar on moral psychology and political disagreement (90 minutes, closed book, so the question has to be fully self-contained — no "compare the readings" stuff).

I want the question on moral foundations theory, but I'm tired of prompts like "describe the five foundations and discuss Haidt's claim that conservatives understand liberals better than vice versa" — anyone who skimmed the summaries can fake their way through that. What I actually want to test is whether they can do something with the descriptive/normative gap. Here's the angle I've been toying with, and I'd like you to build the question around it.

Haidt's framework has liberals resting morality mainly on care and fairness while conservatives draw on the fuller set — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity. Sometimes this gets pitched as a breadth advantage, along the lines of: the conservative moral palate has more taste buds. The thing I keep calling the palate analogy is what I want them to work through. If someone thinks all the value in food is sweetness, and someone else registers sweet, sour, bitter, umami, the whole range, we'd say the second person has a richer palate — but we would NOT say they're more correct about food, because there's no fact of the matter about what food is objectively tasty. But now swap in perception: someone missing a photoreceptor really is missing real information about the world. So the exam question needs to make them decide whether moral foundations are supposed to be like taste (breadth is just variety) or like perception (breadth might be a kind of sensitivity to values that are actually there), what turns on choosing one horn, and whether either horn can get you to a normative conclusion without a blatant is-ought slide. And I want a third option on the table too: foundations as coordination tools, where the question stops being who's right and becomes which bundle of foundations sustains cooperation at what scale — "right for what," basically.

So, deliverables:

1. The exam question itself, one paragraph, written so a student who has only a generic understanding of the framework could still parse it. Paraphrase rather than quote anything, and don't name specific papers.
2. A model answer, roughly 700 words, that actually commits to a position instead of surveying.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 points: four or five must-hit moves with points attached, a concrete statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer, and for each must-hit move one trap students reliably fall into — e.g. invoking the is-ought gap as if it's a knockdown reply when it isn't, or endorsing the perception horn without noticing it needs a story about which foundation reliably tracks a value that exists independently of our having the foundation.
