I work on preference inference, and I've been trying to pin down where my disagreement with a labmate actually lives. Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can:

For a typical adult human H, there exists a stable preference structure P over outcomes (a ranking, or utilities, or something in that family — I don't want to legislate the format) such that (i) H's ordinary behavior is well-modeled, at least across the everyday regions of H's life, as approximately optimizing P relative to H's beliefs, and (ii) after careful reflection, H would endorse P as a better summary of what H cares about than H's own current explicit self-report does.

My credence in this is about 0.35. My labmate's is about 0.75. I want your number, but I care much more about the decomposition, because I suspect the two of us aren't really disagreeing about the same term.

Please decompose the proposition into the sub-questions you think it rides on. To make sure we're working the same ground, here is my own tentative decomposition — you should repair or extend it, not merely endorse it:

(a) Existence: whether there is any stable, reasonably unified structure to H's practical life at all, as opposed to preferences that are substantially constructed on the fly, indexed to context, or genuinely inconsistent across domains.

(b) Behavioral fit: whether H's behavior is approximately optimizing anything. I'm not worried about ordinary noise, but about systematic deviations — hyperbolic discounting, status quo bias, habit, procrastination. The question is whether these are noise around the optimization or structure that any optimizing model must either absorb into P (thereby miscounting a bias as a preference — the person who stays in a bad job getting credited with a deep preference for job security) or leave as unexplained residual.

(c) Identifiability: whether behavior, even if it is approximately optimizing something, determines P rather than a large equivalence class of candidate preference structures. I have in mind the fact that in the standard inverse-reinforcement-learning setup, an agent's choices in an environment only identify a preference structure relative to assumptions about the environment the agent takes itself to be in, the discount rate, how the agent trades off risk, and so on — and two quite different P's can be made to rationalize the same choices by adjusting these. Humans also sample a vanishingly small fraction of their state space, so the data is thin in a way I suspect is structural rather than incidental.

(d) Prior repair: whether background priors over preference structures — simple preferences, discounting assumptions, the interpretive charity we already apply to each other — can break the underdetermination in the right direction often enough.

(e) Endorsement: whether condition (ii) is even coherent — whether H's reflective preferences and whatever best explains H's behavior could come apart so far that "endorsement" no longer measures success, and whether the proposition should then be counted false or merely mis-stated.

Assign a rough probability to each term, with one line of justification each, then combine them (tell me what combination you're using and whether you treat the terms as independent — I'd expect (b) and (c) to be entangled). Then:
