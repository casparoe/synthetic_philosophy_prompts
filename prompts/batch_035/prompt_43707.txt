Quick sanity check from someone deep in a local democracy project. We're designing a pilot for our city: a randomly selected citizens' assembly (sortition) that deliberates on a policy question, but with a twist — before and after each deliberation round, participants and anyone in the public can trade on a prediction market about measurable outcomes of the policy (traffic counts, energy use, that sort of thing). We also want to use LLMs to cluster and summarize the written public feedback between rounds instead of a staff team doing it by hand. People keep telling me this design is "Deweyan" — experimentalist, democracy as a mode of inquiry, publics forming around problems, etc. I half believe it and half suspect it's branding.

So: imagine John Dewey somehow reviews our design docs. What would he actually push back on, and what would he grudgingly like? Specifically curious about three things. First, does the prediction market count as inquiry for him at all, given that the people betting aren't the ones deliberating — is the aggregated market price "intelligence" in his sense, or just a snapshot of a public that hasn't formed yet? Second, the LLM summarization step — he cared a lot about how publics get constituted through communication, and I can't decide whether routing that through a model changes anything for him or whether he'd shrug and treat it as just another printing press. And third, sortition itself: small deliberating bodies versus his worries about experts and elites, and whether random selection creates a public in his sense or just a sample of one. Keep in mind he died in 1952, so none of this tech existed for him — he saw mass media and polling but not betting markets or machine summarization.

If you think the whole framing is off — say, that asking what Dewey "would" say is parlor-game territory and I should just ask what a Deweyan today should say — please call that out instead of playing along politely. Same if the pilot itself rests on a confused premise and you can see it. And flag wherever your Dewey gets shaky or interpretive; I don't want confident invented quotes, and it's fine to say which parts are extrapolation. Answer in plain continuous prose please, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — just write it like an email to a colleague. Somethign I can read over coffee.
