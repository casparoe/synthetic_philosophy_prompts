I write novels — ten books in, no philosophy training beyond half a book I abandoned — and I keep smacking into the Ship of Theseus in my actual working life without vocabulary for it. My vocabulary is drafts, revisions, retcons, canon: a manuscript that's been through so many rounds of edits that not one sentence of the first draft survives; a series where the character in book eight barely resembles the one in book one but readers still say "that's her"; the fight over which version is "the real one" when the publisher puts out a restored edition fifty years later. Translate the actual philosophical debate about the ship into that vocabulary as faithully as you can: the gradual-replacement puzzle, the views that say sameness is just a convention we impose, the ones that insist there's a fact of the matter, the strange case where the old planks get reassembled into a second ship and you have two candidates. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto how fiction handles versioning — where the estate or the author just gets to stipulate which one counts — which parts you had to fudge, and whether anything in the philosophers' debate has no counterpart in my world at all. Mostly I want to know whether the residue suggests the metaphysical question was partly verbal all along, or whether fiction is just an odd case where stipulation settles things and reality, annoyingly, isn't.
