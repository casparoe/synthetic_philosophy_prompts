As a retired physicist who has only lately taken up philosophy of language, I have been working through Donald Davidson's papers on radical interpretation, and I find myself stuck on what looks like a decisive result that the field seems reluctant to celebrate. My reading is this: the principle of charity entails that an interpreter cannot rationally take a speaker's beliefs to be mostly false, since a speaker who was mostly wrong about the world could not be interpreted at all. This strikes me as a kind of conservation law for truth — no reassignment of meanings can make error grow without bound, just as no physical process can create energy. If most of any speaker's beliefs must be true as a precondition of interpretation, then it would appear that massive error, and with it Cartesian skepticism about the external world, has been shown to be impossible in principle. Yet contemporary philosophers continue to treat skeptical scenarios such as brains in vats as serious options rather than as refuted artifacts of a pre-Davidsonian epistemology. Is this lingering skepticism merely sociological inertia, or is there a genuine flaw somewhere in Davidson's argument — and if so, precisely where does the physics-style analogy break down?
