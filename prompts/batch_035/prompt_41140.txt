My discussion group has spent a semester on infinity, and I've noticed that I give verdicts on the cases that don't hang together, so I'm trying to find out what I actually believe. Here is my record so far. Hilbert's hotel, fully occupied, making room for one more guest by shifting everyone down: completely fine, no paradox, it just shows that "full" for an infinite hotel doesn't mean what it means for a finite one. The naturals and the evens being the same size, one-to-one: fine, I accept the definition of "same size" and I accept the verdict. Cantor's diagonal showing the reals outnumber the naturals, and then the power set of the reals outnumbering the reals, an endless tower of bigger infinities: fine, even beautiful. But Thomson's lamp, toggled once at each of infinitely many steps compressed before noon — I can't accept that; either answer at noon (on, off) seems arbitrary, so I say the scenario is impossible, not merely unknown. The Ross–Littlewood urn, where at each step you add ten balls and remove one, and after infinitely many steps it turns out the urn is empty if the balls are numbered, in a way that seems to depend on which ball you remove when — that offends me even more, and I half-suspect the case is incoherent rather than merely strange. And Banach–Tarski, one ball cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls the same size as the original: I say this is a theorem about non-measurable sets, fine, but that it tells us nothing about what physical balls could do.

My tentative principle was: infinities are fine as mathematical objects and incoherent as physical realities. Set theory can keep its cardinals; nothing actual could be infinite. But that principle immediately overgenerates, and I can see it failing on cases where my verdicts are the opposite. A line segment, a real physical stretch of a ruler, contains infinitely many points, and I'm fine with that. A universe extending without bound, containing infinitely many stars, seems possible to me — I wouldn't say cosmology has proven it, but I wouldn't rule it out a priori the way I rule out Thomson's lamp. Worse, Benardete's paradox, where each of infinitely many gods holds a barrier at some point ahead of a traveler and each vow to activate it if the traveler reaches them, so the traveler can never pass any god and never reaches any god either — nothing performs infinitely many actions there, each god just sits with its barrier, yet something impossible seems to happen, and I don't know whether my principle even applies. And the case I keep going back and forth on, like a loose tooth: an infinite past. If the universe had no beginning, then the present is the "end state" of infinitely many completed earlier events, which sounds exactly like the lamp at noon; but saying an infinite past is impossible feels like doing cosmology from an armchair, and it collides with my comfort with infinitely many stars spread out in space. Somehow infinity spread out over space seems fine while infinity compressed into a finite stretch of time or a finite container seems rotten, and I can't tell whether that's a principled distinction or just a failure of my imagination.

What I'd like from you is a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to have consequences: what exactly distinguishes the cases I accept (hotel, evens, tower of cardinals, maybe the infinite stars) from the cases I reject (lamp, urn, and whatever Benardete's paradox turns out to be)? For each clause of the principle, tell me which of my verdicts motivates it, and then apply it to the cases I haven't settled — the infinite past, Benardete's gods, and one or two you think would stress-test it further. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save, and whether you think the right move is to overturn the verdict or complicate the principle. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which of my intuitive verdicts, if I dug in on it, would most pressure your preferred account, and where in the reasoning do you think it's most likely that you (or I) are being seduced by a picture rather than an argument.
