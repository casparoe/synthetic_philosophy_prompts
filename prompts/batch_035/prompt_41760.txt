Act as an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory, and think carefully before you write — start with a bit of private brainstorming about the possible diagnoses, then commit to one.

So here's my situation: I teach decision theory, and for three years running my seminar has run aground on the same shoal — the fight between orthodox expected utility theory and Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility. The students can all reproduce the machinery by week six: REU with a risk function that weights the worse outcomes more heavily, the claim that it can be rational to prefer a sure thing in a way EU theory forbids, the Allais preferences treated as respectable rather than as a bug to be explained away. And yet nobody budges an inch. The EU partisans keep insisting that if you're dynamically consistent and won't pay to avoid flipping a coin between two gambles, you're already an EU maximiser in disguise. The REU camp replies that treating a 50/50 mix of two gambles as exactly their average is itself a substantive assumption, not a deliverance of rationality, and that the whole "you'll take each gamble separately so you must take the mix" argument begs the question against them.

My working hypothesis is that the camps aren't actually disagreeing about one thing. The EU side is answering something like "what would the preferences of an ideal agent look like?" and the REU side is answering "which preferences are beyond criticism from the inside?" — a permissivist question about instrumental rationality — and the mixing argument is downstream of that mismatch. But I genuinely don't know, so I want a diagnosis, not a verdict for one team. Is this a substantive normative disagreement, a verbal one, two questions travelling under one name, or a hidden fight about how to individuate outcomes (if the risk-averse agent "really" values certainty as part of the outcome, the disagreement starts to look trivial)? Use what each side actually says to back the diagnosis, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a theorem, an empirical result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or whether we should just expect the impasse to be permanent, and why.

One more thing: be scrupulous about seperating what is actually settled in this debate from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic. My students are bright but they'll believe anything said confidently, so don't hand them a contested reading dressed up as consensus.
