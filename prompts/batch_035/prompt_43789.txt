I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and no recurring debate there has confused me more than the one about uploads. Someone proposes whole brain emulation: scan a brain, simulate it, and since the brain is "just wetware implementing computations," the simulation thinks. Then a skeptic replies that this proves far too much: with enough ingenuity you can describe the causal dynamics of a wall — or a bucket of water, or the weather — so that its states map onto the formal structure of any program you like, and the mapping is as faithful as the one between the brain and its simulation. So if implementing the right computation suffices for mind, the wall thinks everything, and the claim that the brain is a computer stops discriminating between anything. The defenders protest that this is too quick, and the thread dies with no one spelling out what the actual rejoinder is.

What I want to know is whether this argument pattern — roughly, "a claim of the form X implements Y is made non-trivial only by some restriction on which descriptions count as implementations, and absent an account of that restriction, the claim collapses into triviality" — has an established name and literature. It resembles the underdetermination of theory by evidence, but I don't think it's that: the issue isn't that the data fail to decide between theories, but that the interpretation relation seems unconstrained on one end, so that the same physical system can be read as computing anything whatever if we're free to gerrymander the description. I've since seen the same shape well outside the mind debates: someone says a market "implements" an optimization process, or that evolution "computes" fitness maxima, and a critic replies that by that standard every dynamical system computes something.

If there is a recognized term, tell me what it is, who developed it, and how it bears on the upload case — in particular whether anyone has said precisely what distinguishes the brain's causal organization from a wall's such that only one counts as computing, and whether the standard responses actually succeed or merely push the problem back a level. If there is no established name, coin one, define it so that it is carved off from underdetermination, from conventionalism about mere words, and from an ordinary appeal to simplicity, and then give the boundary cases: when is a restriction on acceptable interpretations a substantive discovery about the physical system, and when is it just an ad hoc fence built to protect a thesis? Please close your reply with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing only the term you settle on — either the established name or your coinage — with nothing else on that line.
