I have no training in philosophy; I've just been reading about Buddhism on my own, and I've worked myself into an argument about the doctrine of anatta — the teaching that there is no enduring self — that I can't find my way out of. The trouble is that it seems to prove far too much: not only that the Buddhist path is incoherent, but that no one should ever save for retirement, and I can't accept that either.

The argument, as I've written it out:

(1) There is no enduring self: what exists is a stream of passing experiences — perceptions, feelings, thoughts, intentions — and none of them, nor the collection of them, is a "me" that persists and owns the rest.
(2) If that's right, then the one who makes a sacrifice and the one who later benefits from it are never the same person: the bundle that made the sacrifice has dissolved before the benefit arrives.
(3) It is irrational to sacrifice now for the benefit of someone who is not you — you don't put money in a bank account for a stranger, and we'd call someone foolish who did.
(4) So under no-self, no one has any reason to sacrifice for their own future, let alone for a future life: the being who enjoys the fruit of good karma is not the one who earned it, and the one who attains liberation is not the one who did the practicing.
(5) Yet the whole path is presented as advice to someone: practice now, and suffering will diminish and eventually cease.
(6) If (4) holds, that advice is addressed to no one, and karma is a system of rewards that always lands on strangers.

What convinces me something has slipped: if the argument works, it equally proves that saving for retirement is irrational — the retiree who spends my savings is just a later bundle, no more "me" than my neighbor is — and that no promise binds anyone, since the one who keeps the promise isn't the one who made it. Ordinary Buddhists save money and keep promises, and the Buddha's own path, on the traditional story, was planned across many lifetimes. Either all of that is irrational, or one of my steps is false. If I had to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and let something later go — I find no-self persuasive on its own terms — but I'd like to know which step you'd reject, why that step looked so innocent to me, and what rejecting it costs. And one more thing I want understood: if your fix works, does it fix the retirement and promise cases by the same move, or does it rescue only the Buddhist ones? If the two come apart, I need to know where.
