At the last meeting of my building's residents' committee, two members — a retired insurance adjuster named Anne and a Ben who teaches high school physics — spent forty minutes arguing about whether our committee caused a recent loss, and I was asked to summarize the disagreement for an outside advisor. The facts, as we've been able to establish them: our charter requires an annual inspection of the trees in the shared courtyard; no inspection was scheduled this year (the officer responsible says he forgot, though he disputes what follows); a storm in October toppled one of those trees onto a resident's car; and an arborist we later hired says the rot that made the tree dangerous was visible enough that the annual inspection would almost certainly have caught it, and the tree would have been removed.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Anne said the committee's failure caused the damage, full stop. If the inspection had happened, the tree would have come down in a controlled fashion in August, and the car would be intact. The damage counterfactually depends on our omission, and that's what causation is; she spent her career writing liability determinations and said insurers settle claims like this every week.

(2) Ben said that reasoning proves too much. If absences can be causes, then the causes of the damage are unlimited: every arborist in the city who didn't volunteer to inspect our trees also caused it, the rot in a neighboring tree that didn't spread to ours is a cause, the wind that didn't blow slightly harder last year and felled the tree then is a cause. Real causes are concrete events — the storm, the rot — and calling our failure a cause is a shorthand for blame, not a causal claim at all. He added that the omission didn't do anything; it didn't produce the damage, it merely failed to prevent it, and failing to prevent is a different relation from causing — the storm caused, we merely didn't stop it.

(3) Anne answered that the difference between our omission and the thousand irrelevant ones is duty: the charter assigned us the inspection, which is exactly why our failure counts and a stranger's non-involvement doesn't. Nobody sues the arborist across town, and not because of some metaphysical nicety — because he had no obligation.

(4) Ben said that concedes his point rather than answering it. Duty is about blame, not causation; Anne is smuggling normativity into what should be a factual question. Whether the officer's forgetfulness caused the damage shouldn't depend on whether there's a charter paragraph making it blameworthy — the physical facts are what they are, and either the storm and rot did the work or they didn't. If a passing tree surgeon with no obligation to us had offered a free inspection and we'd declined, Ben suspected Anne would want to call that a cause too, despite the absence of duty — or else she'd have to explain why blameworthy omissions cause and unblameworthy ones don't, which seems to make the causal facts depend on our moral bookkeeping.

(5) Anne's closing move was to say that even if duty is the wrong place to draw the line, some line is needed and it can be drawn descriptively rather than normatively. What distinguishes our missed inspection from all the non-inspections that didn't matter is that this inspection happens every year as part of the building's normal routine; it was the expected, standing next step, and the world where it occurred is the natural comparison case, not the world where some stranger intervenes. She admitted she wasn't sure whether that made her point about causation or only about how people talk about causation, but said that if the committee can't say its own failure caused the loss, it can't explain why it should compensate the car's owner — which it clearly should.

I hold the casting vote on the compensation question, and I found myself unable to give it, because I couldn't tell whether the dispute was verbal, factual, or moral, and the answer to that seems to determine what the vote is even about. I'd be grateful if you could adjudicate the exchange move by move — saying for each of the five steps whether the speaker is right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why — rather than just declaring a winner. I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing; I have a suspicion there is something, since their argument kept circling a distinction neither could quite name. Finally, please say how confident you are in each of your judgments, and single out the point in their disagreement you are least sure about how to settle.
