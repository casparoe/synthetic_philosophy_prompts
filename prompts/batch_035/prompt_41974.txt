I'm writing a seminar paper on Augustine on lying (we're reading both On Lying and the later Against Lying, the one he wrote to Consentius about the Catholics who wanted to pose as Priscillianists so they could infiltrate that crowd), and I've argued myself into a corner. Everyone knows the headline view: you may never lie, not even to save somebody's life. Fine. But when I try to say *why*, I keep landing on reasons that are about the person deceived — the hearer comes away believing something false, trust between minds gets corrupted, language exists to hand on what's in your mind and a lie turns that channel into a weapon aimed at the hearer. Out of that reading I built an argument that now looks like it proves way too much:

(1) The reason lying is always wrong, on Augustine's account, is what happens on the recieving end: the hearer ends up with a false belief, produced through your speech, that the whole purpose of language was supposed to rule out. (2) If a false belief in the hearer, produced within your speech situation, is the wrong, then it can't matter whether the belief got there by an assertion against your mind or was merely left standing by your silence when you could easily have corrected it — the hearer's picture of the world is corrupted the same way either way, and in both cases you were the one positioned to prevent it by speaking. (3) So knowingly letting a false belief stand when you could correct it is wrong in the same way, and to the same degree, as lying to put it there in the first place. (4) Therefore, if lying is never permissible, neither is letting a false belief stand when you could correct it. (5) But that obligates me to correct everyone: the stranger at the bus stop with the wrong date, the coworker's superstition, every confident mistake at a dinner party — a life of nonstop correction that neither Augustine nor anyone else ever preached. (6) And Augustine himself won't bite: for him a lie is having one thing in your mind and producing another in words or signs, so it's judged from the speaker's mind, not from whether the hearer ends up deceived.

So by his own lights the argument can't be sound, and yet every premise still looks like something he'd accept, or at least something his rhetoric suggests. My instinct says the slip is somewhere between (2) and (3) but I honestly can't name it, and I'd rather keep (2) than not, since it's what makes his view feel like more than a definitional trick. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine to me in the first place, and what rejecting it costs him — what does he gain and lose by locating the wrong where he locates it? Under 450 words, and put the step you'd reject in your first sentence.
