Two friends of mine have argued for over a year and have finally agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I've been asked to help them scope the joint statement. One is a longtermist of the strongly expectational sort: he holds that reducing existential risk dominates other moral priorities because the expected value of humanity's potential future is astronomical, and that it can be rational to pay large certain costs for very small probabilities of very large payoffs — he accepts this even where it strikes others as fanaticism. The other takes the simulation argument seriously in its disjunctive form: given the trilemma, she thinks we are probably in an ancestor simulation, and she argues that this should drastically dampen her friend's fanaticism about the far future. Her reasons, briefly: if we are simulated, the future we might shape is one run among perhaps billions of near-duplicate runs whose continuation depends on simulator purposes we cannot model; interventions that look epoch-making from inside may be terminated, edited, or rerun at will; and stacking astronomical value onto speculative simulated futures is structurally closer to Pascal's mugging than to expected-value reasoning done responsibly. He has lately been developing a counter she takes seriously: if simulators run our history many times over, a beneficial action may be instantiated across all those duplicates, so simulation uncertainty could amplify rather than dampen the expected value of shaping the run.

Draft the joint statement they should both be able to sign. Include: the claims both accept — about the trilemma's structure, about what a simulated agent can reasonably treat as fixed, and about how expected value should handle multiplied, terminated, or rerun futures — wherever they genuinely converge; their remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; and the cruxes, with for each one the argument, model, or case both would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. Flag any part of the dispute that is merely verbal. Use a numbered section of agreed claims, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes. If you think the setup itself is off — say, that simulation uncertainty and the propriety of fanatic-style expected-value reasoning are orthogonal, so that neither party should concede much from the other's premise — say so plainly at the top rather than manufacturing a consensus.
