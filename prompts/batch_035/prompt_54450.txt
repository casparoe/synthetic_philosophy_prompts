I've been rereading Orwell's "Politics and the English Language", and I keep noticing a phenomenon his catalogue of vices doesn't quite capture. Certain phrases in political speech — "the right side of history", "common-sense reform", "not who we are" — carry no propositional content at all: you cannot extract from them a claim that could be true or false, and nobody using them expects you to. Yet they are not vague or slovenly, which is what Orwell was mainly attacking; they are perfectly precise about their actual job, which is to display which side the speaker is on. The tell is what happens when you ask what such a phrase asserts: people react as though you had impugned the allegiance it signals rather than asked a question about meaning. Rival movements can even use the same phrase of opposite policies, and it still works. Is there an established term for vocabulary whose function is allegiance-display rather than assertion? "Dogwhistle" doesn't fit, since there is no concealed message aimed at a subset of listeners — the display is the whole of it. "Phatic" comes closest, but it is normally reserved for greetings and small talk, not loaded political vocabulary. And Orwell's own thesis — that "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts" — assumes language is failing at its proper job of expressing thought, whereas these phrases seem to be succeeding at a different job entirely. Does the phenomenon have a name of its own? If not, I'd like you to coin one and mark its boundaries against euphemism, shibboleth, and plain vagueness; a bare label won't satisfy me.
