I'm a philosophy PhD student (decision theory, mostly), and I've TA'd our intro critical thinking course enough times that I can recite the sunk cost fallacy in my sleep: rational choice looks forward only, the money and time already spent are gone, no decision you make now can change them, so they count for nothing. Concorde is the poster child. And yet when I actually go through my gut verdicts on cases, they don't line up with the doctrine I've been grading students on, and I'd like to know if there's a principle that captures them or whether some of them just have to go.

Here are the verdicts. Reading the last 80 pages of a novel I've soured on, because I'm 300 pages in: fine, maybe even rational — the ending is worth something to me partly *because* of the 300 pages. Finishing everything on my plate because I paid for the meal: not fine, push the plate away. Staying in a floundering relationship because three years are already invested: not fine, textbook fallacy. Continuing a war so the dead won't have died in vain: monstrous reasoning, the paradigm case. But staying loyal to my football team through three decades of losing — honoring all those wasted Sunday afternoons — seems not just excusable but somehow admirable, which is bizarre, since the "so it wasn't all for nothing" structure is the same as the war case, just with lower stakes. And finishing a jigsaw puzzle that's 95% done, even though the remaining pieces are fiddly and actively unpleasant: fine, though structurally it looks just like the plate case. I have a tentative principle of my own: sunk costs are irrational to honor *except* when the past investment itself changes the future payoff — the last chapter only has value conditional on having read the earlier ones, so the investment isn't fully sunk. Call it a completion-value principle, and note it's weirder than it sounds, because it makes some payoffs backward-dependent in a way textbook decision theory has no slot for.

Test that principle against my cases and repair it if you can: does it explain why the war case is worse than the relationship case, or does it wrongly license them both? What does it say about the team loyalty case, where there's no completion event at all — the "payoff" is just more of the same losing? And then the case I actually can't stop chewing on, since it's my own life: should I spend a seventh year finishing a dissertation whose topic no longer excites me, given that the degree only means anything to the me who's already sunk six years into it — is that completion value or just the fallacy wearing a tweed jacket? I'd like candidate principles stated precisely, each clause tied to the case it earns, what it predicts for the dissertation, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. End with a single line in the form \answer{...} naming the principle you'd endorse in a few words and the one verdict of mine it overturns (or \answer{none overturned} if you save them all).
