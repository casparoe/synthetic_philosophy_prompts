I am a retired experimental neuroscientist who, after a long career in the laboratory, has taken up the study of philosophy rather late in life. This term I have agreed to serve as a second grader for a third-year undergraduate course on the philosophy of mind at my local university. The instructor, aware of my background, has asked me to mark the essays on the Libet experiments and free will. I have no difficulty judging the experimental claims, but I am still finding my footing with respect to the standards expected of philosophical argument, and I would value a careful second opinion before I commit marks to paper.

Below is the essay question exactly as it was set, followed by the full text of one student's answer. Please grade it for me. I would like (i) a letter grade on the department's scale, from A through F, with pluses and minuses; and (ii) a set of specific end-of-paper comments and suggested marginal notes, organized point by point, that would justify the grade to both the student and the instructor. In particular, please tell me which claims are correct and well supported, which are subtly mistaken in ways a charitable grader might overlook, and which mistakes are mistakes of empirical fact as opposed to mistakes of inference. If parts of the answer are strong enough to deserve credit despite the errors elsewhere, please say so explicitly.

The essay question:

"In the early 1980s Benjamin Libet and his colleagues reported experiments suggesting that the brain begins preparing a so-called spontaneous voluntary movement before the agent is aware of any intention to move. Explain the design and the principal findings of these experiments, and assess how far they threaten the claim that conscious intentions are causally efficacious. You may confine your attention to the original experiments and need not discuss later neuroimaging work."

The student's answer, in full:

"Libet's subjects were wired for electroencephalography and asked to flex a wrist or finger whenever they felt the urge, while a spot of light revolved on an oscilloscope screen like the hand of a clock. Back-averaging the EEG time-locked to the movement revealed the readiness potential, a slow negative shift over motor cortex that begins roughly half a second before a self-paced movement. Subjects were asked to note, afterwards, the position of the spot at the moment they first became aware of the urge to move; this W time fell on average about 200 ms before the movement itself. The readiness potential was therefore already under way some 350 ms before any awareness of intending. Libet concluded that the initiation of a voluntary act is unconscious: the brain starts the movement, and the feeling of willing arrives only afterwards.

Since the clock method required subjects only to report the position of a spot on a dial, the W measurements can be treated as accurate to within a few tens of milliseconds, and so the timing comparison stands on firm ground.

The original design was later extended with functional magnetic resonance imaging in Libet's own laboratory, where activity in prefrontal and parietal cortex was found to predict which of two buttons a subject would press up to seven seconds before the subject reported becoming aware of any decision. The conclusion is hard to resist: the brain has decided long before the subject has, and the conscious decision is at best a readout.

Libet himself accepted this conclusion and gave up his belief in free will. If a neural process sufficient to produce the movement is complete before the conscious intention appears, then the conscious intention cannot be a cause of the movement; a cause cannot arrive too late to the scene.

There are, to be sure, limits. The subjects had already agreed in advance to move at some point; the only open question was when. So the experiments concern the timing of a decision already made, not deliberation between genuinely open options, a point critics of Libet were quick to press. Still, within the domain they cover, the experiments show that conscious willing is epiphenomenal, and the burden of proof now lies with anyone who claims otherwise."

A few constraints on your reply. Please base the grade on what is actually on the page rather than on what a fuller answer might have said. Distinguish carefully between what the student has merely asserted and what the student has shown. Finally, since the instructor and I will compare marks, tell me where you think a scientifically informed grader and a philosophically trained grader might disagree about this answer, and which disagreements should affect the grade.
