Okay so here's my situation. I'm in a little type design meetup and two of the regulars have been going at it for like two years now, and I've finally convinced them to do something like an adversarial collaboration and write up a joint document. I want to commission a draft of that document.

The two positions, roughly:

Position A (call her the stroke theorist, she's deep into the Hague school / Noordzij tradition): what makes every 'a' an 'a' is that it's a stylized trace of a writing motion. The double-story 'a' is what it is because of a sequence of strokes, a ductus, that broad-nib and pointed pens produced over centuries of handwriting, and printed letters inherit their identity from that gestural history. She likes Noordzij's line that typography is essentially writing with prefabricated letters. On her view, a shape that no plausible human gesture could have produced isn't really an 'a', it's an 'a'-shaped something.

Position B (the systems guy, basically a structuralist about letterforms): the essence isn't in any letter taken alone, it's in the grid of contrasts. An 'a' is whatever plays the 'a'-role in a functioning system of distinctions -- it has to stay distinguishable from o, e, d, s and every other letter across weights, sizes, and styles, and the particular features that carry that contrast are just the local implementation. He thinks the stroke theorist is doing paleontology, not theory: handwriting history explains how we got here, it doesn't tell you what the identity consists in.

I want the document both of them could actually sign. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims -- and I'd want it to include the phenomenon they both have to account for: cross-font recognition, we read a blackletter 'a' and a geometric sans 'a' and a child's pencil 'a' as the same letter without noticing anything. Maybe also whatever they'd both concede about Hofstadter's old LetterSpirit idea, that a lowercase letter's identity in a typeface is partly fixed by the whole alphabet around it (each letter's sibling shapes), since that seems to pull against both of them in different ways.

2. The disagreements, in a table, each phrased so that the person holding that view would accept the wording as fair to their own side.

3. The cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence both of them would agree bears on it, with each person's prediction written down in advance. Some cases I'd like to see them forced to confront (but add your own if you have better ones): a geometrically constructed 'a' in a typeface deliberately designed so no continuous pen stroke could produce it; the single-story 'a' vs the double-story 'a' -- same letter or a coincidence of names; whether the enclosed counter (the little white bubble) is essential or just one historical implementation; scripts like Cherokee or constructed scripts with no real handwriting tradition, where the gesture story seems to have no purchase at all; and the psych result that people recognize letters faster in real words than in isolation -- what, if anything, that says about where letter identity lives.

If any part of their fight turns out to be verbal -- e.g. they're using "essence" for two different questions, like what makes a token recognizable vs what makes the category what it is -- say which part and why, because honestly I suspect some of it is.

Format as: agreed claims (numbered), then the disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions. Keep the wording neutral enough that both could sign, but dont sand it down to nothing -- a joint statement where neither side actually concedes anything is worthless to me.
