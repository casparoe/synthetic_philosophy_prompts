Macroeconomic policy institutions — central banks, the IMF's model shops — run DSGE models to forecast inflation and output and to inform interest-rate decisions. Nearly everyone involved concedes that the models' signature assumptions are false: a single representative household with rational expectations stands in for millions of heterogeneous, partly confused agents. The standard defense is Friedman-style instrumentalism: unrealistic assumptions are acceptable so long as the predictions are accurate enough. Yet the same institutions responded to the 2008 crisis not by discarding badly predicting models and keeping the well-predicting ones, but by inserting "financial frictions" to make the models more realistic — treating the falsity of the assumptions itself as the defect to be repaired. What view of the role of assumptions in models does this practice presuppose? Is it a coherent middle position between the F-twist, on which only predictive success matters, and a realist demand that assumptions be true — perhaps the thought that idealizations are permissible only when their falsity is harmless, too small to propagate into the outputs the decision depends on? Reconstruct the implicit methodology, say whether the practice's two halves (defending falsity in general, while repairing particular falsities) presuppose consistent things, and indicate where the practice is underdetermined, such that several quite different philosophies of idealization could license it. Finally: which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — a strict realist demand on the one hand, and on the other a pure curve-fitting pragmatism on which theoretical assumptions play no justificatory role at all — and what would central-bank practice have to look like if the F-twist were adopted in full?
