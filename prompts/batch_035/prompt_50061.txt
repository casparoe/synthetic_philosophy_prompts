I'm a retired microbiologist who finally has time for the philosophy I dodged as a student, and I've joined a little reading group working through Indian epistemology — we're on the pramāṇa literature, mostly Nyāya with digressions. One member, Priya, has become our resident judge: whenever we cook up a case she hands down a firm verdict, and she's been consistant enough that we've started trying to reverse-engineer her rule. Her rulings so far: a ripe mango on the table in good light — she knows it's there; smoke rising from the far hill though no flame is visible — she knows there's fire on the hill; her grandmother, who knows the wells, tells her the water is bad this monsoon and she never tests it herself — she says she *knows* it's bad; a farmer she trusts describes the gavaya as a wild ox like a stocky cow, and months later she spots one at the market and says that's the gavaya — knowledge, she insists, not a lucky guess; a vivid dream that the bus would be late, which it was — not knowledge, because dreams aren't a source of anything; and one more I bring up becuse it seems to matter: from her window she clearly sees no smoke and concludes there's no fire on the hill — she refused that one, saying smokeless fire is perfectly possible, so that conclusion wasn't knowledge.

Next week's agenda is absences and odd inferences, and we're taking bets on her verdicts. The cases: (1) she hunts for her keys on the fully lit, clutter-free desk and, not seeing them, immediatly concludes they're not on the desk; (2) Devadatta in the flat downstairs is famously fat, but nobody ever sees him eat during the day, and she concludes he eats at night; (3) she spends a whole morning walking a large forest where tigers were reported and, never spotting one, concludes there are no tigers in it today; (4) the kitchen smoke alarm, which is well maintained, stays silent while she cooks, and she concludes there's no smoke in the kitchen. Will she count each of these as knowledge, and via what source, in her way of talking — perception, inference, trustworthy word, or some shelf she'll have to invent for it? Our professor keeps saying she sounds like the Mīmāṃsā camp, who apparently wanted two extra pramāṇas on top of Nyāya's four, but Priya just smiles.

Honestly, the four yes/no's matter less to me than the underlying rule: which rule is she running, which rival rules would fit her track record just as well, and which case — one of these four or a fifth you cook up yourself — would best pull those rivals apart? Please open with your bottom-line predictions in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
