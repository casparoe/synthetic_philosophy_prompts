I'm writing a term paper for an early modern philosophy course in which we've spent several weeks on Leibniz, and I want to attempt something speculative that my instructor has warned will only work if it's carefully grounded in his actual commitments. The assignment is to predict how Leibniz would react to large language models: systems that can be prompted into answering questions about moral dilemmas, that produce elaborate chains of reasoning on demand, and whose internal workings — weights, activations, attention over tokens — are in principle fully inspectable by their creators. I've anchored my prediction in three threads from his work, and I'd like your help thinking through what his most likely position would be on each.

First, his lifelong project of a universal characteristic and a calculus of reasoning. Leibniz dreamed of an alphabet of human thoughts and a formalism exact enough that disputes, including moral and legal ones, could be settled by reducing them to computation, so that instead of arguing, the disputants could sit down together and calculate. Would he see LLMs as a crude, deformed step toward that dream — machines that do settle verbal disputes by mechanical operations — or as a betrayal of it, since their reasoning emerges from statistical association over vast text corpora rather than from an exact mapping of concepts to symbols? Second, his mill argument against explaining thought mechanically: he asks us to imagine blowing up a thinking machine to the size of a mill and walking around inside it, where we would find nothing but parts pushing parts, and never anything from which perception or consciousness could be read off. An LLM seems like a vivid modern test case, since we can literally walk through its internals. Would he conclude that LLMs, being in the end just tokens pushing tokens, lack the apperception and reason that genuine moral judgment requires — or would his insistence that every true unity in nature is a soul-like monad that mirrors the whole universe from its point of view pull him toward granting them some degree of perception? And if a machine genuinely possessed wisdom — if wisdom is, as he defined justice, the charity of the wise applied with knowledge of what is good — could it be just?

Third, and most delicately, I want to connect this to his optimism. Leibniz held that God, being omnipotent and benevolent, had to choose the best of all possible worlds, so that every evil is permitted only because it figures in a greater overall good. Some of my classmates think this implies he would be complacent about the possibility that AI development goes catastrophically wrong — after all, even an AI-caused catastrophe would have to serve the best world's overall harmony. But it strikes me that Leibniz also believed God works through natural means, that our own efforts and precautions are part of the chosen plan, and that our duty is to act so as to diminish evil, not to sit back and admire its necessity. For each of the three threads, please reason from his stated positions rather than giving me a generic verdict, indicate where his commitments would pull in different directions, and take a definite stance on what he would most plausibly have said — I'd rather have a committed, well-argued prediction than a hedge.
