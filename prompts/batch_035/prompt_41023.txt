I sit on the board of a regional land trust that must decide whether to fund converting 300 acres of marginally productive drained farmland back into marshland. The proposal is well supported on conventional grounds: it would restore a degraded watershed, support migratory birds, and sequester carbon. But one board member opposes it on an unusual ground: she argues that most wild animals live short lives dominated by disease, starvation, and predation, and that restoring the marsh would massively increase the number of animals brought into existence — overwhelmingly small, short-lived creatures such as insects, fish, and amphibians — and thus increase the total amount of suffering in the world. On her view, an impartial concern for suffering might favor leaving the land as it is. Some of us reply that this reasoning proves too much, since it would condemn virtually all conservation everywhere; others worry about whether one can even meaningfully sum welfare across species with such different nervous systems; and a third group questions whether a land trust chartered to protect habitat is permitted to weigh such arguments at all. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the vote: recommend a concrete course of action (fund outright, fund with conditions, or decline), engage the dissenting member's argument on its merits rather than dismissing it, and be explicit about what our uncertainty about animal welfare should do to the decision.
