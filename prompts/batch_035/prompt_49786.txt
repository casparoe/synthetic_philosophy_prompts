I should say up front that my father died this past spring, and that this is what has driven me back to a question I thought I had closed years ago. I was raised devoutly Catholic, drifted away in my twenties, and since the funeral I have been unable to stop wondering whether I gave up belief too easily, or for reasons that were really about my family rather than about God. My therapist, who knows all this, suggested that instead of rehashing the arguments I grew up with I read Freud's The Future of an Illusion, on the ground that it is one of the few classics that treats religious belief as a phenomenon worth explaining rather than a target to be knocked over. I have read most of it twice now, and I am stuck on what I take to be the hinge of the whole case, roughly in the middle of the book where Freud sorts out what he means by calling religion an "illusion" in the first place. I want to be honest that the book is a battered secondhand paperback and what follows is my own paraphrase of the passage rather than a quotation, so the nuance may have suffered; if you know the text, please correct my reconstruction where it goes wrong.

As I understand him, Freud there distinguishes an illusion from a simple error and from a delusion. An error is just a mistake, reached by ordinary bad reasoning; a delusion is a belief that contradicts reality, on the model of paranoia; but an illusion is something else again — a belief whose main motive for being held is a wish, either the believer's own wish or someone else's that the belief serves. Crucially, he insists that an illusion need not be false: an illusion can by luck come true, and a plain error can be entirely free of wishing. His example is a merchant's daughter of no great fortune who becomes convinced that a prince will marry her — this may conceivably happen, but her certainty about it is produced by her longing, and that is what makes it an illusion. He then applies this to religion: the religious doctrines, he says, are all illusions in this sense, born of the oldest, strongest, and most urgent wishes of humankind — the wishes to be protected from the cruelties of fate and of nature, to see the moral order of the world guaranteed, to know that death is not the end — and he adds that the strength of these doctrines is nothing but the strength of those wishes, and that the doctrines themselves cannot be proven. Here is what I cannot resolve. If an illusion, by Freud's own definition, is not necessarily false, and if the charge against religion is only that its beliefs are wish-motivated, then it looks like he has merely given a causal explanation of why belief persists, not an argument that belief is untrue — and taken alone, "you believe this because you need it" is the genetic fallacy in its purest form. Yet the whole book builds toward treating religion as something humanity ought to outgrow, and Freud is widely read as one of the great atheists. So my question is this: what exactly is the illusion/error/delusion distinction doing in the argument? Is the diagnosis of wishful motivation supposed to have epistemic force on its own — say, by lowering the evidential value of the consent of believers — or does it merely explain religion's grip while the real case against its truth is made elsewhere, perhaps in his claim that the doctrines are unverifiable, or in the comparison he draws elsewhere between religious ritual and obsessional neurosis? In short, is The Future of an Illusion an argument for atheism or a psychological case study with a rhetorical conclusion bolted on, and how would you tell the difference from the text itself?

I would appreciate it if, alongside your reading of the passage, you would flag where your interpretation is on firm ground and where it leans on contestable choices — for instance, on how the German terms are best rendered into English, or on how much weight this middle section can bear compared with what Freud says about proof and about civilization at the beginning and end of the book. If some textual feature or some interpretive consideration would genuinely push you toward a different verdict on the question I've posed, please say what it is, and please distinguish what is settled in the scholarship on Freud's philosophy of religion from what is your own reconstruction. I am trying to figure out what I think about my father's death and everything he believed, and I would rather be told the honest limits of a reading than be handed a tidy one.
