I recently stumbled onto the "discursive dilemma" from judgment aggregation theory and it smells familiar in a way I can't quite pin down, so I want a real translation, not an analogy-for-laypeople.

Here's the setup as I understand it, correct me if I've got it wrong: three judges vote on whether a defendant is liable. Liability follows from two premises (say, the contract was valid AND the defendant breached it). Judge 1 accepts both premises and votes liable; Judges 2 and 3 each accept only one premise and vote not liable. So a majority accepts each premise, but a majority votes not liable. Premise-based aggregation (apply majority voting to the premises, derive the conclusion) says liable; conclusion-based aggregation (vote directly on the conclusion) says not liable. Same voters, same votes, opposite verdicts. And apparently the general results are worse: any aggregation rule for a large enough agenda that always outputs a consistent, complete collective judgment and respects some fairness conditions collapses into an oligarchy — one voter's ballots effectively decide everything.
