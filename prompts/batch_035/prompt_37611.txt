I am a novelist, roughly three hundred pages into a long book in which an older character — a charismatic aunt, in effect the novel's moral center — gradually reshapes the values of a younger protagonist. My problem is that, scene by scene, I cannot tell whether I am depicting persuasion or manipulation, and the distinction matters. If the aunt's influence is manipulation, this is a darker book, and the narrator's warmth toward her becomes a kind of unreliability; if it is persuasion, the book is about inheritance and mentorship. Readers of my early chapters split exactly down the middle on this, which tells me the text is not controlling the distinction.

Please write a procedure I can run on any scene in which one character moves another's beliefs, desires, or decisions. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure can stop and declare the scene persuasion, manipulation, or genuinely ambiguous, and — importantly — where it must hand the final call back to me because what remains is a question of values rather than of craft. Ground the procedure in what moral philosophy actually says separates manipulation from legitimate influence: bypassing the target's reflective capacities rather than engaging them, working through characteristic vulnerabilities such as grief, fatigue, infatuation, or self-doubt rather than through reasons, exploiting information asymmetry, and framing or omitting so that the target's choice is made for her rather than by her. But translate all of that into tests I can apply to dialogue and stage business, since I have no training in philosophy, and keep it fast enough that I can run it on a scene in five or ten minutes during revision.

One wrinkle I want built in, because I suspect it decides more scenes than content does: timing. The aunt says the same true, loving things throughout, but she says some of them at a graveside and some over breakfast, some when the protagonist is exhausted and some when she is steady. My half-formed view is that the same words can be persuasion at one hour and manipulation three hours later, and that readers register this even when I do not. So the procedure should treat the target's temporary state — and the influencer's choice of moment — as a first-class question, not an afterthought.

Then run the procedure on three scenes from my draft. First: the aunt waits until the day after the protagonist's father's funeral, when the protagonist is drained and suggestible, to talk her out of keeping the family house, arguing only from things that are true. Second: the aunt talks a friend into taking a job he half wants by praising his talents and passing over the risks, again saying nothing false. Third: a scene I intended as the emotional center of the novel, in which the dying grandmother asks the protagonist to come home for a last Christmas, trading openly on the protagonist's guilt. Tell me what the procedure concludes about each, and be blunt if it classifies the third scene as manipulation. I need to know whether to rewrite that scene or to accept that a morally good character can manipulate someone she loves — and I would like you to say what each of those two choices would do to the book as a whole. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of scenes it will misclassify, and the places where no procedure can substitute for my judgment.

Please write the response in American English.
