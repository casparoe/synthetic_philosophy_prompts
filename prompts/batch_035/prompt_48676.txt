I'm a PhD student working on dynamic choice and decision problems with imperfect recall, and I'm trying to write a section for a paper arguing that Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000) is, in effect, a feature-length dramatization of the absent-minded driver problem — but I want to stress-test the analogy rather than just gesture at it. The setup, for reference: in Piccione and Rubinstein's 1997 example, a driver travels a highway with two identical intersections. He is absent-minded in the technical sense that when he reaches an intersection he cannot remember whether he has already passed one, so he cannot condition his action on which intersection he is at. Exiting at the first intersection yields a payoff of 0, exiting at the second yields 4, and continuing past both (he falls asleep and crashes, or ends up somewhere bad) yields 1. The best deterministic plan — exit at the second intersection — is unimplementable for him, because at the second intersection he cannot distinguish it from the first; the best implementable policy randomizes, continuing at each intersection with probability 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3. The literature that grew out of the paper, including the exchange in Games and Economic Behavior, mostly treats this as a game among the driver's temporal selves, whose information sets cannot be distinguished and whose "planning" and "acting" perspectives come apart. That is exactly the frame I want applied to Leonard Shelby.

Leonard, with his anterograde amnesia, never leaves the car: every waking moment is an identical intersection, and every choice he makes must be made from an information set he cannot enrich by remembering. His system of Polaroids, notes, and tattoos is a precommitment device — a way of breaking the history-independence the driver is stuck with — except that the information it injects into each future information set was itself selected by an earlier self. And the ending, where Teddy reveals that Leonard already killed his wife's attacker long ago and may have confabulated the Sammy Jankis story onto his own life, takes the analogy somewhere the decision theory doesn't go: Leonard deliberately burns the evidence, writes Teddy's license plate onto his body as one of his established facts, and leaves himself a note not to trust the man — a prior self curating the beliefs of his successors while deleting the record of why. So my question is this: read the film through the absent-minded driver problem in both directions. What does the formal setup illuminate — Leonard's notes as an implementable policy, his inability to reoptimize at the moment of action, the divergence between what the planner-self wanted and what the acting-selves will do? Where does the mapping break — Leonard can condition on artifacts, his payoffs are partly self-authored rather than exogenous, and he substitutes manufactured certainty for randomization? And does the ending show something the multi-self literature misses but shouldn't: that the planner is not necessarily benevolent, that one self can strategically manipulate the information partitions of the others, and that responsibility can quietly fall between the selves?

Please be disciplined about where the formalism is silent rather than forcing it to speak — I'd rather have an honest list of what the driver problem cannot model (narrative desire, self-deception, the fact that Leonard's past selves have interests of their own) than a reading that flattens the film into an illustration. But I also want to know whether the film earns a claim I'm tempted to make: that it dramatizes a version of the absent-minded driver in which the ex ante perspective is occupied by a self with an agenda, and that this is a genuine extension of the problem, not just a thematic rhyme. If you think the analogy is ultimately superficial, say so and tell me what the stronger framing would be.
