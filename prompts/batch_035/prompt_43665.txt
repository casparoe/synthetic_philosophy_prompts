I lead evaluation at a mid-sized AI lab, and we're about to pilot AI safety via debate — two models arguing for opposing answers to a question while a human judge, or a weaker judge model, decides between them — as a way to oversee outputs we can't directly verify ourselves. At this week's design review, our technical lead waved off my worries about the setup by saying it's just the adversarial legal system: cross-examination has been extracting truth for centuries, two motivated advocates plus a neutral arbiter is the best truth-finding machine anyone has built, and therefore we don't need to bolt extra verification machinery onto the pipeline. I don't have a considered view yet, but I suspect the courtroom is doing a lot of quiet work in that argument, and the go/no-go decision is due at the next review, so I want the analogy stress-tested before I sign off either way.

I'd like you to take on the role of someone who thinks rigorously about analogical reasoning across law and mechanism design — say, a philosopher of law who has also sat through enough ML safety discussions to know the terrain. Please start with some brainstorming and lay out the full mapping explicitly: what plays the role of prosecutor and defense counsel, the judge, the jury, the rules of evidence, cross-examination, discovery, perjury sanctions, appeal, the burden of proof, the public gallery. Then audit the analogy: say where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care about most — separate the disanalogies that matter for the specific conclusion it's being used to license (that debate alone suffices as an oversight mechanism, with no extra verification) from the ones that are harmless. One candidate to get you started: in a courtroom the judge typically understands the subject matter of the dispute, whereas in our setup the whole point is that the judge can't independently verify what the debaters claim — the arbiter is epistemically weaker than the parties, which is more like a parent refereeing an argument between two specialists than anything a real court has to handle. I'd also like you to check which features of the source the analogy quietly imports — sanctions for fabricating evidence, symmetrical access to the record, obligations of disclosure, the possibility of appeal to a higher court — that our setup either lacks or possesses for only one side, since a misaligned debater may face none of the costs a lying lawyer does. Please conclude by telling me whether the "no extra verification machinery" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy for what debate-based oversight actually is — an arbitrage, a duel, a tenure fight, something else — I'd genuinely like to hear it.
