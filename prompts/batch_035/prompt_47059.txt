I'm taking a philosophy of economics class this semester and we just spent two weeks on revealed preference, and the whole time I kept thinking about my summer internship at a state environmental agency, so now I need someone to untangle this for me before I write my term paper on it. At the internship I learned that when the government wants to put a dollar value on something with no market — the existence of a pristine coastline, a wilderness area that basically no one will ever visit — the standard tool is a huge survey where you ask households how much they'd hypothetically be willing to pay to protect it, and then you add up the answers and use that number in actual cost-benefit analysis and even in litigation for damages (that's literally where the method got its big break, valuing the harm from the Exxon Valdez spill). But in my course we learned that the entire revealed preference tradition (Samuelson etc.) exists precisely to say that preferences just ARE what you demonstrate in actual choices under real budget constraints — talk is cheap, hypothetical answers aren't binding, so me saying I'd pay $50 to protect a wetland isn't data about anything. If utility and preference are defined by choice, what is a survey answer even measuring? It looks like the whole method presupposes the old pre-Samuelson view my professor keeps making fun of, that utility is an introspectable mental magnitude you can just ask people about. Heres the wrinkle though: the agencies obviously don't fully trust the answers either, because the surveys have to pass validity checks — like asking different groups what they'd pay to save different numbers of migratory birds from drowning in uncovered oil ponds, and if people will pay about the same to save 2,000 birds as 200,000, the survey gets flagged as garbage. Which is such a strange combination when you think about it: the practice simultaneously assumes we CAN ask people their utility and that people DON'T reliably know their utility when asked. So what I want to know is: what theory of utility and preference does contingent valuation actually presuppose? Is a hypothetical, non-binding choice still a "choice" for purposes of revealed preference theory, or is it something else entirely — a self-report, an expression of attitude, a moral performance? Can a consistent revealed preference theorist accept hypothetical-choice data at all, or is the only honest alternative to value unpriced environmental goods at basically zero (which is what Exxon argued, lol)? And does the whole validity-check apparatus secretly concede that preferences are constructed and context-dependent — that the survey doesn't measure a preference but creates one — and if so is that a fixable noise problem or a fundamental one? Basically I want to know whether there's a coherent third view of utility underneath this practice that my course is ignoring, or whether the agencies are just being pragmatic with no theory at all, and what would have to change in how we do cost-benefit analysis if we took the revealed preference critique fully seriously.
