My flatmate and my old supervisor have been arguing for about two years about whether the demandingness objection actually does any damage to consequentialism, and they've finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Guess who got roped into drafting the joint statement.

The flatmate is a consequentialist who thinks demandingness intuitions are basically self-serving bias with a philosophy degree: of course morality feels too demanding to the very people who'd have to pay for it. She favours a fair-share cap along the lines of: your obligation tops out at what you'd owe if everyone did their bit, so when others shirk, the residue lands on them, not on you. The supervisor thinks this is exactly backwards — the cap just smuggles in the non-consequentialist morality it was supposed to vindicate. He also insists the objection was never only about *how much* is demanded; it's partly about the *shape* of the deliberation, that being a mere conduit for impartial value is alienating even when the arithmetic comes out fine. She thinks the alienation point was answered decades ago and that he keeps ignoring the answer; he thinks she keeps answering a different point than the one he's making.

Write the joint statement they could both actually sign. Exactly five numbered sections, nothing else, no intro or conclusion:

1. Agreed claims — including what the objection is, which versions of it they both consider failures (they agree, e.g., that "consequentialism demands too much" said without a comparison class is empty), and what would need to be true for the objection to be decisive.
2. Residual disagreements, each phrased so the side holding it accepts the wording — my flatmate insists the statement must not say consequentialism "ignores the seperateness of persons," since she thinks that's a strawman of the capped version, and the supervisor insists it must not be watered down to "consequentialism is more demanding than we'd like."
3. Cruxes — I suspect one is whether demandingness intuitions carry evidential weight at all given how convenient they are, and another is whether the fair-share cap can be made principled rather than ad hoc, but they may have better ones; you pick.
4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. The supervisor is fond of cases where the cost to the agent is calibrated to exactly equal the benefit produced, so intuition can't lean on any slack between sacrifice and payoff — use something like that if it helps.
5. A candid closing section on which parts of their remaining dispute, if any, are merely verbal.

Keep it a document they'd genuinely put their names to: formulations neither vacuous nor slanted, and their own positions described as they'd describe them. Don't try to settle who's right — the whole point is that the residual disagreement comes out exact.
