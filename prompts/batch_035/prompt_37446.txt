I'm a junior and the student representative on my school district's Curriculum Materials Review Committee, and I need help writing the formal opinion I'll circulate before our vote next month. Some background: our district is midway through its textbook adoption cycle for U.S. history, and the textbook on the table is, everyone agrees, accurate but bloodless — lots of dates and unit objectives, no story anyone could love or hate. Two petitions have arrived at once. One, organized by a group of parents and two history teachers, asks us to adopt a supplementary unit built on the 1619 Project's framing, which centers slavery and its afterlives and argues that this is a truer spine for American history than the usual march toward liberty. The other, backed by a vocal contingent at school board meetings, wants materials modeled on the 1776 Commission's approach, which treats the founding ideals as the core of the national story and warns that a grievance-centered narrative will corrode students' civic attachment. Our chair has laid out four options on the table: adopt one supplement, adopt both as "competing perspectives," adopt neither and stay with the textbook, or commission a locally written unit that tries to thread the needle. The committee is deadlocked 3–3 and several members have said privately that they'd rather delay the whole cycle than take a vote that ends up in the local paper.

I'd like you to act as an expert in the philosophy of education and the politics of collective memory — someone who has thought seriously about what history education is *for* in a democracy, not just about curriculum mechanics. Before drafting, please take a few sentences to brainstorm the live options and, for each, the strongest version of the argument its supporters would actually give (steelman both the 1619-aligned and the 1776-aligned positions — and the "just the textbook" position, which is not neutral either, since a narrative vacuum leaves students to assemble their story from the internet). Then draft the written opinion I'll circulate, about 700–900 words, with these features: a concrete recommendation among the four options (or a defended hybrid, but only if you specify what it would actually contain), criteria the committee can apply when reviewing the specific materials later, an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than a token paragraph, and a direct response to the worry several members keep raising — that whatever we choose is itself a political act, so shouldn't we just abstain? Please avoid the reflexive "teach the controversy" move unless you can say precisely what it looks like in a tenth-grade classroom over nine weeks, including who selects the competing texts and what the assessment looks like. Keep in mind that I'm seventeen and this will be read by adults who have been fighting about this since before I enrolled — I need it to sound like a serious piece of policy reasoning, not a book report.
