My closest friend and I rewatched Christopher Nolan's The Prestige last weekend, and the argument we got into afterward ran well past midnight; I'd like to hand the disagreement to someone who can do it justice. We agree on the mechanics: Tesla's machine doesn't transport Angier, it duplicates him, and every performance one Angier drops through the trapdoor into the water tank beneath the stage while the other reappears in the balcony to take the bow. What we cannot agree on is what the nightly drowning is about. I maintain that Angier's dread is philosophically confused: read through Dennett's "Where Am I?" (the 1978 piece in Brainstorms where his brain stays in a vat in Houston while his body is sent to Tulsa, and he oscillates between locating himself with his brain, with his body, and finally with his point of view before conceding that personal location is a confused notion), the duplication case dissolves the question he's torturing himself with. There is no fact of the matter about whether he will be the man in the tank or the man in the balcony, so the dread, however understandable, is aimed at nothing. She counters that the film understands this perfectly well and is about something Dennett's essay brackets out: the dread is not residue to be explained away but the datum, and a metaphysics on which the question is confused has to say something about people who must nonetheless climb into the box every night.

I would like a reading of the film through Dennett's puzzle that takes both sides of my argument seriously and adjudicates between them, and that is honest about where the lens fits badly. In particular: Dennett's case keeps a single continuous stream of experience intact -- brain and body split, but one point of view -- whereas the machine gives Angier two full psychological twins every night, which makes the film closer to a fission case, and I want you to say whether that disanalogy means the essay simply doesn't transfer, or whether Dennett's deeper conclusion (that the self is a construction, an abstract center of narrative gravity rather than a thing with a location) is exactly what the film is about. The nested diaries -- Angier reading Borden's account of Borden reading Angier's diary -- strike me as the film formalizing the narrative-gravity idea: the person just is the story that keeps being told, and whoever controls the telling controls the man. And I want the reading to take the Borden twins seriously as the film's alternative answer: two bodies sharing one social identity, no metaphysical question asked, no one drowned. If Borden's solution is Dennettian pluralism lived out and Angier's is a search for a further fact that isn't there, then the film has a thesis -- but I'd want you to tell me where the film resists that neat division (Angier's final scene, the tanks in the basement, the trial that treats one drowned copy as a determinate murder victim, the late exchange about what drowning actually feels like). Refer to scenes and lines by description rather than quotation. Close by telling me what the film gives the debate that Dennett's essay, and the standard literature on fission, do not: whether the affective fact -- that a fully persuaded non-identitarian would still be afraid to step into the machine -- counts as evidence against the dissolution of the question, or as merely its illustration.
