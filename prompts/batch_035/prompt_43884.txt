I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and we just finished a unit on Van Fraassen's Reflection principle. As I understand it, Reflection requires that if you are now certain that tomorrow you will assign credence p to a proposition A, then your current credence in A conditional on that future assignment should itself be p: you should defer, in advance, to your future opinions. Van Fraassen motivated the principle with diachronic Dutch book considerations and with a picture of rationality as a kind of full-blooded commitment to one's own future epistemic conduct, and the principle has since been treated in much of the literature as a core norm of rationality, standing alongside ordinary probabilistic coherence. My seminar instructor is sympathetic to it; I'm not, and I'd like your help constructing the strongest case against it.

Please argue that Reflection is not a genuine requirement of rationality but a conditional norm of deference, one that binds only agents who trust the processes by which their future beliefs will be formed. Develop the argument through cases in which an agent knows, in advance, that her upcoming belief change will not be truth-conducive: someone who knows she will be drunk by midnight, someone about to undergo a cult's recruitment program whose manipulative techniques she has herself studied, someone anticipating medication or sleep deprivation that reliably distorts judgment, or a detainee who knows skilled interrogators can extract sincere-seeming recantations regardless of the facts. In each case, the agent who satisfies Reflection seems not coherent but gullible — she pre-commits to trusting opinions she has excellent present reason to discount — which suggests the principle presupposes, rather than establishes, the epistemic authority of the future self.

The hard part, and what I most want from you, is to deal with the obvious rejoinder: that these are deviant or contrived cases, or that the Dutch book argument coerces Reflection on us regardless. Make the case that the Dutch book machinery yields a sure loss only for agents who would accept bets that reflect their anticipated future opinions even while distrusting the process producing those opinions — that is, that the argument quietly smuggles in the very self-trust Reflection was supposed to vindicate, and so cannot be used to justify it. Then say what a defensible restricted version of Reflection would look like, one conditional on the anticipated update arising from a genuinely epistemic process like learning from evidence, and close by addressing whether such a restricted principle does any independent normative work, or whether it collapses into the far more mundane requirement that one trust learning and distrust indoctrination, sedation, and fatigue.
