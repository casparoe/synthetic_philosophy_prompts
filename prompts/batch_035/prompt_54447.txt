I'd like to know whether decision theory has an established name for a certain kind of gamble, and if it does, exactly what the name covers — I'm not looking for a bare label, since a label without boundaries won't help me.

Two concrete instances of what I mean. First, the classic game in which a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if the first heads is on flip n, you are paid 2^n dollars. If you compute the average payoff in the obvious way, multiplying each possible payoff by its probability and adding everything up, the sum never converges: every term is just 1, forever. I'm told this game has a name, and at least its oddness is definite — the average is "too big," full stop.

Second, and this is the case I actually care about: modify that game so that if the first heads is on flip n, you gain 2^n / n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n / n dollars when n is even. The terms you add up now go roughly +1, −1/2, +1/3, −1/4, and so on, and summed in the natural order they converge to a specific number. But if you reorder the bookkeeping — tally several of the gains first, then their matching losses, then further gains — you can make the "average" come out to a different number, or drift around without settling. I remember from calculus that a series with this property, where the gains and the losses are each unbounded on their own, can be rearranged to sum to anything whatsoever, and that this is a theorem rather than a trick. So the number I compute isn't a property of the gamble at all; it's a property of how I happened to list the outcomes, and there appears to be no privileged way to list them.

So my question is this. Is there an established term for the second kind of case — a bet that seems to have a well-defined expected value until you notice that the value depends on the (arbitrary) order in which the outcomes are enumerated? If so, how is it distinguished from the first kind, where the expectation is merely divergent rather than manipulable? Are the two treated under one heading, or are they importantly different — one having no value and the other having too many? And what about intermediate cases: gains summing to something finite while losses are unbounded, or games whose average exists but whose spread does not? Where do those fall?

If the field has no name for the class I'm pointing at, I'd like you to coin one. Define it precisely enough that I can tell which games fall inside and which merely resemble the ones inside; say how it differs from the nearest established concepts, whatever they turn out to be; and say something about whether the standard repair strategies I've heard suggested — capping the number of flips, summarizing a gamble by something other than its average, treating the enumeration order as part of the gamble's description — actually dissolve the problem or merely relocate it. What I need is the carving, not the name.
