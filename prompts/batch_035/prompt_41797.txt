I'm a PhD candidate giving a job talk in a history department that leans heavily on philosophy of history and archival studies, with several members who work on public memory and one who runs the university's manuscript collections. My thesis: the slogan that history is written by the victors conflates two different mechanisms. The first is control over which sources survive — an archival fact about whose paperwork, inscriptions, and testimony got generated and preserved, which skews the record toward states, administrations, and literate elites even where later historians are scrupulous. The second is control over interpretive framing — a historiographical fact about who wrote the canonical narratives, and here the slogan is arguably false or at least badly oversimplified, since defeated and marginalized actors have repeatedly produced durable master narratives (the Confederate Lost Cause shaping American memory of the Civil War is the obvious case, and émigré historiography of the French and Russian Revolutions offers others). My claim is that the victors dominate the archive but not necessarily the historiography, and that useful debates about power and perspective in historical writing keep foundering because the two mechanisms are run together. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise — the conceptual ones from the philosophers, the ones about archival silences and appraisal from the archivists, phrased the way each group would phrase them — ordered by how much damage they would do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Indicate what I should concede up front about the looseness of "victors" and "archive" as categories, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
