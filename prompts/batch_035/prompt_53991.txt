I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and over the last couple years I've written pieces on chatbots supposedly passing the Turing test — first the 2014 Eugene Goostman stunt, where a chatbot posing as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy fooled some judges in a five-minute chat, and more recently the studies where GPT-4-class models got judged human about half the time. Each time I call up philosophers and each time I realize the underlying argument is exactly where it was in 1950. Nobody has moved an inch and honestly I'm starting to wonder if the question is even the right one. So this is a job for you: please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind who has thought way too much about the Turing test, think carefully, maybe do some visible brainstorming before you commit to a diagnosis, and then give me your honest read.

Here's the lay of the land as I understand it. On one side you've got the people who say the test is basically a behaviorist dodge: passing it only shows you can fool a judge for a few minutes, and Ned Block's old Blockhead thought experiment — a giant lookup table that stores a canned reply to every possible conversation — is supposed to show that perfect verbal behavior is compatible with zero understanding. Searle's Chinese Room gets trotted out in the same spirit. On the other side you've got the ones who say, no, sustained flexible interrogation is actually a pretty good operational grip on 'thinking', and the lookup-table objection is cheating because no real machine could be built that way, so the counterexample is a fantasy that tells us nothing about actual systems. And now the LLM people have added a third layer: they say the test was never really about intelligence anyway, it was about the imitation game as a social protocol, so who cares.

What I want is NOT a survey of the positions — I can get that anywhere, and honestly my readers can too. What I want is a diagnosis of why this debate is permanently stalled. My working theory, and I want you to tell me if it's wrong, is that the disagreement was never really about machines — it's about whether 'thinking' is the sort of term that COULD have purely behavioral criteria, which smells more like a verbal dispute about what we want the word to do than a substantive one. But there might also be a hidden empirical question doing the work underneath, something about whether conversational fluency produced by a system trained on oceans of human text is the same kind of fact as conversational fluency produced by a person — i.e., the test's validity may now be confounded by how the candidates are built, not just how they perform. Is that a real distinction or am I overcomplicating? And where exactly does the Eliza effect — humans attributing way too much mind to anything that chats back — fit in? Is the test measuring the machine, or measuring the judge's propensity to anthropomorphize?

So: is this substantive, verbal, or are the two sides secretly answering different questions (one about intelligence, one about the meaning of a term, one maybe about social attributions of mind) under a single heading? Support the diagnosis with what the sides actually argue, not caricatures. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a new kind of adversarial protocol, a conceptual distinction, some result from cognitive science about how humans mind-read, or your considered view that this particular wheel is just going to spin forever and why. And since I actually have to file a story: end by telling me the one question I should be asking the researchers in my next piece, given your diagnosis. Don't be shy about saying my working theory is dumb if it is, I'd rather be corrected before print than after. Thanks — and dont just dump everything you know about Turing, diagnosis first.
