A friend of mine who works on decision theory swears up and down that the Dutch book argument for probabilism either proves way to much or refutes itself, and I can't find where it slips. He lays it out like this:

1. Your degrees of belief are rational only if you're not exploitable — there's no set of bets, each of which you'd accept, that together guarantee you a loss.
2. You accept a bet iff its expected value, computed using your own degrees of belief, is positive.
3. So a rational agent accepts every bet that has positive expected value by its own lights.
4. Now take an agent whose credences are coherent, so by the standard result no Dutch book is possible, and offer them a single bet on a fair coin: they lose their entire savings of $10,000 on tails and win $10,200 on heads. Expected value is +$100, so by 3 they must take it.
5. But refusing to risk everything you have for a 1% edge is obviously rational — he'd refuse it himself — so by 2 he's irrational, and so is basically everyone.
6. Therefore either 2 is false, in which case the Dutch book argument for probabilism collapses (it needs 2 to get from "your credences are incoherent" to "you'll accept a package guaranteeing a loss"), or 2 is true and rationality demands taking ruinous bets.

My hunch is that something equivocates in step 2 between what the original argument actually needs and what "positive expected value" means when the stakes are your whole bankroll, but I can't put my finger on it cleanly. Which premise should he give up, and what does giving it up cost the probabilist? Also want to hear whether the standard argument survives in some weakened form, and exactly what it's weakened to — alot of people wave at "small stakes" here without saying what work that assumption is doing.
