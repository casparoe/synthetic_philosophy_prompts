So here's my situation. I'm a data scientist at a mid-sized fintech, and last week our VP invoked Goodhart's law to kill my team's churn dashboard project — his version was roughly that once a measure becomes a target, the correlation that made the measure useful gets destroyed. Fine, heard that a million times. But then my teammate Dev took it further over beers, and now I can't stop thinking about it and genuinely can't find the flaw. His argument, numbered so you can point at a step:

(1) Any real goal G we care about — actual user wellbeing, actual task competence — is too messy to measure, so we always operate through a measurable proxy M that is only correlated with G. (2) Goodhart's law: once M becomes the target of optimization, the gap between M and G gets exploited, and the more pressure you apply to M, the worse G does relative to M. (3) Machine learning is just applied Goodhart-bait: training loss is a proxy for the real task, and gradient descent is the most relentless optimization pressure anyone has ever aimed at a proxy — way beyond anything a manager with a dashboard could do. (4) So by it's own logic, models should degrade at their real tasks as training loss goes down, and scaling should have hit a Goodhart wall years ago. (5) But they don't and it hasn't — loss goes down, real-world performance goes up, decade after decade. (6) Dev concludes the degradation is real but invisible: the models look great on every measure we have precisely because every measure we have is the proxy. Another friend jumped in to say that if Dev's argument worked, it would also prove evolution should never have produced functional organisms, since fitness is evolution's dashboard — so the argument must break somewhere, but she couldn't say where either.

Here's what I want to hold onto: the everyday truth of Goodhart's law (the VP isn't wrong about dashboards and KPIs) AND the plain fact that gradient descent on a loss produces real competence. My vague suspicion is that step (2) is doing something sneaky — treating every proxy-goal relationship as the same kind of thing — but I can't articulate it, and Dev's step (6) escape hatch feels like a cop-out without me being able to prove it's one.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no wrap-up: which step you reject and why it looked fine; what rejecting it costs Dev; whether the evolution counterexample actually locates the flaw or just dramatizes it; whether Dev's we-can't-see-the-degradation-yet response is a legitimate possibility or an unfalsifiable dodge; and what survives as the honest, usable version of Goodhart's law that I can actually apply at work.
