So here's my situation: I'm a science and ideas journalist, and a couple times a year I run these live events -- kind of a pub-night-meets-philosophy thing, "Curious Odds," at a bookstore bar downtown. About 30 adults, mixed crowd, some academics but mostly curious normals, and I have roughly 45 minutes for the interactive segment before people start drifting to the actual bar. It works best when the crowd *does* something instead of me lecturing at them, so that's what I want this time.

The topic I've landed on is the identity of indiscernibles -- the old Leibnizian thought that if two things share every property, they can't really be two things. The classic setup is Max Black's universe with just two perfectly identical spheres in it, and the puzzle is that the universe seems to have two spheres in it, but there's no fact whatsoever that could ever tell you *which one is which*. I love this because it sounds like a cute riddle and then it quietly wrecks you: in that universe, "the sphere on the left" doesn't name anything, because left of what?

What I want from you: design the actual activity. Something where my crowd runs headfirst into the individuation problem themselves before I name anything. A few constraints and half-ideas I'd love you to work with or toss out:

- No slide deck, no lecture intro. The concept should hit them through the game mechanics.
- I've been toying with a mechanic where part of the crowd plays inhabitants of the symmetric universe -- maybe surveyors or cartographers who can only send out "probes" that report pure qualitative facts -- and the rest of the crowd plays outside observers trying to use those reports to write down a sentence that is true of one sphere and not the other. If the probe answers come back perfectly symmetric, the sentence should be impossible to write, and I want them to *feel* that impossibility rather than be told about it.
- I also had this idea of a "swap daemon": a volunteer who secretly exchanges two identity tags between rounds, and then we see whether anyone in the room can prove a swap happened from the qualitative reports alone. Good idea or too cute? You tell me.
- Materials should be cheap -- index cards, tape, maybe chairs. No apps, the wifi in that bar is a war crime.
- Payoff scheme: something better than candy. Peer status works on adults, honestly.

Then the debrief: help me map the specific moments in the game onto the philosophical pieces -- the identity of indiscernibles itself, the qualitatively symmetric universe, and the outsider-vs-insider perspective thing that makes the puzzle so slippery. And I need you to be straight with me about two things: what will predictably go sideways the first time I run this (my crowds love derailing things, someone WILL try to game the probe rules), and which part of the philosophical problem no game can actually reproduce and I'll have to just supply in words at the end.

Bonus if the activity sets up the natural follow-up question I want for the column I'll write after: whether "indiscernible but two" is a genuine metaphysical fact or just a confession about the limits of description. 45 minutes, 30 tipsy-ish adults, one exasperated bartender. Go.
