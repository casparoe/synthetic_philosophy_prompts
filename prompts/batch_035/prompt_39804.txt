I am a high school student in an ethics elective. Last term we read part of Sharon Street's 2006 paper A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value, and this term we studied the sensitivity view of justification in epistemology, on which a belief is justified only if it tracks the facts: had what it says been false, you would not have believed it. I put the two together into an argument, and my teacher says it proves too much, but she would not tell me where it goes wrong, and I cannot find it myself.

(1) A belief is justified only if it is sensitive: had its content been false, we would not have held it.
(2) Take the belief that tormenting the innocent for fun is wrong. Natural selection built our moral psychology out of feelings and behavioral dispositions, and it would have installed the same dispositions whether or not such torment is actually wrong, since the moral facts, whatever they may be, do not determine which traits are fit.
(3) So even in a world where such torment was permissible, we would still believe it to be wrong; the belief is insensitive.
(4) By (1), the belief is unjustified.
(5) Nothing is special about this belief: every moral conviction we owe to our evolved psychology is equally insensitive.
(6) Therefore none of our moral beliefs are justified.

Here is what unsettles me. When I run the reasoning of (2) on my belief in principle (1) itself, it seems to go through as well: the sensitivity principle reached me through a contingent curriculum and a particular teacher, that history was never steered by the moral facts either, and had my schooling gone differently I would reject the principle with equal confidence. But I would rather keep (1) than give up on all moral justification, and I do not want an argument whose principle devours itself.

Which step or premise should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost? Please begin your response with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
