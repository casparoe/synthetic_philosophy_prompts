At my reading group on technology and society we took up Langdon Winner's 1980 essay on whether artifacts have politics, and afterwards two members argued well past closing time in a way I couldn't referee, so I'm hoping you can. The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Diego held up the essay's famous case — Robert Moses supposedly designing the Long Island parkway overpasses low so that buses, and with them the poor and Black New Yorkers who depended on them, could not reach the beaches — and said this is what it means for an artifact to be political: the exclusion is built into the concrete, it operates with no policeman standing there, and once the bridges are up, nobody has to make a fresh decision to exclude anyone for the next fifty years. (2) Priya answered that the politics never left the people: the low overpass excludes only because of an entire surrounding regime — because no one runs a shuttle from the train station, because the transit authority doesn't complain, because housing and zoning sorted the bus riders where they are — and she said that calling that politics *in* the artifact is a reification; the defensible claim is just that artifacts can be politically *sticky*, which is a fact about us, not about bridges. (3) Diego said stickiness is the entire point, and that Priya's move proves too much: Winner himself distinguishes technologies that *require* a particular social order from those that are merely *strongly compatible* with one — his nuclear plant needing centralized, hierarchical control is supposed to be the first kind — and if every case dissolves into surrounding human decisions, then the category does no work and we lose our grip on why some designs are worth fighting about before they're built. (4) Priya replied that the flagship example is folklore anyway: Bernward Joerges went back and checked, and the documentation that the overpasses were deliberately designed as barriers is thin to nonexistent — and, worse, she said that in the *strongly compatible* cases the phrase is doing all the work, since nearly any artifact is compatible with several different orders, which makes the thesis unfalsifiable. (5) Diego conceded the Moses story might be embroidered but said the structural point survives on cleaner cases — Winner's mechanical tomato harvester, which transferred California farming to large operations and displaced farm workers regardless of anyone's further intentions — and offered a modern version: a recommendation algorithm that quietly sorts the public into audiences does politically consequential work without a committee ever meeting. (6) Priya said she could accept all of that and the dispute would still be mostly verbal — whether we say the artifact is political or merely that people made it politically consequential and then locked the consequence in — and Diego insisted the dispute is substantive, because it turns on whether *any* technology could be politically neutral in principle, and accused her of dodging that question by reclassifying it.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it succeeds, half-succeeds, or fails, and why, treating both sides as having made their strongest available points rather than strawmen. Then tell me whether the disagreement between them ultimately bottoms out in a verbal issue about the phrase "artifacts have politics" or in a genuine substantive one — and, if substantive, what exactly separates their positions. Finally, identify anything important that both of them missed, and say who comes out more right overall. Please answer in American English, in reasonably formal prose.
