Quick background: I've been asked to give the closing talk at a two-day workshop on wellbeing measurement and national accounts. The room will be about 40 welfare economists plus a few psychologists who build subjective wellbeing scales, basicaly no philosophers, and they invited me as the token ethicist.

My thesis, in short: when you aggregate welfare across persons, suffering should sum additively but happiness should sum with sharply diminishing returns. So a thousand mildly pleasant lives never add up to outweigh one case of torture-level suffering, no matter how many you pile on — but a thousand small headaches genuinely do add up, they don't get discounted the way pleasures do. The asymmetry is about aggregation across people, not within a single life, and it's not full negative utilitarianism: happiness still counts, it just saturates.

I'm told the Q&A there runs long and these people are not shy. Prep me a sheet with the objections they'll actualy raise, phrased in their vocabulary — I'm bracing for stuff about interpersonal comparability, whether a lexical-ish threshold makes the social welfare function discontinuous and the optimizer just lands on corner solutions, whether my diminishing returns on happiness is just scope insensitivity or a psychophysical log-scale artifact dressed up as ethics (include that one, and tell me straight whether I should concede part of it). Order them by how much damage each does to my case, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes for each, and flag where the reply is thin and I should pivot instead of doubling down.

Two more things. First, if someone drags me into population ethics — total versus average, whether a huge population of barely-content lives beats a small happy one — I lose the room, so tell me how to keep that door shut without looking like I'm dodging. Second, include the question I'd honestly rather nobody in that room thinks of, you know the kind, and don't soften it: I want to know how bad it realy is for me.
