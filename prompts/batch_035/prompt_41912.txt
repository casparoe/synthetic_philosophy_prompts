I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and after writing a piece about Augustine (who apparently held that every lie is wrong, even the murderer-at-the-door one, since a lie is speaking against your own mind) I've been cataloguing my own gut verdicts and they're a mess. Lying to a killer at the door about where my friend is: obviously fine. Telling my aunt I love the sweater: fine. Telling a source I've already spoken to their rival when I haven't, to loosen them up: not fine. Telling a grieving widow her husband didn't suffer when I have no idea: not fine. Here's my problem case though — telling someone something literally true but phrased so they'll draw a false conclusion, like answering 'is he seeing someone?' with 'he hasn't mentioned anyone', when I know full well he has. That feels to me worse than a kindly lie, which seems hard to square with any principle where lying is basically about asserting what you believe is false, and hard to square with Augustine too, since on his own definition I haven't lied at all. Also not sure what to do with exaggeration everyone expects — 'I've told you a million times' — which feels like not even a lie, more a licence everyone's signed up to. So: is there a principle, or set of them, that captures all my verdicts, or do I have to give one up? And what does the winner say about the case I keep flip-flopping on, going undercover — telling a source I'm not a journalist when they ask point blank, for a story I think matters? If the principle says that's fine but the rival-loosening lie isn't, I'd want to know what does the work. Keep it usable, I'm writing a follow-up column on this, not a treatise.
