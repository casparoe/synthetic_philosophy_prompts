Two of my friends had a massive row after a pub quiz night about quantum immortality and I need help working out who was actually right, because I couldn't follow it by the end.

Background: Dev is fairly committed to the many-worlds interpretation and has read a lot of pop stuff about quantum suicide. Priya is doing a physics degree and is sceptical of the whole framing, though she says she doesn't reject many-worlds itself. The argument went roughly like this:

(1) Dev claimed that if many-worlds is true, he literally cannot die. Whenever he faces a lethal risk, there will be branches in which he survives, and since you only ever experience the branch you're in, he'll always find himself having survived. So his subjective probability of death is basically zero, and playing Russian roulette is, in an odd sense, safe.

(2) Priya said this confuses surviving with surviving intact. The branches where the bullet misses or the tumour spontaneously regresses are a vanishingly small fraction of the total, and the more typical "survivor" branches involve surviving in agony, or with catastrophic brain damage — so even granting his setup, quantum immortality is a guarantee of *something*, not of a life worth having. She called it the "mangled worlds" worry, roughly.

(3) Dev pushed back on the whole idea of weighting by branch fraction. He said that if there's no objective fact about which future "you" is really you, then measure is just a bookkeeping device, and his future self in the lucky branch will be just as much him as any other. Anticipation, he said, should be all-or-nothing: either there's a successor experience or there isn't.

(4) Priya replied that by that logic an ordinary person who never touches a gun also "always survives" in the same sense — every time someone faces ordinary risks (crossing the road, a cancer diagnosis), there are branches where they pull through, so Dev's claim proves too much and has nothing to do with Russian roulette at all. It's a fact about self-observation that applies to everyone, and it gives no one any decision-relevant guidance. She also pointed out that ageing and heat death seem to guarantee branches running out eventually, so the immortality isn't even immortal.

(5) Dev's last stand was an anthropic one: he said Priya's weighting mistake is like the gambler treating a fair coin's history — the mere existence of unlucky branches can't be evidence against the lucky ones, and since death is never observed, the observation-selection effect is doing all the work and it favours him.

They then started arguing about whether Priya's appeal to the Born-rule weights was itself just an extra assumption many-worlds can't justify (she said the weightings are empirically anchored, he said that's exactly the thing other interpretations get for free), at which point everyone else left.

Please adjudicate this move by move — go through the five moves and say who's right about each one, not just who wins overall, including whether Dev's "proves too much" point in (4) actually lands against him or against Priya. Also flag what, if anything, both of them are missing that would reframe the whole argument. And since I'd like to actually use this to settle it with them: tell me what consideration or argument would most plausibly change your verdict about the central moves, and where you think your adjudication is most likely to go wrong — e.g. if some step leans on a contested reading of how branch weight relates to subjective anticipation, say so rather than presenting it as settled.
