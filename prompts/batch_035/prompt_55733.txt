So I'm a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a three-generation family novel, and my editor and I are at war over the ending. She wants it to land on hope — the granddaughter character standing at a protest, feeling like the worst of the family's history is finally being answered for. My instinct, which I've been channeling through the pessimist grandfather character, is that this is cheap. Into this fight people keep lobbing the same line at me: the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice. My editor said it in our last call, my niece sent me a commencement speech that used it, and there was basically a banner with it at the march I went to for research. MLK made it famous, and I gather he borrowed it from some 19th-century Boston preacher, so it's been doing rhetorical work for a long time. Everyone deploys it like it settles whether my ending should be hopeful, and I don't believe one sentence can carry that much weight — but I can't quite articulate why, and I need more than a feeling to win the argument with my editor (or lose it, if that's what it comes to).

Can you lay out the different precise claims that line could actually be making, as a menu I can bring to my next editorial call? I can dimly see there are several non-equivalent things packed in there, and I'd like them separated out. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, and — more important for me — whether it would actually support a hopeful ending, versus just being wishful thinking dressed up as history. For instance, I'd want you to cover things along these lines, though organize it however makes sense: a providential claim that history has a direction built into it (providence, basically, with or without God — is that even coherent?); an empirical claim that we can observe arcs bending, and if so over what timescales, since "long" is doing alot of work in that sentence; a survivorship-bias reading where we only tell the stories of the struggles that did eventually win, which would make the slogan a artifact of who gets novels written about them rather than a fact about the world; a self-fulfilling reading where believing it is partly what makes it true, which would make it more of an exhortation than a description; and a morale-boosting reading where it's not meant as a claim at all, just something you say to keep people in the street. If there are readings I haven't thought of, add them. And be blunt about whether the strongest reading is even true — I'd definately rather find out now than from reviewers later. Knowing which reading my hopeful ending would actually be committing me to, and whether that commitment is honest, is what I need to decide if I write the ending my editor wants or the one the grandfather would have written.

One last thing: answer in American English, and start with your bottom line in the very first sentence — whether the slogan can bear the weight my editor is putting on it — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating my question back at me before you get going.
