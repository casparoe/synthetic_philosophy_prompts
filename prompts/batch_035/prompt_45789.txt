For three years my closest philosophical sparring partner and I have been arguing about just deserts, in the way Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso argued in their 2021 debate book, and we have finally agreed to attempt a real adversarial collaboration: not another exchange of essays, but a single joint document that both of us would put our names to. I want you to draft that document. Her position is a compatibilism in Dennett's mold: sufficiently rational adult agents can genuinely deserve blame and punishment for what they have done, in a sense that does not reduce to the forward-looking benefits of holding people responsible, and she regards that desert as what keeps our responsibility practices honest. My position is free will skepticism in the Pereboom–Caruso mold: no one ever deserves suffering in the basic desert sense — the sense in which an offender would deserve blame even if blaming had no further good effects — and the justification of punishment must be rebuilt on prevention, rehabilitation, and protection, on something like the public-health quarantine model. Her standing worry about my view is that it drains responsibility practices of something she takes to be indispensable to moral progress: the thought that being held responsible is part of how people become better. My standing worry about hers is that basic desert is a metaphysical leftover that legitimates cruelty. Neither of us accepts that the dispute is about physics, about the truth of determinism, or about whether it is useful to hold one another responsible at all, and neither of us is a libertarian about agent causation — the statement should not pretend otherwise.

The document must contain, in order: (1) a numbered section of claims we both can sign, substantive rather than vacuous — including, at minimum, claims about what the dispute is not about, about what we jointly take the experimental philosophy of ordinary people's responsibility judgments to establish, and about the actual state of most real-world punishment regimes, where I expect us to agree that existing institutions punish more harshly than even her own view would license; (2) a disagreements table in which each disagreement is stated twice, once in wording she would accept as a fair statement of her view and once in wording I would accept for mine, covering at least the justificatory standing of basic desert, the correct understanding of blame as moral address rather than mere sanction, and what follows for sentencing practice; (3) a cruxes section identifying the subquestions such that settling any one of them would move at least one of us — I suspect these include whether basic desert can be reconstructed from within the practical standpoint, from what agents owe each other when they take and assign responsibility, without smuggling in anything metaphysical, and whether blame can retain its character as communication once desert is denied, but replace or supplement these as the analysis demands rather than taking my word for it; (4) for each crux, at least one case, policy, or argument both of us would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. One case we keep circling and have never managed to settle: a sentencing hearing for a thirty-year-old first offender, armed robbery, chronic opioid addiction, steadily employed until two years ago. What, if anything, does justice require the judge to treat him as deserving, as opposed to what forward-looking considerations require? The document should put our two answers side by side, each phrased so its owner could not disown it. We also both accept that comparative evidence on rehabilitation-centered versus desert-centered sentencing regimes bears on whether retributive desert does moral-education work that purely forward-looking sentencing cannot, while disagreeing about what that evidence actually shows — so the document should record both predictions rather than adjudicate. (5) A closing section taking a stand on which of our residual disagreements are verbal: we both suspect the word "deserve" is doing several jobs at once — blaming-desert, fairness-entitlement, fittingness of response — and the document should say which parts of our remaining dispute turn on that ambiguity and which do not.

Two constraints on the drafting. First, do not let either side win: formulations only one of us would recognize as her own view are worthless, and so are agreed claims so thin that two philosophers chosen at random could sign them; the test of every line is that both of us would defend it under our own names at a colloquium. Second, the response should be the statement itself, beginning directly with the first agreed claim, with no preface, no explanation of the assignment, and nothing after the closing section except the statement's own signature lines.
