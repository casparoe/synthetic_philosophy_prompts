I am a high school senior enrolled in an elective called "Puzzles and Paradoxes," and our current unit is on decision theory. We have just spent two weeks on Newcomb's problem, and for the culminating assignment each of us must write and then orally defend a persuasive piece arguing for one side. I have been assigned the two-boxing side, which is awkward, because the class has gone heavily one-boxer — our teacher frames one-boxing as the "sophisticated" answer and treats two-boxing as the position you hold before you've really thought it through, so I will be arguing uphill against an audience that already believes I am wrong.

Please construct the argument for two-boxing on my behalf, but built around a specific line of reasoning rather than the usual dominance talk, since my classmates have already heard "the boxes are already sealed, so take both" and find it tiresome. The line I want runs as follows: the one-boxer's expected-value calculation conditions on the act of one-boxing, but what I actually observe when I deliberate is not my act but my entire reasoning process, and if the predictor's forecasts are only, say, ninety percent accurate, the correlation between one-boxing and box contents may be carried largely by agents who choose without deliberating at all — impulsive agents the predictor reads easily — so that once I condition on being the kind of agent who works through the expected values, the apparent evidential advantage of one-boxing thins out considerably. Please develop this conditioning argument carefully and honestly, including where it is weakest (I suspect a classmate will say that a smart predictor should predict deliberators as well as impulsive agents, and I need a real reply, not a shrug).

Two further requirements. First, my teacher has said that the version with a perfectly accurate predictor is "the real problem," so the piece must say something about the perfect-predictor case rather than hiding behind the ninety percent figure; my own thought, which you are welcome to use or discard, is that if the predictor is literally infallible, then something in the setup quietly conflicts with the assumption that both boxes are genuinely open to me, and the two-boxer can perhaps argue that the case then describes a fixed story rather than a choice. Second, the piece must confront rather than ignore the one-boxer's most emotionally powerful point — that the two-boxer walks away with a thousand dollars while watching the one-boxers collect their millions — and explain why the two-boxer thinks this regret-based framing misidentifies what makes a decision rational. Please write it at a level suitable for a strong high school audience: technical machinery beyond conditional probability and expected value is off-limits, so any notions like conditioning on one's own reasoning must be explained in plain terms as you go.
