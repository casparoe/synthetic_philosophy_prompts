I spent thirty-eight years as a biostatistician, most of it designing and analyzing clinical trials, and since retiring I have drifted, somewhat to my own surprise, into philosophy of science. A reading group I recently joined has been working through questions about the interpretation of statistical evidence, and our last session ended on Simpson's reversal — the situation where a treatment looks better than the control in the pooled data, yet worse in every subgroup, or vice versa. I met this pattern more than once in my working life (a drug given preferentially to the sickest patients can look harmful in the aggregate while helping everyone it was actually given to), and I found our discussion of it unexpectedly moving: the unsettling part is not the arithmetic but the fact that both summaries are true at once, and that deciding which table to trust is not itself a statistical operation but something closer to a philosophical judgment about which population a decision belongs to.

I would like to mark this with a poem, and I think the villanelle is the right form for it, for reasons I hope you will find congenial. Write a villanelle about Simpson's paradox in which the two refrains are, respectively, the claim made by the pooled data and the claim made by every subgroup. Each refrain must be literally true as stated — the poem should not cheat by making one of them a mistake — and their alternation, each return displacing the other's confidence, should enact the reader's experience of being pulled back and forth between the two summaries. The middle stanzas should carry the explanation: let them quietly introduce the confounding variable, the fact that the treatment was not handed out at random to the two populations being compared, so that the paradox is demystified without being dissolved. The closing couplet, where the refrains finally sit side by side in the same breath, should register that the reconciliation is not arithmetic but a choice about which population is ours — and it should leave that choice open rather than settle it.

Please ground the poem in a concrete scenario of the sort I used to encounter — a therapy that appears superior in the aggregate yet loses within each severity stratum, or something structurally similar — rather than gesturing at the paradox in the abstract. Accuracy matters to me: a statistician should find the poem right (no invented numbers, no false claims about what aggregation does), and a poet should find it a good poem rather than a diagram in disguise.
