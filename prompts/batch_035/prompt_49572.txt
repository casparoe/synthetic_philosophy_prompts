I edit a philosophy-of-technology blog with a mixed readership — some academics, mostly gamers and general readers — and I want a decision tree titled "Which view of virtual objects do you actually hold?" that takes a reader from an everyday starting point, such as having had a rare in-game item deleted by a server glitch, to a named position in the metaphysics of virtual worlds. The tree must be built from the logical space, not from a list of schools: each branch node should be a concrete case with two or three answers, phrased so that someone with no philosophy background can answer it without knowing where it leads.

Please make sure the following cases appear as branch nodes, since the positions genuinely come apart on them: (1) a server is shut down and later relaunched from a backup — is your character the same one or a new duplicate?; (2) a duplication bug creates two numerically distinct copies of a "unique" item — does this tell us anything about what kind of thing the item is?; (3) an item bought with real money through a third-party seller — did you acquire a thing, a right, or nothing?; (4) another player deliberately destroys a build someone spent a year on — is that vandalism against property, or just rude behavior in a game?; and (5) a distressing act performed on an avatar in a shared virtual world, with the player behind the avatar left genuinely shaken — where does the harm live, in the world, in the player, or nowhere yet?

The leaves should at least include Chalmers-style virtual realism, the view that virtual objects are fictional props, an abstract-artifact view on which a virtual sword resembles a poem rather than a sword, an institutional or social-construction view, and a flat irrealism. Add a separate branch of the tree for virtual harms rather than folding them into the ontology, and flag explicitly: (a) any two leaves your cases cannot actually separate, (b) any combination of answers no named position occupies, with one sentence on whether that gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Ten to twelve nodes total; keep all wording accessible to non-philosophers.
