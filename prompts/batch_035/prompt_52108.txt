I teach an upper-level course in philosophy of biology, and for the first meeting I want a diagnostic exercise in the form of a quiz — something like "which species concept do you actually hold?" — whose point is to sort students by their verdicts on concrete cases rather than by whichever label they picked up in reading. Please construct it under the following constraints.

Build the logical space first and locate the named positions in it only afterward. The forks I care about are these: (1) whether species membership requires reproductive isolation from other groups, or whether persistent interbreeding across a boundary — hybrid zones, introgression — is compatible with two species remaining two; (2) whether asexual and uniparental lineages (an ancient asexual clade like bdelloid rotifers is the canonical test) form species in the same sense as sexual organisms, in some diminished sense, or not at all; (3) whether membership in a species is a matter of organisms sharing some property — a diagnosis — or of their position in a genealogy; (4) whether species are individuals with organisms as parts, or classes with organisms as members; (5) whether there is exactly one correct species concept or several legitimate ones relative to scientific purposes; (6) what happens at a lineage split — one parental species continuing alongside a new one, or both parents ending and two new species beginning; (7) whether a species may be paraphyletic with respect to some of its descendants; (8) whether the rank itself is dispensable — whether there could be perfectly good biological systematics without the species category at all. If you think a fork is missing or redundant, say so in the preface rather than silently adding or dropping it.

The quiz itself: ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. The following cases must appear somewhere: a ring species in which the terminal forms coexist without interbreeding even though gene flow connects them around the ring; cryptic sibling species distinguishable only by genetic or behavioral assays; a recognized species of well-documented hybrid origin; a fossil lineage changing gradually enough that any boundary is arbitrary; and an asexual clade. Fill out the remaining questions with cases that split other forks. No question may invite self-identification or mention labels, authors, or positions — every question must be a verdict about a case, and every option must be the answer someone holding one of the leaves would sincerely give; no straw options.

These leaves must appear: the biological (isolation-based) concept; the diagnosability version of the phylogenetic concept; a monophyly-based version of it; the general lineage concept on which all serious concepts are competing criteria for one and the same kind of entity; the evolutionary concept, on which a species is a lineage with its own role and tendencies; the cohesion concept; pluralism; and eliminativism about the category. Attach conventional attributions where you are confident of them and write "unnamed" where you are not — do not guess a name to fill a slot. Each leaf carries one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the case that costs its holder the most, stated as the case rather than as a criticism.

After the scoring key, include two diagnostics of the instrument itself. First, the unoccupied combinations of fork answers: for each, say whether nobody holds it because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored. Second, the pairs of leaves the quiz cannot genuinely separate — for instance, I suspect that if the general lineage concept really does treat the other concepts' criteria as lines of evidence about one entity, then no student answer separates it from eclecticism, and I want to know whether that suspicion is right and what else is structurally indistinguishable here.

Format: a prose preface of at most two hundred words laying out the logical space and any adjustments to the fork list, followed by a single JSON object and nothing else. The object should have five top-level fields. "questions": an array of ten objects, each with "id" (a short string), "case" (the concrete scenario), and "options", an array of objects each with "label" (a short string), "text" (the verdict), and "fork_effects" (an object whose keys are fork numbers, given as strings, and whose values are the stance that option commits the answerer to, in at most one clause). "leaves": an array of objects, each with "name" (including attribution or "unnamed"), "fork_signature" (the full vector of stances that defines it), and "awkward_commitment" (one sentence). "scoring_key": an array of objects mapping fork signatures or answer patterns to leaves, written so that a partial or inconsistent pattern resolves to the nearest leaf with a note on the residue. "unoccupied": an array of objects with "combination" and "why_empty". "inseparable_pairs": an array of objects with "pair" and "why_inseparable". Assume the quiz will be taken with pencil and paper by students with background in evolutionary biology but no prior allegiance; do not rely on anything they could not answer from the case descriptions alone.
