In our reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, we have spent months on what Bell's theorem actually establishes, and the discussion has ossified into two camps that talk past each other. Camp one holds that Bell decisively refuted local realism: the violations of Bell-type inequalities show that the correlations are incompatible with any theory on which influences are confined to the light cone, full stop. Camp two responds that the theorem refutes only a conjunction of assumptions — factorizability, measurement independence, and perhaps the very framework of hidden variables with definite pre-measurement values — and that since quantum mechanics itself respects no-signalling, locality in the operationally meaningful sense (no controllable faster-than-light signaling) survives untouched, so the popular refrain that Bell "killed locality" is sloppy. We keep circling the same moves: someone raises the Jarrett-style decomposition of Bell's locality condition into parameter independence and outcome independence; someone else insists that abandoning measurement independence amounts to a cosmic conspiracy, while the other side replies that it is merely an assumption about the initial conditions of the universe, no more objectionable than any other assumption about initial conditions. Nobody has budged in months.

My working hypothesis is that a good part of the impasse is verbal: the camps mean different things by "locality" — no-signalling at the operational level versus Bell's factorization condition at the level of the underlying theory — and once the term is disambiguated, some of the apparent disagreement evaporates. But I suspect something substantive remains underneath: a genuine dispute about the epistemic status of measurement independence, where one side treats denying it as fine-tuning bordering on pseudoscience and the other treats affirming it as an unjustified extrapolation from ordinary laboratory practice to the early universe; and perhaps a further dispute about how much explanatory work a physical theory owes us, that is, whether quantum mechanics' refusal to supply any account of how the correlations are produced is a defect that hidden-variable and collapse programs rightly try to repair, or an idle demand for mechanism where none is needed.

Is this diagnosis right? If it is, say precisely how much of the disagreement dissolves once the terms are fixed, and characterize the residue as a genuine dispute — about hidden empirical commitments, about standards of explanation, or about starting intuitions regarding fine-tuning that neither side can argue from shared ground. If it is not, tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is and what evidence from the exchange supports that reading. Please engage what each camp actually claims, including the superdeterminist flank of camp two, rather than surveying positions. And given your diagnosis, be honest about what, if anything, would count as progress here: a sharper theorem, a conceptual distinction that splits the dispute into tractable parts, a specified experimental program, or nothing short of one side's research agenda dying out on its own.
