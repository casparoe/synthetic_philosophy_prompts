I'm drafting a paper on whether self-deception is even coherent, and before reading further I want to work out what an adequate account of it would actually have to accomplish. Please write me the desiderata: the full specification of what a theory of self-deception must deliver — which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it has to respect, and which phenomena it is not allowed to explain away — with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. The cases and phenomena I want covered: the person who interprets a spouse's unexplained absences and coldness charitably while giving brutally clear-eyed advice to a friend in the identical situation; the avoidance behaviors (never opening the test results, steering conversation away from the topic); the fact that the belief survives even as the costs of being wrong mount; the "I knew all along, really" avowals that suggest something between full belief and full ignorance; and the observation that the self-deceived respond to exposure with anger and embarrassment rather than the "oops" of the merely mistaken, which suggests they are somehow responsible in a way the plain error is not. The distinctions it must respect include: self-deception versus wishful thinking or mere optimism; versus akrasia (I know the truth but act against it); versus cold cognitive bias, where nothing is at stake for the believer; and the difference between what one believes and what one is trying to make oneself believe. Please also take a position on two borderline requirements: whether the belief must be false (can one self-deceive into a truth?), and whether the process must be in any sense intentional — and then flag honestly which pairs of your desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. The tension between the intentionality requirement and the requirement that the person be genuinely, and not just officially, ignorant of the truth is the obvious candidate: say whether you think that tension is real or whether it dissolves on inspection. Tell me, finally, which single requirement does the most to knock out candidate theories. Then, and only briefly, run two contenders against the list: a deflationary account on which self-deception is just ordinary motivated reasoning bent toward a desired conclusion (roughly Alfred Mele's line), and a partitioning account on which the mind is divided into quasi-independent centers of agency, one of which manages what the other gets to see (in the spirit of Donald Davidson or, in a different register, Sartre's bad faith). Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard should be an afterthought, since the criteria are where the real question of whether self-deception is possible gets decided.
