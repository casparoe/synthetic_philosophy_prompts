So a bit of backstory: I moved to a new city last fall and joined a language-exchange meetup mostly to force myself to keep up my Spanish, and somehow last week I got talked into running next month's "topic night" — 45 minutes, a whiteboard, and a corner of a brewery. The topic I unwisely proposed is whether the language you speak shapes how you think, mostly because of two things that happened to me recently. First, a friend from the club who's a native Serbian speaker keeps insisting she's "a meaner, blunter person" when she switches to English — like her humor and her patience are different people depending on the language. Second, I watched a documentary where a simultaneous interpreter said something similar, that after years of working between languages she feels like she hosts slightly different selves. I don't actually know if that's the Sapir-Whorf thing at all or just... register, persona, something else, and I'd rather not build the whole session on a confusion I can't name.

The audience is going to be maybe fifteen people: hobbyist learners (Spanish, Japanese, French), one semi-professional translator who is actually the reason I'm nervous, and a guy who speaks only English but read a pop-science article once and has Opinions. I already know the snow-words thing will come up in the first five minutes, and someone will mention the business about German speakers describing a bridge with feminine adjectives. Also the English-only guy will probably say programming languages prove it — like, you "can't think" certain thoughts in a language without garbage collection, or something. And I have my own temptation to bring in the stuff I half-remember about color words and communities that navigate by absolute directions instead of left/right, but I don't know which of these is load-bearing and which is trivia.

What I want from you is a teaching plan, not an explanation for me personally — I've got the general picture, roughly, it's the pedagogy I can't figure out. Specifically: what idea has to be in place before any of the fun examples mean anything (I suspect there's a distinction between "your language forces you to notice things" and "your language forces you to believe things" that has to come early or everything collapses, but sequence it properly); which ONE example should carry the whole session and what it needs to have that the others lack; the misconceptions people will walk in with or form during it, in the order they'll actually arrive, and where exactly in my 45 minutes I should pre-empt each one; whether my friend's "meaner in English" thing is usable as an opener or whether it just muddies everything; what I should openly refuse to get into at this level (the translator will ask me about the Hopi stuff and I plan to say "great question, out of scope" unless you tell me otherwise); and one concrete question I can throw at the group in the last five minutes that would tell me whether they actually understood the distinction or just enjoyed the anecdotes. Keep in mind there's no projector and people will be a beer or two in by the midpoint.
