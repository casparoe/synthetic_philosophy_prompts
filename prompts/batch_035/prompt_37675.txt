I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on Indian epistemology, and I've become fascinated by a question my professor raised offhandedly in office hours: how would a classical Nyāya philosopher classify the epistemic status of outputs produced by a large language model? Nyāya recognizes four pramāṇas — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda) — and, as Annaṃbhaṭṭa and others put it, śabda is defined as the statement of an āpta, a speaker who is reliable, which Nyāya thinkers unpack as someone both competent to know the truth and sincere in reporting it. My question is this: suppose Gaṅgeśa (the fourteenth-century founder of Navya-Nyāya, author of the Tattvacintāmaṇi) were brought to the present day, given a clear explanation of how LLMs work — statistical models trained on vast corpora of human writing, producing fluent and often accurate answers about chemistry, history, and mathematics, but with no beliefs or intentions, no concern for truth, and a tendency to confidently "hallucinate" falsehoods. Would he treat the model's outputs as a genuine pramāṇa, as a mere instrument that transmits the testimony of the humans who wrote the training corpus, or as something epistemically worthless — and in the latter case, what would he say about the accuracy of the many true claims such systems do deliver?

Two complications I'd like you to weigh carefully. First, on the traditional picture the reliability of testimony is grounded in the speaker's virtues, and Nyāya philosophers also hold that valid testimony must be non-deviating, like other pramāṇas; but Nyāya is also famously anti-reductionist about testimony, insisting that hearing a trustworthy speaker can generate knowledge directly rather than via an inference to the speaker's reliability, so a defender of LLM outputs might try to piggyback on that irreducibility.
