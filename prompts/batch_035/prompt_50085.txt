I should say at the outset that until this term I had no involvement in university governance, and I have no training in formal social theory or economics. I have just begun a three-year appointment to my department's promotions committee, and in my first months of service I have run into a configuration in our deliberations that I am convinced must have a name, though I cannot find it. Let me describe it in plain terms.

Our committee votes on propositions rather than on candidates as wholes. In a recent promotion case, the chair put three claims to us in sequence: first, that the candidate's research record meets the standard we normally require at promotion (this passed, four to three); second, that her teaching meets our standard (this also passed, four to three); and third — not put to a vote, but treated as settled policy from earlier cases — that anyone meeting both thresholds is to be recommended. We then voted on the direct question of whether to recommend her, and the answer was no, again four to three. What unsettled me was not the outcome but its structure: the majority that had affirmed the research record and the majority that voted against the recommendation were composed of different members. No individual colleague said anything inconsistent — each person's final vote followed from the claim or claims that person had doubts about — and yet the committee as a body had committed itself to a set of positions that jointly entail the opposite of what it decided.

A second phenomenon has emerged from my reading of the minutes of earlier years. Faced with this kind of impasse, the committee's practice is to set aside whichever claim received the weakest support and let the rest stand. But I have found structurally identical cases from past years in which the outcome reversed depending on the order in which propositions were put to a vote: when the recommendation was voted first, the committee subsequently softened its assessment of the file to match, and when the file was assessed first, the recommendation was made to follow. The verdict appears to track the agenda rather than anyone's convictions, and I cannot tell whether this order-dependence is a separate phenomenon or just the first one viewed over time.

I half remember, from an undergraduate course long ago, that Condorcet discovered something about majority voting producing results that go in a circle, but I believe that concerned rankings of candidates rather than yes-or-no claims, and I do not recall it covering a committee that affirms premises while rejecting what follows from them. So my questions are these. Does the first configuration have an established name? Is there a separate one for the second, the dependence of the group's verdict on the sequence of votes? And how, if at all, do they relate to that older result about cycling majorities — is my case a version of it, or something distinct that merely resembles it? I should warn you that a bare label will not satisfy me. What I want is the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, which of the two descriptions (order-dependence included or excluded) marks its true boundary, and if any part of it lacks a name, a proposed term with a definition and the sorts of cases that would fall just inside or just outside it. I would rather understand the shape of the thing than be able to cite it.
