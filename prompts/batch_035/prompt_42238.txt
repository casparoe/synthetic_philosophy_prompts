A colleague claims to have knocked out the transitivity of 'better than', and the argument bothers me more each time I run through it. Consider twenty outcomes, O1 through O20, where Oi is a headache lasting 2^i minutes whose intensity drops gradually as i rises. So O1 is two minutes of near-unbearable pain and O20 is roughly two years of mild but constant headache, with each outcome sitting between its neighbors on both dimensions. The argument:

(1) 'Better than' is transitive.
(2) For every adjacent pair, O(i+1) is better than Oi: the pain is a shade milder, and the duration merely doubles, and nobody would trade a genuinely milder headache for one that lasts twice as many minutes.
(3) By transitivity, O20 is better than O1.
(4) But O20 is plainly worse than O1: nearly anyone would take two minutes of excruciating pain over two years of low-grade constant headache.
(5) So either transitivity fails, or one of the pairwise judgments in (2) fails.

He then adds the part that really unsettles me: nothing here is special to headaches. Any two outcomes between which one seems clearly better can presumably be connected by an interpolated series of this shape, so if we save transitivity by discarding pairwise judgments whenever a distant endpoint judgment contradicts them, no comparison is stable — even the verdict that two years of headache is worse than two minutes of agony could be undone by a cleverly constructed series. That seems to prove too much.

Something feels off between (2) and (3), but each transitivity step taken alone looks airtight. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs. I'd much rather keep transitivity than give up (4), but I want to know exactly what that purchase includes — including, if you think (2) cannot survive the whole series intact, an explanation of how the description of the series conceals that.
