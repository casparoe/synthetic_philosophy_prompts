I'm a philosophy PhD student who is TA-ing an undergraduate course on the history of political philosophy this term while also taking a graduate seminar on Kant, so I find myself grading midterm answers about a text I've recently reread quite carefully — which makes it awkward when my own reading of the exam question is clearer than what the students had time for. Before assigning a grade, please take a moment to mentally separate the claims in the answer that are correct and load-bearing from those that are correct but decorative, and those that are subtly off — I want a point-by-point assessment, not a general impression.

The exam question, worth 25 points, read as follows: "Kant's 1795 essay Toward Perpetual Peace distinguishes preliminary articles from definitive articles. (a) Briefly explain the difference between the two sets of articles. (b) Why does the second definitive article call for a federation of free states rather than a single world state with coercive authority — what does Kant fear would happen to a universal republic, and how is the federation meant to avoid this? (c) What kind of constitution does the first definitive article demand of states, and why does Kant prefer his chosen term to the more familiar 'democratic' one? (d) Finally, what does Kant mean by saying that nature 'guarantees' perpetual peace, and what does the third definitive article's right of hospitality amount to?"

Here is the student's answer: "Kant divides his program into preliminary and definitive articles. The preliminary articles are conditions that states can and should satisfy immediately — no peace treaty concluded with a secret reservation about a future war, no acquisition of a state by another through inheritance or purchase, no taking on of national debt to fund wars, no interference by one state in another's internal affairs, and no acts of wartime hostility, like inciting treason, that would make trust after the war impossible. Standing armies must be wound down as well. The definitive articles, by contrast, describe what a durable peace requires and can only be approximated over time. The second definitive article says that international law must be based on a federation of free states. Kant does not want a world state because a single global government holding all power would eventually degenerate into despotism; a huge unified state would lose the liberty and liveliness of its parts, and once it slid into tyranny there would no longer be any outside check to pull it back. A federation preserves the independence of states while subjecting their external relations to shared law, and this is Kant's replacement for the balance-of-power politics that he thinks just produces arms races and shifting alliances. On the constitution of states, the first definitive article says the civil constitution of every state must be republican, which for Kant basically means democratic: the people rule themselves, and the more democratic a state is, the more its citizens will hesitate before voting for a war they would have to fight and pay for. Kant anticipates the later claim that democracies don't fight each other. As for the guarantee, Kant's essay closes on a somewhat religious note: perpetual peace is guaranteed by divine providence, which steers human selfishness toward good outcomes, so the establishment of the federation is really God's work unfolding in history. Finally, the third definitive article establishes a cosmopolitan right of universal hospitality, which for Kant includes a right of foreigners to settle and make a home anywhere on earth, since the earth is a sphere and all its peoples are ultimately one community. One can see here the outline of Kant's later cosmopolitanism, with its enthusiasm for the spread of European commerce and settlement as a force drawing humanity together."
