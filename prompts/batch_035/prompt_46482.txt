so i've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for like 15 years at this point, ever since I first ran into Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery as a teenager, and there's one fight I've never seen actually play out well anywhere: superrationality versus plain ol causal decision theory. Every time it comes up on a forum it goes the same way — someone says "if the reasoning is symmetric everyone lands on the same answer, so pick the cooperative one," someone else says "the other players are not copies of you, touch grass," and then it's fifty replies of one-boxers vs two-boxers talking past each other. I want the real version of this fight. Write me a dialogue.

Setup (use these rules, don't worry about matching Hofstadter's exact column): a magazine runs a lottery with a fixed $1 million prize, split evenly among everyone who enters, entry is free, and here's the wrinkle — you can submit as many entries as you like, but the pot doesn't grow, so every extra entry dilutes everyone's share including your own. Two characters, both longtime readers of this magazine, sitting at their kitchen tables with the entry form in front of them, on the phone with each other. One is a Hofstadter-style superrationalist. The other is a causal decision theorist who has actually read the superrationality stuff and remains unconvinced — they're not a philistine, they just think it's a mistake.

Constraints, because this is the part nobody gets right:

- Both characters have to be genuinely sharp. No strawman CDTer who just repeats "correlation isn't causation" until the superrationalist wins by vibes, and no strawman superrationalist who's basically a mystic gesturing at "the universal harmony of reasoners."
- The dialogue needs to hit these beats, in roughly this order: (1) the symmetry argument laid out in its strongest form, (2) the CDTer's reply that this is just an expected value calculation over the actual population of magazine readers, most of whom have never heard of any of this, (3) the superrationalist reaching for the one-boxing analogy and trying to stretch it to cover this case, (4) the CDTer explaining where they think the analogy snaps — there's no predictor with a track record here, so what exactly is the correlation resting on — (5) the superrationalist making the move that the *relevant* population isn't all readers but the subset who read Hofstadter's column and are, right now, going through "the same" reasoning, and the CDTer has to engage with that move, not wave it off as cheating.
- At least one of them needs to raise the thing I basically never see raised: entry counts. Fine, "everyone enters" — but everyone entering *once* is itself a coordination act. The pot only survives if nobody submits a second entry, and the reasoning that says "enter iff the others enter" says nothing about how many times. Where does "exactly once" come from? Let them actually chew on this, neither should resolve it in a single slick sentence.
- The CDTer gets to make the strongest real point on their side, which is something like: superrationality quietly assumes a population of near-clones running your decision procedure, that premise is empirically false about actual magazine readers, and the whole framework only looks good because it was tested on idealized populations. The superrationalist must answer the strongest version, not a watered down one.
- Casual register, they know each other, they've had this exact argument before and it always ends with someone hanging up annoyed. No moderator, no narrator, just the phone call.
- Do NOT hand me a winner. End unresolved, but with both of them having actually moved — the superrationalist conceding where the symmetry assumption needs empirical backup it doesn't have, the CDTer conceding that straight CDT gives a strange answer in this specific case and that the strangeness needs explaining rather than just swallowing.

Length: whatever it takes, but if the dialogue comes in under ~1200 words someone got shortchanged. And a bonus paragraph afterwards, outside the dialogue, on whether updateless / functional decision theory actually dissolves this fight or just relabels one side of it — but only write it if the dialogue leaves that question genuinely open, which it should.
