Write a blank-verse dramatic monologue, 18–24 lines, in the voice of the global workspace itself — the broadcast system that, on the Baars/Dehaene picture, is what makes information conscious. The speaker should be vainglorious about its job: it alone decides which coalition ignites and wins the system's full bandwidth, while the specialists it never broadcasts — the syntax parsers, the retinotopic maps, the cerebellum's silent bookkeeping — grind away in the dark, doing real work that never "makes it on." Let it also be stung by a heckler's charge it cannot quite answer from the stage: that there is no theater and no broadcast at all, only coalitions winning and losing and the illusion of a central show. In the final third, the monologue should turn on the one question the speaker cannot settle from inside: whether being globally broadcast is the same as being felt — whether access is all there is — or whether the phenomenal is a kind of lighting the speaker merely assumes is on. End not with an answer but with the microphone live and nothing certain on the other side of it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who works on workspace theory would recognize each beat — ignition, winner-take-all competition, the audience of unconscious processors, the zombie systems — with the hard problem appearing only as what the broadcast cannot carry.
