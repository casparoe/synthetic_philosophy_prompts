I work as a lab technician in a synthetic biology group, and last week two of the graduate students — Maya, who does tissue engineering, and Josh, who joined the program from philosophy of biology — spent an entire lunch break arguing about how to describe the function of an organ we are developing: an implantable gas-exchange device built from cultured cells, intended to supplement failing lungs. I could not follow all of it, and I would like a careful verdict on each step. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Maya said the device's function is to oxygenate blood, full stop: that is what it is for, that is why everyone calls it an artificial lung, and that is what the clinical protocols need to refer to — when it underperforms, the patient has a failing organ, and the monitoring targets are all defined in terms of the function it is supposed to perform.

(2) Josh said that in the strict sense she is wrong: biological functions are inherited from a history of selection, and this device has no evolutionary history at all. In the strict sense it therefore has no function — only a use, namely the use we put it to. We can call that a function if we like, but it is a courtesy extension of the term, not the real thing.

(3) Maya answered that his criterion has an absurd consequence: the very first organism in a lineage to develop a novel organ — say the first light-sensitive patch, or the first proto-feather — would have had a functioning organ with no function until many generations of selection had passed. On his view a brand-new trait cannot be functioning at the moment it appears; it seems crazy to say that function arrives only retroactively.

(4) Josh said yes, exactly, that is his view, and it is not crazy: the function accrues when selection gets to work, and until then the trait is merely present and doing things. He then added that the device can in fact have a function even on his own account, because selection histories include intentional ones: the engineers kept this design over alternatives precisely because of how well it exchanged gas, so there is a genuine selection history here, just an artificial and brief one. The function is whatever the design-and-testing process favored.

(5) Maya called that a rescue that costs more than it saves. Suppose, she said, the original design brief had been for a drug-delivery scaffold, and the engineers kept iterating on it only after discovering that it exchanged gas unusually well. On Josh's account, the function would be fixed by whatever the design process favored — yet if you asked any clinician what the implanted organ is for, "drug delivery" would be flat wrong and "oxygenating blood" obviously right. A theory that vindicates the wrong answer in the clinic is not a refinement; it is a mistake.

(6) Josh replied that she was trading on an equivocation: what the organ is for in the clinic picks out the role it plays in the body, and of course that matters for treatment — but role is not function. Function-talk earns its keep in explanations: why the organ has its structure, why it was built, what future revisions will be selected for. For that, the history is everything, while the organ's current effects explain nothing about why the device exists at all. The clinical question and the explanatory question come apart, and his account answers the second one; hers cannot even address it.

They eventually went back to work, and I honestly could not tell who had the better of it. Please go through the six moves in order: say for each whether the speaker is right, wrong, or partly right, and justify the rating briefly. Then tell me, if you can, what — if anything — both of them missed.
