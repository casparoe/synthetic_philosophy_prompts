I am a novelist, and I would be grateful for your help with a philosophical knot that is holding up the third act of the book I am writing. My protagonist is a civil servant who has spent fifteen years working on long-horizon climate policy, and she is by conviction a utilitarian: she believes the right act is the one with the best consequences, all things considered. In the middle of the novel she reads the philosophical literature on what has come to be called consequentialist cluelessness, and she realises with something like vertigo that the argument applies to her own work in full force: the direct effects of a carbon price, say, appear to be swamped by the indirect and reverberating effects on technological trajectories, political coalitions, and the lives of people not yet born, and nobody can say in which direction the balance tips. I want her crisis to be philosophically honest rather than a strawman, so I have been reading the debate myself, going back to James Lenman's paper in Philosophy and Public Affairs in 2000 and Hilary Greaves's later treatment, and what strikes me is that the argument appears to have stalled: the same moves are made on both sides, everyone grants the same facts about our predictive powers, and nobody changes position.

So that I can write her arc truthfully, I need a diagnosis of the stall rather than a verdict on it. One side in this debate insists that cluelessness is nothing new: deliberating without knowing outcomes is just decision-making under uncertainty, which expected utility theory was built for, and the cluelessness theorist is merely discovering risk. A second side replies that there is a distinct phenomenon here, sometimes called complex cluelessness, in which we have no basis even for the probabilities — not because the problem is hard, but because our attempts to weigh the ripple effects in different directions run out. A third side grants the phenomenon but says it shows only that consequentialism is a bad decision procedure while remaining true as a standard of rightness, so the utilitarian can keep her ethics and just accept that she must act on some rougher heuristic. And a fourth treats the whole thing as a refutation: a criterion of rightness that cannot guide anyone to right acts is a criterion worth nothing. To make my protagonist's reflections concrete, I have had her distinguish three kinds of not-knowing, and I would like to know whether the philosophical parties actually map onto them: not knowing which of a known menu of outcomes will occur; not knowing the menu itself, that is, not knowing which causal channels there even are for our acts to travel down; and knowing the channels but having no way to weigh them against one another. My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis, is that the sides are answering different questions under one heading — some talking about what makes acts right, some about what a deliberator can know, some about what makes a life of practical commitment coherent — and that this is why the argument never seems to land. But it may instead be a substantive disagreement hiding inside decision theory, perhaps about whether expected utility reasoning even applies where there is no principled partition of the possible futures, or a dispute about how much of morality must be visible to the moral agent at all.

Please tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, engaging with what each side actually claims; whether my character's threefold distinction does any real work or merely relabels the problem; and what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing and why. Finally, since it bears on how the novel ends, tell me plainly whether her crisis is a genuine discovery that something is wrong with her ethics, or the discovery that she was demanding of her ethics something it never promised to provide. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
