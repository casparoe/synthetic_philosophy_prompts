Two things about how my colleagues and I talk about evidence have been bothering me, and I want to know whether epistemologists have names for them, and whether they are in fact one phenomenon or two.

I am a structural engineer. The first thing I've noticed is that when the stakes are low, we say we know things on thin evidence. If a preliminary calculation suggests a beam is generously oversized, someone will say they know it will hold, initial the drawing, and move on. When the stakes are high — a stadium roof, a school — the same kind of calculation, the same margin of safety, no longer licenses the same claim, and everyone insists on another round of checks before anyone is willing to say they know it will hold. No one received new information in between; what changed is what we are about to do with the belief. And it does not feel like mere policy, like demanding a bigger safety buffer. It is framed, by us, as a change in what we are entitled to assert and to believe: whether we know seems to depend partly on the cost of being wrong.

The second thing is subtly different. The very same belief appears to change its status depending on who is asking. Over coffee I will say I know the retrofit is sound, and nobody blinks. Put me under oath, or in front of the client's risk consultant, and the identical evidence base seems to license only hedged phrasing — that our assessment is that it is sound, that we are confident it will hold. Here it is not my situation that has changed; the bridge, my evidence, and my exposure are all the same. What has changed is the conversational setting and, perhaps, the standards of whoever I am answering to.

So, three questions. First, is there an established term for the idea in the first observation — that whether a person knows something can depend on practical stakes rather than on evidence and truth alone? Second, are the two observations the same phenomenon, or do philosophers treat them differently, given that in the first case what I know seems to vary with my own situation while in the second it seems to vary with the context of the knowledge attribution itself? Third, and most importantly, how exactly does the field draw the boundary here — where do the nearest established positions part ways, and which of them best captures what happens to an engineer between the coffee room and the witness stand? I am not after a bare label; I want the carving, including the cases near the seam where the positions give different verdicts about one and the same utterance.
