My roommate Dev and my friend Maya got into it last week after a quantum mechanics study session and I couldn't referee it, so I'm passing it to you.

It started when Dev mentioned many-worlds — the idea that the wavefunction never collapses and every quantum outcome happens in some branch. Maya said that can't be right, because quantum mechanics is all about probabilities, and probability seems to presuppose that some things don't happen. If you tell me an atom has a 30% chance of decaying in the next minute, she said, that means something like "it decays in about 30% of the cases where things are set up like this, and not in the rest." But in many-worlds the atom decays in some branch, period. Nothing is ruled out. So "30% chance" is either trivially 100% (decays somewhere) or it's just words.

Dev answered that the uncertainty is about which branch *you* end up in. Before you look at the detector, you don't know whether you're about to become the person who sees the decay or the person who doesn't, and that not-knowing is exactly what probability has always been for. He bets accordingly: offered a payoff if the atom decays, he takes odds around 30/70 like anyone else.

Maya said that's where it falls apart. Suppose you try to justify the 30% by counting branches — betting on decay because it comes out in 3 of every 10 branches. Branches don't come in countable units: any branch can be redescribed as two half-size branches, so how finely you slice them changes the count, and you can get any "probability" you want that way.

Dev said fine, forget counting branches, the way to do it is decision theory: if every copy of you is going to exist and you're rational, your preferences over gambles have to obey certain coherence axioms, and the argument is that those axioms force you to weight branches by their Born weights — the actual 30% — and not by anything else you might pick.

Maya's reply was that this smells circular. The axioms are supposed to be about pure rationality, she said, but one of them effectively bakes in caring about a branch in proportion to its amplitude, and if you don't already think amplitude is the right measure, the theorem just hands your own assumption back to you. And behind all of it is the question Dev keeps dodging: who is "you" going to be, when all of the successors exist, remember being you, and are equally real? Asking "which branch will I end up in" seems to presuppose there's one answer when the theory says there are many.

Dev's last word was that the question isn't which one is *the* future you, it's which one is *this* person, self-locating uncertainty, like not knowing what time it is — and that plenty of serious people think the decision-theoretic route is fine and the circularity charge is overblown. Maya rolled her eyes at that.
