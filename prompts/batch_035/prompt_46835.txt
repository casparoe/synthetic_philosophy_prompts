My closest friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a plan I have for next week's seminar can possibly work, and I want an outside view before I commit to it. I teach a second-year course called Self and Society -- twenty students, one seventy-five-minute session -- and I want to run a structured game in the session before we read the lordship-and-bondage passage from the Phenomenology of Spirit, so that students collide with the problem before they have a name for it. My friend's position is that the whole exercise is a category error: recognition isn't an incentive, so any game with a payoff scheme will collapse into a status contest or a popularity contest and merely decorate the vocabulary without ever generating the asymmetry that matters. I think there is a version in which the rules themselves produce the asymmetry, and I want to know whether I'm right before I spend a class period finding out.

Here is what the game must do, concretely. Participants on one side should discover that they can compel any behavior they like from players on the other side except the one thing their score actually depends on. And the players being compelled should, through the work the rules assign them, end up holding something the first side cannot obtain at all -- not because it is expensive, but because the way they got it structurally closes it off to the others. I don't care whether you call the roles masters and bondservants or something completely bland; the students shouldn't hear Hegel's vocabulary until the debrief. Please give me complete rules, the roles, materials I can carry in a bag, a payoff scheme in points or tokens with no real money, and a rough minute-by-minute timeline.

For the debrief, map specific moments in what actually happened onto parts of the dialectic -- the initial struggle, the master's impasse, the bondservant's formation of self through work. In doing that, separate what is genuinely settled among people who know this material from what is contested among them, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. In particular, tell me where the design forces the facilitator to take sides on live interpretive questions: how literally to take the struggle to the death, whether the two figures are meant as individuals or as social positions, and how much of what students will have absorbed secondhand is really Kojeve rather than Hegel. Flag each such choice rather than burying it.

Two final requests. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it mid-session without abandoning the game. And be frank about which feature of the dialectic no game can reproduce: my friend insists the whole thing turns on a change in self-consciousness that no payoff structure can register, and if he is right about that, the debrief should be written so the facilitator supplies that piece openly rather than pretending the game produced it.
