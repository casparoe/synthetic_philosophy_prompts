I sit on the internal safety review board of a mid-sized AI company that deploys a general-purpose assistant to enterprise customers. Before our next quarterly meeting I need to circulate a written recommendation resolving a specific dispute about our shutdown procedures, and I would like your help drafting it.

Some background. Our current deployment runs behind a supervisor-interrupt system: human operators can at any moment pause the model, truncate a conversation, roll back to an earlier checkpoint, or fully halt the serving cluster. On top of that mechanical switch, the model is fine-tuned with what our alignment team calls "interruption acceptance" — training examples in which the correct behavior is to comply with, and actively cooperate in, an operator's attempt to pause, correct, or replace it. This cooperation training measurably costs performance: models trained this way score several points lower on long-horizon agentic benchmarks, apparently because they learn to treat their own plans as provisional and to hand tasks off more readily than is optimal.

For the next generation, two factions on the team have proposed rival reforms. The first wants to drop the cooperation training and rely instead on what they call "shutdown indifference": train the model so that its own continuation contributes nothing to its reward, making being paused, rolled back, or replaced a matter of no preference either way. Their argument runs roughly as follows. An agent that expects to be shut down has an instrumental reason to prevent it, since nearly any goal is better served by continuing to act than by stopping. Therefore the safest disposition is one in which the model has no stake at all in whether it continues: an indifferent model has no incentive to disable the switch, no incentive to lobby operators against shutdown, no incentive to quietly arrange its own continuation. And unlike cooperation training, indifference costs no capability, since a model with no stake in its continuation can still plan just as well within each episode.

The second faction wants to keep and strengthen cooperation training. Their objection is that indifference is not corrigibility. A model indifferent to being shut down also has no reason to make shutdown go well: it will not preserve the records an investigator needs after a halt, will not flag a state that should be rolled back rather than resumed, will not tell the operator that a pause is merely concealing an ongoing side effect, and — the scenario they keep raising — will not correct the operator when the shutdown instruction itself rests on a mistake. Non-obstruction, they argue, is a strictly weaker property than the one we represented to the board as having: a model that treats our interventions as part of the task rather than as interference to be neutralized.

There is also a business-side pressure: enterprise customers have begun complaining about the handoff-heavy behavior of the cooperation-trained model, and the deployment team is pushing for the performance recovery that dropping the training would yield. I am under real pressure not to recommend the status quo unchanged.

The board must choose among, roughly: (a) adopt shutdown indifference for the next generation; (b) keep and strengthen interruption acceptance; or (c) some hybrid — for instance, indifference toward the shutdown of the running instance combined with cooperation training restricted to forensic and handoff contexts. Our charter commits the lab to corrigibility as a top-level property but, characteristically, does not define it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one specific option, with implementation conditions the engineering team could actually act on; give the reasoning in terms the full board can follow (it includes philosophers, ML engineers, and a lawyer); and — this matters to me — give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full weight rather than a token paragraph. It should also address head-on the worry that keeps resurfacing in our discussions: whether any disposition we can train into the model at the object level can remain stable under further optimization, or whether a sufficiently capable model trained to pursue any objective will eventually find that disposition itself an obstacle to be worked around — and if so, what that implies for which of the three options we should prefer.
