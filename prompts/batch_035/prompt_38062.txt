At the last meeting of my metaphysics reading group, two members, Petra and Jonas, spent the better part of two hours arguing about the special composition question — under what conditions do some things compose a further thing — and ended by asking me to referee. I couldn't, and I still can't. Here is the exchange as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Petra argued for unrestricted composition: absolutely any things, however scattered, compose something, so there is an object whose only proper parts are her left shoe and the Eiffel Tower. Her argument was the argument from vagueness. Start with particles arranged as a living human: they compose something. End with particles scattered one to a galaxy: they compose nothing. Now move continuously from the first arrangement to the second, one particle at a time. If composition is restricted, it must switch off somewhere; and wherever it switches off, two arrangements differing by a single particle straddle the cutoff, so that reality contains a perfectly sharp line with no qualitative difference to mark it — which she claimed nobody could believe in. The only escape from universalism, she said, is to let composition be vague, and existence cannot be vague: there cannot be an indeterminate fact about how many things there are.

(2) Jonas replied that Petra's argument conflates vagueness in a word with vagueness in the world. He offered a moderate answer: some things compose something when their joint activity is sufficiently unified — lives, artifacts, whatever metaphysics ends up crowning. And yes, there will be penumbral cases where we cannot tell whether composition occurs. But that is just what a sorites-sensitive predicate looks like, and the standard treatment is epistemicism: a determinate but unknowable boundary. Sharp boundaries in the penumbra of "heap" disturb no one; why should sharp boundaries in the penumbra of composition be metaphysically impossible rather than merely uncomfortable? His view needs no theory of where the line falls, any more than our concept of a heap does.

(3) Petra answered that "heap" gets its sharpness from us: its penumbra reflects the sloppiness of our conventions, and nothing in the world answers to it. But composition purports to answer a question about what exists. If the line between composing and not composing were like the line between heaping and not heaping, then how many objects exist would depend on the contours of our vocabulary, and reality would be settled by where English happens to give out. She pressed the point with counting: on Jonas's view, adding a single electron to a swarm that does not compose anything can bring it about that there are exactly n+1 objects rather than n — a difference in the inventory of the world with no qualitative difference to ground it. Sharpness in "bald" costs nothing because baldness is not existence.

(4) Jonas said the counting point cuts against her too, and harder. On unrestricted composition, for any objects whatever there are all the fusions: the shoe-Tower thing, the thing composed of Caesar's nose and every currently falling snowflake. Ask how many objects are in this room, and her honest answer is an astronomically large number, almost all of them things no science or common sense has any use for; if a metaphysics of composition is tested by delivering sane counts, hers fails more spectacularly than the moderate's. Then he made what he called his real move: the deflationary one. Strip away the composite-talk, and Petra and he agree on absolutely everything about which particles exist and how they are arranged. When she says the shoe-Tower thing exists and he says there is no such object, what — beyond a preference about how to use the quantifier — could their dispute be about? The special composition question looks deep only because we mistook a convention about when to count as one for a discovery about when there is one.

(5) Petra's last word was that the deflationary move proves too much. The mereological nihilist says the desk does not exist — that there are only simples arranged desk-wise. If that dispute is verbal, then it is also verbal whether there is anything besides simples, and verbal whether there are persons — and even the philosopher famous for denying the existence of chairs insisted on a criterion of composition that keeps living things, himself included, on the existing side of the line, which suggests our composition judgments track something we cannot simply write off as bookkeeping. The shoe-Tower case is where substantiveness becomes uncomfortable for her, she conceded; but the alternative is to tell people that whether they exist is a matter of how we choose to talk.

They asked me for a verdict, and I genuinely did not have one. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five steps, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then say who comes out ahead overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them missed.
