My sister has decided views on whether everyday conventions carry genuine moral weight, and over the years I have collected her rulings. Cutting a queue is wrong, even in a nearly empty pharmacy where the person displaced would never notice. Jaywalking across a completely deserted street at 3 a.m. is fine, though jaywalking in daylight, in front of drivers who then brake for her, is wrong. Stiffing a waiter in the United States is wrong even though she regards the American tipping system as itself unjust, while not tipping at all in Japan is fine and tipping there gratuitously is merely gauche. A visitor who keeps shoes on in a Japanese home has done something wrong. And a tourist who jostles for position at a bus stop in a country where queueing is simply not the custom has done nothing wrong. Next month a friend of ours travels in a country where small routine payments to officials — the expediting fee at the border, the customary consideration to the clerk who processes your paperwork — are universal, expected, and relied upon, the officials' effective incomes being set on the assumption of them, and the written rules against them are dead letters nobody treats as live. Our friend, recalling my sister's verdicts on tipping, intends to pay without hesitation. Predict her verdict on the friend's conduct, lay out the rules that fit her track record equally well and what each one predicts here, give your best guess with reasons, and name a further case that would best tell those rules apart.
