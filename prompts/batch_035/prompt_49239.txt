After our reading group discussed the question whether anyone deserves punishment in the basic, retributive sense — suffering imposed because of what the offender did, not for any further good it does — two members got into an argument I couldn't referee. Both accepted that character and choices trace, in the ordinary way, to heredity and environment the offender didn't choose. The exchange went as follows:

(1) Maya said that on those assumptions nobody deserves suffering for its own sake, so the justification of punishment must be entirely forward-looking — deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation — on the model of how we confine someone with a dangerous infection: without supposing he deserves it, and only as far as the risk requires.

(2) Jonah replied that this smuggles punishment back in: a confinement deliberately made unpleasant, and calibrated as a deterrent, is punishment in a medical gown. Deterrence works by threatening something feared, so Maya hasn't abolished desert; she has renamed it.

(3) Maya answered that this confuses the public-facing justification with what is done to the offender. Deterrence is addressed to everyone; the offender is confined for others' protection, about which he has a complaint, but not a claim of injustice — exactly as with quarantine. She conceded that the harm of confinement needs a real justification; it just isn't retribution.

(4) Jonah said desert can't be excised alone: if "she earned it" has no basic-desert content, then praise and grades are mere incentive maintenance, which Maya, a teacher, plainly doesn't believe when she marks a paper — so either she is inconsistent or desert survives.

(5) Maya's last word was that there is a desert worth keeping, tied to reasons-responsiveness — to what a person did, given that she could register and weigh reasons — which licenses praise, gratitude, and trust, but never suffering for its own sake; anything imposed beyond protection and rehabilitation remains wrong.

Please adjudicate the five moves one by one, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing.
