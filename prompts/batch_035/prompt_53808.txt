In a seminar on nuclear strategy I'm sitting in on, one of the participants keeps defending deterrence by way of the game of chicken: two drivers barreling toward each other on a one-lane road, where the crash only happens if someone is irrational or badly miscalculates, so a rational player always swerves. On his telling, as long as leaders on both sides are sane and grasp the stakes, the balance of terror is stable — mutual assured destruction means neither government would ever actually drive into the other, and therefore pushing a crisis hard is a safe move for rational actors. The analogy is doing real work in his argument, and I'd like it audited rather than dismissed. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the car, the swerve, the steering wheel, the spectators — and then work through where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I'd like weighed: chicken is a single simultaneous choice, whereas a crisis is a sequence of moves in which each side signals, misreads, and faces pressure to escalate to prove it isn't swerving; in chicken the collision happens automatically if neither driver turns, while a retaliatory strike depends on human beings deliberately choosing to carry out a threat whose deterrent purpose has already failed; and the Schelling-style device of a threat that leaves something to chance — resting your credibility on accidents and partial loss of control you don't fully govern — seems to have no counterpart on the road at all. Tell me which disanalogies are fatal to his stability conclusion and which are harmless, whether chicken can be patched up or whether the picture itself should be replaced (he has also heard deterrence described as two men standing in a room full of gasoline where either can strike a match, and doesn't see why that's supposed to be worse), and, if you think no everyday analogy can carry this load, say why.
