I teach an upper-level seminar on bargaining and fair division in a PPE program — sixteen students, one 80-minute session, and no lecture time to spare. Design a live negotiation exercise that makes the students feel why the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution part ways, before they ever hear the axioms named.

Structure it in three phases. Phase one should establish a baseline: a symmetric, continuous divide-the-dollar problem on which both solutions coincide and the class converges on an obvious split, so students acquire the confidence that there is a uniquely fair answer to be found. Phase two should disrupt that confidence: you will need to construct the actual payoff sheets yourself — a menu of discrete joint alternatives with a specified disagreement point, kinked or otherwise non-linear in utility space — such that the Nash recommendation (maximize the product of the players' utility gains over the disagreement point) and the Kalai–Smorodinsky recommendation (equalize each player's fraction of the gain to their ideal point) come apart visibly, with one favoring each player. Phase three is the heart of it: unbeknownst to half the room, distribute a revised sheet in which one player's ceiling has risen because a new alternative has been added — one attractive to that player alone, and one that neither solution would actually select. This is the classic construction that pits monotonicity against independence of irrelevant alternatives. The player whose opportunities have expanded should find herself demanding more than she did in phase two, while the other insists that nothing relevant to their bargain has changed. Let them argue it out; do not adjudicate.

The conflict should surface in the negotiation, not in the debrief. The debrief comes afterwards and maps each moment onto its formal object: which changes were changes in the disagreement point (and so move both solutions), which were changes in an ideal point (and so move only Kalai–Smorodinsky), and where the group's own instincts traded monotonicity against independence of irrelevant alternatives. Only then name the axioms and the two solutions.

Practicalities: pair assignments, roles, printed payoff sheets with the numbers you compute (show your arithmetic so I can check it against the two solutions' formulas), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff scheme that uses no money — points, candy, or raffle tickets — but still gives the players something to lose. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be frank about the feature of the formal theory the exercise cannot reproduce and I must supply by fiat: the solutions describe a single benevolent arbitrator computing over cardinal, interpersonally non-comparable utilities, not two people talking to each other, and no classroom mechanic will change that.

Finally, since the whole design rests on bets about how students will behave at each phase, close with a note on which of those bets you are least confident in. Say what you would expect to observe in a pilot run that would indicate the core mechanic is failing — for instance, if the phase-three demand shift simply does not appear — what you would redesign in response, and what observation would instead vindicate keeping the design as it stands. Tell me, in effect, what would change your mind about your own activity.
