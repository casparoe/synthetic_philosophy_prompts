I'm a machine learning engineer who works on interpretability and training-diagnostics, fluent in the standard vocabulary of the field — objectives and loss landscapes, inductive bias, spurious correlations and shortcut learning, distribution shift, pretraining and transfer, ablation and retraining, seeds and ensembles — but whose biology comes entirely from popular science. I keep running into a pattern in my own work: when a colleague discovers that a network uses some feature, the default assumption is that the feature must be helping the objective somehow, and if the first story about how doesn't pan out, a second story is invented rather than the usefulness of the feature being questioned. Someone told me evolutionary biologists had exactly this fight around a famous 1979 paper by Gould and Lewontin, "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm: A Critique of the Adaptationist Programme," and that the fight never fully ended.

Please restate that debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Cover, at minimum: what the adaptationist programme amounts to if "fitness" is read as "the training objective" and "the phenotype" as "the learned solution"; what a spandrel becomes if it is read as a feature that is a byproduct of the architecture and optimization process rather than something selection/gradient descent is directly rewarding; what exaptation becomes if it is read as a feature that formed for one purpose being repurposed by later training or fine-tuning for another; and how the adaptationists' favorite kind of evidence — the same solution appearing independently in separate lineages — looks if it is read as different seeds or different architectures converging on the same feature.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Sort every claim in the debate into three bins: translates cleanly; translates only with distortion (say what had to be bent); and has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. In particular I want you to address three candidate residues I've been chewing on, though you should add your own. First, biologists cannot ablate a trait and retrain the lineage; we can intervene on our systems at will, and I suspect some of their arguments exist only to compensate for that missing experimental access — if so, parts of their dispute may dissolve under our tools rather than persist. Second, in my world the objective function is fixed by a designer and the training distribution is an artifact, whereas in theirs nobody wrote the objective and the "distribution" is open-ended; I can't tell whether that difference is what they mean by "constraint," or whether their constraints are claims about the space of reachable solutions that my notion of inductive bias does not capture. Third, a trained network is a passive function, while an organism behaves, chooses, and modifies its own environment; whether that asymmetry matters for the translation is exactly the kind of thing I can't see from inside my framework.

Finally, tell me plainly what you think the residue shows: is it a blind spot in my vocabulary that the biologists can see and I can't, or does it reveal that some of what they were fighting about was less substantive than it looked? Do not assume the answer flatters either side.

Epistemic request: end with a short section saying what would change your mind about the residue report — which consideration or result would move a piece from the "no counterpart" bin into "translates with distortion," or vice versa — and identify the single step of the translation you are least confident in and why.
