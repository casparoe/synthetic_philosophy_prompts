For some time I've followed the project of abolishing suffering through biotechnology, as defended by David Pearce under the banner of The Hedonistic Imperative. Whenever skeptics raise the familiar objection that a world engineered to be free of suffering would be flat or unmotivating — that we need negative states to want anything, to care about anything, or to be able to tell better lives from worse ones — the abolitionists almost never concede the point. Instead they reply that a post-suffering world would not be uniform, because we could live by gradients of bliss.

I'm confident there is something to this reply, but I've never seen it spelled out, and I suspect the phrase is doing several jobs at once that should be pulled apart. Take the most recent exchange I witnessed: someone argued that if no state of a mind can fall below some neutral baseline, then nothing within the mind can function as a signal of getting worse, and a motivational system needs signals of getting worse to steer by. The abolitionist answered with the slogan and moved on, which felt like a change of subject rather than an answer — or possibly a real answer whose content I'm missing.

Please lay out, as a structured menu, the distinct precise theses that gradients of bliss could be expressing, ordered from the most modest to the most radical. For each reading, I'd like three things: a statement of the claim; a verdict on whether it is plausible, and on what grounds; and a verdict on whether it would actually answer the skeptic's objection as stated, rather than merely softening it. At least consider readings along the following lines, and add others you think are in the mix: (1) a merely verbal point, that 'up' and 'down' relative to a raised baseline are still ups and downs, so nothing about motivation follows from the sign of the states at all; (2) an empirical psychological claim, that motivation is driven by the magnitude and direction of change in hedonic tone, not by its sign, so a system that ranges from mildly pleasant to extremely pleasant still contains gradients capable of steering behavior; (3) a stronger informational claim, that the machinery of wanting and acting can in principle be decoupled entirely from hedonic tone, so even a completely flat mind could be motivated, which would make the slogan irrelevant to the skeptic's challenge as I framed it above; and (4) a normative or aesthetic claim, that a life whose every moment is positive but uneven is not diminished for lacking the negative contrasts we currently rely on to give our experiences structure.

Close by telling me, first, which reading or combination the abolitionist probably intended, given the work the phrase was being made to do in that exchange, and second, which reading you would actually defend, distinguishing carefully between what is plausible, what is a promissory note about rewired minds that no evidence currently settles, and what is false. I would rather have honest verdicts, including that the slogan was doing less work than its users suppose, than a charitable reconstruction that saves it by making it trivial.
