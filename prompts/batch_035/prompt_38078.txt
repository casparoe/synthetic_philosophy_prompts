I've been a regular on the rationalist blogs and forums for a long time now — long enough to have read the Sequences-era decision theory posts, the recurring arguments about utility functions, and the periodic flare-ups over whether a real agent can even have one. So I assumed I had the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization straight in my head. Then I ran across a summary of the position in a recent forum post. I won't name the author, and I've lightly smoothed the wording, because the phrasing is close to things I've seen in several places over the years, so please treat it as representative of a genre rather than as one person's slip. Here is the summary:

"Rationality, on this view, is just consistency, and the mathematics has been settled since the 1940s: von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that any agent whose preferences are even minimally consistent — transitive, plus a couple of bookkeeping axioms — behaves exactly as if it were maximizing expected utility. The converse direction is the Dutch book argument: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, or your choices among bets violate the expected utility axioms, a bookie who can see your preferences can construct a series of bets, each of which you will accept, that leaves you strictly worse off in every possible outcome. Putting the two results together: any agent that is not an expected utility maximizer is exploitable in principle. And since markets are full of agents looking for free money, an exploitable agent does not stay merely irrational for long — it leaves money on the table, someone comes along and collects it, and enough interactions like that will eventually bankrupt it. That is why coherence theorists say an agent that fails to weight outcomes by probability times utility is not imperfectly rational but incoherent — inconsistent in the same sense as simultaneously asserting a proposition and its negation."

I'm inclined to accept the bottom line; I've internalized the idea that a rational agent is an expected utility maximizer, and I'd like the summary to be basically fine. But a couple of phrases felt off even to me — especially the slide from a bookie's constructed sequence of bets to "enough interactions like that will eventually bankrupt it," which seems to quietly change who the counterparty is. The trouble is that I can't locate the errors reliably myself, because the summary is close to things I've half-believed for a decade.
