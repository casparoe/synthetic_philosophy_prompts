In my philosophy of physics reading group we have spent two meetings on quantum immortality, and a friend of mine has an argument I cannot dismiss, largely because the two refutations I built against it failed for instructive reasons. I would like to know which step, if any, to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

His argument: (1) On the Everett interpretation, every outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized on some branch; branches differ in weight, but the physics does not single out any of them as the one I will experience. (2) In any situation that threatens my death — a firing squad, a diagnosis, plain old age — there exist branches on which I survive: the bullets miss, the tumor regresses, the neurons hold out one more year. (3) Afterward, my future experiences simply are the experiences of the branch-continuants who remember being me, and I can only find myself where there is someone to do the finding; the dead branches contain no experiences of mine. (4) So I should expect, with subjective certainty, to keep finding myself alive: from the inside, I am immortal. He appends a corollary: quantum Russian roulette, with the trigger wired to a quantum random number generator and a fortune paid to any survivor, is a sure thing from the inside, and declining it is irrational.

My first refutation: (5) the reasoning never uses lethality; it requires only that some branch contain an observer continuing my stream of experience, and that condition holds for every threat to consciousness — there are branches where the anesthetic fails, branches where I do not fall asleep tonight; (6) so the argument predicts I should never lose consciousness at all; (7) but I have been under general anesthesia, and what I got was a gap — counting backward, then the recovery room — which is exactly what my friend says death would be like from the inside. So the parallel does not refute him; it merely confirms that "you will never experience being unconscious" is trivially true and predicts nothing.

My second: (8) if conditioning on my own existence is licensed, it seems I may equally condition on whatever else I like — survival undamaged, survival wealthy, survival painless — since branches satisfying those conditions also exist; (9) but then I should expect whatever happens on some branch, which abolishes expectation altogether, and my own record refutes it: I get headaches, I lose arguments, and I have never once seen a macroscopic event break in my favor against its Born weight. My friend replies that death is special because the conditioning there is forced rather than optional: I do not get to condition on health unless the unhealthy branches contain no observers, and decrepit survival still contains an observer — an observer, anyway. (10) Yet if the survivor at 150 is demented enough, it is not clear there is any me left to find myself as, in which case the guarantee extends only up to the last flicker of psychological continuity: an immortality that terminates, and terminates at the worst available moment.

So which step do I give up? Rejecting (3) looks like it costs the Everettian the standard story of self-locating probability. Rejecting the slide inside (4) — from "all my future experiences will be survival-experiences" to "I should expect to survive" — seems to require a principled account of why Born weights govern ordinary bets but go silent exactly when my existence is at stake; the decision-theoretic derivations of the Born rule (Deutsch's, Wallace's) were supposed to deliver such weights, and I do not see them licensing step (4). Accepting everything leaves me playing his roulette. I would like to keep (1); nearly everything else is negotiable. Tell me where the argument is weakest, and what I pay for breaking it there.
