I'm teaching a comparative epistemology course next term and one of my units is on the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, roughly: "non-one-sidedness" — the claim that reality is manifold and every single proposition about it captures only one aspect, so any flat assertion like "the pot IS" is at best true from some standpoint, in some respect. The Jains packaged this as the sevenfold predication, sapta-bhaṅgī: for any claim, you can say (in some respect) it is; (in some respect) it is not; (in some respect) it is and is not; (in some respect) it is inexpressible; and then three combos stacking "inexpressible" onto the earlier ones. The "in some respect" part (syāt) is doing the philosophical work — it's not "maybe," it's "from this standpoint, given this angle." The classic image is the blind men and the elephant, which is actually a Jain story originally.

For the film unit I want to use Twelve Angry Men (the 1957 Lumet film, not the play, though they're close enough), and I want a reading of it through this doctrine. Here's the tension I keep hitting and I'd like the essay to really wrestle with it, not paper over it: the film LOOKS like a perfect anekāntavāda parable — twelve jurors, each initially absolutist about "he did it" or "he didn't," and Juror 8 slowly dismantles each one-sided certainty (the knife, the old man's walk to the door, the woman's glasses, the kid's memory of the movie) by showing that each "definately established" fact was only true in some respect, from some standpoint, under some assumption the juror didn't know they were making. So far so Jain. But the film's actual epistemology at the end seems to be the opposite of many-sided: it converges on ONE true verdict, reached by eliminating the standpoints one by one. And Juror 3, the holdout, isn't vindicated as holding a genuine partial perspective — his side of it is that his own son hurt him, and the film treats that not as one more face of the elephant but as bias, i.e. as noise to be cleared away.

So my questions for the reading:

1. Does anekāntavāda illuminate the film better than the usual reading (Anglo-Saxon "reasonable doubt + civic virtue"), or does it get forced? Where does the mapping genuinely work, and where should the honest critic admit the film is doing plain empirical verification, which a Jain would say is fine but is not the same thing as many-sidedness?

2. Is there a reading where Juror 3's testimony IS a standpoint with its own syāt-true content — e.g. "in some respect, from the standpoint of a father who's been hurt, this kind of boy IS dangerous" — and if so, what does the jury do with a truth like that? The film can't quite answer, and I want the essay to sit in that discomfort instead of resolving it too fast.

3. Would a Jain philosopher actually say the jurors at the START were closer to wisdom than at the end? Or is the progression from flat assertion ("he's guilty, no question") to stand-point-indexed claims ("from what this witness COULD have seen through the el train, the identification is unreliable") exactly the move from one-sidedness to conditioned truth that the doctrine wants? I genuinely can't tell which way this cuts and I'd like your view.

A few constraints: the students will have watched the film recently so you can refer to scenes by what happens in them, but no pretending to quote dialogue word for word. No bibliography or citations — just the reading itself. And please be frank about the points where the film resists the Jain lens rather than quietly bending the doctrine to fit. If it helps, end with what you think the film adds to the doctrine that a philosophical article on anekāntavāda couldn't give students on its own — I have a hunch it's something about how painful and slow the move from one-sidedness to conditioned truth actually looks in a room, but tell me if you think thats wrong.
