My thesis advisor and I keep circling a version of the moral luck problem and can't locate our real disagreement. Consider the proposition: there exists at least one pair of agents who differ in how blameworthy they are, where the difference is entirely attributable to factors beyond either agent's control. My credence in this is around 0.7; hers is around 0.25. Give me your own credence, but more importantly decompose the question: assign a rough probability to whether the control principle — that moral assessment should be sensitive only to what lies within an agent's control — is a genuine constraint on blameworthiness or merely a defeasible starting intuition; to whether circumstantial luck cases, such as the negligent driver who kills a child only because a child happened to run into the road, survive the revisionist reply that blame properly tracks the risk the driver accepted rather than the harm that materialized; to whether the intuition pulling us toward blaming the unlucky driver more tracks something real but distinct from blameworthiness itself, such as obligations of repair or the moral remainder of harm done; and to whether constitutive luck, where the character from which one acts is itself beyond one's control, is different in kind from resultant and circumstantial luck or just a more vivid instance of the same problem. Include any further terms you think belong in the decomposition. For each term, give a number and a one-line justification, then show how they combine into your overall credence. Finally, tell me which term most plausibly explains the gap between our two numbers, and what case or argument would move your credence the most in either direction.
