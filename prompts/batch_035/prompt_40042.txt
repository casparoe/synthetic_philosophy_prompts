I'm a PhD student in an STS-adjacent philosophy program, finishing a paper on technological determinism, and my advisor has asked me to run a workshop session where participants figure out what they actually believe about the question posed by Langdon Winner's famous 1980 Daedalus article on whether artifacts have politics — but without letting anyone hide behind school labels like "constructivist" or "determinist." Please act as an expert in philosophy of technology who has taught both Winner and his critics, and help me build a decision tree that sorts a participant from a starting commitment to a named position.

Here is the structure I need. The tree should start from a single commitment everyone at the workshop can be assumed to hold (something like: the design and deployment of technologies shape social life in ways worth calling political) and then branch at six to eight internal nodes. Each node must be a question answerable without knowing any position's name — preferably a concrete case: the famous Robert Moses low overpasses that Winner argued were shaped to keep buses (and so poorer, largely Black riders) off the parkways; the claim that nuclear power plants, by their very architecture, demand centralized, quasi-military authority regardless of who designs them; a modern case like a spam filter or a hiring algorithm whose thresholds embed choices about who is disadvantaged; and the observation that a technology like barbed wire was developed for one purpose and ended up restructuring land tenure and colonial borders. Phrase the branches so they turn on real splits: whether an artifact's politics can be intrinsic to it (given fixed function) or only derivative from the intentions of designers and the flexibility of use; whether contingency in a technology's development undermines attributions of politics to the artifact itself; whether some technologies render certain social arrangements effectively impossible or merely costly; and whether the right unit of analysis is the artifact, the sociotechnical system, or the design process.

The leaves must include at least: strong or autonomous technology views in the vein of Jacques Ellul; Winner's own position; the liberal instrumentalist view he was attacking (technology as a neutral tool whose politics is entirely in the hands that wield it); social constructivism of the Pinch and Bijker style, which insists design is radically open and politics resides only in the closing of interpretive flexibility; and a critical-theory leaf in the spirit of Andrew Feenberg, plus whatever else the logical space demands. Two requirements from the workshop plan. First, build the tree from the space of possible answer combinations before locating the named positions in it, and explicitly flag any combination nobody defends — say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, be honest about separations your tree cannot achieve: for instance, if your diagnostic cases cannot distinguish a Feenberg-style critical position from a sophisticated reading of Winner, say so rather than papering over it, and note what extra case would do the job. Before writing the tree, brainstorm the candidate splitting questions and briefly explain which you chose and which you discarded and why. Keep the whole thing to roughly the length of a two-page workshop handout, with a short scoring or traversal guide telling a participant how to read their own path through the tree.
