My reading group has spent the better part of a year on group minds and we've officially stalled. We started with Hofstadter's ant fugue dialogue -- the one where the anteater is friends with an ant colony he calls Aunt Hillary, and keeps eating individual ants while insisting he's doing the colony no real harm, since her intelligence lives in the pattern of the colony and not in any particular ant, the same way your mind isn't in any particular neuron. Then we branched into the collective intentionality literature, and what I've noticed is that half the group is basically sitting where Hofstadter's anteater sits: cognition is a property of organization, dumb parts are no barrier at all, so there's no principled reason a colony (or a corporation, or a market) couldn't be a genuine thinker, and maybe even a subject of experience. The other half keeps making what feels like the same objection from a bunch of different directions: the Searle-style point that any intentionality in the system has to bottom out in individual heads, the deflationary line that saying the colony decided something is just compressed bookkeeping for what a bunch of ants did, and the observation that a rock ant colony that picks the better of two nest sites using quorum counts looks like a lovely distributed algorithm and not like anybody's home. And here's the frustrating part: everyone in the group can state the other side's arguments as well as their own, everyone grants the same experiments and the same thought experiments, and nobody has moved an inch in ten months.

My working theory is that we talk past each other because the word mind is doing two jobs nobody separates: one side is answering something like "which systems should we treat as unified information-processors with goals and beliefs" -- a question about where our best explanatory and predictive models live -- and the other is answering "what things could there be something it is like to be," a question about subjecthood. The colony plausibly wins the first and, per the second half of my group, obviously loses the second, but since we all just say mind and cognition we never notice we're answering different things. But I'm honestly not sure that diagnosis is right, because the emergence-friendly people do seem to claim more than modeling convenience (they think the colony-level facts are real, not just useful fictions), and the deflationists seem to claim more than a hunch (they seem to think there's an actual fact of the matter about where subjects stop). So help me figure this out: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive dispute about organizational facts that we just haven't settled, a fight about which cases count as data (ant colonies vs. split-brain patients vs. nation states), or maybe a sign that the question of what owns a mind is just ill-posed? And whatever your diagnosis is, tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis -- some new distinction we haven't drawn, a theory of how to individuate subjects, more empirical work on how integrated colony behavior actually is, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. I'd rather have a structural diagnosis with reasons than a rerun of the arguments we've already worn out on each other.
