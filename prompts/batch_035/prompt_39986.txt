I've been keeping a list of my honest reactions to cases where pleasure and pain have to be weighed against each other, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them — perhaps one on which pleasures and pains are commensurable below some threshold of intensity but not above it — and, if not, which of my verdicts has to go. My verdicts: enduring a nasty head cold for a week to attend my best friend's wedding, clearly worth it. A drug that permanently removes my chronic, gnawing back pain but also permanently flattens my capacity for enjoyment — food, music, and friends all going grey — worth it, and I'd still say so if the enjoyment lost were several times the pain removed. A year of daily severe migraines as the price of a decade of steady, deep contentment: not worth it, somehow, and the longer I sit with it the less willing I am to make the trade. Any amount of ecstatic experience, however long, bought with a single day of torture: no, without hesitation. Painlessly ending the life of someone in a comfortable but pleasureless existence, on the theory that a long pleasureless life leaves them exposed to future aches: monstrous — so avoiding pain can't be all that matters to me. And creating a child you can be confident will have a hard life with real joys in it, say a subsistence farmer's child who will know hunger but also love: I let myself permit this, even though in the migraine case I refused to trade a year of pain for a decade of contentment for a person who already exists. Is there a principle that fits all of these — something like a threshold above which pain cannot be bought off with pleasure, plus a rule about whose trade it may be — or must I give something up? If you can state such a principle, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: someone who volunteers for a month of torture-adjacent hardship to have an experience they have dreamed of all their life, when we would forbid a third party from imposing that same trade on them.
