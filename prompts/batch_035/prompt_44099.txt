I'm taking a third-year course on philosophy of history, and for a 75-minute seminar session with about 18 classmates I have to run a discussion on contingency in historical explanation — the "for want of a nail" cluster of issues, set against structural or materialist accounts in which outcomes are overdetermined by larger forces. Please design a group activity rather than a discussion prompt: I'd like the participants to feel the fragility problem themselves before any theory gets named, so no lecture up front and no course terminology until the debrief.

The ideal design has them running some kind of parallel mini-simulation — a stylized historical scenario played out in several rounds, with one deliberately tiny difference seeded between otherwise identical runs — and then confronting the divergence. Two mechanics I'd like built in, if you can make them work: first, a prediction stage where participants bet in advance on which of the small differences will matter and which will wash out, so they experience their own failure to forecast sensitivity; and second, periodic checkpoints at which the group must vote to declare a run "closed" (the divergence has locked in and further play won't matter) or "still open," which forces them to judge in real time between genuine junctures and mere noise — exactly the judgment historians have to make retrospectively without being able to rerun anything. No real stakes or money; find some payoff scheme that still makes the bets and votes costly to make carelessly.

The debrief should map specific moments in the runs onto the distinctions I actually need them to walk away with: path dependence that locks in, noise that reabsorbs, and outcomes that were overdetermined from the start regardless of the nail — and it should press on what evidence, if any, the players could have used mid-run to tell these apart, since that's the same epistemic problem the historian faces. If it helps set the frame, I'm imagining something like the Gouldian idea of replaying the tape of life, except applied to human affairs where we can actually try the replay.

Please also include: materials and time breakdown, roles if any, and an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, plus which feature of real historical contingency no classroom simulation can reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.
