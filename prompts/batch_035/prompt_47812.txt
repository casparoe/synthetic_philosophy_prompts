At a recent seminar on the ethics of markets, the discussion turned to proposals for legal markets in votes — systems in which citizens could sell their ballots to the highest bidder, or pool them through an intermediary for a share of the proceeds. Someone objected that such markets violate the principle of 'one person, one vote', and when I replied that under a vote market each citizen still receives exactly one ballot and casts exactly one vote — so the principle seems satisfied on its face — I was told I was being obtuse about what the principle really means. Perhaps I was; but I would like to see, laid out explicitly, what I would have to be conceding.

Could you give me a menu of the distinct precise claims that the slogan 'one person, one vote' might be standing for? To start you off, and to indicate the register I have in mind: I believe the phrase originated in the American reapportionment cases of the early 1960s, where it meant something quite narrow — that legislative districts must contain roughly equal populations, so that a vote in a rural county is not worth less than a vote in a city. That narrow reading is plainly compatible with vote markets, since it concerns the weighting of votes across districts, not the circumstances under which a ballot is filled in. From there, the candidate readings run deeper, and I would like you to work through at least the following: a procedural reading, on which each citizen casts one and only one ballot, and each ballot enters the tally exactly once; a non-transferability reading, on which the vote is the citizen's own act in a way that excludes compensated transfer, whatever the mechanics of the ballot; an equal-influence reading, on which citizens must end up with equal substantive political power, not merely equal formal voting rights; and any further readings you can find hiding in the slogan — for instance, ones that concern the meaning or expressive function of voting rather than its causal effect.

For each reading, I would like three verdicts: first, whether it is plausible as a requirement of political legitimacy or justice, or instead a platitude dressed up as a principle; second, whether it is genuinely captured by the phrase 'one person, one vote' at all, or smuggled in under cover of an arithmetic-sounding slogan; and third — the question the seminar actually turned on — whether it rules out vote sales. On the third point, please test each reading against cases most people accept: voting a straight party ticket written by someone else; following a union's or church's instructions at the ballot box; working as a professional lobbyist or speechwriter, which converts political skill into income; and unequal campaign spending, which lets the wealthy amplify their influence without any vote changing hands. A reading that forbids vote sales but also forbids most of these looks too strong to be plausible; a reading that permits them owes an explanation of why compensated transfer of the ballot itself differs in kind. Please present the result as a numbered list I can bring back to the seminar, ordered from weakest to strongest, closing with your judgment of which reading the objector was most likely relying on and whether it survives scrutiny.
