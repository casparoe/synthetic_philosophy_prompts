I am a junior at a public high school, and I am the sole student member of a committee our principal convened to review the names of our four school houses, each named after a historical figure, after a petition circulated to rename one of them. The committee — two teachers, two parents, the school librarian, and me — meets for ninety minutes once a month, no one on it is a historian, and any recommendation goes to the school board, which requires written reasons and votes only at quarterly meetings, so a renaming takes about a year end to end. We cannot outsource the thinking: whatever we adopt has to be run by busy non-specialists in a single meeting with a short homework step at most.

Our debates keep collapsing into what one parent calls the "man of his time" defence, and I have noticed that people use that phrase to mean at least four different claims that we never manage to pull apart: that most of the figure's contemporaries shared the views we now condemn; that the figure could not reasonably have known better, because no better position was genuinely available to someone in his situation; that he was in fact somewhat better than the average of his contemporaries on that very question; and that his moral failings are simply irrelevant to the achievement we named the house for. People also slide between judging a whole person and judging the specific deed or role we are commemorating, and nobody ever asks who bears the burden of checking the empirical part — whether dissenters actually existed in the figure's own time and place, since if they did, "everyone believed it" is not just an excuse but a factual error.

Please write a decision procedure the committee could actually follow: an ordered list of questions, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop with a clear recommendation to keep the name or remove it, and where it must hand the question to the committee as an irreducibly moral judgment rather than pretending the procedure settled it. It should generate the intermediate options people keep ignoring — a contextual plaque, a teaching unit, adding a second name — rather than only rename-or-keep. It should state whose job it is to look up the historical facts and how deep that lookup has to be given our time constraints, and it should be honest about how the procedure could misfire, for instance by treating the mere existence of contemporary dissenters as enough to condemn a figure who never encountered them.

Then please run the procedure on three cases: a house named for Thomas Jefferson; a hallway mural honoring Winston Churchill; and our school's own founder, a local industrialist who paid for the building and endowed the scholarship I currently hold, but whom a student research project last term found on the board of our state's eugenics society in the 1920s. I want to see whether the procedure can honestly reach a verdict on that last case, since I am the person on the committee least likely to accept whatever it concludes.
