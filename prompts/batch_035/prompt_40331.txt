I spent thirty-five years in condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have been working through decision theory and political philosophy, partly because my reading group has taken up the rationality of voting. I have reconstructed what I take to be the standard argument that voting is irrational, and I cannot find my way out of it without paying somewhere I would rather not. It runs as follows.

(1) An act is rational only if its expected benefit exceeds its cost. (2) The only way a single vote can affect an election's outcome is by being decisive — by creating or breaking an exact tie. (3) In a large national election, the probability that a given vote is decisive is of order one over the electorate: call it one in a hundred million. (4) Even valuing the difference between the candidates extravagantly — say, fifty thousand dollars of personal welfare riding on the result — the product of that value and one in a hundred million comes to a twentieth of a cent. (5) Voting has real costs: time, travel, standing in line. (6) So anyone who votes in order to affect the outcome acts irrationally. (7) And since the argument used no feature of elections except that the electorate is large and each individual contribution negligible, it applies equally to a donation to famine relief, to a single emitter's abstention, and to the canvasser who knocks on one more door: none of it can be rational either.

Let me say plainly what I would like to keep. I want to keep (1), which is the maximization principle I used my whole professional life; I would rather give up almost anything else than discover in retirement that expected-value reasoning was the wrong standard for action all along. The escape offered at our group meetings — that people vote for expressive reasons, the way they cheer at a stadium — seems to concede (6) about instrumental rationality rather than answer it, and it does nothing for (7).

Two things trouble me about (3). If I model the electorate as a hundred and fifty million independent fair coins, the probability of an exact tie is not of order one over the electorate but of order one over its square root — one in a few tens of thousands, not one in a hundred million — and with that probability the product in (4) sits in the dollars, which changes the verdict entirely. But I cannot tell whether this shows (3) is false or only that the coin model is hopeless: a real electorate is not a population of fair coins, and I dimly recall that what governs the probability of a near-tie is the spread of the expected vote share, not the size of the electorate alone. My instinct from physics is that a near-tie is like a system poised at a critical point, where the response to a tiny perturbation is enormous; but I do not know whether that analogy is a rebuttal to (4) or merely a physicist's reflex. Nor am I sure whose welfare belongs in the product in (4): one colleague insists on counting everyone's stakes, not just the voter's own, and I can see that this amendment would transform the charity case in (7) as well.

Tell me which premise or step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If you would amend (4) to count stakes other than the voter's own, tell me whether that amendment rescues the famine donation in (7) or whether votes and donations differ in kind. And tell me honestly whether my worry about square roots is a genuine crack in (3) or an artifact of the coin model.
