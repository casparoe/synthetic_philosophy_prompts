(1) I'm an updateless reasoner: I one-box in Newcomb's problem, I pay in the counterfactual mugging, and I think the proper object of rational evaluation is the policy you would have endorsed before learning your situation, not the action that looks best from where you now stand. (2) By the same logic, I think agents heading into negotiations should lock in their bargaining stance early — before they learn whether they will turn out strong or weak — and that committing in advance to something like an even split, regardless of eventual leverage, is the updatelessly rational move; whoever commits first to a firm-but-fair stance shapes the bargaining landscape everyone else faces. (3) At the same time, I take commitment races very seriously: a scenario in which powerful artificial agents (or states, or firms) begin making broad and hard-to-reverse commitments before they understand the strategic landscape, each other's values, or their own relative strength, strikes me as one of the more plausible routes to a catastrophic standoff, and my instinct is that each individual agent has strong reason to slow down and delay committing until it knows more.
