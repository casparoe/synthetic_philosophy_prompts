I'm a PhD student in philosophy, serving as a grader for a second-year course on the ethics and metaphysics of technology. One of the final exam questions concerned virtual objects and virtual harms; below is the question, followed by one student's answer. Please assign the answer a mark out of 20 and give the point-by-point comments that justify the mark — I want the comments specific enough that nothing a quick read would let slide gets let slide.

Exam question: "In a persistent online world, players build and furnish houses on land rented with in-game currency purchased with real money; construction takes dozens of hours. The game's rules permit destroying another player's house once the attacker controls the surrounding territory, but a long-standing unwritten community norm forbids doing so outside of formally declared conflicts. Player A destroyed Player B's house in a way the rules of the game permit but in breach of that norm. B claims a real wrong has been done to him. A replies that nothing real was harmed: the house is just pixels, and its destruction is a change in a database, no more a real harm than capturing a queen in chess is a real act of destruction. Present the strongest version of each side's case, and adjudicate: if a real wrong was done, say in virtue of what it is real."

Student's answer: "A's position is that virtual objects are not real objects. This is the view defended by David Chalmers, who argues that virtual objects are fictional entities, so the house is on a par with the rooms of Baker Street, and when the code stops rendering it, nothing real is destroyed. B's strongest reply is that the house was produced through real work and real money, and that his distress at its loss is a real psychological event. Moreover, the house is a real digital artifact — a structure of stored data — and A really did destroy it. The harm is therefore real in every sense that matters: real resources were consumed, a real artifact was damaged, and real suffering resulted. So the 'just pixels' defense fails, and A's act was wrong. The most that can be conceded to A is that the wrong is relatively minor, since B can rebuild."

Please grade strictly but fairly: the mark should reflect both what the answer gets right and where it goes wrong, and the comments should make the reasoning behind each deduction explicit.
