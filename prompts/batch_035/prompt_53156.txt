I chair the grants committee of a county charitable foundation set up by a Victorian industrialist whose deed requires the income to be spent relieving the poor of this county. Local emergency need is at a historic low, and a trustee proposes redirecting a quarter of next year's grants to a global health fund covering insecticide-treated bed nets and deworming, where each thousand pounds does far more measurable good than anything available to us locally. A minority resists: some because our benefactors gave for this county specifically, some because we know our local applicants personally while the distant beneficiaries are statistics, and one who argues, in Singer's manner, that declining would be like walking past a drowning child to avoid ruining a suit. Please draft the memo I will circulate before our next meeting: it should commit to a definite allocation for next year and a standing policy for future ones, not table the options. It should take the drowning-child analogy seriously but test it where it is weakest — our case is preventive risk-reduction for unidentified people at a distance rather than rescue of an identified person in immediate peril, and we would not exactly be walking past anything, since no one nearby is drowning — and it should say how much weight, if any, the century-old donor intent ought to carry against the trustee's proposal. Write in British English.
