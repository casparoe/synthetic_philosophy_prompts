I'm on the faculty senate of a mid-sized university, and I've been tasked with drafting our recommendation on a grade inflation proposal that has split the body for two years running. The background: our median grade has crept up to something like A-, students openly shop for sections and majors on the basis of expected grades, advisers steer at-risk students toward known lenient graders, and several departments have quietly adopted an unwritten policy of minimum grades (a B- floor for anyone who completes the work) to protect their enrollment numbers. Meanwhile our honors college has started ignoring internal GPA entirely and recomputing applicants' grades against each department's historical distribution, which strikes me as an admission that the official signal is close to dead. I've been reading about multipolar traps and races to the bottom — situations where every actor is behaving rationally given the others' behavior, everyone would prefer the cooperative equilibrium, and yet no one can unilaterally get there — and I've become convinced that grade inflation is a textbook case: no department can deflate alone without losing majors, and any individual professor who grades strictly just imposes costs on her own students without restoring the signal's value. The proposals on the table are (a) mandatory median-grade bands or departmental GPA targets, (b) printing each course's historical grade distribution alongside the grade on the transcript, (c) an institutional curve recentering everyone at once, and (d) doing nothing and letting the honors college's workaround become the de facto system.

Please draft the written recommendation I'll circulate before the May senate meeting. It should take a definite position — pick one option (or a specific combination, or reject all four and propose something better), not lay out the pros and cons and leave the vote to us — and it should argue for that position in terms that my colleagues, who range from skeptical chemists to political theorists, will actually engage with. I'd particularly like the memo to take the multipolar-trap framing seriously rather than treating grade inflation as a matter of individual faculty virtue or student entitlement: that framing, if it's right, has consequences for which remedies can work (e.g., does it follow that only binding, all-at-once changes can move us to the cooperative equilibrium, or can weaker measures like transcript distributions change the payoffs enough to unravel the trap on their own?). It should also confront the strongest objection to whatever you recommend — for the coercive options that they override legitimate departmental and disciplinary differences in what grades mean, and for the informational options that they ratchet up surveillance of teaching and give students even better tools for gaming — and say honestly what would make you reconsider. Aim for something the senate could vote on as written: concrete mechanics, a transition timeline, and an explicit statement of what we'd count as the policy having failed five years out.
