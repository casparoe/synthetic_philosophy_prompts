In my economics class we learned that under the Federal Trade Commission's Cooling-Off Rule, a customer who signs a contract during a door-to-door sales pitch or at a fair booth typically has three business days to cancel for a full refund, no questions asked — while someone who makes the same kind of impulsive purchase in a store or at a midnight online checkout is usually bound immediately. I take it the rule assumes that a choice made under a salesman's pressure, in the moment, doesn't really count as the buyer's decision. What view of rationality does that presuppose? For instance, does it assume that people's preferences are unstable across time, so the law has to decide which moment speaks for the "real" you — and if so, why does the calmer, later slice win, rather than the one that actually signed? I've also read a little about philosophers' "money pump" arguments, where someone's choices are each locally acceptable but, taken in sequence, leave them worse off while another person gains by construction, and it seems like the door-to-door tactics these rules target work by pumping people through exactly that kind of sequence. Does the cooling-off rule therefore presuppose that consumers' choices can be pumped like that, and that the state's job is to break the pump before it starts? Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which competing theories of rational decision-making over time would all license a cooling-off period, so that the rule's existence doesn't tell us which theory the law is actually committed to?
