I'm in a reading group that just worked through Keynes's Treatise on Probability (we skipped most of the technical parts, thank god) and at the next session I have to present where I actually stand on this stuff, so I wrote out my positions and now I'm worried they don't hang together at all. Here's the list. (1) I'm on board with the core logical-probability idea: a probability is an objective relation between a hypothesis and your evidence, not a feeling and not a frequency — so if two people have the same evidence, there's one right probability, and if they disagree, at least one of them is just wrong. (2) I also buy Keynes's claim that some of these relations are non-numerical — the relation is there but it isn't a number, and some pairs of probabilities aren't even comparable. (3) But I think the Ramsey/de Finetti Dutch book argument is basically sound: if your betting rates violate the probability axioms you can be milked, and that's a genuine rationality failure, and betting rates are obviously numerical. (4) I think it's just a fact of life that two experts with the same data can both be rational while giving different probabilities — I work adjacent to insurance and I refuse to believe one out of every two actuaries is irrational. (5) The principle of indifference is broken (carve up the possibilities differently and you get different answers), but I can't see how logical probability delivers any actual numbers without some restricted version of it, so I keep half-endorsing it anyway.

Then there's the later Keynes stuff, which I'm maybe conflating with the Treatise, I honestly don't know. (6) I think the weight-of-evidence idea is right: more evidence can leave your probability exactly the same while making it more solid, and when weight is low I think it can be rational to ignore the expected value calculation entirely and fall back on conventions, staying put, or plain optimism — but I also think expected utility maximization is the backbone of rational decision theory. (6b) And the beauty-contest thing from the General Theory seems right to me too: rational investing under competition is largely a game of second-guessing average opinion, mass psychology and all that, which feels like it's drifting away from anything "logical." (7) Finally, on the famous passage where Keynes shrugs at the prospect of the copper price decades out — I think there just is no probability at all for things like that, no number, nothing, no matter what your evidence is. Maybe that's fine because of (2), maybe it contradicts (1), I can't tell anymore.

Audit this for me before I present. Which of these actually conflict, which only look like they do, and how badly? Rank the conflicts by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad, tell me what the cheapest repair is for each, and say which single revision would clean up the most. I'm not so attached to any one of these that you need to tiptoe.
