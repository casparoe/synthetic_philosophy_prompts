Last Saturday my friend Elena and I spent about five hours — two over dinner, three over increasingly cold tea — arguing about consequentialist cluelessness, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since. I want to ask you the question at the bottom of our disagreement, and I want an answer in a very specific form: a probability with its derivation laid out.

First, so we are on the same page, the two distinctions our argument kept circling. Some decision situations involve what philosophers call simple cluelessness: we are ignorant of the far-future effects of our actions because we lack information, but in principle we could assign rough probabilities if we knew more. Others involve complex cluelessness: we can foresee that an action will have far-future effects of magnitude comparable to its direct effects, but we have systematic reasons to think the effects could go either way — reasons pulling in opposite directions that we cannot convert into a single probability distribution at all, even in principle. The stock example: distributing malaria nets saves identifiable children, but it also changes population growth, carbon output, and the political trajectories of the affected regions, and every attempt to tot up those ripple effects yields considerations pointing in conflicting directions with no honest way to weight them.

Here is where Elena and I ended up, somewhere past midnight. She holds that even in complex cluelessness cases, the consequentialist should simply maximize expected value using her best (admittedly crude) probability estimates, because the alternative — declining to act on any verdict — is decision-theoretically spineless, and because cluelessness afflicts every option equally and so cannot be a difference-maker between them. I kept insisting that this conflates two questions: whether there is an objective fact about which act would produce the better world, and whether the agent's evidence supports a sharp probability assignment over that fact; and that if the second fails, expected-value maximization is not merely hard but ill-defined, so the "subjective ought" must be rescued by some other device — imprecise credences with a tie-breaking rule, or a fallback to the near-term effects we can actually see. She says that fallback is just capitulation to moral uncertainty aversion dressed up as epistemology.

So, the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can: assuming consequentialism is true, in a canonical case of complex cluelessness, the correct account of what the agent subjectively ought to do yields a determinate verdict favoring one option over the others, such that a conscientious agent could be blamed for choosing otherwise.

Give me a number, and then decompose it. I would expect the decomposition to include at least the following, though add or prune terms as you see fit: the probability that there is a well-defined objective betterness ranking of complete world-histories at all; the probability that, given such a ranking, there are determinate probabilities over which available act leads to which history, versus the probability that even idealized agents could never have more than incomparable partial reasons; the probability that sharp credences are the right epistemic model when one's evidence is of this conflicting, systematically canceling kind, versus imprecise credences; the probability that, if credences are imprecise, there is a principled secondary decision rule (a tie-breaker among options that are admissible across the credence set) that consequentialists should endorse; and the probability that the subjective ought, once thus constructed, lines up determinately with one option rather than licensing many. Assign each a rough probability with a line of justification, show how you combine them, and arrive at your bottom line.

Then tell me two more things. First, which single term in your decomposition is doing the most work, and what argument or consideration would move it the most. Second: my credence in the proposition is about 0.35, and Elena's is about 0.8. Given your own decomposition, tell me which sub-question you think the two of us most likely disagree about — and, since I suspect we may not actually disagree about that one but about something upstream of it, tell me whether you think our real dispute is about cluelessness at all, or about whether the subjective ought must always be action-guiding to be an ought.
