so I'm an environmental historian, and I've been trying to wrap my head around something that happened in geology a couple years back. as I understand it (correct me if I've got it wrong), the Anthropocene Working Group spent over a decade building a formal case: they picked plutonium isotopes from hydrogen bomb tests as the marker, picked Crawford Lake in Ontario as the reference section, fixed the start at 1950 — the whole golden-spike apparatus, exactly like any other epoch boundary. and then in 2024 the stratigraphy subcommission voted the proposal down by a wide margin, and the international union of geological sciences confirmed it, while in the same breath saying the term will remain, roughly, an invaluable descriptor of human impact on the earth system. so geology kept the word and refused the epoch.

meanwhile my own field, and ecology, and half the social sciences, happily say "since the Anthropocene began" with start dates all over the place — some people say the industrial revolution, some say early agriculture, some say 1945. nobody in my discipline finds that embarrassing. the geologists apparently found it disqualifying.

here's what I want: not a history of the debate and not a defense of one start date. I want to know what each practice presupposes about what a historical period IS. the stratigraphic one seems to presuppose that a period is a globally synchronous physical fact — that there has to be one layer, in one lake, that everyone can point at, and that "epoch" means something like what it meant when the boundary is diachronous or too recent. is that fair? and what does the loose interdisciplinary usage presuppose instead — that a period is something like a story we tell about causal importance, where "when it began" depends on which consequence you care about? if that's right, then the two camps aren't disagreeing about a date, they're disagreeing about the category of thing an epoch is — and the 2024 vote was really a vote about whether geology's concept of periodization should be lent out to other disciplines. does that reading hold up, or am I overphilosophizing what was partly just turf protection and unhappiness with a 70-year-old "epoch"?

also: tell me where this is actually settled versus genuinely contested. I assume the mechanics of the golden-spike procedure are just fact and not up for debate, but things like whether the geologists' demand for synchrony is a real principle of stratigraphy or an ad hoc bar raised for this one case, or whether the diachronous-boundary objection was the real reason for the vote — those I'd want flagged as contested rather than presented like everyone agrees. and if you think the two usages are just equivocation on the word "Anthropocene" rather than two theories of periodization, say so plainly; I'd rather know if my framing is wrong.
