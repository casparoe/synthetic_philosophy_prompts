I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics, and I've been asked to run a two-hour session of our department's ethics-of-emerging-technology reading group on suffering risks — the idea that future agents, especially misaligned or adversarial AI systems, could produce suffering on a scale that dwarfs anything in human history, whether through deliberate coercion, accidental "mind crime" in large simulations run by systems optimizing for something else, or simply the strategic conflict that breaks out when several powerful agents can't strike enforceable bargains. The group is mixed: a couple of moral philosophers, some political theory people, and a few ML students, so almost nobody arrives with the labels already loaded. What I want is a decision-tree handout (one page, at most five branching questions, ideally eight or nine leaves) that sorts participants onto a position by asking about cases and mechanisms rather than about school names — nobody should ever be asked whether they are a "suffering-focused ethicist," only about things like whether ten billion future beings in permanent torment is worse than or merely different in kind from a clean extinction, or where the expected moral damage of a multipolar AI outcome with imperfect bargaining mostly comes from.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: start with the questions and the answer combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space. The distinctions I want as branch nodes are ones I've found the group genuinely splits on: whether disvaluable experience has lexical or at least superlinear priority over death and non-creation; whether the dominant mechanism of astronomical future suffering is conflict and cooperation failure between roughly equal agents, internal subgoals and simulated minds, or something in the agents' values themselves; whether the right response is qualitatively different from ordinary existential-risk work (speed-up versus caution, instilling cooperative dispositions versus capability restraint) or just a change of emphasis; and whether the expected value of the whole cosmic future is positive or negative once you net the suffering against the value — a question that quietly does enormous work. Leaves should include at least: the view that s-risks are largely continuous with x-risks and best handled by the same alignment agenda; the cooperation-failure diagnosis on which the central task is making future agents pact-worthy rather than safe; the suffering-focused position on which avoiding worst-case outcomes dominates creating best-case ones; the precautionary or decelerationist stance that takes the sign of the whole enterprise as uncertain enough to prefer not running the experiment; and the deflationary view that the entire s-risk framing is an artifact of misplaced decision-theoretic humility. Include the unoccupied combinations too, and diagnose them in a sentence each — e.g., the combination of lexical suffering-priority plus enthusiasm for rapid cosmic expansion, which almost nobody holds, and I'd like to know whether that's coherence, taste, or just lack of imagination.

Two constraints on the diagnostic questions, since they're where the exercise lives or dies. First, each should be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels: pose concrete scenarios (a race between three labs with no verification mechanism; a superintelligence running ancestor-simulations for instrumental reasons; a choice between one future with vast happiness and a smaller risk of vast torment, versus a quieter future with neither) rather than asking for allegiance to doctrines. Second, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — I already suspect it can't cleanly distinguish a negative-leaning utilitarian who supports mainstream AI-safety work for cooperative reasons from a classical utilitarian who merely assigns extreme suffering a very large negative weight, and I'd rather the handout admit that than paper over it. If some branch is under-determined in practice (people refuse to answer it, or answer it inconsistently), say so and suggest what second question would disambiguate. The purpose of the session is that each participant leaves knowing roughly what they believe and what it costs them, not what they should call themselves.
