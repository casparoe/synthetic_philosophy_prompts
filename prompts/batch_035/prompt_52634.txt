I'm a high school junior and I got obsessed with the St. Petersburg paradox for a math essay: the game where a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads and you win 2 to the power of the number of flips, so the expected value is infinite, but nobody would pay more than a few bucks to play. What I can't figure out is why the argument about what *resolves* it has basically gone nowhere in 300 years. Bernoulli said the fix is diminishing marginal utility (the log thing), some people say it's just that no casino could actually pay out infinite money, others say we simply refuse to weight tiny probabilities of huge payouts, and now there's this ergodicity crowd who say expected value is the wrong tool altogether because you can't time-average a single play of a game. I brought this up at math club and we argud for an hour and ended exactly where we started, which seems to be what the professionals do too. So what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it substantive (about what decision theory should actually say), verbal (about what counts as "resolving" a paradox), or are the sides secretly answering different questions under one name, like what a real person should pay vs. what the math of the expectation means? And could anything settle it — a better decision theory, a psychology experiment on what people would really pay, or is the question just ill-posed until you pin down the setup? Diagnose it for me, don't just survey the proposed solutions, and be honest if you think nothing ever will settle it. Please answer in American English.
