I'm a retired pharmacologist -- thirty-odd years in drug metabolism -- and since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy and decision theory to keep the gears turning. My son-in-law is a grad student in decision theory and he showed me a problem that's been eating at me for two weeks. It's called the absent-minded driver problem, from a paper by some economists in the 90s (Piccione and Rubinstein, I believe). Setup: a driver heads home on a highway with two identical exits. He's absent-minded -- at an intersection he can't remember whether he's already passed one, and the two intersections look exactly the same. Exiting at the first gets him a payoff of 0 (long hazardous detour), exiting at the second gets him 4 (home sweet home), and continuing past both gets him 1 (hotel at the end of the road). While he's still at the pub, he can plan, and the natural move is to randomize: at each intersection, continue with probability p. Expected payoff works out to 4p - 3p^2, maximized at p = 2/3, giving 4/3. So far so good -- that's just calculus, I've done harder optimizations on PK curves.

Here's where my son-in-law lost me. He says: now put the driver at an intersection, mid-drive, committed to the 2/3 plan. Given that plan, he reaches the first intersection on every trip but the second only 2/3 of the time, so conditional on finding himself at some intersection, he's at the first one with probability 3/5 and at the second with probability 2/5. Then:

(1) A rational plan is one the agent has no reason to deviate from partway through executing it -- otherwise "optimal plan" doesn't mean much of anything.
(2) If the driver exits right now, his expected payoff is (3/5)·0 + (2/5)·4 = 8/5, which beats 4/3.
(3) No new information has arrived -- he knew he'd be absent-minded when he made the plan -- so deviating to exit is the rational thing to do at the intersection.
(4) But a driver whose stratedy from the start is "exit at every intersection" collects 0, which is worse than 4/3.
(5) So the plan it's rational to adopt at the pub is one it's irrational to follow on the road, and the action it's rational to take on the road is one it would be irrational to adopt at the pub. There's no plan he can rationally both adopt and execute.
(6) Therefore decision theory issues incoherent advice, full stop, to any agent with imperfect memory.

My son-in-law half-endorses (6), or at least enjoys watching me squirm over it. I think it proves way too much. Half the patients in my old trials forgot their doses and we still modeled them fine, and "rationality just breaks for forgetful agents" sounds to me like a physicist saying the laws of motion break for left-handed particles. My chemistry-trained instinct says the bookkeeping in (2)-(3) is crooked: the 3/5 and 2/5 were computed under the 2/3 plan, and then used to evaluate the payoff of abandoning it, which feels like using yesterday's calibration curve to validate today's assay on different samples. But I can't quite make that stick, because the driver really is sitting at an intersection with exactly that credence distribution and no way to tell anything else apart. So either the bookkeeping is fine and the problem is elsewhere, or premise (1) has to go -- and if (1) goes I want to know what "optimal plan" even means for an agent who can't check his own work. Which step do I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and is there some version of the driver's at-the-intersection reasoning that isn't crooked? I'd honestly rather give up (5) or the son-in-law than (1), but tell me what I'm paying either way.
