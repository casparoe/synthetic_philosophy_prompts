I teach a second-year elective on ethical theory to students with no philosophy background, and I've been given a single fifty-minute seminar on moral particularism versus generalism. Most of them will arrive assuming that ethics just is the application of rules — that the point of moral thinking is deriving verdicts from principles, so that anyone who rejects principles must be a relativist or is saying that anything goes. Plan the session rather than explain the doctrine to me: what has to be in place first (I suspect the distinction between a moral principle and a moral reason, and what the holism of reasons amounts to, before any '-ism' gets named — but tell me if I have the dependency structure wrong), which one example should carry the whole hour, and where exactly in the sequence it should enter. I'm considering the case where the fact that an action would give someone pleasure — normally a reason in favor — becomes a reason against when the pleasure is the sadist's cruel amusement at a victim's suffering; tell me whether that's the right carrying case or whether it invites a confusion I'm not anticipating, and if there's a better one, say what it must have and lack. Map the misconceptions the students will form in the order they'll form them — holism collapsing into 'it all depends,' particularism collapsing into intuitionism, generalism defended as though it were just legalism — and mark the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than allowed to harden. I want the generalist to get a fair hearing: the strongest principled ethics should be on the table, especially the worry that without principles moral judgment becomes unteachable, uncontestable, and hostage to bias, and I want the sequence to absorb that objection rather than wave at it. Close with one question I could ask at the end that would distinguish a student who has genuinely grasped the holism of reasons from one who has merely decided that rules are boring. Finally, tell me where this teaching plan is most likely to fail — which step, if it goes wrong, takes the rest down with it — and what I should watch for during the session that would tell me the sequence isn't working and needs to be changed on the spot.
