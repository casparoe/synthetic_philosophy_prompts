I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and I've become dissatisfied with the standard treatments of the paradox of voting. The setup is familiar: in a large election, the probability that any single ballot is decisive is astronomically small, so the expected instrumental payoff of voting is swamped by even trivial costs of time and effort, and yet millions of people vote anyway. The usual escape routes — adding a term for civic duty to the utility function, or modeling the vote as an expressive act like cheering at a match rather than an instrumental one — all share a premise I've come to think is the real culprit. They all treat the election outcome as an exogenous state of the world whose value is fixed independently of how it was produced, so that voting can only ever be a costly input to a lottery over outcomes, and abstention the cheap way to stand in the same lottery.

I want to argue that for at least some votes, this framing misdescribes the situation, because the value of the outcome is partly constituted by the manner of its production. If the legitimacy of a referendum result depends in part on turnout, or if the worth of electing a candidate is partly a matter of how many fellow citizens joined in, then the abstainer who reasons "my vote is negligible, I'll free-ride on everyone else's turnout" is not obtaining the same outcome more cheaply; he is, in a small but real way, obtaining a worse outcome. On this line, the paradox isn't solved by bolting extra utility onto the act — it's dissolved by noticing that the expected-utility calculation presupposed an independence between the act and the value of the outcome that the most interesting electoral cases simply don't have.

Please make this argument as rigorous as you can. In particular, I'd like you to confront the most obvious rejoinder head-on: that this maneuver just smuggles the duty term back into the utility function under a different name, since "outcomes are worth less when produced by low turnout" looks like one more entry in the agent's payoff table rather than a change of framework. Distinguish the two moves if you can — say precisely what it would mean for the outcome's value to depend constitutively on turnout rather than instrumentally. Then tell me where the argument overreaches: does it apply only to legitimacy-sensitive contests like constitutional referenda and founding elections, and can it really explain turnout in dull, low-salience local races where nobody's legitimacy story seems in play? I'd rather know the argument's honest limits than have them papered over, since the seminar instructor is the type to find them.
