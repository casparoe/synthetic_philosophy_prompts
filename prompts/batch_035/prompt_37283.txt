I am a novelist at work on a novel that is confined almost entirely to two characters: an elderly woman who owns a large house she no longer leaves, and the live-in carer she employs. Over the course of the book the balance of practical dependence between them shifts — the carer ends up managing the woman's affairs, her medication, eventually her identity papers — while the woman retains, and wields, the power to dismiss her. Early readers have told me that my situation "is basically the master–slave dialectic," and rather than gesture at Hegel, I would like to understand the machinery properly. I have read the relevant part of the Phenomenology of Spirit and a few companions, and I find that the summaries give me the plot but not the working parts. I would be grateful for an explanation of the following specific points, at the level of the argument rather than the label:

1. The opening fight. Why must the original struggle be waged to the death, and why must neither party actually die? I understand that each self-consciousness demands confirmation of itself from the other, and that each would rather risk life than accept the other's independence. What I do not understand is the exact logical role of the risk: why is a willingness to die required, and why does an actual death, or an actual capitulation before any risk is run, spoil the result in different ways? I want to be able to say precisely what goes wrong in each case.

2. The reversal. The familiar gloss is that the bondsman frees himself through labour while the master stagnates. I want the actual argument, disaggregated. What does the experience of fear contribute that work alone would not, and what does work contribute that fear alone would not? My reading suggests that fear without work is a paralysis that yields no selfhood, and work without fear is a mere craft that leaves the given world standing; together they somehow produce a self that has seen its own negativity objectified in the world. Please make that joint contribution intelligible, since my novel turns on which of the two characters is genuinely transformed.

3. The defective recognition. The master's misfortune is that he wins acknowledgment from someone he regards as a thing, so that his victory delivers something worthless. Please explain why recognition from an inferior confers nothing, and what this implies about the end-state of the dialectic: does it follow that genuine recognition is possible only between parties who already regard each other as equals, and if so, does that not make mutual recognition a condition of itself — something that must already obtain in order to be achieved? This circularity, if it is one, matters to me, because my two characters cannot plausibly end as equals, and I need to know whether the logic itself forbids it or merely declines to guarantee it.

4. Status of the story. I need to know what kind of claim the passage makes. Is it an empirical conjecture about how people actually behave, a logical structure that can be instantiated in many materials — individual psyches, households, classes, institutions, perhaps even the internal divisions of a single person — or something in between? I have heard that a famous French lecture course recast the whole thing as a story about humanity emerging through work, and that later readers found this vivid but distorting. Please tell me where that reading illuminates the text and where it adds commitments that are not Hegel's.

5. Load-bearing versus staging. Finally, and most practically: assuming I want my novel to instantiate the structure rather than merely allude to it, please tell me which elements are load-bearing and which are dramatic costume. Could the opening struggle be conducted without any physical violence — through, say, a mutual risking of livelihood or reputation? Must the work be material labour, or can the negation of the given take other forms? Could the roles be occupied by the two halves of one character, or by an institution and a person? I want a clear account of what I may alter for fictional purposes without breaking the dialectic, and what, if I removed it, would leave me with only the word.

I would prefer a self-contained explanation in your own words, at whatever length the argument requires, and I do not need bibliographical apparatus — only that the reasoning be laid out carefully enough that I can test my plot against it.
