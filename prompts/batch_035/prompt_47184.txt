My book club has been reading a popularization of Gödel's theorems, and I've been put in charge of presenting the chapter on whether mathematics might one day collapse under an internal contradiction. The author sums things up like this: "Gödel proved in 1931 that any formal system strong enough to do arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. So the axioms of mathematics can never be shown to be free of contradiction — not by mathematicians working within the system, and not by anyone else either. Russell's paradox had already shown that a genuine contradiction was lying in wait inside naive set theory, and Gödel generalized the lesson: incompleteness is just what the danger of contradiction looks like once you've patched things up. The upshot is that the consistency of arithmetic is not merely unproven but unprovable in principle, which means the whole edifice of mathematics ultimately rests on faith." I'm sympathetic to the dramatic conclusion, but a friend in the group who studies mathematical logic said the passage "sneaks several separate claims into one" and that she'd push back on parts of it, though she wouldn't tell me which parts and wanted me to work it out myself.

One further wrinkle: another member swears that there is a proof of arithmetic's consistency from the 1930s that works by doing induction along a certain infinite ordering of the natural numbers, and he says that if such a proof exists, it contradicts the book's whole story about what Gödel showed. I can't tell whether he's misremembering, or whether he's right and the book glossed over something important, or whether both the proof and Gödel's theorem are somehow compatible.

Please go through the book's summary roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what a logician would accept, what they'd describe as a simplification with a defensible core, and what is simply a mistake — and then tell me whether the conclusion that mathematics "rests on faith" follows from whichever parts of the summary survive scrutiny. Please also explain how the 1930s-style consistency proof my second friend mentioned fits in, if it's real: does it show that the book is conflating "unprovable inside the system" with "unprovable by anyone," or is there some other way to reconcile the two? I have some background in first-order logic, so you can be reasonably technical, but I'd like the verdict on each sentence stated plainly.
