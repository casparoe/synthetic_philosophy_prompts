I teach a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and we have just finished P. F. Strawson's Freedom and Resentment. A student of mine has produced an argument about it that I find myself unable to dismiss and unwilling to accept, and since our next meeting is not until Thursday, I would be grateful for your help in locating the trouble. The argument concerns what her conclusion would commit us to: a thoroughgoing relativism about responsibility. It runs as follows.

(1) For Strawson, what makes a person responsible for an action is not an attitude-independent fact that our attitudes track, but her being an appropriate target of the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, guilt, and their kin — and he insists that these attitudes neither need nor could receive justification from a standpoint wholly outside the participant perspective; any such justification would address the wrong kind of question. (2) Which actions attract the reactive attitudes is, as a matter of sociology, settled by what the local community actually feels, and the sentiments vary widely across cultures and epochs, as Strawson himself allows when he observes that the patterns of personal and local life shape who resents whom and on what occasions. (3) The extension of the responsible therefore travels with the local ecology of sentiment: an act counts as blameworthy in a community just insofar as the reactive attitudes are, as a matter of fact, directed at it there. (4) By parity, a community whose members sincerely and uniformly feel indignation toward, say, women who read in public thereby makes those women genuinely blameworthy for reading in public; there is no further fact to which we could appeal in protest, since any appeal would have to be issued from within some participant stance or other, and the local stance has already spoken. (5) That conclusion is intolerable: we do not want to say that in such a community the women really are guilty and merely have the misfortune of living among the easily scandalized; we want to say the community's attitudes are misplaced. (6) So (1) is false, and responsibility must be fixed by facts about agents that obtain independently of anyone's attitudes.

She adds, in a footnote, that she would much rather keep (1) than swallow (4), and that she suspects the fault lies somewhere between (2) and (3), but she cannot say what. Neither, at the moment, can I, at least not with the confidence I would want in front of a seminar. So tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent when it was introduced, and what rejecting it costs. If the repair depends on distinguishing attitudes that are actually felt from attitudes that would be fitting, I want you to say where the standard of fittingness comes from — and, in particular, whether it can be located within the participant perspective, as Strawson's own talk of a demand for reasonable goodwill might suggest, without quietly reintroducing through the back door the very external standpoint that (1) was meant to bar. If instead you think the parity claim in (4) is where the argument breaks, explain why attitudes directed at what a person is, rather than at how she wills, fail to be reactive attitudes in Strawson's sense, and say what that exclusion costs him elsewhere. Please write your response as continuous prose only, as one would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
