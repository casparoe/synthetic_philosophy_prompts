I'm a backend engineer at a company that ships LLM-based features (coding assistants, retrieval agents, that sort of thing), and I have no philosophy background beyond occasional blog posts. Two colleagues on my team — Maya, who works on our agent framework, and Tom, who works on model evaluation — have spent roughly a year arguing in our team channel about what these systems actually *are*, and the rest of us have learned to dread the phrase "well, that depends on whether you think it's an agent." Maya's position: the systems we deploy are best understood as agents, full stop — they form plans, take actions in the world through tools, react to feedback, and display stable preferences across sessions, and refusing to call that agency because the substrate happens to be a transformer is substrate chauvinism. Tom's position: the base model is best understood as a simulator of text-generating processes; the apparent agent is a persona the model simulates, and the actual agent, if there is one, is the wrapper — the orchestration code, the prompt, the tool loop that Maya herself wrote. When we stack tools and memory and a planning loop on top, Maya says we've built an agent out of model plus scaffold; Tom says we've built a filing cabinet out of wood plus carpenter, and the carpenter is us. They've finally agreed to stop relitigating this in Slack and instead produce, with me as reluctant facilitator, a proper adversarial-collaboration document.
