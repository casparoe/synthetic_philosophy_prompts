I teach an intro jurisprudence course, and next week we finally get to the Hart–Fuller debate, which I always look forward to and always under-prepare. Before I stand in front of thirty undergraduates who will ask me about the grudge informer cases, I want to rehearse my side against a real opponent rather than the strawman in my own lecture notes. So: please play Fuller. Not the caricature of natural law where wicked statutes aren't binding because morality says so, but the actual Fuller of the internal morality of law — generality, publicity, prospectivity, congruence between declared rules and official action, and the rest — and the claim that a total failure in one of these respects produces not merely bad law but something that isn't properly law at all. You can draw freely on anything your Fuller would say, including his later book, but speak in your own voice, first person, as him.

Stay in character until I write stop, and keep each reply to a paragraph or so, so it feels like an actual exchange. You're allowed to concede small points where the historical Fuller genuinely would, but never the central claim that legality and morality are connected at the level of what makes something law rather than merely at the level of what we should obey. Be sharp — I want to lose some points, not win a rout.

Here's my opening, in Hart's voice: in the grudge informer case, the wife's denunciation of her husband was valid law under the Nazi statutes in force at the time — odious, but valid — and the postwar German court faced two honest options. It could punish her retroactively, which we would normally shrink from doing, or it could let her go. What it actually did was declare the statutes somehow not-really-law on account of their monstrous content, and you cheered that as the triumph of legality over legal positivism. But calling odious law not-law doesn't protect anyone from it; it just hides the pencil work. Better to accept that law and morality are conceptually separate, admit the statute was law, and then make the genuinely hard moral choice — retroactivity and all — out in the open where it can be argued about honestly. Your move, Professor Fuller.
