my draft so far (rough, dont judge the spelling lol):

The fine-tuning argument says the constants of physics — like the strength of gravity, or the cosmological constant, or how strongly the strong nuclear force binds — all fall in crazy tiny life-permitting ranges, and that this is way to improbable to be an accident unless something like a designer chose the values. I want to argue the whole thing rests on a hidden assumption that does all the work: that there is some default "spread" of possible values for the constants, like every value is equally likely, so that our life-friendly value looks like a needle in a haystack. But why uniform? Nobody has ever seen whatever "machine" sets the constants, so we have zero basis for thinking the values are spread evenly to begin with. Compare: if I roll a normal die and get a 6, you can say that was 1-in-6 because you know the die. But if I press a mystery button and a 6 comes out, and you've never seen the button or whats behind it, you cant calculate how improbable that was. Maybe the button just prints 6's every time. Physicists measuring the constants is like reading the number off the printout — it tells you what came out, not how likely anything was to come out.

Second point, the lottery thing: even if you grant some spread of possible values, only beings in life-permitting universes are ever going to stand around wondering why the constants came out right. So the improbability of "constants right for us" can't be evidence of design, anymore than a lottery winners winning ticket is evidence the draw was rigged — the winner was always going to end up holding a winning ticket no matter what.
