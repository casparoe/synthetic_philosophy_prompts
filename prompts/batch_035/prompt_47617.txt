longtime LW reader here (since the Overcoming Bias days), mostly lurk, first post, so go easy.

Background: I do interpretability as a hobby — small models, nothing fancy, no lab affiliation. Last month I trained a sparse autoencoder on a little 4-layer transformer I'd trained myself on story-like text, and I found a feature that seems to fire right before a character in the story is about to break a promise. It fires pretty cleanly in the right places, and here's the part I was chuffed about: if I clamp the feature up, the model's continuation has the character break the promise; clamp it down and the character keeps it. Feels like I found an actual circuit-level thing.

My brother is a physicist and thinks I'm fooling myself. His line: there's no "promise-breaking representation" in there, what I found is just a direction in activation space that happens to correlate with how I labeled things, and the story that the model "understands" promises is me imposing narrative structure on linear algebra. He says it's like seeing shapes in clouds — the direction is real, but the interpretation is mine, not the model's. And he says if I'd gone looking for a "commitment-tracking" feature instead, or sliced the corpus differently, I'd have found something equally satisfying and written it up just as confidently.

My counter was basically the clamping test. If it were pure projection, why does meddling with the direction causally change downstream behavior in the semantically sensible direction? And by his standard nothing has representations — a thermostat, a bee, and eventually a brain, it's shapes all the way down. But he says I'm sliding from "causally relevant" to "actually representing something," and honestly I couldn't give a crisp account of what separates those two. He also pointed out that the clamping test is run and interpreted by me, the same guy who found the feature, so it's not fully independent evidence. Which... fair?

So, questions for you all:

1. Is there any principled criterion for when a direction in a network counts as a representation of the thing, rather than just a convenient handle for us? Like, some combination of causal control + counterfactual robustness + not-being-found-by-a-confirmation-biased-researcher? Or is the distinction just vague and we should live with that?
2. Am I right that my brother is basically pushing an instrumentalist take on interpretability, where the "concepts" belong to the observer? If so is there a standard realist reply, or do the realists mostly win on vibes?
3. And the part I actually care about: does this matter for alignment? If interpretability only ever gives us human-usable handles rather than the model's own concepts, is that enough to lean on when we scale to models we can't eval directly? My gut says a handle that lets you steer behavior is worth having even if it's "ours not theirs", but my brother says a handle that worked on the training distribution can silently stop corresponding to anything on a new one, and you won't get a warning. That last point keeps me up at night a bit.

Steelmans of either side welcome. Also if anyone has actually run the "search for the opposite-label feature and see if you find an equally convincing one" experiment on your own models, I'd love to hear how it went — that seems like the crux and I haven't seen it written up anywhere.
