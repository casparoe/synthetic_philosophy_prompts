For the final session of my graduate seminar on the Harlem Renaissance, I want a poem in the voice of the aging Du Bois answering the younger writers behind the single 1926 issue of Fire!!, who had rejected his insistence that Black art serve the struggle. The poem should be modeled on the Sorrow Songs: exactly five quatrains in common meter, each closing with the same refrain line, in the way a spiritual carries its burden through the verses (the same songs he used as epigraphs in The Souls of Black Folk). The refrain must be a single new line — do not quote Du Bois or any actual spiritual — and its meaning must migrate without a word changing: in the opening stanzas it should sound like his polemical claim in The Criteria of Negro Art that he wanted art that served his people rather than art for its own sake, and by the final stanza it should have become the essay's closing reversal, in which the old equation of beauty and truth collapses the distinction between propaganda and beauty. The middle stanzas should carry the concrete reasons the demand mattered — a despised people as the audience, the cost of pretty lies, the dignity at stake in what gets depicted — so that a reader who knows the essay recognizes the argument, and one who does not still finds a good poem. Give me only the five quatrains, with nothing before or after them, in American English.
