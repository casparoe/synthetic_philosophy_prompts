The position I'll be defending is roughly this: in game theory, the assumption of common knowledge of rationality — I know you're rational, you know I know, and so on up the tower — is treated as load-bearing for backward induction and iterated dominance. I think that, in this role, it functions like a specification that no real system satisfies. In my line of work we have a name for this pattern: the code is proven correct against a spec that nothing implements. Call it the spec-bug view of game theory: the theorems are right, but almost vacuously, because in situations involving actual humans the epistemic premise fails a few levels up the tower — and since nobody can say in advance how many levels a given situation requires, the theory loses to rough heuristics as a predictor, and its normative authority is borrowed from a premise it never delivers.

My opening move: take the centipede game. Backward induction, run under your assumption, says a rational player takes the small payoff on the very first move, so the larger pot down the line is never even allowed to exist. In experiments, people pass for several rounds, and the ones who do come away with more than your theory says they should get — and, worse, they seem right to. Your standard reply is that the players lacked common knowledge of rationality, but notice what that costs you: the theory now predicts nothing except in a world it stipulates into existence, and it can't tell a player, in advance and from the inside, whether they're in that world or not.
