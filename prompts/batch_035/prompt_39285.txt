For a while now, a friend of mine who studies neuroscience and I have been arguing about when a mental event — an intention, a decision, a pain — genuinely causes a physical event, and I've been keeping a list of cases and my gut verdicts on each, hoping a principle would emerge. It hasn't, at least not cleanly, so I want help extracting one. Here are my verdicts. When I deliberately raise my arm to flag a taxi, my deciding to raise it is a cause of the arm's rising, not merely something that happened alongside it. I hold this even though I accept that the neural activity in my motor cortex was, by itself, causally sufficient for the rising: the intention still seems to be doing causal work, not riding along as an epiphenomenon. In a case with the same structure but two bodies instead of a mind and a brain — two marksmen fire simultaneously, either shot alone sufficient to kill — I'm comfortable saying the death was overdetermined and that each shot caused it. Yet when I try to describe the taxi case in the marksmen's vocabulary, as the arm's rising being overdetermined by the intention and the neural event, each sufficient, I recoil; it sounds wrong in a way the marksmen case doesn't, and I can't say why. By contrast, a patient whose motor nerve is severed: she intends to move her arm, and a machine, on a doctor's orders, moves it at exactly that moment — there I say flatly that her intention did not cause the rising, even though it was present and directed at precisely that outcome, which tells me that an intention's being there and being aimed at the effect isn't enough on its own. And finally, my friend and I agree on this one: two people with numerically identical neural events, one acting intentionally and one in a reflex, seem to differ in what caused their movements, even though nothing physical distinguishes them.

The tension should be visible: the taxi case and the marksmen case appear to share their causal structure — two sufficient causes converging on one effect — but my verdicts treat them very differently, embracing overdetermination in one and refusing to even name it that way in the other, and I can't currently justify the difference. So what I'm asking for is this: state, precisely, the single principle that best captures my pattern, if one exists; say which case motivates each clause of it; tell me what it predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on, a man whose arm rises through a purely reflexive spasm at the very moment he was also intending, for reasons of his own, to raise it — did the intention cause the rising, did the spasm, both, or neither; and then be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and which of them you think I should give up, with your reasons. Don't lay out a menu of candidate principles for me to choose among — commit to the account you actually think is right, even if it means overturning one of my verdicts, and defend that commitment. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and give the reasoning after, with no preamble and no restatement of my cases back at me.
