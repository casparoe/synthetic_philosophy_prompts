A student in my graduate seminar on rational choice has produced an argument against satisficing that I find better than I would like, and I cannot locate the faulty joint with any confidence. Each premise looks defensible; the conclusion is that there is nothing for satisficing to be.

(1) A choice is rational only if something counts in favor of it over the alternatives the agent actually recognizes.
(2) For the satisficer, what counts in favor of an option is that it clears a threshold of acceptability — it is good enough.
(3) A satisficer who recognizes a dominating option — one better in every respect she cares about, and no more costly to obtain — and who nevertheless takes the merely good-enough option, has nothing counting in favor of her choice over that better option; "good enough" says something for it against the unacceptable options, not against the better one.
(4) A choice with nothing counting in its favor over a recognized alternative is not rational.
(5) So a satisficer is rational only where she does not recognize a dominating option; where comparisons are cheap, she must decline to compare.
(6) Declining to compare when comparisons are cheap is the very incuriosity we count against agents, not a form of rationality.
(7) And where the threshold is set by the expected costs of further search, the "satisficer" is just a maximizer with a richer objective function — search costs in, maximizing out.
(8) Therefore satisficing is either disguised maximizing or a rationalized name for choosing worse for no reason, and the satisficing tradition's suggestion that bounded rationality revises our model of the agent, rather than merely enlarging the list of constraints, collapses.

The argument also seems to prove too much: by its lights, a hiring committee that stops at a candidate who is clearly good enough while a marginally stronger one remains in the pool, at trivial further cost, behaves irrationally — and that strikes me as the wrong verdict about the same people who are behaving admirably.

I don't want to accept (8). My suspicion is that something is smuggled into (1) or (4) — that "rational" is being made to track act-level justification under full recognition, when the interesting claim of the satisficing tradition concerns dispositions, characters, and the shape of a life rather than any single choice. But when I say this, the student replies that dispositions are rational only derivatively, in virtue of the acts they produce. So tell me: which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? Is there a reading on which (8) follows but says something weaker than it appears to — perhaps because it attacks satisficing as a requirement while leaving intact satisficing as a permission — or a reading on which it does not follow at all? For my part, I would most like to keep (6).
