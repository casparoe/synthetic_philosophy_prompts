I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and by virtue of being the token ethicist in the building I sit on the ethics committee at the regional hospital a few towns over. We have a case on the docket for next month that is squarely my area, and I keep going back and forth on it, so I want a written opinion I can circulate to the committee ahead of the meeting recommending what we should actually do.

The patient is a 74-year-old woman with moderately advanced Alzheimers. Her advance directive was drawn up 16 years ago, shortly after her mother died of the same disease, and it refuses antibiotics if a life-threatening infection sets in once she has reached the stage of no longer recognizing her family. Two complications. First, the trigger condition: most days she knows her husband fine, but a couple weeks back she called her daughter by her sister's name, and the attending says there are evenings when she doesn't seem to place him at all. Whether she "recognizes her family" seems to shift by the hour, and the directive obviously says nothing about that. Second, three years after signing she added a handwritten line at the bottom — the handwriting has been confirmed as hers — to the effect that if she is still enjoying her days, we are not to give up on her. And honestly she is doing OK: she lights up at the music group, eats well, seems content. Now she has a pneumonia that is drifting toward "life-threatening," and the family has split down the middle. The daughter wants the antibiotics, leaning on the handwritten line and on who her mom is right now; the husband wants the directive followed to the letter, because he promised her he would see it through.

The memo needs to do three things. (a) Recommend a concrete course of action on this specific case, with reasoning the committee members can actually follow step by step — not a "both sides have merit" dodge, we get enough of those. (b) Take the strongest version of the losing side seriously rather than the strawman version. (c) Sketch what this case implies for how the hospital should handle directives with vague trigger conditions, or with later addenda that cut against the original text, since this will not be the last one we see.

Assume the committee already knows the Dworkin stuff about critical versus experiential interests, and the standard worry that the woman who signed the directive is not, in some relevant sense, the woman in the bed — don't recite the literature at us, work the case. And don't lean on what any particular state's law requires; we want the ethical recommendation and will have counsel check the legal bits ourselves.

Keep it short enough that the committee will actually read it before hand — call it two pages if it were on paper.
