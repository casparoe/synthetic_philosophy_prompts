I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of mind seminar on free will, and I've settled into a position that I suspect is more exposed than its advocates usually admit, so I want to map out its vulnerabilities before committing ten pages to it.

The position, roughly: what we call free will is not a metaphysical achievement like ultimate sourcehood but a matter of degree, and the degree in question is flexibility — the kind of thing the digger wasp conspicuously lacks. You know the Sphex story: the wasp drags prey to the burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes in to inspect, and if you move the prey a few inches while she's inside, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and re-inspects — and she'll do this indefinitely, never noticing the loop. Hofstadter called this sphexishness, and my paper takes it as the contrast class for freedom: a system is free to the extent that its behavior is sensitive, in the right way, to perturbations of its circumstances rather than keyed to a fixed trigger. To make this more than a metaphor, I'm proposing that flexibility be read as something like a perturbation profile: vary the situation along many dimensions and see which variations the system's behavior tracks, which it ignores, and whether the ignored ones are the ones that would reveal a loop. Free will, on this picture, is non-sphexishness — high-dimensional, well-organized sensitivity to how things actually are.

What I want from you is an honest map of what would count against this. I'm especially interested in cases that cut in the confusing direction. Human habits, for instance — the stimulus-response routines that the animal learning literature says gradually detach from outcome value — look sphexish by my criterion, yet we don't think the habitual coffee-drinker unfree. Is that a fatal problem, or can the view be saved by saying the whole person remains flexible even when a subsystem isn't, and if so, hasn't I merely relocated the question rather than answered it? Symmetrically, it seems like a system could have a rich perturbation profile — sensitive to endless contextual variation, never caught in a loop — while still being intuitively unfree: imagine a chameleon-like agent whose behavior is exquisitely context-sensitive but in a merely reactive way, with nothing worth calling deliberation. And there's a rigidity-in-the-midst-of-freedom problem too: someone whose principles are genuinely unshakeable — who would tell the truth under any perturbation whatsoever — has a flat spot in their profile exactly where we'd most want to locate their freedom.

So: rank the defeaters for me, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Tell me which ones a committed defender of the flexibility picture would concede as counting against it and which they'd brush off, and whether the brush-offs amount to insulating the view into unfalsifiability — and, if they do, whether that's disqualifying for a view of this kind or just what we should expect from a position that's meant as a reconceptualization rather than an empirical hypothesis. I'd also like to know what the view predicts that its live rivals don't — the sourcehood theorist, the reasons-responsiveness crowd who'd say I've just reinvented their view with worse notation — so I can tell whether anything is actually at stake. If it helps you aim: the objection I personally find most frightening is the chameleon one, because I can feel myself wanting to respond by quietly appending and the sensitivity must be to reasons, which smells like doing all the work with an unexamined clause.
