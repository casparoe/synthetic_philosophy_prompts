My company just rolled out one of those "employee engagement" programs — annual survey asking whether I find my work meaningful, whether I feel connected to the mission, quarterly pizza parties, a Slack channel for peer shout-outs, and a manager training module on "creating a sense of purpose." HR pitched it to us as fixing "disengagement," which they described as workers not feeling invested. The whole thing made me think of the old Marxist idea of alienation — work feeling empty, cut off from the product of your labor and from your own capacities — except here the diagnosis puts the problem entirely in the worker's inner state, and the remedies never touch pay, autonomy, or who owns what gets produced. In fact the survey is anonymous precisely so nothing said in it can be traced to anyone, and compensation was declared off the table before the first meeting even started. So what view of work does a program like this presuppose? Is it a straight repudiation of Marx's account of alienation, or a weird adaptation that keeps the symptom-list and quietly drops the cause? And does the practice actually require rejecting the Marxist diagnosis, or could a sincere Marxist run the program anyway, treating it as palliative care for an incurable condition? Lead with your bottom-line verdict on what the program presupposes, then give your reasoning, and finish by telling me what the program would have to look like if the company took the Marxist diagnosis seriously instead.
