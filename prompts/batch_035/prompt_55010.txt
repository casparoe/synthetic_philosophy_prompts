I've come to hold that a certain kind of deference to one's future self is a genuine requirement of rationality — the position usually called the Reflection principle, associated with van Fraassen's work in the 1980s. In my own terms: if you now know that at some later time you will assign credence r to a proposition A, then your present credence in A, conditional on that knowledge, should itself be r; knowing what your future self will think gives you license to adopt that opinion in advance. The picture behind it, as I understand it, is that rationality is not just momentary coherence but a kind of integrity across time: your dispositions to form future opinions are themselves part of your present opinion. In ordinary cases this strikes me as obviously right — if I know that tonight, after the election returns come in, I will be 90% confident the incumbent lost, it seems perverse for me to sit here at 40%.

What I want from you is a map of the principle's exposure. Please tell me what would count against it, covering at least the following:

- Cases where matching one's future credence seems irrational even when the future credence is known in advance: foreseen impairment (you know you'll be drunk, or terrified, or concussed), foreseen manipulation (brainwashing, persuasion by someone with an interest), foreseen loss of information, and the self-correction of a bias you know you currently have. For each, say whether a defender can defuse it by conditioning on the right proposition, and whether that move is principled or a patch.
- The training problem: it can seem that if the principle holds, the very episodes in which we learn — studying logic, acquiring statistics, being convinced by an argument we can anticipate finding convincing — are in tension with it, since we are apparently told to adopt now the confidence we will have only after learning. Is this a genuine tension or a confusion about what the principle actually conditions on?
- Whether the principle can be derived from more basic commitments — coherence, decision-theoretic arguments, or the idea that one's future self's opinions are evidence — and what would have to fail for each derivation to break.
- Whether the qualified versions (defer only when there is no loss of information, no known irrationality, only expected evidence-gathering) preserve anything interesting, or whether they amount to abandoning the principle while keeping its name.

Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, distinguishing what defenders of the principle have themselves conceded from what only its critics allege, and be explicit about where the two sides would draw the line. Then give an honest verdict: is the principle genuinely exposed, or has it been insulated by successive qualifications — and if insulated, say whether that is a defect in a principle of this kind, which aspires to constrain rational belief over time rather than to redescribe whatever a rational person happens to do. If you think the best response is to keep the principle only for a restricted class of cases, characterize that class and say what falls outside it and why that restriction doesn't collapse into the triviality that one should expect to believe what one will have good reason to believe.
