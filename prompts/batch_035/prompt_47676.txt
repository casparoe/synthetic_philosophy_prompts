Analyze Ashoka's conduct after the Kalinga war as a case study in the debate over whether Buddhist ethics is best understood as a form of consequentialism, and say candidly where each interpretation breaks down.

The facts to work with, which are common ground: around 261 BCE the Mauryan emperor Ashoka conquered Kalinga in a war whose carnage the edicts themselves do not minimize — the tally given runs to a hundred thousand or more killed and many times that deported. In the edicts issued afterward, Ashoka expresses profound remorse for the suffering he caused, renounces further military conquest in favor of what he calls conquest by dhamma, and institutes a reform program: wells and rest houses along the roads, shade trees planted, medical treatment provided for humans and animals, royal kitchens restricted in what they may slaughter, royal hunts curtailed, officials appointed to oversee welfare and to check on conditions of prisoners and the vulnerable, and decrees of tolerance toward all religious sects, urging restraint in praising one's own and criticizing others'.

The interpretive question I want run in both directions. On one side: map Ashoka's post-Kalinga conduct onto Buddhist ethics. The relevant machinery is karma (moral causation attaching consequences to the agent's own mental states, ripening over this and future lives), the primacy of intention in fixing the moral quality of an act, compassion as a motive, and the claim that suffering is rooted in craving. On the other side: map the same conduct onto a classical consequentialist framework, with impartial aggregation of welfare, agent-neutrality, and the weighing of large losses against large gains.

Where I want the analysis to bite: first, the remorse itself. If Ashoka's regret is karmically structured — he is polluted, he has laid up future suffering for himself — then is it even a moral response in the consequentialist's sense, or a prudential one, and does that difference show up anywhere in what he actually did? Second, the structure of consequences: Buddhist consequences fall on the agent; consequentialist consequences fall on patients. Does Ashoka's welfare program, which appears agent-neutral and aggregative (roads, wells, medical care for whoever comes), fit a karma-centered system, or does it suggest he was reasoning in a way Buddhist ethics as such does not mandate? Third, the renunciation of conquest: a consequentialist could justify it by the expected costs of war; a Buddhist ethic by the intention it would manifest; a cynic by imperial overstretch. Say which explanation does most work and how one would tell. Fourth, the aggregation problem: Buddhist ethics has, as far as I know, no procedure for summing welfare across persons, yet Ashoka's program looks exactly like an exercise in summing. Is this a genuine point against consequentialist readings of Buddhist ethics, or just an artifact of statecraft?

I am aware that scholars dispute how Buddhist Ashoka's dhamma actually was — it may have been a deliberately ecumenical moral code rather than Buddhism as such. Flag that where it matters, but do not let it stall the analysis; treat the case as one of a ruler self-consciously reorienting his rule around Buddhist-inflected categories.

Finally, discipline about silence: where neither framework predicts or explains some feature of the episode — the tolerance edicts, say, or the fact that he renounced war rather than reforming its conduct — say so plainly rather than stretching the theory to cover it, and note whether the episode, taken as evidence, strengthens or weakens the case that Buddhist ethics is a consequentialism.
