I'm taking a third-year philosophy of mind course, and in the week on functionalism our lecturer defended computationalism with the classic analogy: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. Same program, different machine — a word processor runs on a laptop from one manufacturer and a desktop from another, so if cognition is a program, the carbon substrate is as incidental as the brand of processor, and minds should in principle be realizable in silicon, or in a suitably organized simulation. She used the analogy to support two conclusions in particular: that mental states are multiply realizable, which she took to ground the possibility of AI with genuine minds, and that whole brain emulation is in principle no more mysterious than porting an application to a new platform. A friend doing computer science pushed back in tutorial: brains are not stored-program machines in the relevant sense — there is no clean separation between the "program" and the "hardware," since synaptic weights are at once the memory and the machinery; the "hardware" is constantly rewired by its own "processing"; input is continuous and analog rather than a discrete fetch-execute cycle; and evolution never gave us an instruction set architecture that anyone could compile against. The lecturer's reply was that the analogy is meant to hold at the right level of description, not the level of implementation — roughly Marr's point that the same algorithm can be realized in neurons or in silicon even when the underlying machinery looks nothing alike. I would like an audit of this analogy. First, set out the correspondence table explicitly: what in the brain is supposed to play the roles of program, processor, memory, input and output, instruction set, and compiler. Then go through the disanalogies my friend raised, plus any others that seem important — for instance, whether the brain does anything corresponding to a stored program at all, given that its structure is shaped by learning and development rather than loaded from somewhere, and whether "running the same program" is even a well-defined notion for a system like that. For each disanalogy, say whether it actually threatens the lecturer's two conclusions or is a mere implementation difference — the kind of thing Marr's lower level is supposed to abstract away from. Finally, tell me whether either conclusion survives the audit; and if the software/hardware picture has to go, whether there is a better comparison that could carry the same load (the brain as a trained network rather than a programmed machine, as a dynamical system, as something whose "code" is its own history) — or whether the multiple-realizability thought is better defended with no analogy at all.
