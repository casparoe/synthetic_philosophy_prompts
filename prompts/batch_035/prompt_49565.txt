A student in my graduate seminar, revising for her comprehensive exam, has produced the following summary of Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," and I want to give her precise feedback rather than my usual marginal scrawl:

"Quine proves that there are no analytic truths. The analytic/synthetic distinction collapses because every statement, including the laws of logic, is a hypothesis tested against experience, so nothing is true by virtue of meaning alone. This means Carnap's whole project of linguistic frameworks and explication was vacuous, that mathematics is just a very successful empirical theory, and that the same argument dissolves the fact/value distinction. Once Quine is accepted, the necessary/contingent distinction has to go as well, since necessity was just analyticity in disguise."

I suspect this compresses several distinct theses into one argument, and converts what is partly a challenge about how the notion of analyticity could ever be satisfactorily *explained* into an outright denial that the distinction holds. Please audit it for me, sentence by sentence: which claims a sympathetic Quinean would accept, where the summary misrepresents the structure of the essay — in particular, how its two dogmas are supposed to be connected, and whether the second is doing any work in her rendering — where a modest thesis is overstated, and which of her conclusions survive once the errors are fixed. I'd also like you to indicate where a Carnapian, or a critic along the lines of Grice and Strawson, would insist that what she calls a proof is nothing of the sort, and whether her final slide from Quine's conclusion to the rejection of modal notions is a legitimate Quinean move or a further commitment requiring independent argument.
