Two colleagues of mine — I'm a philosopher, and we co-teach an ethics and political psychology module — got into a proper row in the pub about Haidt's moral foundations theory, and I want an outside referee. I'll try to reconstruct the exchange fairly.

Marcus (psychologist) opened with the asymmetry finding: left-wing moral psychology supposedly runs on fewer foundations, roughly care and fairness, while the right engages all five or six, including loyalty, authority and sanctity. His claim was that this isn't just an empirical curiosity — if the foundations are evolved detectors for real features of social life, then a morality operating on two detectors is like colour-blindness: functional, but missing part of the picture. So the right has better epistemic access to the moral landscape, and the left's narrower matrix explains why progressives often can't model what motivates ordinary people — Haidt himself claimed conservatives understand liberal morality better than the other way round.

Rachel (political theorist) pushed back in stages. First, it's an is-ought slide: from the fact that the mind comes with six modules, you get nothing about which modules track value. Second, the detectors were shaped by selection for coalitional success, not truth — loyalty and obedience can be adaptive without being good, the way a taste for sugar is adaptive without being good for you. Third, some of what the "missing" foundations detect are things we've deliberately learned to override: purity applied to caste, authority applied to kings — and nobody calls that moral truncation or thinks we're thereby worse off.

Marcus answered that he never claimed the foundations are moral truths, only inputs; but a moral system blind to a society's actual motivational machinery is defective even on its own terms — you can't promote welfare if you can't predict people. He also said calling the left's restraint "progress" just begs the question of whether anything was gained.

Rachel's closing move: that's a strategic point masquerading as an epistemic one, and the colour-blindness analogy smuggled in the assumption that more detectors is better, when the progressive project is partly the considered judgement that some detectors deserve to be switched off.

They eventually agreed to disagree and went back to their beers, but I espeically want to know about the move-by-move. Can you say who's right about which specific point, whether either of them is just plain confused somewhere, and what (if anything) both are missing? Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating of the question. And British English, please.
