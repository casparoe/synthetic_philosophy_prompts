I am writing a novel set in a small progressive boarding school in the 1930s, and my headmaster character — an admirer of Alfred North Whitehead — has taken to ending staff meetings with Whitehead's warning against inert ideas: ideas that are merely received into the mind without being utilized, tested, or thrown into fresh combinations. I am paraphrasing from memory, but that is roughly how it goes. At different points in the draft he invokes this warning to justify abolishing examinations, dropping the history syllabus, and letting the boys follow whatever subject seizes them for a term; I need to know whether these uses are licensed by the idea or merely fashionable gestures in its direction. Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from the mildest, something nearly no one could deny about understanding versus parroting, through a stronger claim about knowledge going stale when merely stored, to the most radical reading a process metaphysics might motivate, on which handing down an inheritance of ideas at all is corrupt — and for each reading say whether it is plausible, whether Whitehead himself could have held it (my sense is that the same essay argues for teaching a small number of subjects thoroughly and even defends the place of the classics, which ought to constrain the radical readings, but correct me if I have this wrong), and whether it would actually support what my headmaster wants to do. Then commit to a verdict: tell me which reading the phrase really carries, and whether my character can invoke it in good conscience or whether I have written him into a misuse of it. I would rather have a definite position I can build the character around than a menu left for me to choose from.
