My sister has bipolar disorder, and a few years back, while she was stable and lucid, she signed what her doctors call a psychiatric advance directive: if she ever gets sick enough to refuse treatment again, the instructions she wrote down now override whatever she says then. When that actually happened last spring and she fought the injection, everyone involved treated restraining her as respecting her autonomy. Then a few months later my grandmother had a stroke, and she'd left a living will years ago saying no feeding tube ever — but after the stroke, in a confused but genuinely happy state, she kept asking for food, and the doctors fed her, saying her current wishes were what mattered now. Nobody seemed to think there was any tension. It's been bugging me ever since.

So here's what I want to understand: what do these commitment practices actually presuppose about the self? Does the psychiatric directive picture presuppose that the stable sister is the "real" one and the psychotic one is basically an impostor to be overruled — and can that even be right, given that my sister says her episodes, though awful, sometimes felt like the most vivid and most *her* experiences she's ever had? Maybe it's more like the sane self being allowed to bind the future self, the way I can bind my future self with a mortgage — but then why doesn't the grandmother's prior written self get to bind her post-stroke self the same way? Or is the thought something like: the stroke changed what she values in ways the earlier self couldn't have anticipated, so the earlier consent couldn't really cover it — but surely nobody can anticipate what a psychotic episode is like either, so how did my sister's signature count as informed consent to *that*?

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of personal identity over time (and of autonomy, and of whose preferences get to count) that these practices are running on. Tell me which rival theories would license which practice — like, would a view on which the self is just psychological continuity license honoring past wishes in one case but not the other? — and whether the two practices presuppose consistent things or are just flatly contradictory while both waving the autonomy flag. And be honest about where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. places where several theories of the self would all be fine with what the doctors did anyway. Finally: is there a principled line between the cases where we honor the past self and the cases where we honor the present one, or is the real driver just that we're squeamish about withholding food from someone who's asking for it but less squeamish about restraining someone who's screaming at the nurses? If it's the latter, say so — and tell me what a policy that took the implicit theory seriously (whichever it is) would actually have to change. Please dont just tell me these are hard cases and it depends.
