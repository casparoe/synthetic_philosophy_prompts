For several years now my graduate seminar on value theory has ended at the same impasse, and I have begun to suspect that this tells us something about the debate itself rather than about my students. The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, as I present it, runs as follows: the fitting-attitude analysis holds that for something to be admirable, or desirable, or amusing is for it to be a fitting object of admiration, desire, or amusement — and, on the reasons version of the analysis, for there to be sufficient reason for the relevant attitude. The counterexamples have by now become a genre: a demon will torture you unless you admire a mediocre man's talents; a lecture is tiresome, but you will be rewarded for being amused by it; you will be punished for failing to desire what is in fact worthless. In each case you seem to have overwhelming reason for the attitude without the object's seeming to possess the value. The response literature has accordingly become a catalogue of repair strategies: distinguish object-given from state-given reasons and confine the analysis to the former; hold that the wrong kind of reason gives you reason to bring it about that you have the attitude, not reason for the attitude itself; go hybrid, letting a single class of reasons play two different roles; deny that the wrong kind of reason is a reason to do anything at all; or abandon the analysis and take fittingness as primitive. What I notice is that every one of these families dispatches the original demon cases with ease, and every one is then undone by second-order cases engineered against it — cases in which the state-given reason happens to track a feature of the object, or in which a supposedly object-given reason owes its force to the subject's situation. The debate has thus settled into a cycle of increasingly baroque examples and increasingly baroque distinctions for neutralizing them, with no one that I know of changing sides.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement is not really about the cases at all. It is a disagreement about what the fitting-attitude analysis is for. One party treats it as a metaphysical thesis about what grounds evaluative facts, on which the demon cases are not even counterexamples — the analysis need not claim that every reason for an attitude derives from its object. The other party treats it as a reductive thesis about reasons, on which the demon cases are fatal from the start. If that is right, the two parties are answering different questions under a single name, and a good deal of the literature consists of shadow-boxing: each side's favorite cases embarrass only the other side's question, and each side's favorite repairs answer only its own.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a survey, and a diagnosis with reasons. Is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a symptom that the question has been posed in the wrong form? If substantive, what exactly is the hidden question doing the work — the individuation of kinds of reasons, the direction of explanation between reasons and fittingness, or the explanatory ambitions of the analysis itself? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a case all parties are required to classify the same way, a principled criterion for individuating kinds of reasons, a sharper statement of what the analysis is meant to accomplish — or should we expect no progress at all, and if so, why?
