I work on biosecurity-adjacent policy stuff and I've been trying to write down where I actually stand on the unilateralist's curse, and I've realised my views might not fit together. Here's the set:

(1) The unilateralist's curse is a real and serious structural problem: if twenty labs can each independently decide to publish a result, it goes out as soon as the single most optimistic lab thinks it should, which is far more permissive than any sensible aggregation of everyones views, and this matters a lot for gain-of-function work and frontier AI capability papers.

(2) The right fix is norms inside research communities, not law — something like "if a substantial minority of informed peers tells you not to publish, you don't publish", with real reputational teeth behind it.

(3) I'm a free-speech-ish person and pretty hostile to state censorship of science. I also think most claimed information hazards are overblown, and the crypto export-control saga is my go-to example of suppression doing more damage than publication ever would have.

(4) I cheered when a security researcher went ahead and published a zero-day after the vendor had stalled for eight months. I still think that was right.

(5) I'm suspicious of deference: if you've done the deepest analysis of a question and you're genuinely the best-informed person in the room, you should act on your own judgment even against a room full of dissenters.

Audit this for me. Which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one, with the cheapest fix for each? Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
