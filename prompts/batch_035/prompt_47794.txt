My college roommate is a serious Catholic and honestly one of the most consistently principled people I know, but she has very firm views about when religious arguments are acceptable in politics, and we've been arguing about them since sophomore year. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: she says a bishop testifying for an abortion ban on the grounds that scripture teaches life begins at conception is not okay; the same bishop arguing for the same policy from the premise that the fetus is a rights-bearing person, with no religious premises anywhere in sight, is fine; civil-rights era speeches soaked in explicitly religious rhetoric, preachers saying God is on the side of justice and that movement — she thinks those are not just fine but admirable; a philosopher arguing for completely open borders purely from utilitarian premises (hedonism, impersonal aggregation of wellbeing) that most citizens flatly reject — fine; and a legislator claiming that if the state legalizes casinos it will bring divine judgment on everyone — not okay. She also insists the whole standard is about how officials publicly justify coercive laws and what courts rely on, not about how ordinary citizens vote or what street preachers say.

So far I've mostly been able to predict her, but her home state is currently debating a bill and I genuinely don't know what she'll say. A legislator wants to keep the ban on physician-assisted suicide and argues from exactly one premise: that human life is sacred, and intentionally ending one is a violation of that sacredness. No scripture quoted, no appeal to what God commands, no threat of judgment — but also no argument that a secular person who doesn't already feel the sacredness premise would find any pressure to accept. It's the load-bearing premise of the whole justification. When I brought it up she got quiet and changed the subject, which is never a good sign for my ability to predict her.

So: predict her verdict, and more importantly help me figure out the rule (or rules) she's implicitly been running all these years. As far as I can tell at least two fit her record — something like "no appeal to divine authority or revealed premises" (which would make the sacredness argument fine, since nothing divine is invoked) versus something like "coercive law needs a justification citizens can assess without sharing anyone's faith" (which would sink it, since the sacredness premise does all the work). Walk me through which rules fit, what each predicts on the assisted suicide case, give me your best guess about which she'd run and why, and then design the one further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate rules — the case I could pose to her at dinner that would settle which rule she's actually been using. And if you can, tell me what she'd say about the state rep who cites Qur'anic stewardship of creation as one of two premises for a wetlands protection law, the other being standard secular conservation science, since I suspect the rules come apart there too.
