I'm deep in worldbuilding for a novel and I've hit a snag that turned out to be more philosophical than I expected. The people in my story speak a conlang I've been constructing, and early on I made two design choices that seemed cool at the time: the language has no grammatical tense at all (time is marked by aspect and context, so "last autumn" comes out something like "after-ripening time"), and it uses absolute directions instead of left/right — you literally can't say "the cup is on your left," you have to say "the cup is to the northeast of you."

When I showed my draft chapters to a friend who studied linguistics, she said I'd accidentally committed myself to a strong version of the idea that language determines thought, which she claims got debunked decades ago, and that realistically my speakers would just be people who talk funny but think exactly like you and me. My other friend (a programmer, sigh) insists the opposite: that without tense the speakers literally couldn't conceive of the past as past, so I'm obligated to write them with a totally alien relationship to time — like, barely any narrative memory, no history-keeping, the works.

Who's closer to right, and more importantly for the book: what's actually realistic? Are there real languages that do these things — tenseless ones, ones with absolute directions instead of left/right — and if so, what do their speakers actually seem to do differently versus not at all? Is there a middle position where the grammar nudges habits of attention without imprisoning thought? I don't want cartoon aliens, but I also dont want to be lazy and ignore my own worldbuilding. Basically: how much of a character's inner life should the grammar of their language dictate, and where's the line between "plausibly shaped" and magical thinking?
