I am a novelist with a background in narrative craft rather than philosophy, and I have been drafting a long-form essay intended for a general literary magazine on the theme of contingency in history — the old "for want of a nail" idea. Since the essay strays into territory where philosophers of history and historiographers work, I would be grateful for comments from someone with the relevant training before I send it out. Below are the essay's draft argument, roughly as I have it so far, followed by the specific kinds of feedback I want.

The draft, provisionally titled The Nail and the Plot, runs as follows.

First section: I claim that the familiar trope of the lost nail deciding a kingdom is not a claim about history at all but a claim about stories. Small causes with vast effects are the cheapest possible way to generate narrative significance, because disproportion between cause and consequence is precisely what makes an anecdote memorable and quotable. So the trope survives not because history is full of nails but because nails are what narrative selection preserves. The record itself, I suggest, is a machine for discarding the boring middle-sized causes and keeping the disproportionate ones.

Second section: I introduce a distinction I have not seen stated this way, though I doubt it is original. Call some historical events switch-points — moments where a trajectory could have gone otherwise in a way that propagates forward indefinitely (a defection, a rider taking the wrong road, a monarch dying heirless) — and call other historical facts pressures — structural conditions such as soil, demography, fiscal arrangements, or a church's machinery, which do not decide outcomes but constrain which switch-points exist and how much they can matter. My claim is that most quarrels about whether history is "contingent" or "determined" are really quarrels about where, in a given case, the ratio of pressures to switch-points lies, and that both sides keep talking past each other because the words contingent and determined are doing double duty between causal claims (what actually produced the outcome) and modal claims (how else things could have gone).

Third section: I press what I half-seriously call an inverse-square law of historical contingency: the further an event lies from a historian's own subfield, the more contingent it looks to them, and the closer it lies, the more overdetermined it looks. The specialist sees the deep pressures; the general reader sees only the nail. I offer this as an epistemic, not metaphysical, observation — a fact about historians as observers rather than about history itself.

Fourth section: I bring in Tolstoy, who in the essayistic passages of War and Peace mocked the great-man theory of history and argued that historians explain an event by citing a few salient persons while ignoring the innumerable small causes, and that no single cause deserves the credit. I read him as an early ally of my second section — as insisting on pressures over switch-points — but I also accuse him of a sleight of hand: he rails against contingency-by-great-men only to smuggle in an equally narrative necessity, the tide of collective will, which is really the novel's own demand for large-scale structure projected onto the past.

Fifth section: I turn to my home ground and argue that the practice of writing a novel is the best apprenticeship for this problem, because plotting is exactly the art of disguising contingency as necessity. The novelist plants a rifle in the first act and fires it in the last, and the reader experiences the ending as inevitable; but any novelist knows the rifle was put there after the ending was chosen, and that a hundred other rifles went unused. Plots are histories written backwards. I close by suggesting that this is why both nail-anecdotes and grand determinist schemas feel false to the working novelist: they are both stories about history wearing the costume of one or the other.

What I would like from you, in order of importance:

1. Accuracy and rigour. Where does my handling of the causal-versus-modal distinction go wrong or go slack? Is the switch-point/pressure distinction coherent, does it collapse into distinctions that already exist under other names, and if so, whose? Is my reading of Tolstoy defensible or a caricature — in particular, is my accusation in the fourth section fair, or does it rest on conflating his literary practice with his philosophy of history?

2. The inverse-square claim. Has something like this been argued seriously by historians or sociologists of knowledge? If it has, I would rather cite them than pretend to novelty. If it has not, is that because it is false, or because it is obvious?

3. Interest. Be blunt about which section a reader would put the essay down during. I suspect it is the fifth, but it is also the reason I wanted to write the thing at all, so tell me honestly whether it earns its place or is a novelist's self-indulgence.

4. Structure and suggestions. Any concrete recommendations — sections to merge, moves to add, a better organizing question — and especially anything in the current draft that will make an informed reader conclude that I do not know what I am talking about.

Assume I can follow technical material if it is explained; do not spare me the real vocabulary of the field. I would rather be corrected now than in print.
