I'm a philosopher by training, and next month I'm giving a talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on bargaining and armed conflict, which means a 20-minute Q&A I'm dreading. The thesis I'm defending: many destructive contests are Pareto-inefficient -- both sides would be better off with some alternative to fighting -- but they can't bargain their way to it, because any negotiated split of the gains fails (no one accepts a deal that looks worse than their odds in a fight, and no deal is self-enforcing anyway). The move I'm pushing is what the decision-theory literature calls a safe Pareto improvement: a standing commitment that, in exactly those situations where the costly contest would have broken out anyway, both parties instead follow some fixed cheap procedure -- a coin flip, or the pre-agreed default stands -- and each party signs on in advance because, from its own point of view, the procedure beats the fight it was privately expecting to win, once you count the cost of fighting. The example carrying the whole talk: two states with a valley they've skirmished over for decades, where even the side that's confident it would win a war over it expects the war to cost more than the valley is worth -- the fighting recurs anyway because no bargained split is acceptable or enforceable -- and what they can do instead is commit in advance that whenever troops would mobilize over that valley, the coin comes out. The "safe" part is that nobody is gambling on being the winner: the rule only ever fires in cases where the fight would have happened regardless, and both sides judge the outcome better than that fight, each by its own lights. I should stress that my point isn't really about states -- I think these commitments matter more for the long run, for agents (human institutions, eventually AI systems) that can actually bind themselves -- but the workshop wants the war framing.

The room is about fifteen people and honestly I'm a bit out of my dept with them. Roughly half are just-war theorists -- double effect, proportionality, jus ad bellum, the whole vocabulary, and they will not talk in expected-utility terms at all. There are a couple of game theorists who will pounce on anything sloppy I say about commitments and beliefs. And there's a scattering of IR people who model war as a bargaining failure (the Fearon crowd), so a chunk of the room will hear my thesis as just "yes, and also flip a coin" and wonder what's new. What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the objections this particular room will actually raise, in the order they'll raise them, phrased the way each group would phrase them; a short reply to each; a note on which objections genuinely have force and what I should concede up front rather than fight; and the one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the session. Please also flag the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- a friend who works on verification treaties keeps telling me the whole thing lives or dies on what stops the loser of the coin flip from tearing up the deal the moment the coin lands badly, and I suspect she's right, so tell me how bad that one is and whether my long-run answer (agents that can genuinely bind themselves) is something I should say out loud or save for the paper.
