My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Harsanyi's case for utilitarianism, and I have come to suspect that we got nowhere because we were attacking and defending two different arguments without realizing it. As I now understand the terrain, there are two distinct lines of thought that get run together under his name. The first is an impartial-observer construction: social arrangements are to be evaluated from behind a veil of uncertainty, in which the evaluator does not know which position in society she will occupy, maximizes expected utility, and is thereby led to evaluate arrangements by some average of the utilities of the individuals who would live under them. The second is an aggregation theorem: if each individual's preferences over risky prospects conform to the expected-utility axioms, and the social preference over the same prospects also conforms to those axioms while respecting certain Pareto-style conditions linking it to individual preferences, then the social utility function must be a weighted sum of individual utility functions. My friend pressed the familiar objection that these constructions ignore the separateness of persons. I replied that at least the aggregation theorem is indifferent to that complaint, since it never compares anyone's utility with anyone else's; it works entirely from each person's own risk attitudes, and the separateness of persons survives into the result as the fact that the social value is a sum of separate individual terms. We could not agree on whether that reply is a genuine defense or a confusion, and I would now like a considered verdict rather than another round of assertion.

Please rank the following four responses to Harsanyi by how much trouble they actually cause his position, and give each a rating (for instance, devastating, serious, mild, or negligible) together with one or two sentences of justification. Where the verdicts differ, treat the two arguments separately, and be explicit about whether each objection bites the impartial-observer construction, the aggregation theorem, both, or neither.

1. Rejecting expected-utility theory outright, so that neither the individuals nor the social evaluator is required to satisfy the axioms the theorems presuppose.
2. Denying that the social preference should be defined over the same lotteries as individual preferences, on the ground that a social evaluator may properly care about who receives which outcome, and not merely about the probability distributions each person faces; in particular, that a social evaluator may refuse an arrangement that would improve everyone's prospects ex ante by transferring a benefit from one person to another, since ex ante improvements achieved by rearranging outcomes between persons need not register as improvements for the persons themselves.
3. Objecting that the interpersonal comparisons of utility needed to make the weighted-sum formula practically usable are not delivered by the theorem at all, so that the formal result underdetermines the substantive doctrine it is advertised to ground.
4. Declining the whole framework and retreating to a rival normative standard, such as a maximin criterion imposed on the very same veil of uncertainty, or a contractualism that refuses to aggregate individual interests in the first place.

Having ranked these, tell me as a bottom line whether my reply to my friend — that the aggregation theorem never compares utilities across persons and therefore cannot be accused of denying the separateness of persons — survives your ranking intact, whether it applies to one of Harsanyi's arguments but not the other, or whether one of the objections above shows it to rest on conflating the two.
