I teach an upper-level philosophy of science course (mostly juniors and seniors, a couple of grad students auditing) and I'm putting together the take-home final. The unit they just finished was on prediction, falsification, and rescue moves, and I want the centerpiece question to be about Malthus. The thing I keep circling is the "right but early" defense: Malthus's population principle predicted that population growth would outrun food supply and force wages down to subsistence, the demographic transition and the Green Revolution came along instead, and yet people still call Malthus "right about the mechanism, wrong about the timeline" — or even say he was vindicated *because* his warnings prompted the innovations that averted the catastrophe he predicted. I want a question that forces students to say whether that's a legitimate scientific stance or an immunizing stratagem, using the actual falsificationism/Lakatos machinery from the course rather than vibes.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks total. The question should seed the students with the concrete material so nobody is penalized for not remembering history: the 1798 Essay's arithmetic/geometric contrast, the demographic transition as the apparent refuter, the 1980 Simon–Ehrlich wager (five metals, Ehrlich paid up in 1990), and the fact that if you extend that same metal basket to 2011, Ehrlich would have won on four of the five — I want that last detail in there because it flips the "right but early" move back onto Simon, and students who notice that the move is available to *both sides* of any long-run prediction dispute are the ones who actually get the epistemology. The crux should be: what distinguishes a legitimate auxiliary-hypothesis revision from an ad hoc rescue when the prediction has a century-scale payoff date, and can a theory whose doom date keeps sliding forward ever earn disconfirmation at all? Also happy for the model answer to invoke the self-defeating/vindicated-by-warning twist (Malthusian fears causing the Green Revolution) as a case students have to classify, not just mention.

For the marking scheme, please give me the five or so must-hit points with their marks, and for each one a sentence on the mistake that costs marks — I've found the predictable failure mode is students who either (a) treat "it wasn't falsified, it just hasn't happened yet" as obviously fine or obviously cheating, with no criteria offered, or (b) recite the Popper/Lakatos definitions from lecture without applying them to the sliding-timescale problem at all. Also one bonus mark available for anyone who articulates what evidence, even in principle, *could* settle a dispute where both parties accept the mechanism and disagree only about the horizon. 90 minute time limit, open notes. Keep the wording of the question itself something I could paste straight onto the exam.
