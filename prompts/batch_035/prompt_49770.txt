I am a philosophy graduate student whose dissertation has recently hit a snag that is, unfortunately, of my own making. At my prospectus meeting last month, my two supervisors — one a fairly orthodox Quinean about ontology, the other sympathetic to a Carnapian deflationism about existence questions — spent the better part of an hour disputing whether my planned chapter on mereological composition addresses a substantive question at all. The meeting ended without a decision, and I have been told, in effect, to go away and write something that adjudicates the metaontological question as it arises in my case. Since the chapter has to be written either way, I have drafted a short essay arguing that one strand of the composition debate is merely verbal, and I would be grateful for your comments on it. The draft follows.

---

**How many things are in the gallery? A case for the verbalness, and then against it, of the composition dispute**

**1. The setup.** Take the familiar disagreement between the mereological nihilist, who holds that there are no composite objects (or, on Peter van Inwagen's variant, none but living things), and the mereological universalist, who holds that any non-overlapping collection of things composes a further thing. Place them before the same room and ask how many objects it contains. The nihilist counts the simples in the room; the universalist counts those simples plus, roughly, every candidate collection of them. Each side is confident, the disagreement appears to be about a plain matter of fact, and yet nothing in the room's future behavior is ever at issue between them: they agree on the complete microphysical description, on every causal consequence of it, and on every prediction about how the room will look, sound, and smell to any observer at any time.

**2. The swarm.** To make the point vivid, consider a case I want to introduce. A gallery commissions a work built from autonomous micro-drones, each about a cubic millimeter, that begin as a loose cloud and, over the course of a month, progressively physically dock with one another — first into clumps, then into a rigid lattice that is indistinguishable by touch and sight from a conventional steel sculpture. Suppose we can control a single parameter, the fraction of possible docking bonds that are present at a given time, and that we turn it up continuously from zero to one. The nihilist and the universalist will disagree, at every intermediate value of the parameter, about how many objects the gallery contains; and the disagreement will shrink and grow in odd ways — entire new objects will pop into the count when bonds are added, though no observer could notice anything. They will agree, at every value, about everything else.

**3. The elimination, and its failure.** The standard deflationary diagnosis now applies: run the elimination test. Restate each theorist's claims in microphysical vocabulary, without the words "object," "compose," or "how many." What survives is a body of claims on which the two theorists never disagreed, plus, on the nihilist's side, a recommendation about which sentences of English to keep. That, I want to say, shows the dispute to be verbal as far as the aims of fundamental ontology go. But here is where my draft turns against itself, and where I suspect the interesting part lies. The gallery's insurance policy indemnifies the owner for "damage to the artworks" — and the curator now asks, in all seriousness, whether the half-docked swarm is one artwork, so that a single act of vandalism is one claim or several thousand. This question cannot be answered in the microphysical vocabulary: nothing in the physics of bonding marks a threshold of arthood. Yet it is plainly not thereby empty. The term "object" (and "artwork") is here doing work that no amount of physics can do; the dispute has become load-bearing relative to a purpose — adjudicating claims under a contract — even though it remains idle relative to the purpose of inventorying the fundamental facts.

**4. Conclusion.** So the thesis I arrive at is this: whether a dispute is verbal is not a property of the dispute alone but of the dispute together with the aims of the parties to it. The composition debate is verbal relative to ontology and substantive relative to the curator's contract; and the interesting question is not which verdict is correct but what determines which purposes can confer substance on a term that fundamental description leaves unconstrained.

---

I would appreciate comments of three kinds. First, and most importantly: is the argument of section 3 sound? In particular, does the interest-relative conclusion follow from the case, or have I conflated a dispute's being verbal with its being merely practically important — that is, can a defender of the substantive view about composition reply that the insurance question inherits its content from the underlying ontological one, rather than conferring content on it? Second, a check on accuracy: have I mischaracterized the nihilist, the universalist, or the elimination test as it is usually formulated, in ways that a specialist would object to? Third, a judgment on interest: assuming the argument is sound, is this line — verbalness as relative to aims rather than intrinsic to the dispute — a contribution anyone would want to read, or a restatement of things deflationists have already said? Any suggestions for restructuring the chapter around the argument would also be welcome.
