I run a second-year course in philosophy of biology and we've just wrapped the units of selection module. To close it I want a sestina. The scene should be one small event: a young worker bee who never reproduces, spending her days in the nest feeding her sisters. As the stanzas turn, narrate the same life at different levels — the gene's bookkeeping (the copies are sitting in her sisters, so they get out either way), the individual's ledger (a whole life spent, no offspring of her own), the colony's division of labour doing its thing — and let the six fixed end-words be the causal ingredients that never change (I'm imagining something like cost, copy, sister, colony, self, death), so the form enacts what kept coming up in our seminars: the same facts shuffled between spreadsheets, with half the gene-vs-group fight looking like a dispute over who's doing the accounting. One stanza can turn on the Price equation's between-group/within-group split if you can make it sing. The envoi is the only place the poem is allowed to ask outright whether these are three explanations or one with three names. Don't let it become either a Dawkins pep rally or a group-selection revenge piece — somone who knows the Williams/Sober side of the literature should find it fair, and someone who doesn't should just find it a good poem. If you reckon a sestina is the wrong form here and something else would do the work better, say so plainly and propose it rather than ploughing ahead, and add a short note after the poem flagging anywhere you lean on a contested reading.
