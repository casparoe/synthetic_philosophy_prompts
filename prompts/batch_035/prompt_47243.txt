I work as an economic appraiser at a national transport ministry, and my division's output is ultimately a single number: for any proposed project — a new metro line, a motorway widening, a bus-priority corridor — we produce a monetized benefit–cost ratio. Let me lay out the ingredients, because I've come to suspect their philosophical weight is greater than we admit. We value travel-time savings by inferring what commuters would pay to save time, drawing on both "revealed preference" studies (actual choices between slower-but-cheaper and faster-but-costlier routes and modes) and "stated preference" surveys in which respondents pick among hypothetical bundles. We then apply a uniform standard value of time to everyone, distinguish commuting time from employer-paid business travel, and apply premium weights to time spent walking to a stop or waiting for a connection — our guidance values waiting time at roughly twice in-vehicle time. We infer the value of avoided statistical deaths from the wage premiums workers demand for risky occupations, then add all these monetized benefits — hours saved, deaths averted, accidents avoided — across millions of heterogeneous citizens, discount them into the future, and subtract costs. Yet the theoretical training I received alongside this work was strict Samuelian micro: utility is ordinal, utility functions are artificial constructs meaningful only up to monotone transformation, and interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless. My puzzle is that the apparatus I help operate seems to presuppose nearly the opposite: a cardinal utility, measured in money, summable across persons.

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of utility and preference that this appraisal practice rests on. Which components strictly require the money-metric, interpersonally comparable view — is the aggregation step the only one, or does even the standard value of time (uniform across income levels, despite willingness-to-pay for time tracking income) already commit us to something cardinal? Which components could be re-derived under a strictly ordinalist or behaviorist reading, so that the Samuelian foundations and the practice could be reconciled? And where is the practice merely underdetermined — compatible with several theories of wellbeing — such that its authority doesn't actually depend on any of them? Please also confront the tensions I can see but can't diagnose myself: we claim a behaviorist foundation in observed choice, yet we fill data gaps with answers to hypothetical surveys (what does it mean to "reveal" a preference no one has ever acted on?); we claim to respect preferences, yet we override several the market would price (we don't let the value of a statistical life vary with the victim's income, and we value waiting time above in-vehicle time even where the traveler's actual trade-offs suggest the reverse); and the whole edifice presents itself as being about preferences while its numbers are used to make interpersonal and intergenerational welfare judgments via discounting.

Go through the apparatus component by component — the value of time, the waiting-time premium, the value of statistical life, the aggregation and discounting step, and the mixture of revealed and stated data — and for each component say what it presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it's underdetermined. Then tell me whether the ministry's implicit view amounts to a coherent theory or an unstable hybrid; if a hybrid, which strand would have to be abandoned for consistency, and what would concretely change in our numbers if we rebuilt the appraisal on the strand you'd keep. Conclude with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing a short label naming the implicit view — for instance, cardinal money-metric utilitarianism, ordinalist behaviorism with a compensation convention, or incoherent hybrid — a few words at most.
