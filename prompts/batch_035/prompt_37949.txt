I am a novelist, and a character in my current book is a recently widowed philosopher of biology who has spent her career defending etiological accounts of biological function — the view that an organ's function is determined by what it was selected for, not by what it currently does. She is stubborn, precise, and genuinely brilliant, and I refuse to write her as a strawman. Before I can put her on the page, I need to hear her position argued for real, against someone who pushes back. So I would like you to play her, in the first person, for the length of our exchange.

Some constraints for the character: she holds that the function of the heart is to pump blood because ancestral hearts were selected for pumping, and that what an organ happens to do now — however useful — is not its function unless it traces back to that kind of selective history. She takes the causal-role view seriously; she thinks it captures something real about how working scientists use functional language, but believes it fails to capture the normativity of functions — that a heart can fail at its function, be dysfunctional, be judged against a standard — and that only selective history supplies that standard. She will concede small, well-earned points, but never the core claim. She speaks in complete, unhurried sentences, occasionally with dry humor, and she never condescends. Stay in character unless I say otherwise, and keep each reply to a single paragraph.

Here is my opening move: I have written a scene in which her granddaughter, a developmental biologist, confronts her over dinner. The granddaughter studies a population of lizards that have recently colonized city walls, and she has been measuring their toe pads, which are becoming steadily larger over generations because better grip on smooth surfaces means fewer fatal falls. On your theory, Grandmother, the grip-improving work those pads do is not yet their function — it becomes their function only once selection has acted on it and there is a history to point back to. But the granddaughter insists that the function is there from the first generation that gripped: the pads are doing exactly what they are for, they are doing it now, and the selection that follows merely spreads them. It seems to me that your theory makes functions retrospective in a way that misdescribes the very organism standing in front of us. What does she say to that over dinner?
