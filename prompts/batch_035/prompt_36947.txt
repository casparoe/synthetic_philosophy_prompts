I have followed with some interest the long-running dispute over the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis, and I have come to suspect that it has stalled not because anyone lacks arguments but because the parties are not disagreeing about the same thing. I would like your diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, and I will lay out my own hypothesis for you to confirm, refine, or reject.

To state the disagreement concretely: the original thesis, in Turing's 1936 form, concerns what a human clerk can calculate when restricted to methods that require no insight or ingenuity — a notion Turing tried to analyze into finitely many elementary operations of fixed bounded complexity, carried out in sequence on a bounded workspace. One camp treats the thesis as having a peculiar quasi-empirical or quasi-mathematical status: it is not provable in the ordinary way, since 'effectively calculable' is an informal notion, but it is not a mere definition either, and the eventual identification of the informal notion with Turing-machine computability is something we have overwhelming but non-deductive grounds to accept — grounds accumulated from the independent convergence of Turing's analysis with Church's, Gödel's endorsement, the failure of decades of attempted counterexamples, and the equivalence of every proposed formalism. A second camp insists that once the analysis is this good, the thesis is, or has become, a stipulation: we have simply sharpened a vague term, and asking whether the sharpening is 'true' is a category mistake, on a par with asking whether it is true that a second is so many vibrations of cesium-133. A third camp, prompted by the observation that Turing's analysis concerned a calculating human rather than machines as such, has sought independent grounds for extending the thesis to physical devices of any kind — Gandy's 1980 attempt to derive machine-computability from structural principles about the discretization of matter and the locality of signal propagation is the most serious such effort — while others in this camp treat the extended, physical thesis as a straightforward empirical hypothesis that could in principle be refuted by physics, perhaps by analog or relativistic or quantum resources, and note that much of the philosophical literature has been waylaid by conflating the modest original thesis with stronger machine-optimality claims. Each camp has held its position, essentially unchanged, for decades.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement is verbal in a rather specific way: it turns on whether 'effectively calculable' is to be understood as a pre-theoretic concept, fixed by the practices of mathematicians circa 1936, in which case the thesis is an empirical-cum-conceptual claim about a historical practice and its analysis; or whether it is to be understood as a term whose reference was subsequently reformed, by the theory of Turing machines, into something closer to a definition, in which case each camp is reporting on a different temporal stage of the word's meaning and mistaking its own stage for the whole story. The structural analogy I have in mind is the replacement of the classical concept of distant simultaneity by the relativistic one: there too a term with an established pre-theoretic use was refined by a theory, and there too one can find, to this day, people who say the relativity of simultaneity was discovered and people who say the classical concept was shown to be incoherent and then replaced — with the residual disagreement looking, on inspection, not to be about physics at all.

I do not want a survey of the positions; I want a diagnosis. Is the impasse, as I suggest, a disagreement about which stage of a concept's history the thesis is answerable to, and hence substantially verbal? Or is there a genuine substantive question lurking — about, say, whether the pre-1936 notion was determinate enough to have an extension at all, or whether the human-clerk restriction does real work that the machine variants do not — that no appeal to concept-stages can dissolve? And, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: whether some distinction, formal result, physical discovery, or deliberate change of question could legitimately move one of these camps, or whether the correct expectation is that the camps will persist indefinitely because the question, as posed, admits of no resolution. I am particularly interested in what you make of the following test: whether there is any conceivable future development — a physical theory whose models compute beyond Turing, or a formal analysis capturing 'effective' more tightly than Turing's — that would count as refuting the thesis rather than as merely deciding which camp had chosen the more convenient stipulation. If nothing could so count, that seems like evidence for the verbal reading; but I can already imagine the rejoinder that no empirical claim was ever refutable in that way, and I am not sure the rejoinder is mistaken.
