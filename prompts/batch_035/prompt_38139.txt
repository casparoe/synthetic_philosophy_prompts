For the take-home final in my upper-level seminar on democratic theory and its discontents, draft one essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks. The question should concern the ethics of voting for the lesser evil, but it must not be answerable by reciting the standard civic-duty material. Set it around a case of my devising: a voter in a genuinely competitive constituency faces three candidates — a frontrunner whose platform she considers seriously unjust, a slightly less bad challenger she believes will win without her vote, and a candidate she actually endorses who is polling in low single digits. She thinks voting is not merely an instrument for electing people but also a way of standing for something; she also thinks letting the frontrunner win through her abstention or protest vote would be worse. The question should force students to separate two arguments that usually run together: the causal one (that her vote is a tiny contribution to an outcome, in which case she must explain why a negligible causal effect generates any obligation at all, and why the same reasoning doesn't dissolve the duty to vote for anyone) and the expressive one (that casting a ballot is partly an act of endorsement, in which case she must explain how one can be required to endorse what one disavows). The model answer should show which argument, if either, carries the weight, and the marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an A from a B — in particular, penalizing students who assert the "one vote never matters" truism without confronting its implications for the duty to vote at all, and rewarding students who notice that the expressive argument cuts against compulsory-voting intuitions the same students typically hold. Include one note on how students most often go wrong.
