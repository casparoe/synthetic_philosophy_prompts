I am about two-thirds through a novel set in a small farming town, and I have reached a chapter I am uncertain how to carry off honestly. At the annual firemen's fundraiser, a traveling auctioneer stages what he calls his last-two-pay novelty: a crisp twenty-dollar bill is sold to the highest bidder, but once the bidding has passed a dollar, the second-highest bidder must also hand over his last offer and receives nothing. My narrator's brother, Hal, a retired schoolteacher who has prided himself all his life on never being gulled, enters almost as a joke at fifty cents and finds himself locked against the feed-store owner, whose son works for Hal's daughter. By the time I have written them to nineteen dollars, it is clear both men will end the evening with a loss, and I must decide how the scene resolves.

I would be grateful for guidance on two fronts. First, the analytical one: setting aside pride and the crowd, is there any principled point at which Hal's continuing to bid is rational, or is the game so constructed that each individual overbid looks locally sensible while guaranteeing a worse aggregate outcome — and if so, what does that tell me the scene is actually about? Second, the psychological and moral one: I want Hal's persistence to be more than a man losing money. I want the chapter to carry the weight of how he explains his own bidding to himself while it is happening — the story he tells about recovering what he has put in, about not being the one who flinches, about what folding would cost him in that room. Is it truer to him, as a man who trusts his own reason, that he recognizes the trap in a flash of clarity and folds anyway, or that he never quite formulates it? And ought the novel to let him exit the tent believing he played it right? Any counsel on how to shape the scene so that it earns its ending rather than merely depicting a folly would be most welcome.
