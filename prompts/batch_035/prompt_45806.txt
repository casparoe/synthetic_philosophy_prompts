I'm writing a term paper arguing that morality cannot be reduced to facts about evolutionary biology, and my professor has marked me down on a point I don't think matters. I wrote that Hume committed the naturalistic fallacy when he observed that no ought can be derived from an is, and that Moore, in Principia Ethica, simply gave Hume's gap a name. She insists the correction is substantive: the naturalistic fallacy is Moore's, from 1903, and the two arguments are distinct. To me this looks like pedantry, because both arguments forbid the same move — passing off the normative as merely natural. Moore's own discussion seems to confirm my reading: he concedes that we can state the physical equivalent of yellow, the light-vibrations that stimulate a normal eye, but insists those vibrations are merely what corresponds in space to the yellow we actually perceive, not the color itself. Substitute the right evolutionary account for the light-vibrations and you get exactly Hume's point that you can never reach the normative from the descriptive. If that's right, my professor is quibbling over labels, and I shouldn't lose points for it. If she isn't right, then I need to understand what each argument actually forbids, because I can't see how anyone could accept one and reject the other. Which is it? And, more urgently for my grade: given that my thesis is about whether evolutionary biology can ground ethics, which of the two arguments should the paper actually be built on?
