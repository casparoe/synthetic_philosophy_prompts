I serve on the research ethics board of a comprehensive cancer center, and I need a decision memo for our next session. We are reviewing the renewal of a Phase II trial of psilocybin-assisted therapy for existential distress in patients with advanced cancer, and the review has split the board down the middle. In the first cohort, several participants reported that the sessions did not merely relieve their anxiety but reorganized their values: one declined a further round of palliative chemotherapy he had been eager to receive before dosing; another redirected his estate toward a spiritual community he had joined weeks afterward; a third described her pre-trial self as someone whose judgments she no longer trusts. Our current consent form discloses "lasting changes in values or worldview" in the same table as transient headache and elevated blood pressure. Half the board holds that this discharges our duty: the participants are competent adults, they were warned, and most of them welcomed the changes. The other half argues that the disclosure is a formality masking a structural problem: a participant cannot weigh, in advance, a change to the very preferences that would be doing the weighing, and our entire model of informed consent — which asks whether the participant understands the risks and benefits in light of his own values — presupposes that the values evaluating the outcome afterward will be the ones consenting now. On this view, either the consent process needs fundamental redesign or some transformative interventions cannot be meaningfully consented to at all, and the trial should not continue in its present form.

Please draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — that we can circulate before Thursday. It should reach a concrete bottom line and, if you recommend conditions, specify them in an implementable way: anything you would change about the consent process and its framing of value change, any waiting-period or decision-delay mechanism before major post-session decisions, any role for family notification or independent review, and how trial staff should respond when a participant's post-session decision contradicts his documented prior wishes. It must engage the philosophical problem directly rather than resolving it by reciting autonomy principles — in particular, address the objection that people routinely and legitimately choose transformative experiences such as marriage, migration, military service, or ordination without anyone concluding consent is impossible, and explain what, if anything, is different when the transformation is rapid, chemically occasioned, and largely unforeseeable in its content. Give the losing side's strongest point a fair hearing rather than papering over it, and say what the board should do if it cannot agree on a philosophical framework — specifically, how much deference is owed to the post-session participant's own retrospective endorsement of her changed values, and who, in the board's eyes, is now the relevant bearer of the earlier consent.
