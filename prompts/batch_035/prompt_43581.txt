A long argument with a friend about political ethics ended unresolved, and before I write up my side of it, I want to check whether my own position actually hangs together. Here is roughly where I stand: (1) dirty hands is real — a leader can face situations where the right thing to do is a morally wrong act (a lie, a betrayal, an order that harms) undertaken to avert a greater catastrophe, and I'm broadly with Walzer in thinking a politician too fastidious ever to get his hands dirty is a danger to the rest of us; (2) when this happens, the act remains genuinely wrong — its rightness does not cancel its wrongness, the agent has something to answer for, and feeling guilt afterward is appropriate rather than neurotic; (3) I reject consequentialism in ethics generally; (4) I reject the idea of a distinct political morality — the same norms that bind private persons bind politicians, and politics does not get its own rulebook; and (5) the deliberate targeting of innocents is never permissible, whatever the stakes — permissible harm to innocents must be foreseen but never intended. My friend's refrain, throughout the argument, was that on points (1) and (2) I am simply a consequentialist about politics who refuses to say so. Is he right, or does the package hold together? Please tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and how badly, which tensions only look real and dissolve on inspection, and what the cheapest revision would be in each case — ordered by severity rather than presented as a flat list. I honestly don't know where the problem is, so don't assume I'm protecting any particular one of these.
