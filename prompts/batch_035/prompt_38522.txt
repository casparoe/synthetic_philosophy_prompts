A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the surprise exam paradox, and I came away committed to a particular resolution of it. I would now like that position stress-tested.

The setup as we used it: on Friday, the teacher announces that there will be an exam at some point during the next school week (Monday through Friday), and that on the morning of the exam day the students will not be in a position to know, on the basis of the announcement plus everything that has happened so far, that the exam is that day. The famous student reasoning eliminates Friday first: were the exam on Friday, then by Thursday evening the students, having ruled out Monday through Thursday, would know it was Friday, contradicting the announcement; then Thursday, by the same reasoning run one day earlier, on down to Monday — from which they conclude the exam cannot occur at all, and are then surprised by the Wednesday exam.

The position I came to hold is that the backward induction fails at the very first step, and for a self-referential reason. The students' Thursday-evening "knowledge" that the exam is on Friday would have to be derived from the announcement's truth, yet the announcement is precisely what the argument is being used to discredit, and the announcement refers to the students' own epistemic state, which makes the deduction circular. My friend pressed the analogy with Moore-paradoxical assertions — an assertion of the form "the exam is on Friday, but you won't believe it in advance" — and with the formal knower paradox, where a knowledge attribution concerning a sentence that contains that very attribution can be inconsistent even though the sentence's content is unproblematic. On my view that is exactly what happens here: the announcement is consistent and satisfiable, the teacher can hold a genuinely surprising exam, and the students' elimination of Friday is not a piece of knowledge but the first move into a Moorean trap.

She was not persuaded, and left me with two problems I have not fully answered. First, the one-day version: if the teacher announces only that there will be an exam tomorrow, and that you will not know in advance that it is tomorrow, then there is no induction at all, and yet the announcement still seems to put the students in an impossible epistemic position — which suggests the trouble lies in the announcement's structure rather than in the iterability of the reasoning, and that threatens my claim that the announcement is consistent. Second, she observed that my position is perilously close to the early Quine-style move — the students cannot know the announcement to be true, so their final deduction lacks a premise — and that if I take that line, I owe an account of why the students' conditional reasoning is fine all week ("if there is an exam left, it must be Friday") while their outright knowledge claim collapses at the moment it becomes needed, without this looking like an ad hoc epicycle installed wherever the argument gets embarrassing.

So: what would count against my position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. Include formal results — if someone could derive a genuine contradiction from a demonstrably consistent formalization of the announcement, that would be close to fatal, and I would like to know how live that possibility actually is. Include cases and variants my position must handle but struggles with: the one-day version, iterated re-announcements of the same condition, versions where the surprise condition is weakened to the absence of certainty rather than the absence of knowledge. Include results elsewhere that my position stands or falls with — about self-reference, about the pragmatics of assertion, about whether epistemic closure holds for self-referential bodies of information. Throughout, distinguish what I, as a defender of this resolution, would be obliged to concede as genuinely counting against it from what I could reasonably dismiss as an artifact of the telling. And tell me honestly whether the position can be quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — whether every apparent counterexample can be absorbed as "one more Moorean blindspot" — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a resolution of this kind or simply what self-reference does to any solution of the paradox.
