I'm a third-year PhD student in behavioral ecology, and I've hit a methodological disagreement with my advisor that I suspect has a deeper structure than either of us has been articulating, but I can't quite name it, and my dissertation proposal is due in six weeks.

The empirical situation: I study a ground-nesting bird across five populations. In two of them, females line their nests with conspicuous pale stones; in the other three, they don't. There's no obvious habitat variable that tracks the difference. My advisor wants the proposal built around a sexual-selection story: the decoration is a signal of female condition, males preferentially invest in parental care at decorated nests, and I should formalize the hypothesis, derive its predictions (decoration degree correlates with condition, males respond to experimental removal of stones, etc.), and test them. He's dismissive of the alternatives I keep raising — that the behavior is a side effect of a general attraction to light-colored objects that evolved for some other function entirely, that it drifted up in frequency in two small founder populations, or that it's transmitted socially and is functionally arbitrary. His view is that these alternatives are unfalsifiable hand-waving, that "drift" and "byproduct" can explain anything after the fact and therefore explain nothing, and that the only productive scientific posture is to articulate the best adaptive hypothesis as sharply as possible and put it at risk.

I have two problems. First, I genuinely can't tell whether his dismissal is right: when, if ever, does an appeal to a nonadaptive explanation count as a substantive hypothesis with testable predictions rather than an escape hatch? Second, even granting that his preferred hypothesis is the most testable one, it doesn't follow that it's the most likely one, and I don't want to write a proposal whose framework quietly presupposes the conclusion. Yet if I frame the project around skepticism, I risk proposing nothing that could ever be confirmed.

So my question: how should I think about where the burden of proof actually sits here, and is there a principled way to design the proposal — specific comparisons, experiments, population-genetic reasoning, whatever — so that the adaptive story and the alternatives are genuinely competing rather than one of them being protected from test by fiat? I'd like advice that takes seriously both my advisor's insistence on testability and my worry that testability is doing rhetorical work it shouldn't.
