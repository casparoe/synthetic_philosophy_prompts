I'm a graduate TA running the discussion section for an upper-level epistemology course, and next week's topic is the norms of assertion. The professor has already assigned the standard debate about whether assertion is governed by a knowledge rule, a truth rule, or a belief rule, so my students will arrive having read the theory but, predictably, without feeling it. I have 22 students, one 75-minute section, a whiteboard, a deck of cards, and a strong preference for not using real money. Design a structured activity in which students actually assert things to each other under varying rule sets, run into the difference between the candidate norms themselves, and only get the theoretical vocabulary afterwards in the debrief. Here is the sort of thing I have in mind, though please improve on it: a series of rounds in which one group of students draws cards or is handed lottery-style tickets (say, 20 tickets, one winner) and must decide whether to assert a claim to a second group, who must then accept or reject the claim under some forced-choice rule; the payoff scheme shifts from round to round, sometimes penalizing only false assertions, sometimes penalizing assertions made without adequate backing, sometimes making the hearer's losses the asserter's problem too, so that assertion starts functioning like a guarantee the speaker has underwritten. I want the lottery-style case to do real work — the student holding 19 of the losing tickets who asserts that hers will lose should find the move both tempting and somehow shameful — and I would like at least one round that tempts someone into a Moore-style assertion of the form: such-and-such is true, but I have no idea whether it is.

Please give me the full package: concrete rules and materials for each round, the payoff scheme in points rather than money, a timeline that fits 75 minutes with the debrief included, and a description of how the debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the knowledge, truth, and belief rules — including which round is supposed to embarrass which rule, and why the hearers' reactions (not the facilitator's verdicts) are the evidence. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I expect things like students treating assertion as a dare, a lucky guesser becoming a class hero and muddying the point, hearers forgetting the forced-choice rule, or too few trials for the payoff structure to bite — say how I should handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce: my worry is that in ordinary conversation the sanction attached to a false or unknowing assertion seems internal to the practice, whereas in a game the penalties are just stipulated by me, so the facilitator has to supply something the game can't show — explain what that something is and how to bridge it in the debrief. Please write the whole answer in American English.
