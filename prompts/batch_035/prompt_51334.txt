I am a graduate student who has spent this year serving as moderator and rapporteur for a structured exchange between two members of my committee, and I now have to draft the document that exchange was supposed to produce. Professor A is a committed deontologist who holds that there are agent-centered constraints: certain acts, like killing an innocent person, are simply not to be done by me, whatever the consequences of not doing them. Professor B is a consequentialist who thinks this position collapses under its own paradox. Their exchanges have been genuinely good-faith but have started going in circles, and the chair of my committee — who organized the exchange and will read the final document — has asked me to prepare the joint statement they could both sign. Since I drafted the summaries of every session, my credibility with both of them depends on the draft being neither vacuous nor slanted.

The core of their dispute, so that the draft is self-contained: the deontologist accepts that each of five killings, committed by five separate wrongdoers, is exactly as wrong as the one killing I could commit to prevent all five. Yet she maintains that I may not commit it. Professor B argues this is incoherent — that a constraint against killing cannot coherently forbid the act that minimizes the number of violations of that very constraint — and that the position gets worse, not better, when iterated: a world of deontologists each refusing to kill one to prevent five will contain far more killings than a world of consequentialists. Professor A responds that this misrepresents the constraint, which governs *my* agency rather than tallying wrongs done by *others*.

Please draft the joint statement. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what the consequentialist's challenge actually establishes (and what it doesn't), about which adjacent positions both reject (e.g., both may reject threshold deontology as unstable, or as secretly consequentialist — settle only what the parties could genuinely co-sign), and about any part of the dispute that both would concede is partly verbal, e.g., whether "deontology" is being defined as constraint-based or merely as non-consequentialism.

2. A disagreements table with rows for each remaining substantive disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view. Include at least: whether constraints are grounded in an agent-relative wronging relation to the particular victim or must answer to agent-neutral value; whether the doing/allowing distinction carries intrinsic weight or is a heuristic; and whether iterated constraint-following producing more total killings counts against the constraints or is simply irrelevant to them.

3. A cruxes section: for each crux, a concrete case or argument both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about the case written down in advance. Since this is ethics, the "tests" will be thought experiments — for example, variants where the same agent faces the kill-one-to-prevent-five choice repeatedly, or where the person I would kill is one of the five future victims, or where the five killings would be done by my own earlier act.

Finally, since I will have to defend this draft in front of both professors: append a short section flagging which agreed claims are most likely to be rejected by one side as not genuinely agreed, where the wording most plausibly smuggles in a contested assumption, and what consideration, if raised, would most likely force you to restructure the crux list itself.
