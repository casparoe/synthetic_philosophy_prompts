I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized payments company, and I've been made incident owner for an outage that I can't stop thinking about, for reasons that aren't really technical. A race condition in our ledger service double-credited about $1.2 million across roughly six hundred customer accounts before anyone caught it. By the time we froze the affected accounts, many customers had already moved the extra money out and spent it — rent, cars, one person paid off student loans in full. Our general counsel needs a recommendation on who should eat the loss, and the question the team keeps arguing about is what the double credit actually *was*.

One camp says we corrupted a record, full stop: a balance is just an integer in a database, the bug wrote wrong values, and a customer who spent money that was never really there owes it back, same as someone who spends against a misprinted gift card. The other camp says that can't be right either, because for weeks the extra balances functioned exactly like money — people paid real bills with them — and, they point out, the balances our system writes under normal conditions are *also* just integers in a database, and those are unquestionably money. So either the bug created real money, or our normal operations never create real money either, and both of those sound wrong to me.

I have to write the postmortem and a recommendation. Leaving aside what the law says, is there a principled difference between a balance our bug produced and a balance our system produces on purpose? What exactly is it that makes one of them money and the other one not — and does that difference settle who should bear the loss when the numbers were wrong?
