Quick background in case it helps: I'm a philosophy of mind / cognitive science grad student applying for a one-year fellowship, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a roughly two-page proposal. The idea sits between the old Bongard problem literature and recent ML. Bongard problems, if you haven't seen them, are puzzles with six figures on the left that all instantiate some rule (say, every figure contains a circle) and six on the right that violate it, and the task is to infer the rule from those few examples and say which side a new figure belong on. The classic line, going back to Bongard himself and later picked up by Hofstadter's group in the 80s, was that solving these requires coming up with new perceptual descriptors on the fly — closedness, symmetry, "figure contains figure" — i.e., genuine concept induction, not pattern matching. Fast forward: people have built large synthetic benchmarks in this style (Bongard-LOGO, something like 12,000 programmatically generated problems rendered from turtle-graphics programs), and current vision models do okay on some splits and badly on others, which has reignited the argument about whether the remaining gap is "real concept learning" or just insufficient data.

Here's my half-formed idea, and this is where I want the actual conceptual work: everyone in this debate talks as if there's a clear difference between (a) a system that already has the right features in its repertoire and just has to find which combination separates the two boxes, and (b) a system that has to invent a new descriptor mid-problem. But nobody explains how you could tell those apart from the outside without already stipulating what's in the system's representational base — which seems circular, and for a neural net it's not even clear what "the base" is. So my thought is a project that (i) works out what a non-circular criterion for "representational novelty" would even look like — maybe something operational, maybe evidence from generalization to problems that share the deep structure but not the surface, maybe the honest conclusion is the distinction can't be saved and that's a finding too — and (ii) uses Bongard-style problems as the test case, since they're about the cleanest setting we have for few-shot concept induction.

What I need from you is an actual proposal document: a precise statement of the research question, why the existing positions (pattern-matchers, concept-inventors, and the people who think the whole framing is confused) leave it open, a month-by-month plan for the year that someone like me could really execute (I can run small experiments with existing models but I'm not training anything from scratch), the possible conclusions and what would decide between them, and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing — including the live possibility that the novelty distinction just collapses into behaviorism or unfalsifiability. No bibliography, I'll slot in references myself; you can gesture at "the standard accounts" without naming chapter and verse. And if you think the question needs reframing to be tractable in a year, say so and do the reframing, rather than politely planning an impossible project.
