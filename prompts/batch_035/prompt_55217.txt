For about fifteen years I've been the person in my rationalist circles who brings up the St. Petersburg paradox, and I've watched the same fight play out over and over. A quick statement of the setup so we're aligned: in the St. Petersburg game you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if heads first appears on the nth flip you are paid 2^n dollars. Every term in the expected-value sum equals one, so the expectation is infinite, and standard decision theory seems to say you should pay any finite price to play — which almost nobody accepts, since with probability 3/4 you walk away with $4 or less. The classical responses are a century old and everyone knows them: cap the utility of money (log utility, as the Bernoullians suggested), note that any real offer has a bounded bankroll behind it, invoke risk aversion or finite life expectancy. Fine. But the debate that interests me is a newer one, and as far as I can tell it has been completely static for about two decades.

Some years ago a pair of philosophers proposed a variant, sometimes called the Pasadena game, that in my view breaks the classical playbook. Keep the coin and the stopping rule exactly as before, but if heads first appears on flip n, you are paid (-2)^n / n dollars instead of 2^n dollars. The payoff is positive when n is even and negative when n is odd, and the expected-value series becomes the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 and so on: a series that converges, but only conditionally. It sums to ln 2, roughly 0.69, if you add the terms in the order given. But a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real number you like, or to diverge — and there seems to be no principled reason to privilege the order in which outcomes happen to be listed in the game description. So the expected value of the very same gamble appears to be whatever you want it to be. The standard resolutions of St. Petersburg don't even engage this. Log utility doesn't fix it, because the problem isn't that the expectation is infinite or that utilities are unbounded — it's that the expectation is not well-defined without an ordering convention, and ordering conventions are not something decision theory was ever supposed to supply. The people who press this point say the Pasadena game reveals a genuine lacuna in the theory: expectation is defined by a limiting process, and when that process is conditionally convergent the definition quietly fails. The other side says the lesson is smaller: an artifact of an idealization, a curiosity for the mathematicians, with no consequences for any decision an agent could actually face; truncate any real gamble and the pathology disappears.

As far as I can tell, nobody has moved since these arguments were first laid out, and I've started to suspect the standoff isn't really about decision theory at all. My hypothesis: the two sides are disagreeing about what it takes for an idealization's failure to matter — whether a flaw in the mathematical definition of expectation is a flaw in the theory, or just noise that shows up only in cases the theory was never intended to cover, the way a frictionless-plane model doesn't owe us anything at low Reynolds numbers. One side treats decision theory as a body of theorems that are exactly right or exactly broken; the other treats it as an engineering approximation whose domain excludes gerrymandered gambles. If that's right, the dispute is methodological rather than substantive, and no theorem about series or any new coin game will move it.
