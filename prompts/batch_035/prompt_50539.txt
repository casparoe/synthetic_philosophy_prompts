I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I keep catching myself having strong gut opinions about situations where my choice is one drop in a bucket of near-identical choices. I want to know if there's a principle behind them, because right now it just looks like mood. Cases:

- Voting in a big national election: my vote has essentially zero chance of mattering, and I know it, but not voting feels like freeloading. Verdict: I should vote.
- One-shot prisoner's dilemma against a random stranger online, no enforcement possible (think swapping something with a guy from a forum): I'd defect and feel nothing about it.
- Same dilemma, but my opponent is a bot trained to imitate my own decision-making: cooperate, obviously. Doesn't even feel like a decision, more like a fact about myself.
- Hofstadter's lottery from one of his Scientific American columns: readers send in a postcard with a positive integer on it, that counts as that many entries, and the prize is a million bucks divided by the total number of entries, winner drawn at random. The game-theoretic "smart" play is some astronomically huge number so you nearly guarantee winning a nearly worthless prize. I'm pretty sure submitting 1 is the only decent thing to do, and I think that's my actual verdict.
- But: if I somehow knew most readers wouldn't enter at all, submitting like a 5 instead of a 1 seems totally fine to me. These two verdicts about the same lottery don't feel like they should both be true.
- My roommates' "if everyone left the dishes for a week" argument has never moved me, ever. If everyone left them, we'd eventually do them, so what? That's a broken argument, everyone knows it. Except I vote for the exact same reason and think it's a good argument there.

So: is there one principle that says when I should act as if my decision is bound to similar deciders (voters, entrants, other mes) and when it's just mine alone? And if the principle is something like "tie your choice to people reasoning the same way you do," I need to know why it gets me into the lottery with a 1 and also gets the roommate argument laughed out of the kitchen. Feel free to tell me some of my verdicts have to go — I'd rather lose the dishes one than the voting one, but be honest about which is actually load-bearing. And tell me what the principle predicts for a case I didn't list: I'm matched in an anonymous one-shot game against a total stranger, but I know the whole player pool got there the same way and is a lot like me.
