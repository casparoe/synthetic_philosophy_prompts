The setup: every year we have to rank a list of programs for partial funding. This year the two finalists came down to (A) extended home ventilator support + round-the-clock aides for the ~30 most medically devastated residents in the county, versus (B) a mobile clinic that would meaningfully improve life for a few thousand residents who are struggling but nowhere near the bottom. I'm the resident prioritarian on the committee -- my view is basically that a benefit counts for more the worse off the recipient is. My colleague Dana is an egalitarian of the relations-between-people variety, and she came loaded for bear. Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. A distributive theory is unacceptable if there are circumstances in which it tells us to accept a pure or near-pure loss of overall good for the sake of the pattern it values.
2. Egalitarianism that values the equality of outcomes famously fails this test -- the leveling-down objection. If you level everyone down to the worst position, things get more equal, some people are worse off, and nobody is better off.
3. Prioritarians like me claim to pass the test because we never value a pattern at all -- we just weight benefits to the worse off more heavily, and a state where someone gains and nobody loses can still be ranked however the weighted math says.
4. But if the worse off get ANY extra weight, then in a crunch like this one, the prioritarian has to keep feeding the worst off first. Fund (A), not (B), even though the total good produced by (A) is a rounding error compared to (B).
5. So the prioritarian is also committed to destroying enormous amounts of good in order to keep honoring what she values -- priority to the worst off. That is leveling down in everything but name.
6. So the leveling-down objection refutes prioritarianism just as hard as it refutes outcome-egalitarianism, and the only consistent positions left are ones that weight everyone equally (straight maximization) or, like hers, ones that locate equality in how people treat each other rather than in how outcomes stack up.
7. And she'd add: her own relational version doesn't get touched by any of this, since she never claimed the shape of an outcome was good in itself.

Dana thinks this is airtight. Our third member thinks step 4 is where it breaks but couldn't say why beyond "it just obviously does," which convinced no one. I have a half-formed suspicion that the word "near" in step 1 is doing something shady -- leveling down proper involves nobody gaining, and in her case the thirty residents do gain, so it feels like she's smuggling the whole question (how big a gain justifies how big a loss) in through that one word. But when I said that she replied that if "near-pure" doesn't count then no loss short of literal annihilation could ever count against a theory, which seems too strong. I'd also like to keep step 1 if I possibly can -- it feels like the only moral anchor in the whole list.

If it matters: if her argument works, it seems to torch more than just me. Our committee already gives informal priority to the worst off in every triage-ish decision we make, and so does basically everyone's common-sense morality. Would be good to know if Dana has refuted all of that or just my fancy version of it.
