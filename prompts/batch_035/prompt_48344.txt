I've kept a running list of my old politics tutor's verdicts over the years. He is a lifelong historical materialist of the sternest sort, and for him the interesting question about any social phenomenon is always whether the economic base — the relations of production and the forces of production they rest on — explains it, or whether the superstructure is doing its own explanatory work. His verdicts so far:

- The adoption of standardized time zones in the nineteenth century: yes, the base explains it, and completely.
- The specific doctrinal content of Luther's theology of grace: no. But the rapid uptake of those doctrines among urban artisans and the lesser gentry: yes.
- The shift from the extended household to the nuclear family in industrial England: yes.
- The decade-by-decade cycling of Victorian skirt silhouettes: no.
- Football's becoming the global sport of the working class: yes. The offside rule in its current exact form: no.
- Why jazz became a commercial force in the 1920s: yes. Why people still listen to it a century later: no.
- Corporate wellness programs and mandatory mindfulness trainings: yes, straightforward labor discipline.

He has now been asked to comment on a paper by a younger colleague. The paper argues that the revival of astrology among university-educated people under thirty-five — the apps, the memes, Mercury retrograde entering ordinary conversation — is base-explained: prolonged economic precarity selects for fatalistic, self-soothing variants from an available stock of cultural material, exactly as the base selected among available religious variants in the sixteenth century.

Predict his verdict. In particular: identify the rules that actually fit his track record. I can see at least three candidates — a selection rule, on which the base explains which available variants spread but never their content or invention; a material-stakes rule, on which the base explains institutions that organize production and reproduction but not expressive culture; and a functional-requirement rule, on which something is base-explained only if the reproduction of the production relations could not proceed without it. Say whether each of these fits his verdicts as well as the alternatives do, whether there is a better candidate I've missed, what each rule predicts for the astrology case, and where exactly they come apart on it. Then commit: I want a definite prediction with your reasons for preferring the rule you think he is running, not a survey of options that leaves me to choose. Finally, tell me which further case — some cultural or institutional phenomenon I could put to him at the same session — would best discriminate among the candidate rules, and why that case rather than any other.
