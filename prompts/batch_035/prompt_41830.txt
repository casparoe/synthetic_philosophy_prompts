I've been drafting an essay, in stops and starts over about a year, on the difference between the things we do and the things we allow — between killing someone and letting them die — and I'd appreciate a serious critique of the core argument before I embarrass myself with the philosophy discussion circle I've joined at my local library. I have no academic background; the whole thing started from arguments with my sister, who is an ICU nurse, about whether switching off a ventilator is genuinely different from never having switched it on. The essay's central claim is what I've come to call the inertia view: what makes something a doing rather than an allowing is that a doer sets a new causal chain in motion toward the bad outcome, while an allower merely declines to interrupt a chain that was already running. On this view, the disease killing the patient is the chain that was already in motion; the ventilator was an interruption of it; so withdrawing the ventilator is refusing to keep interrupting, not starting a new chain — and by symmetry, never starting it can't be much different. I then try to extend this into a picture of people as, roughly, authors versus editors of the world's ongoing story: doctors, courts, and governments are constantly editing drafts of disaster that history is already writing, and there's something different about contributing a new page.

Where I suspect I've gone wrong is a counterexample I raise myself and try to fix, unconvinced by my own fix. Suppose someone is drowning in a lake ten feet from the dock I'm standing on, and a life preserver sits at my feet; if I simply watch them drown, most people feel that's much closer to doing than my inertia view says, even though the drowning was a chain already in motion and I merely failed to interrupt it. My attempted patch is that allowing only stays mere when interrupting is supererogatory or burdensome; when interruption is costless and you are the only one positioned to do it, your omission starts to look like authorship too. But I notice this patch threatens to swallow the whole distinction, since it seems to make everything come down to how much the rescue costs me, which is exactly what I wanted to avoid — and it makes the ventilator case wobble, since pressing a button to restart a machine is costless too. I also have a nagging feeling that my inertia idea is not original and collides with positions actual philosophers have discussed, possibly under names like the default view or something about options, so I'd like to know whether I'm reinventing a wheel, and whether the wheels already out there have the same flat spots.
